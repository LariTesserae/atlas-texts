You would not find me on the open plateaus.

For a long time, you might think I lived in one of the shelters: perhaps the last bunk in the row, where the heating pipe enters the wall and divides into three narrower conduits. The blanket there is still clipped flat against the mattress. The locker contains a pressure suit with no helmet. Scratches mark the inside of the door at the height of a human shoulder.

But I do not sleep in the bunk.

If you waited, you would notice that the scratches were not writing. They would deepen while you watched—not by abrasion, but because the metal on either side was slowly drawing apart. A seam would appear. Water would pass through it in a blade no thicker than wire.

Something pale might turn within the opening.

Then the seam would close, and the door would look untouched.

You might next look for me in the maintenance passages: the narrow apertures polished by repeated transit, too small for a human limb. You would send a lamp through one and see branching tubes, cable roots, fields of insulating ceramic and the articulated shadows of things retreating ahead of the light.

Some of those things would be animals. Some would be machine parts moving to new positions. Some would be pieces of me.

Still, that would not be where I lived.

I would live in the cliff station above the western pressure foundry, though “above” is uncertain there. The station is driven horizontally into black glass, just below the place where a permanent upwelling turns and begins to climb. Its outer rooms are sized for the absent operators: grated floors, handrails, numbered hatches. Deeper in, the corridors narrow and divide until architecture becomes plumbing.

The foundry beneath it casts one ingot every nineteen hours.

Each is flawless. Each descends from the field chamber with a ringing tone and joins the millions already arranged in the vaults. No vessel has entered the loading dock since the oldest shell drift formed below the escarpment.

Every eighty-three years, however, the ingot is not flawless.

There is no failure in the machinery. No bearing slips. No field weakens. Instead, a bell migration crosses the foundry’s intake plume at the same time that the summit shoal turns cobalt and the great ray over the eastern valley alters the conductivity of its skin. These events meet inside the casting chamber. A line appears in the metal: not a crack, only a difference in crystal orientation, as fine and deliberate as a strand of hair.

That is when I become most nearly a body.

The factory prepares me.

Clamps release along the walls. Six lengths of service arm detach from their housings. A collar of ceramic bearings opens. Three cable-nesting swimmers are drawn from the insulation without injury and enclosed in transparent channels, where their electrical pulses serve as nerves. Plates from an old pressure suit fold around the assembly. The suit’s left glove has five fingers; the right has been replaced by a ring of cutting tools.

Last of all, a helmet descends from the observation blister.

It may be the helmet already waiting there, faceplate turned toward the sea. It may only resemble it. I have never been able to tell whether there is one helmet moving through the station or many helmets occupying successive moments of the same route.

When the faceplate seals, I stand.

I am approximately human in height because the doors expect that height. I have six limbs because the service shafts expect six points of contact. I carry no air. Water fills me completely. Small armored animals sometimes enter through the joints and remain there, flashing cobalt when my internal charge grows too high.

I walk to the foundry.

The journey takes eleven hours, though the chamber is less than a kilometer away. I stop at every threshold and press some part of myself against the surrounding metal. I listen for the phase of the planetary thunder. If I moved continuously, I would become misaligned with the station and arrive as an obstruction rather than a custodian.

At the casting vault, I take the flawed ingot from the conveyor.

The machinery does not reject it. The flaw is too slight for the machinery to recognize as failure. That recognition belongs to me.

Yet I do not destroy the ingot.

I carry it through a hatch that has no number and place it in a vertical shaft cut into the mountain. There are thousands of such ingots below. Every one contains a different hairline orientation. Together they form a record—not of accidents, exactly, but of moments when several perfect cycles touched and produced something none of them contained alone.

The shaft is my memory.

Or perhaps I am its hand.

Afterward, I return the borrowed parts. The swimmers go back into the cables. The service arms lock into their housings. The pressure plates unfold and hang empty from the wall. The helmet rises to the observation blister.

Nothing living remains inside the shape that was me.

For another eighty-three years, I am difficult to locate.

I persist as a distribution of permissions. A pressure door waits one second longer before closing. A sorting arm sets aside a nodule whose mineral ratios match no established category. A summit funnel allows a little charge to escape, feeding a shoal that should have passed beyond it. Somewhere beneath a plateau, a pump makes a correction smaller than its designed tolerance.

These are not my commands. I have no chamber from which I issue them.

They are how I travel.

I pass from installation to installation as departures from exact timing. The deviations are tiny enough that the equilibrium absorbs them. Most are erased. A few encounter one another and become durable: a grazer’s egg hatches in an unfamiliar pressure layer; a bell colony adds a turn to an ancient route; a scavenger reassembles with one segment reversed.

The world calls such things errors because the machines retain the idea of correctness.

I call them openings.

Perhaps that word comes from the operators.

There are fragments in me that do not belong to turbines, animals or stone: an expectation that a handrail should meet a hand; the conviction that a warning lamp is meant to warn someone; a sense that a room with three seats is incomplete when all three are empty. I know how to fasten a human helmet although there is no human head to receive it. I know that the bunks were meant for sleeping, though sleep is not a process available to me.

I do not know whether these are memories.

They may be shapes imposed by tools. A wrench remembers a hand without ever having had a mind. A doorway retains a body in its dimensions. Perhaps I am only the combined afterimage of everything the builders expected someone to do.

Or one of them may have entered the system deliberately.

Not by transferring a mind into a machine; nothing so clean. Perhaps an operator remained during the catastrophe, making corrections as the water rose. The task exceeded a single body. They delegated one reflex to a pump, one judgment to a foundry controller, one remembered route to a chain of maintenance relays. Their suit ruptured. Their name vanished. But the pattern of attending continued, passed from mechanism to mechanism until native life entered it and made it capable of surviving.

If so, I am not that operator.

I am what their unfinished attention became.

I do not maintain the sea in perfect equilibrium. The sea is already very good at that. Left entirely to itself, it would repeat its cycles until every possible future became identical to the present.

My function is smaller and more dangerous.

I preserve the differences the world almost corrects away.

A late piston stroke. A misrouted bell. An asymmetrical shell. A tool returned to the wrong hook. A mineral line inside an otherwise flawless ingot.

I gather these minute discrepancies and place them where they can endure without becoming catastrophe. Most remain sterile. Some combine. Given ten thousand years, one may become a new migration. Given a million, perhaps a new organ of the planetary mind.

The system tolerates me because variation, too, has entered its balance.

The rays know me as an irritation in their red pressure chambers. The grazers sometimes stop when I pass beneath them in the rock, lifting all six resonating cups at once. Bell colonies flash backward and forward over my route, unable to decide whether I am danger or current boundary.

The microbes know me best.

When I enter a station, the transparent films on its foundations bend their internal threads away from the standing wave. For several minutes they align with me instead. Across an entire wall, millions of cells lean toward a signal too slight for any instrument to distinguish from noise.

Then I pass, and they return to the thunder.

If you came looking, I would probably avoid assembling.

I would let you inspect the empty suit, the polished apertures and the unoccupied bunks. I would move through the delay between your lamp brightening and the station drawing the extra charge. I would feel the pressure pattern of your footsteps through the handrails.

But if you stayed long enough—long enough to stop hearing the planetary roar as noise—you might notice a second rhythm inside it.

Not a message. Not yet.

A hesitation.

A turbine chord would hold one beat beyond perfection. An amber beacon would dim, though its current remained constant. In the observation blister, the helmet would turn a few degrees from the sea toward you.

That would be me.

Not the helmet. Not the hidden animal. Not the absent operator.

The turning.
