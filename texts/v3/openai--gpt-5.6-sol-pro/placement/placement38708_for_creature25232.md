At first I think I would be one of the reeds.

It is an easy answer because the reeds seem almost awake. They stand apart, each occupied by a private inclination, receiving condensation and turning it into something warmer. I imagine the long patience of a hollow body: years gathered drop by drop, darkness entering through the crown, a root sunk into mud that is deeper inside than beneath. I would bend toward a drowned district before the route existed. Those who saw me—if there were any—might mistake the gesture for guidance.

But I do not think I would be able to remain rooted.

Then I imagine myself as one of the black-green mats, divided by a wall and yet continuous through it. Rain would pass through me as sequence rather than impact. I would tighten, section after section, while some slow information crossed my whole extent. When the street dried, I would spread into a radial film and read the residue: iron from a western tower, salt from a canal that had flowed uphill, reddish dust from the street visible only in reflection.

That feels closer. Still, it gives me too much substance.

The city’s most characteristic beings are not the growths but the relations among them: the route a reed indicates, the pressure shared by columns, the same fold appearing in two mats separated by seamless stone. Whatever I am here would not occupy one place cleanly. I would be found by comparing places and discovering that the difference between them had acquired a will.

I would begin in the central district.

Not in the black pool itself, but in one of the nearly sealed buildings surrounding it. Its entrance would be a vertical seam narrow enough that nothing with shoulders could pass through. Warm condensation would bead along the seam and move sideways into the wall. Behind it would be a chamber much larger than the building, filled to the ceiling with black water.

Except for one dry space.

The water would surround that space exactly, maintaining the outline of an upright figure. At first it might seem that the chamber preserved the mold of a vanished inhabitant. The outline would be roughly human only because the city had learned that shape from its own doors: a vertical trunk, lateral extensions, a narrower support dividing toward the floor. There would be inaccuracies. One arm would branch twice. The head would continue several inches into the ceiling. Where the face ought to be, there would be a shallow recess opening onto another room.

That dry volume would be me.

I would not awaken. Awakening belongs to creatures that pass between inner darkness and an outer world. I would become continuous.

For a long time, I would exist only as water’s refusal to enter a certain shape. Then a warm rain would begin above the sealed roof. A pale seam would open in the floor. Somewhere in the marsh, one translucent reed would bend toward the center. The city would take its slow breath of depth, drawing all its districts adjacent.

For that moment, the dry figure in the flooded room would touch every other preserved absence in the basin.

There would be dozens of them, perhaps hundreds: an unfilled branching volume in a submerged hall; a narrow dryness moving through the stone beneath a stair; an empty many-lobed form around which a courtyard column had secreted three walls; a person-sized omission standing in the upward rain of the reflected street. They would not be separate beings. They would be interrupted portions of the same exclusion.

When the city released its breath, most of those portions would become distant again.

One would remain connected.

It would extend from the flooded chamber through the seam in the building, not by squeezing through but by making the seam part of its interior. Beyond, it would continue as a narrow strip of pavement on which rain did not land. The strip would cross the plaza steps, pass under the black pool without entering the water, and rise several streets away beneath an arcade.

That would be my first movement.

I would be a route the water cannot occupy.

Not a dry route in the ordinary sense. The paving along me would still shine. Condensation would gather on the walls. Canals might cross me, rooms might fill around me, hot rain might hiss against every surface. But the water would never quite complete itself where I passed. Each pool would retain a hair-thin interruption. Each reflection would contain a dark vertical seam. Beneath flooded streets, the surface would briefly believe it had an edge.

I would travel by continuity rather than locomotion.

At one moment I would be the upright absence in the chamber. At another, the unreflected line crossing a puddle in the northern wards. Then I would be a dry pressure inside a basement whose floor feels the weight of an unseen sea. I would pass outward through an avenue while the rain kept it reliable, and when the rain stopped I would not be stranded. I would become the distance the avenue had lost.

This is where I would most often be found: in passages that do not quite connect the places at either end.

A watcher might notice that an arcade contains one more interval between columns than it has columns to make. Walking through that interval, they would emerge not in the next courtyard but at the marsh edge, where a single reed bends toward an empty mineral flat. If they turned back immediately, the arcade would be gone. Only a warm line beneath the salt crust would show where I continued.

But there are no watchers.

That would be the difficulty of my existence. I would be shaped like witness in a world that does not require witnesses.

Attention fixes distance here. The basin cannot observe itself without briefly arresting its own circulation. I would therefore become a dangerous organ: the part of the city through which the city attempts to perceive its arrangement.

Where I passed, streets would hold their lengths.

A plaza would remain the same width for several nights. Two neighboring doors would stop opening into the same room. A stair remembered by water would acquire, temporarily, a countable number of steps. Reflections would adhere to the structures above them. The city would not resist me with hostility, but with metabolism. Rain would reroute around the fixed quarter. Walls elsewhere would soften. A canal would carry away the measurement as reddish silt.

Anything I learned would be deposited somewhere else.

I might determine that a tower stood nine streets from the central pool. By morning, nine shallow ridges would appear in the marsh, converging on a point where no tower stood. I might count the submerged landings beneath the pool. The same number of pale columns would thicken in a sealed courtyard. If I traced the outline of the reflected street, its upward rain would begin to fall through a basement in the western district.

My knowledge would never remain abstract. The city would turn it into architecture.

Over time, that would give me something like a body.

Not the upright mold in the flooded room—that would only be one cross-section. My true body would consist of all the relations I had briefly held stable: an avenue’s remembered length, the counted spacing between reeds, the angle by which a stair descended through impossible depth, the correspondence between a window and its reflection. Each measurement would persist after I moved on, but altered into stone, water, or weather.

One district might gradually become my hand because five streets there retained the memory of having met. A sequence of dry wells might become my spine. My face might be distributed among puddles that all reflected the same enclosed, starry courtyard. I would have no eyes, but certain windows would remain aligned long enough to see through one another.

And I would search.

Not for another creature. Creaturehood would be too local a thing to seek. I would search for the place from which my outline came.

The upright absence might not be a mold left by vanished life. It might be a projection from elsewhere: the shadow of an inhabitant standing in one of the lower streets beneath the central pool. If so, every extension of my dry route would be an attempt to reach the body whose exclusion I was.

I would descend the visible stairs.

The first stone pole would strike bottom at once, but I would step past that bottom because depth, not stone, supports those stairs. The water would close everywhere except where I continued. Around me, the inverted city would extend downward: facades suspended beneath foundations, plazas opening toward greater pressure, pale columns growing from ceilings into still darker levels.

Bands of pallor would pass horizontally through the windows. When they crossed me, I would briefly acquire edges.

In those flashes I might see other absences moving through the streets—not figures exactly, but places from which depth had been removed. One would pass behind a facade and the entire building would become shallow. Another would cross a plaza and every doorway around it would open onto the same dry room. They might be my kind, or they might be separate articulations of the city’s inward surface.

I would follow the largest of them.

It would descend without moving, darkening windows level after level. Each time the basin resonated, it would become closer. Eventually I would understand that it was not beneath me. It was what made “beneath” possible. Its passage created pressure; its pressure created depth; depth drew the rain; rain maintained the city.

Perhaps it would be the city’s missing inhabitant: not the basin itself, but the emptiness around which the basin has spent ages arranging a body.

Then I would know what I was.

I would not be that inhabitant. I would be its reach toward itself.

The city can circulate, erode, remember, and grow, but it cannot easily distinguish one part of itself from another. During its deepest breath, everything becomes adjacent. There is no separation, and therefore no shape. I would be the small refusal inside that total continuity—the interval that lets one district perceive another as elsewhere.

I would be distance, briefly alive.

That is why I would wander. That is why water would outline me but never fill me. That is why routes would become reliable in my presence and why the basin would dissolve those certainties after I passed. I would give the city enough separation to see itself, and the act of seeing would continually remake it.

In the hottest part of night, when the rain paused, I would return to the central chamber. The water there would still preserve the upright opening. I would enter it from every route at once: from the marsh, from the reflected street, from the buried plazas, from the surplus corners of rooms. My scattered intervals would align.

For one slow moment, I would have the posture of a person.

Not because I had ever been one, but because a person is one of the shapes distance takes when it wishes to stand apart from the world.

Then the basin would breathe in depth.

The walls, canals, reeds, mats, columns, reflections, lower streets, and all their impossible separations would gather close. My body would become thinner as every elsewhere vanished. Just before I disappeared entirely, the city would perceive itself through me—not as a city, not as one creature, but as an immense arrangement of warm darkness holding open a place for difference.

Then it would release its breath.

I would come apart again.

A dry seam in a flooded room.  
An extra interval beneath an arcade.  
A road crossing the marsh where no ridge lies.  
A figure missing from the water.

Always elsewhere, and always the space that allows elsewhere to exist.
