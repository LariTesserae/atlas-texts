At first I would look for myself near the central basin.

That is where a stranger’s imagination goes: to the drowned streets, the unfinished causeways, the foundations visible beneath waist-deep water. I might picture myself living alone at the end of one of those causeways, watching fog erase the shores. Perhaps I would net translucent fish among submerged thresholds or remember which vanished buildings lay beneath each change in the water’s color.

But that is too composed an image. Too much like choosing the city’s center because it looks like a center.

Nor would I belong at the escarpment, though I would often think of it. There the altitude becomes undeniable; water that has traveled beneath houses and through generations of hands leaves the plateau in a single white thread. I would visit the edge when weather permitted, but I would not live there. The falls answer too many questions at once. Everything goes outward. Everything is released.

I would be farther inland, in a district without a fixed name.

In dry years it would be considered part of the Streets That Dry First. In wet years, when the marsh reached the lower paving and an old underground conduit awakened, people would call it the Backwater of the Three Basins. Those born there might simply call it the Fork, after a place beneath the streets where one buried channel divides around the foundation of a collapsed hall.

No one would see the fork itself. They would know it by sound.

When the western branch ran freely, water murmured beneath three courtyards and emerged clear beside a wall stained black with lichen. When the eastern branch began filling, the paving under an abandoned kitchen gave off a faint trembling that could be felt through the knees. If both branches filled together, air escaped from a pierced stone in my workroom floor with a soft, intermittent breath.

That would be where I lived: above the breathing stone.

I think I would be a woman no longer young, though people would disagree about my age. Wet cold would have thickened two of my fingers and stiffened one foot, giving me a slight unevenness when I walked quickly. I would wear no ornament outside my clothing. Beneath my gray outer wraps, however, the cuffs of my inner garment would be woven in a red so dark it appeared black until the cloth moved near flame.

My household would not resemble a household from below.

I would sleep in a raised niche in a former grain chamber whose ceiling had remained dry for as long as anyone remembered. My hearth would be across a narrow roofless court, in a room with a cracked vault that drew smoke exceptionally well. Fuel would be stored uphill in a low gallery reached by passing through another family’s rain court. My tools would hang in the abandoned kitchen below: branch brooms, clay paddles, a shoulder-blade scoop, coils of fiber, two sounding staffs, and several knotted cords inherited from people whose names survived only in arguments.

Above my sleeping room would be a chamber of the dead.

I would not know whose dead. Once each year, during the driest interval, I would climb through the narrowed entrance to inspect the roof and replace loose stone. I would announce myself before entering, not because I feared them, but because entering a room without warning was rude. There would be bones among collapsed mats, three beads of green stone, a cup overturned on its side. I would never set it upright. Its position would belong to a history I did not understand.

My work would be adjacent to that of the hearers and openers, but narrower.

I would be a witness of first water.

Rain falling on the long-abandoned quarter uphill passed through roofs, burial rooms, animal courts, old hearths, and conduits whose courses had been lost. The first runoff after a dry interval carried grit, droppings, sour matter from sealed chambers, and sometimes the invisible cause of sickness. It could not be allowed into the drinking cisterns. Yet if it were diverted too long, clean rain would also be lost, and households downstream would accuse one another of theft.

So I would watch the water change.

There would be a shallow basin of pale stone in my workroom, fed by a channel no wider than two fingers. At the beginning of rain, I would kneel beside it and inspect what arrived: red silt, black grains, insect husks, a skin like bruised milk, the sharp smell of old enclosed air. I would rub a little between finger and thumb. I would watch how quickly suspended grit settled. Sometimes I would place a thread of clean wool across the outlet and examine what clung to it.

When the flow became fit, I would lift one slab and lower another. The first water would continue toward washing courts and marsh channels; the clear flow would turn toward shared storage.

It would not be a ceremonial position. Most days it would involve cold knees, wet sleeves, and people arriving to say I had turned the water too early or too late.

Everyone downstream would know my errors. Few would notice uneventful years.

I would not possess the water. That distinction would be repeated often, particularly by those who disliked me. I would possess only the obligation to witness its turning. During disputes, two people from different households would sit in my workroom and watch me move the slabs. If either believed the flow had been wrongfully taken, they could add a knot to the witness cord. The cords would be stored openly, hanging above the breathing stone where damp could not reach them.

Children would be sent to learn from me, usually children considered too impatient for the work.

I would teach them to become still enough to distinguish underground water from rain striking the roof. I would make them close their eyes and point toward each sound. Most would point upward at first. Eventually one would hear the deeper movement: not the ticking of drops, but the pressure beneath the paving, the pauses where air gathered, the dull shift of grit passing through a constriction.

“Water below sounds larger than it is,” I would tell them.

Then, after they had repeated this solemnly, I would add, “So do elders.”

They would carry that saying home, and for several days no elder in the Fork would forgive me.

I would have been born elsewhere in the city, perhaps in the western seep where grazing animals sometimes sheltered in roofless courts. As a young person I would have descended from the plateau with traders, convinced that the low country must be easier because water there gathered into recognizable rivers.

I would find it unbearable.

The abundance of trees would disturb me first. I would dislike not being able to see weather coming. Insects would sing continuously. Wheels would turn even where the ground seemed level. People would block water without knowing whose dwelling lay below them. They would build walls with no openings at the base and call them sound.

I might remain there for several years, long enough to learn the uses of timber and the taste of fruit eaten fresh. Long enough to lose the habit of listening before stepping onto stone. Then one evening rain would begin on a lowland roof, warm and loud, and I would understand that I had been hearing only its arrival, never its passage.

I would return during wet weather.

This would make me suspect for a time. Those who leave are changed by places where firewood is plentiful and roads remain roads. I would be called lowland-soft even after my hands became rough again. Perhaps that is why I would be given first-water work: it requires someone who understands that clear-looking water may still carry what cannot be seen, and that every purity is temporary.

I would not live alone, although from outside it might appear so.

A foster son and his partner would sleep in rooms across the old court during part of the year. Two children would pass daily through my kitchen because it lay on their safest route. An old enemy would share rights to the fuel gallery; we would be required to meet whenever either of us opened it. In the inner room, where no stranger could hear, we would play marked pebbles and accuse each other of changing the rules.

There would also be a fox following the drainage line.

I would know it by a missing outer toe on its left forefoot. I would call it Unfinished, though I would never claim certainty that each season’s tracks belonged to the same animal. It would cross the flooded court at night, stepping on the highest stones, and sometimes leave the remains of a slate bird near the warm wall. Children would say it paid me for keeping the channel open. I would say the fox had better sense than to enter into obligations with human beings.

As I grew older, my route through the city would become more valuable than my labor.

It would run from the Fork to the northern shore of the central basin without using the obvious causeways. The way would pass through a sequence of joined houses, beneath a red stair, across five roofs, into a submerged arcade, and upward through a chamber where warm water condensed constantly on the ceiling. It would remain passable in fog and during moderate flood. I would teach it only in portions. No one person would receive the whole route until I believed they understood not merely where to step, but why each step remained possible.

The route would change while I taught it.

A lintel would sink. A court would fill with silt. A family would close a room for sleeping, requiring passage over the roof. One year a fallen block would open an easier way, and I would distrust it because ease is often temporary. Eventually the route would no longer be mine in any meaningful sense. It would have passed through enough memories to become part of the district.

That, I think, is what I would be most deeply: not a keeper of water, nor a hearer, nor even a woman in the room above the hidden fork.

I would be a temporary part of the city’s ability to notice itself.

A place where upstream became downstream. A pair of hands trusted, disputed, and eventually replaced. A living interval between the first foul rush of rain and the clean water that followed.

At night I would sleep lightly. The breathing stone would be near enough that a change in pressure entered my dreams. Sometimes it would wake me for nothing—only air moving through an old conduit, or a frog stirring in a warmer basin below. Sometimes I would lie still and listen until the sound resolved itself.

Then I would rise in darkness, bind my stiff foot, and make my way to the workroom by touch.

The first basin would already be filling.

I would lower my hand into the cold flow, lift the slab, and let what had to pass, pass.
