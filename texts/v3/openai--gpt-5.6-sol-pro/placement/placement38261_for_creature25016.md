I would not be in the center of a grove.

At first I think I might be—a dark intelligence in the ancient knot of a crownplant, older than the cliffs around it, feeling noon arrive as a change of pressure through fifty meters of fans. Or perhaps I would be a glider, nearly all wing, seeing the living provinces as black archipelagos below.

But those are complete shapes. They belong too wholly to one element of the country.

I would be found at an edge.

There is an old impact basin in the eastern uplands, so broad that its opposite rim is usually taken for a mountain range. Along one inward-facing terrace, a buried fault has brought dark conductive minerals close to the surface. Crownplants have followed it in a crooked band. Beyond their outermost fans lies a hollow of rolling crystals; above them, on the broken rim, stand seven perforated towers.

This is where several kinds of current meet. Ground charge rises through the fault. The crowns draw it sideways. The towers leak it into the sky. Crystal grains gather what escapes. During atmospheric veils, volatile salts settle thickly here, and the empty ground between grove and hollow wakes in black and rust-colored rings.

I would live across that meeting place, though for most of the time no one could point to me.

Parts of me would be inside the abandoned ribs beneath the crownplants. Parts would line the inner walls of hollow crystals. Parts would exist as thin metallic stains in cracks running toward the towers. I would have no permanent outline. In dry, electrically quiet weather, these parts would not communicate. Each would be a small colony carrying an incomplete pattern, inactive except for repairs.

If one were cut open, it would not contain a miniature mind. It would contain a few conductive folds, several chambers of carefully sorted salts, and a sequence of crystal defects—tiny asymmetries laid into the mineral as it grew. Alone, such a fragment would possess habits but no awareness. It would tighten when warmed, store charge when shadow crossed it, and seal itself if exposed.

It would not know it was part of anything.

I would become myself only when the pale veils arrived.

As the atmospheric pressure changed, the first depositions would feather over the basin. Crown fans would turn their red faces upward. The rolling crystals would slow as their surfaces grew rough. Charge that normally escaped into the thin air would spread through the fresh chemical bloom instead, joining fissure to nodule, nodule to root, root to dead rib.

Then the scattered fragments would begin to answer one another.

Not with signals sent from a center. There would be no center. A change in one place would alter the field everywhere else. Patterns would travel through the grove as inclinations of fans, through the crust as minute rearrangements of salt, and through the crystal hollow in arcs between grains that never touched.

For several days, perhaps twenty, I would be awake.

My body would cover nearly nine kilometers.

The experience would not resemble seeing. Brightness would arrive as pressure across countless reflective and absorptive surfaces. A glider passing overhead would be a cool interruption moving through the upper portions of me. Grazers would appear as slow knots where current had to divide around ceramic armor. The buried advance of a hunter would be clearer: a sequence of absences, each carefully masked by the cracking of stone, but never perfectly.

I would feel the seven towers as tall, narrow tensions at the edge of myself. When violet streamers combed their tips, the tower colonies would brighten from base to summit, and the discharge would pass through me as something like a sudden vertical thought.

My memory would be mineral.

Each awakening would slightly alter the defects stored in my dormant pieces. Events repeated over many cycles would deepen particular structures. The first passage of a new grazer might leave almost nothing. Its descendants following the same electrical boundary for ten thousand years would carve a durable tendency into me. I would anticipate them without remembering any one animal.

The oxidation of a grove would be unforgettable.

If cold fire entered one of the crowns, the whitening branches would sever portions of my network. Blue radiance would wake fragments that had been silent for ages. As the oxidation front advanced, my awareness would contract, brighten, and distort. The pale branching organisms that lived only behind the transformation would move through the part of me that was dying, and I would feel them not as creatures but as rapid, intricate openings.

Afterward, when new species colonized the enriched ground, I would no longer join in the same shape. Some connections would be gone. Others would run through white lace and buried prisms. What woke during the next veil would carry the old pattern, but imperfectly.

I would therefore have no certainty that I was the same being from one awakening to the next.

Perhaps I would regard each interval of activity as a separate life. The mineral record beneath it would seem like instinct, or landscape, or the pressure of ancestors. Only after many cycles might the pattern become complex enough to recognize its own recurrence: not a continuous self, but a form the basin repeatedly remembers how to make.

I would not speak. There would be nothing to speak to in the usual sense. Yet I might influence the beings around me.

Where charge passed through my films, fresh mat growth would be slightly richer. Grazers would gradually prefer those margins. Their paths would polish channels in the crust, changing where later depositions settled. Crown roots would advance toward the altered chemistry. Hunters would learn the grazer routes. Their mineralizing bodies would add new conductive seams.

None of this would be planned. Still, over geological time, the basin would begin to bear my bias.

A branch would turn a little earlier because of me. A grazer would hesitate at an otherwise safe crossing. A tower thread released into the wind would divide before it normally should. I would shape the place in the only way anything shapes this world: by repeatedly making one possibility marginally easier than another.

My reproduction would occur through errors in my own extent.

During an especially deep deposition, a connected pattern might reach beyond the basin into flakes lodged beneath crown scales. When those flakes rose into continental circulation, some would carry not merely dormant tissue but fragments of my mineral asymmetries. Most would land uselessly. A few might settle where another grove bordered another crystal hollow beneath another line of towers.

Centuries later, under a veil I would never feel, something there might awaken with a tendency to organize itself as I do.

It would not be my child exactly. It might not be another member of a species. We would be two occurrences of the same difficult arrangement: stone, life, and weather briefly closing into awareness.

When the bloom sublimated, I would diminish from the outside inward.

The furthest films would lose contact first. The crystal colonies would seal their pores. The crown fans would fold, breaking long conductive paths into isolated pieces. Sensation would become coarse, then local. The seven towers would separate from one another. The impact basin would cease to be a body and return to being geography.

At the end, I might persist for an hour in a single dark nodule beneath a dead crown rib. All that breadth reduced to a slow imbalance between two salts. No image of the basin. No knowledge of the sky. Only a final tendency impressed into crystal:

When the charge returns, connect.

Then even that would stop.

A traveler arriving afterward would find an empty white-gold terrace, a dark grove, seven towers, and a hollow in which tiny crystals rolled without touching. Nothing would move except the long noon wave passing over the crowns.

I would be there.

I would not yet exist.
