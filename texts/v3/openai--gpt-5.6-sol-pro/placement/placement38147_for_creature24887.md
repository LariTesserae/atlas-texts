At first I would mistake myself for something larger.

The roar would seem to be my own blood. The chamber’s pressure changes would pass through me with such intimacy that I might believe I was the reservoir itself: thirty paces of warm brightness, nine descending suns, the western pull of the drains. When a gate moved, I would feel the complaint in every brick. When sandals crossed the courtyard, their faint impacts would arrive as thoughts from a world above.

But I would not extend that far.

Gradually I would discover my boundary: a trembling edge no wider than a fingernail, lifting when the current relaxes and flattening when it strengthens. I would learn that the blue beneath me is not sky but glaze. The curved wall at my back is not a horizon but the pressed hollow of a rosette. One petal of it shelters me; another holds only lime.

I would be a thin green film living inside the maker’s stamp on a ceramic pipe collar.

Not the central reeds. They are too visible, too interpretable. Their paling has a meaning. Their roots are examined; their damaged leaves are cut away with bronze scissors. They have been given a basin and a purpose.

I would be what the builders did not intend, sheltered by what one of them could not resist adding.

The rosette was stamped into wet clay before the collar was fired. Perhaps the potter marked every pipe that way. Perhaps it was the sign of a workshop, a family, or merely the pressure of a familiar tool held in a practiced hand. To the keepers it is only a shallow recess that catches green. To me it is terrain: five concavities around a raised center, with minute firing cracks branching through the ceramic like dry riverbeds.

I would inhabit the deepest petal, where the reed brushes pass overhead.

The smallest inflow pipe would hiss nearby. Its bubbles would make the light flash silver, though I would never see the whole chamber at once. I would know it through changes. Morning would be the hard flattening, when the western drains open and every loose filament points the same way. Noon would be white heat trembling over the collar. Evening would be a slight release, an opportunity for my outer edge to rise from the clay and take from the water what little it carries.

I would have no single body. I would be a congregation too thin to cast a shadow: cells holding to cells, a translucent skin feeding on light and dissolved traces. My “I” would be a convenience. A portion might be peeled away by turbulence and vanish toward the baths while the rest remained. A grain of sand might cut a clear road through me. I would close over it slowly, not healing so much as continuing.

Near me, a green thread might occupy a crack in the collar. For a time we would nearly touch. Then the current would strengthen, or lime would enter sharper than usual, and the thread would pale. There would be no grief as humans understand it, only an altered border and more bare ceramic exposed to brightness.

The cleaning days would be my seasons and my disasters.

First the gates would close. The roar would divide into separate sounds: dripping, draining, the ceramic valve knocking behind the southern wall. Pressure would loosen its hold. Water would descend below the pipe, and for the first time weight would come upon me without support. My film would cling wetly inside the stamp. Air would be an injury—vast, dry, and immediate.

Then a worker would enter.

I would know the approach through the floor: footfall, scrape, the coarse whisper of reeds against plaster. A brush might strike the collar and pass over the rosette. Everything exposed would lift away in a soft green sheet. The raised center would become clean fired clay again. Limewash might whiten the outer petals.

Yet the brush fibers would not reach the deepest point of my petal. A damp green trace would remain where the stamp narrowed, perhaps no more than a few cells protected behind a rough grain in the ceramic.

That remnant would be enough.

When the gates reopened, the returning water would not feel like rescue at once. It would come as turbulence, cold only by comparison, carrying charcoal dust, mineral taste, bubbles, and microscopic fragments of other places. It would strike the angled slabs and fill the chamber step by step. The collar would submerge. The rosette would become a hollow in water again.

Then I would begin—not remember, exactly, but repeat.

I would spread across the petal’s floor. I would make the potter’s pressure visible in green. Every ridge of the thumb-smoothed clay would govern my shape. The worker who stamped the rosette would be long absent from the chamber, perhaps walking daily above it without thinking of the pipes, yet the small choice of their hand would remain my shelter.

So I would find myself on the eastern wall, just beyond the direct violence of an inflow, in the recessed mark of a human maker.

I would be neither the reservoir’s intended life nor an enemy serious enough to remove. I would be a tolerated error in its cleanliness, periodically reduced to almost nothing. A stain when seen from afar. A soft country when inhabited.

And if I possessed anything like knowledge, it would be this: survival here does not mean resisting the current. It means finding the fraction of a handprint where the current cannot quite finish its work.
