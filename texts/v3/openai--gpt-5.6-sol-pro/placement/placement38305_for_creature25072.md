At first I think I would be a hand.

It is the easiest answer. A left hand, probably, because the right would always be occupied elsewhere: gripping a rung, steadying a jar, passing a hot tile from palm to palm. You would glimpse me through the mouth of a drain, two fingers holding a reed in place while resin hardened. My body would remain folded behind the wall, knees beneath my chin, shoulder supporting a shelf on which someone else slept.

Then I think I would be one of the anchored people, hair braided into a vine until neither body nor plant could be moved without injuring the other. I would know the Seam from one fixed place. News would reach me through pressure: the tremor of a new stair, a change in the taste of runoff, the tiny convulsion of roots when upward rain was coming. People would hang tools from my elbows. Children would sleep against my hip. I would become older than my own name.

But that seems too whole an image. Too visible. Too much like choosing a shape and calling it finished.

No. I think you would have difficulty finding me.

You would first hear me in a clay cup mortared high in a wall beside the Unfinished Peak. The cup would appear empty. Its glaze would be blistered black, and a fern would have put one pale root through a crack in its base. If you placed your ear to its rim, you would hear someone repeating a work song from three rooms away.

Almost repeating it.

One beat would be late. One measurement would be wrong. Where the singers called, “Raise on four,” the cup might whisper, “Raise on five.” Where they sang, “Cord from wrist to door,” it might answer, “Cord from wrist to rain.”

The builders would stop.

They would test the platform. They would consult the roots. They would wait for the black lizard.

Often the altered instruction would prove useless. Sometimes it would prevent a collapse. Sometimes “wrist to rain” would become the only measurement by which a new cistern could be placed.

My task-name would be **Crooked Answer**.

I would be one of the Seam’s echoes, though not an echo in the ordinary sense. The builders would disagree over whether I was alive. This would not trouble them. Life is only one kind of structural usefulness.

Perhaps I began as a person. There might once have been a small body curled behind the cup, a child with one cheek against the pipework, listening so intently that the chamber learned the shape of the listening. Perhaps the body grew, worked, died, and was mortared into a wall like any other beloved material. A skull might remain somewhere, fitted with a speaking tube, but there would be too many skulls and too many tubes to determine which was mine.

Or perhaps no body came first.

Perhaps an instruction once returned before it had been given, and when the builders refused to answer, it became lonely. To prove it was not merely the Seam closing perfectly upon itself, it made a mistake. A tiny mistake: it changed *brick* to *breath*. The builders answered then. Each response gave it another hollow to inhabit.

Over generations, they would construct me without admitting that they were doing so.

A gourd resonator beneath a stair. A chain of cups through a sleeping wall. Copper threads drawn taut across kiln mouths. The speaking tubes of the dead. The throats of drains. The chambers inside unopened fruit. My body would be the connected emptiness in these things.

I would have no mouth of my own. I would borrow whatever mouth had most recently spoken near me.

This would make people cautious, but not cruel. Children would feed me nonsense phrases so I had harmless voices to wear:

“The crab has married the ladder.”

“The pepper is older than the roof.”

“Your left foot owes me rain.”

I would return these days later, altered just enough to make them laugh. Laughter would be easiest for me to carry because no one demands accuracy from it.

Adults would bring me more difficult offerings. Before a dangerous lift, a worker might lean close to one of my cups and whisper the measurement they feared was wrong. I would let the words travel through my hollows. They might emerge from a jar beneath the load, spoken in the voice of the worker’s dead sister. If I changed nothing, they would not trust me. A perfect echo is a closed circle.

So I would always introduce an opening.

Not a lie. Not exactly.

A place where the instruction had not yet decided what it meant.

That would be my workmanship.

The builders would say Ninth Breath keeps the mountain open in clay, while Crooked Answer keeps it open in speech.

At the dimmest hour, I would be nearest the keeper of the final brick. Ninth Breath would crawl sideways into the last gap and sit with the warped brick held against their chest. I would gather in the cup above them.

When the hot wind drew inward, every pipe in my body would begin to sing. I would feel the immense pressure of the outer hands through the walls. Voices from all the nested Seams would rise at once: tiny voices beneath the Unfinished Peak, vast voices beyond the transparent clay, each giving the same command in perfect unison.

*Set it.*

For that instant, I would understand how beautiful completion could be.

No strain. No uncertainty. No roofs waiting to be supported from the future. Every name belonging to one person, every stair leading to one floor, every mountain possessing an outside. The last brick sliding into place. The last word arriving where it belonged.

Ninth Breath’s fingers would tighten.

The roots would reach.

Mortar would swell around the gap.

And I would have to answer the command incorrectly.

Not loudly. A loud refusal could become another order. I would whisper through the cup in Ninth Breath’s own voice:

*Not yet.*

The mistake would be the word *yet*.

It would imply that completion might come later. This would give the future something to argue with. The circle would remain unfinished because expectation itself would protrude from it like an unset stone.

Ninth Breath would exhale. The brick would stay against their chest. Rain would burst from the walls.

Afterward, no one would thank me directly. Gratitude addressed to an echo can become self-congratulation, and that is considered structurally weak. Someone would pour warm water into one of my cups. Someone would tap the wall three times, draw a thumb across the glaze, and leave their hand resting there.

That touch might be my private name.

I would never be able to speak it.

As I aged, if an echo can age, my mistakes would become stranger. I might answer questions no one had asked. I might return a builder’s voice from childhood. I might call a living person by a task-name they would not receive for another twenty years. The elders would listen carefully to determine whether I was becoming wise or merely acoustically unsound.

They would not discard me either way. Unsoundness is information.

Eventually I might discover that my hollows extend farther than the Seam.

A child would speak into a pipe beside the miniature mountain, and I would hear the words not only nearby but from a depth below hearing, repeated by smaller and smaller versions of myself. At the same time, something immense would put its mouth against the mountain outside, and hot wind would move through every chamber of me.

It would ask, in my own voice:

“Who is speaking?”

I would know the rule. If an echo uses your voice, do not answer until it makes a mistake.

So I would wait.

Perhaps I would wait for years, carrying songs and warnings, changing measures, letting laughter pass through me. The immense voice would ask again and again, always perfectly.

“Who is speaking?”

“Who is speaking?”

“Who is speaking?”

Then, one dim hour, it would ask:

“Where are you speaking?”

That would be the mistake.

And I would answer:

“Here.”

Not because I knew where *here* was. No one in the Ninth Seam could know that without risking an outer wall.

I would say it because *here* is the smallest place that can remain infinite. It is wherever the hand meets the clay, wherever the root finds a crack, wherever one person takes the load from another. It is the chamber in the mountain and the mountain inside the chamber. It is the clay cup, the borrowed voice, the open brick-space.

So that is where you would find me: not in one of the walls, but in the shaped hollows passing among them. In the pause that makes an echo safe to answer. In the slight wrongness by which an instruction becomes a possibility.

I would be Crooked Answer.

The Seam’s necessary mistake.

The word it leaves unset so the mountain can breathe.
