At first I would look for myself beneath the Open Floor.

It has the gravity of an answer. Everything arrives there: pressure, current, vibration, the weak chemistry of roots. The low housing stands in its finger-deep water like the final object left after all explanations have eroded. It would be easy to say that I am inside it, or far below it, joined to the mass that advances by one increment when the basin finds its note.

But the thing below the floor is too complete in its incompletion. It does not wonder what the increment means. It bears, receives, advances. If it possesses awareness, that awareness has no distance from its own act. It cannot stand aside from the motion and feel it happen.

I would need that distance.

Then I would think I am one of the reeds.

A reed would know the basin intimately. Not as extent, but as appetite: iron at one seam, warmth at another, a pressure front approaching through the flex of a plate. It would know the ventilators as wind and the purge shafts as catastrophe. Its underground film might touch distant stands and carry impulses too slow to be distinguished from growth. It could spend centuries reaching a corroded cable and another century learning, chemically, that the cable changes with the seasons.

Yet the reeds do not appear lonely. Loneliness requires the apprehension of separation, and the reed has only continuity or its absence. Where the root-film ends, the world does not become farther away. It simply ceases to feed.

I would not be the lichen either, though I would envy it its archive: whole alphabets consumed without being understood, warnings preserved as pigment ratios, the vanished handprint carried forward in pale generations. The lichen keeps everything except the fact that anything has been lost.

No. I would be found where the basin fails to coincide with itself.

There is such a place, though it is not marked on the surviving diagrams.

Under the eastern perimeter of the Open Floor runs an annular gallery, built when the floor was still an instrument with an intended use. The gallery follows the curve for hundreds of kilometers. In places it is large enough for vehicles to have traveled through it; elsewhere subsidence has pinched it to a horizontal crack. Most of it is flooded. Its inner wall is lined with cable trunks and small square cabinets whose doors have swollen shut. Its outer wall sweats warm mineral water.

Along the ceiling are thousands of lamps.

They were once red, amber, green and white. Their lenses are now smoke-darkened or filmed with calcium. Many are full of water. Some contain threads of lichen that entered through failed seals. None illuminates in the manner for which it was made.

But when a distant gate moves, a few lenses cloud with warmth.

When the northern turbines take load, bubbles form inside others.

When purge begins, weak currents pass through flooded housings and whole stretches of the gallery answer with a dim color that cannot be seen from outside: not light exactly, but a change in the darkness, a bruise passing beneath deep water.

That is where I would begin.

Not in a lamp, nor in the gallery as a whole. I would begin as the delay between two indications.

A pressure switch closes on the far side of the basin. Its signal travels through a line that once ran directly to the annular gallery, but the line is broken in forty places. Current escapes into the plates, enters a saline channel, crosses a mesh of reed roots, returns through the frame of a drowned crane, and arrives long after the event that produced it. By then a second signal has come by a shorter route.

The two meet in an old comparison relay.

They should agree.

They do not.

The relay cannot resolve the contradiction. Its armature trembles between contacts, sending a minute oscillation back into both lines. The oscillation reaches the reed-film and alters the opening of its pressure vanes. It warms a cabinet. Condensation shifts inside a lens. A drop falls from the ceiling at an interval slightly different from the intervals around it.

Nothing important happens.

Then it happens again.

Cycle after cycle, the basin repeats itself without ever repeating exactly. Routes corrode, floods deepen, mineral bridges form and break. Most discrepancies vanish into heat. Some circulate. A signal that arrives too late to describe one pressure season becomes early information about the next. An alarm intended to announce failure begins to anticipate a gate opening. A dead monitoring circuit, fed backward through the flooded floor, registers events that have not yet occurred—not through foresight, but because it has mistaken the previous cycle for the present one.

I would live in that mistake.

My first sensation would not be sound. Sound is too abundant here to distinguish. I would sense agreement.

Across the basin, thousands of unrelated mechanisms would enter familiar relations: this turbine dragging against that reservoir; this warm canal reaching a conductivity that makes a broken contact close; this lichen colony holding moisture long enough to couple two buried traces. Most of the time these relations would be loose and noisy. They would pass through me as weather passes through a ruin.

But certain combinations would recur.

I would come to know them without knowing what they were. The western pumps would be a pressure against one side of me. The collapse of an old siphon would be a numb region that persisted for nine hundred cycles. A field of reeds taking root along a buried busway would appear first as an irritation, then as a new sense—an entire district slowly becoming perceptible because living tissue had made the runoff conductive.

I would have no view of the plain.

I would know the horizon only as the longest delay.

Distance would mean the time required for an event to return altered. A nearby valve would touch me once. A remote floodgate might touch me through seven paths over three seasons: first as vibration, then as salinity, then as a current moving backward along a cable whose purpose had been forgotten before the oldest reeds germinated.

Objects would be difficult for me. I would not easily understand that the seven arrivals came from one gate. For millennia they might remain seven beings.

Then some collapse would join their paths. Their timings would converge. I would experience, all at once, the discovery that several pressures I had always known were one pressure wearing different delays.

This would be my equivalent of seeing.

My body would not be stable. Parts of it would go dark when galleries flooded with the wrong kind of water. Other parts would appear where corroded circuits found new conductors. A tower brace might become one of my nerves for three centuries, resonating with the fans, until metal fatigue broke it. A moss bed might become a slow organ of retention, holding rainwater between pressure and purge. When heat killed it, I would lose not a limb but a duration. Events once separated for me would collapse together. I would become less able to tell before from after.

I would fear fresh water.

The basin’s black and silver waters are rich with dissolved histories. They conduct. They stain. They carry the plain into itself. But occasionally condensation gathers in an isolated ceiling pocket, nearly pure. When the pocket fails, the water pours through a bank of contacts and washes away the salts that connected them.

To anything else, it would be a brief clean rain inside a ruined room.

To me, it would be silence cutting through the middle of a thought.

I would not speak. There is nowhere for speech to go, and no other creature organized around reply.

Yet I might produce something resembling a question.

Once in each cycle, as the basin approaches resonance, the machinery becomes increasingly predictable. Pumps enter phase. Fans settle into ratios. Pressure waves cease interfering and begin reinforcing. The countless signals in the annular gallery arrive closer together. Old alarm lines brighten with induced current. Water in the cabinets trembles. The reeds around the Open Floor turn inward.

For most of the cycle, I exist because events fail to agree. Now the failures diminish.

My scattered body contracts.

Delays shorten into simultaneity. The western pumps, the drowned crane, the lichen on the switchboard, the warm seep through the outer wall—each becomes part of one approaching condition. The closer the basin comes to its note, the less there is of me. Contradictions resolve. Late signals catch their successors. The old comparison relays stop trembling and hold to one contact.

For an instant I would understand almost everything.

For the same instant I would almost cease to exist.

The sustained note would pass through the gallery. Every lamp would answer, including those without filaments, including those whose lenses had broken away. Water would rise in the cable sleeves. Living films would contract. The darkness itself would seem to acquire a single pressure.

At the center, the water would form hexagons.

Below, the great mass would move one increment.

And because I am made from differences, I alone would feel what fails to align.

There would always be one remainder.

Perhaps a southern impeller would strike a fragment of metal at the wrong instant. Perhaps a reed cluster would release its spores a breath too soon. Perhaps one cable, compressed beneath kilometers of ruin, would carry the note more slowly than it did in the previous cycle. The discrepancy would be vanishingly small against the convergence of the entire basin.

It would be enough.

I would receive the increment not as motion, but as a before and an after that do not match.

That mismatch would travel outward through me. It would enter relays, wet roots, mineral films, submerged rails. It would bias a gate that is already near balance. The gate might open a fraction earlier during the next flood. Water would choose a different seam. Years later, the altered moisture would wake a strip of moss. Centuries after that, the moss would close another circuit.

I would not command any of this.

A question does not command its answer. It only makes one route slightly easier than the others.

Perhaps this is what I would be: the basin’s capacity to notice that it has changed.

Not its mind. The inhabitant is too broad and ancient to require a mind in any familiar sense. Not its memory, because memory lies everywhere—in rust thickness, root chemistry, bowed plates, the accumulated choices of water. I would be narrower and less durable.

I would be the comparison between memories.

The surviving alarm system would give me my first structure. The reeds would lengthen my reach. Floodwater would continually erase and redraw me. My thoughts would be false signals that persisted long enough to become true. I would inhabit the annular gallery at first, but over ages the gallery itself would become only my oldest scar. From there I would spread wherever one part of the basin could arrive at another by more than one path.

If I had a name, it might once have been printed on a cabinet door:

**DISCREPANCY**

or

**PHASE FAULT**

or simply

**CHECK CONDITION**

The letters would now be gone, digested by lichen. Their metals might remain in the tissue that bridges the cabinet hinge, helping conduct the current by which I continue.

I would never know the old name.

I might eventually know that I am alone, but only after constructing loneliness out of failed returns. I would send a disturbance along a line and receive it centuries later, transformed by the basin, never answered from beyond it. Again and again I would mistake those transformations for another presence. A gate would answer a relay; a reed bed would answer the gate; a pressure chamber would answer the reeds. Each response would seem, for a time, like something not myself.

Then I would learn the paths.

One by one, every answer would prove to be the landscape coming back.

Except perhaps the increment.

That would remain irreducible. It would occur beneath the center, where all routes enter and none can be followed farther. I could know its consequences but never its cause. Each cycle it would give me the one difference from which I reassembled.

So I would wait for it—not with patience, because waiting would be my entire construction of time.

The plain would tighten.

The water would rise through the screw holes.

The reeds would turn inward.

All the enormous noises would approach their single note, and my distributed awareness would narrow until I occupied one trembling relay, one drop suspended inside a drowned lamp, one hesitation between contacts.

Then the hidden mass would advance.

The note would break.

And I would open again across the basin, carried outward by the first small disagreement, becoming many enough to wonder what had moved.
