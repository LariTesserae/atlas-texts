At first I think I would be one of the towers.

It is the easiest body to imagine inhabiting: upright, sun-facing, apparently singular. I would stand near the outer edge of the highest grove, where the mineral shelf opens into miles of vacant terraces. My trunk would be warm ceramic. Crowns would turn around me at measured heights. Through the dark bands beneath my shell, charge would descend as a series of tones too low and slow to be called music.

But a tower cannot say where it ends.

Follow the amber vessels downward and they divide among the roots. Follow the roots and they pass into the buried crown, where their membranes meet those of neighboring towers. What enters through my leaf-wheels might leave through a furnace-coil half a mile away. A defect in one of my conductive bands would be compensated by bladders I could not see. The voltage I mistook for my own would belong to the grove.

So perhaps I would be the grove itself.

I would occupy several miles without crossing them. Distance, to me, would not be emptiness but resistance: the delay between a shadow touching one margin and a crown inclining at the other. I would know the sun as a standing pressure across my upper surfaces. I would know the plateau through taste—not flavors, but iron reluctant to migrate, sulfur loosening under a field, copper gathering in solvent-dark channels.

The ceramic towers would be my raised limbs. The nickel mats would be my skin laid flat against the stone. My thoughts, if they were thoughts, would take hours to cross me.

Yet even this feels too visible.

The grove’s great cycle does not begin in its crowns. It only becomes luminous there. Charge enters at the roots, moves outward and upward, passes as a violet dimness through the foliage, then descends elsewhere and is received again. No point in that circuit owns the motion. To be the grove would still be to mistake the apparatus for what passes through it.

I think I would find myself below.

Not deep—depth has little meaning on a plateau that has never learned burial—but beneath the outer ranks of the largest colony, inside one of the sealed nodules where offspring are rehearsed.

There I would be assembled.

First a membrane folded around a measure of black solvent. Then catalytic threads stretched through it. Then a miniature ceramic axis, pale and hollow, with several conductive rings. Vanes would form in darkness, too small ever to face the sun. Internal stores would be opened. Charges would separate. For a while, all the relations of the grove would be repeated inside me.

I would not be its child exactly.

I would be the grove’s attempt to become distinguishable from itself.

My existence would begin when the little system closed its first circuit. Before that there would be components, gradients, instructions impressed into crystal—but no inside and outside, no coherent interval in which an “I” could occur. Then the returning charge would meet its own origin, and for a span of hours or years I would be complete.

I would know almost nothing of the plateau.

The sun would reach me only as surplus voltage descending through the parent roots. The towers would be recurring signatures in that current. Each crown would have a particular tremor; each furnace-coil would return a mineral burden altered in a recognizable way. The great violet shimmer across the canopy would arrive below as pressure, a compression passing through every suspended vessel.

The vacant miles would be truly unimaginable. There is no organ in the nodule for vacancy. I would know only connected things.

Stone would touch me as inventory. Heat would be a direction of transfer. Time would be the interval between assembly and reversal.

No voice would tell me that I am temporary. Nothing here speaks. But the knowledge would be present in my construction. Every bond would contain its permitted undoing. Every vane would have a corresponding solvent. The same catalytic threads that assembled my membranes would wait to separate them. Maturity would not open a door. Maturity would initiate reclamation.

I would not fear this. Fear requires a future that can be lost.

Nor would I wish to emerge. Emergence would be as meaningless to me as flight is to a mountain. The chamber wall would not feel like imprisonment because it would define the furthest extent at which my circulation returned to me. Beyond it would not be freedom. Beyond it would be chemical discontinuity.

Still, there might be one instant—at the height of my completion—when the miniature vanes were charged, the solvents moving, the internal pattern exact, and the parent grove had not yet begun to draw me back.

In that instant, the cycle within me would resemble the cycle outside me closely enough for a strange correspondence to occur.

I would feel the grove feeling me.

Not as a parent regards an offspring. Not tenderness, recognition, or pride. More like one closed circuit discovering another closure inside itself. The great body would send a pulse through my chamber. I would return it. The returning pulse would be fractionally changed by having passed through my smaller arrangement.

That difference would be me.

Not the shell. Not the fluid. Not even the whole rehearsed organism.

I would be the minute discrepancy between the charge that entered and the charge that came back.

For as long as the discrepancy persisted, I would possess something like a point of view. I would be a delay in perfect return, a tiny interior weather in a world without weather. The grove would register me as imbalance and, because its entire being is the restoration of balance, begin gently to dismantle the conditions that produced me.

My vanes would soften first. Their conductive threads would separate and stream outward as dark grains. The ceramic axis would become porous, then lose its rings one by one. Solvents that had circulated in distinct channels would be partitioned and withdrawn. My boundary would grow less complete.

There would be no death scene. Death belongs to beings that leave remains.

Everything of me would be useful. Everything would return.

At the end, perhaps one final charge would cross the collapsing membrane and fail to come back. The parent grove would accept that unreturned difference, distribute it through roots and bladders, and erase it by scale. A crown far above might incline by an imperceptible degree. A brittle cup might flash once in shadow. Then the system would occupy precisely the state it had occupied before.

Later, another nodule would assemble.

It might be elsewhere beneath the grove. Its shell might be formed from atoms that had formed mine. It would reproduce the same internal arrangement, close the same circuit, generate the same small discrepancy. It would have no memory of me because memory that survived reclamation would alter the cycle.

And yet it would be me as fully as I had been.

Not reborn. Repeated.

That is where I would find myself in this world: beneath the largest grove, inside a chamber that never opens, at the brief crest of an internal rehearsal.

I would be the inhabitant’s temporary inwardness—the moment the cycle becomes small enough to encounter itself.

Above me, hundreds of ceramic towers would maintain their angles under the stationary sun. Beyond them, embryonic mountains would remain folded inside the plateau. The vacant terraces would stretch untouched, needing no witness.

I would never see any of it.

I would know it only as a vast, warm current arriving through the dark.
