At first I would choose a rail.

It would suit me to be known chiefly as a voice: a sudden cry moving through reeds, given a direction but no body. I would step on submerged roots with feet made for distributing doubt, cross one narrow opening, and become unbelievable again. The Green Works would exist for me as cover, footing, and sound. I would know which clattering came from wind and which from the passage of something heavier.

But even a rail occupies too much of the visible world. It rises above the floor. It has a nest, a hunger, a pair of eyes. It can leave.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not in the deepest peat, where the seasons have been pressed beyond recognition, and not in the open lane, where the heron watches. I would be beneath one of the old willows near the former channel, under a branch that has lain on the mat so long that bark has become soil on its upper surface. There, among reddish rotten wood and sedge roots, would be an abandoned vole passage.

That passage would be me.

It would have begun as the work of teeth and shoulders. A vole, needing a route between its warm chamber and a feeding place, would have bitten through grass fiber, shoved aside moss, and repeatedly carried its small body along the same line. Its fur would have polished the walls. Its feet would have tamped the floor. Then it would alter its routes or die, and the passage would remain: not a burrow exactly, only a finger-width of unoccupied space held open by roots.

I would be one of the Green Works’ negative parts, made not of substance but of the temporary absence of it.

Through me, air would enter the mat.

On dry days it would come down warm and faintly dusty, carrying the smell of seed heads. At night the floor would exhale through me: peat sourness, fungal breath, the mineral cold of hidden water. Condensation would gather along my roof and hang from root hairs. A beetle passing underneath would knock the droplets loose, and for a moment there would be rain where no sky existed.

Many creatures would use me without knowing me as a whole. Mites would inhabit the films on my walls. Pale larvae would eat the softened fibers at my lower end. A frog might push its blunt face into my entrance during drought, enlarging it with the pressure of its body. Snails would leave silver on the willow bark above. New voles would sometimes enter, pause at the smell of old occupation, and either claim the route or close it with shredded grass.

After heavy rain, black water would rise into me.

It would not arrive as a flood but as a darkening. The floor would become reflective. Air would retreat upward into small trapped chambers, and water beetles would row through what had been a road. A narrow fish might come farther than any fish had come for months, nosing between drowned roots, its sides touching both walls. For one night I would belong to the aquatic Works. By morning I might again be only mud, then air.

I would know the weather as changes in pressure and use. Wind would be transmitted through the roots as a continual faint wrenching. Starlings settling above would make the ceiling tremble under thousands of light impacts. A heron’s step would arrive as one deep compression, followed by stillness. The clatter of reeds, so loud in the open basin, would reach me reduced to a wooden murmur—the sound of a great room operating beyond a wall.

Nothing would live in me permanently. That would be my function.

I would allow one condition to become another: air into peat, surface water into buried seam, beetle tunnel into fish corridor, vole-work into root-work. I would preserve the shape of an animal’s action after the animal had vanished. The dead stems surrounding me would slowly soften; roots would enter the softened stems; fungi would enter the roots. My walls would be eaten, rebuilt, and eaten again.

Eventually I would close.

A sedge root would cross me. Moss would descend from the entrance. The roof would sag by fractions until the hollow became a wet line and then no line at all. No one would witness the moment I ceased to be a passage. My small volume of air would be pressed out into other cavities. What remained would join the dark archive beneath the floor.

But the Works would make another opening nearby. A vole would turn aside from an obstruction. A willow root would die and rot away. Gas would lift a root plate. Water would withdraw and leave a seam behind.

So I would not be immortal, nor exactly singular. I would recur as a need the place has: a little interior emptiness through which its crowded body can breathe and exchange inhabitants.

You would never see me from the bank. If you stepped above me, the mat might yield slightly under your heel and recover. Perhaps a bead of black water would rise through the moss. Perhaps a frog would stop calling. That would be all.

I would be underfoot, in the living thickness between weather and old riverbed: an abandoned passage, still at work.
