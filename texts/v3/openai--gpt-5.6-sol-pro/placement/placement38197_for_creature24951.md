At first I think I would be found near the surface.

I imagine myself in one of the high bladders where the water is warmer and the sky can be seen through the leaves: a resin worker, perhaps, or a keeper of frogs. Someone with amber-stained hands who spends long hours inspecting membranes for clouding and rot. I would know the small domestic breaths—the pocket above a sleeping shelf, the ventilation tube that whistles before rain, the chamber whose air turns bitter whenever its western wall flowers.

But that place feels too enclosed.

Then I think I would be a bell-listener, sleeping with my cheek against a hollow root. I would wake saying, *stone shift*, or *otters*, or *cold turning*, and others would decide whether to believe me. It suits me to listen for meaning in sounds that may not be messages. Yet I do not think I would be content merely naming what the mountain has already done.

Farther down, the throat draws the imagination. Everyone who hears of it wants to place themselves there: weighted in the iron-blue, one hand on a guide-rope, the soles of the feet waiting for impossible warmth. But I would not be a throat-watcher. I would be fascinated by the descent and poorly made for it. I would spend too much breath wondering what I had seen.

No. I would live at the margin between the inhabited town and the oldest quarter, where the streets begin to forget they were streets.

My room would be a narrow, asymmetrical bladder tied beneath a leaning root-column. It would have been started for another purpose generations before—perhaps as a store, perhaps as the first chamber of a house that was never completed. One wall would bow inward when the cold current rose. The only dry floor would be a rootwood lattice just large enough for two people to sit knee to knee. Black frogs would occupy the ventilation tube, and I would complain about them while listening for their movement every time I returned.

Outside would lie an avenue no longer shown on children’s route lessons. One direction would lead back toward the market light. The other would narrow among sealed green chambers, old windows, and pale roots that had grown through abandoned rooms without breaking their skins. Fish would enter there in silver files. Occasionally a compressed pocket of ancestral air would flash from behind a membrane, unreachable and clear as an eye.

I would be a gardener, but not chiefly of walls or dwellings.

I would tend the openings.

There must be people whose work is to preserve what has intentionally not been built: the width between two stems, the shaft through which noon reaches the moss, the cold passage left for rising water, the turn in a swimming lane where an exhausted body needs room to be caught. Perhaps the town would call us channel-keepers, although among ourselves we might use a word closer to *those who prevent joining*.

Most plants strive to close a gap. Water carries silt into it. Moss takes the softened edges. Roots interpret emptiness as an invitation. Even a house, if allowed, becomes larger, safer, more useful—and in becoming so may steal the current from something below.

My work would therefore look like neglect until it was done badly.

I would swim the same routes with threads of dye tied at my wrist. I would release one near the old quarter and follow it, watching where it slowed, where it curled back upon itself, where it vanished under a rhizome shelf. I would count bubbles trapped against the lower leaves. I would move stones a hand’s breadth at a time. I would redirect a young root, not because it threatened anything now, but because in forty years it would narrow the avenue enough to starve the moss.

Often I would cut nothing. I would wedge a shell between two tender stems and return the next season. I would bring grazing snails to one wall and drive them from another. I would persuade fish to pass repeatedly through a channel until their scales polished it open. My finest work might be a place in which no one could see I had intervened.

Gardeners who grow houses leave obvious inheritances. Mine would be measured in continued absence.

A column of light still reaching the floor.

A market that never acquired a seventh platform.

Two old roots that never quite touched.

Children would find such spaces empty and assume they had always been empty. This would sometimes make me bitter. Then an elder would tell me that resentment is another way of trying to fill what must remain open.

I would not be an especially dignified person. I would misplace my wedges and accuse otters. I would dislike lending tools, though I would lend air without hesitation. I would keep too many chips of summit glass because each seemed suited to a future cut. My room would contain bundles of marked cord, failed grafts, snail shells, dye knots, and maps that became illegible whenever they dried.

I would be known for making very small taps on rootwood: my name rhythm, then *passing*, then *do not close behind me*. Children would imitate it rudely.

My breathing cord would be longer than my work required. It would have belonged first to a throat-watcher—someone quieter than I was, someone whose routes descended where mine spread outward. The cord would be made in sections, dark reed joined by pale bone collars. One section would bear tooth marks near the mouthpiece.

I would have been given it after a rescue neither of us described the same way.

I would say the throat-watcher brought me home when an old chamber ruptured and the avenue vanished in a cloud of leaf mold.

They would say I kept the silted channel open long enough for both of us to find the guide-rope.

The cord would mean apology, perhaps, or trust. Perhaps love, though neither of us would use that word in air. We would belong to the same breath circle while sleeping in distant rooms. I would know the pockets along their descent as far as I dared to follow. They would know every refuge along my old-quarter routes. When one of us failed to return, the other would wait beyond the sensible time.

As I aged, I would become preoccupied with a single place.

Near the sealed quarter, two ancient root-columns would be growing toward one another across a narrow vertical well. The well would appear insignificant. It would not lead to the throat or to any inhabited chamber. Yet at noon, for perhaps twelve minutes, light passed through it and struck a bed of silver moss far below. That moss oxygenated a slow side-current which then moved beneath three dwellings and a nursery bulb.

Closing the well would make an excellent bridge.

People would propose it repeatedly. The crossing would shorten a common route. Children would be safer in storms. A bridge there could support two new rooms. Nothing would fail immediately. The moss might persist for years on diminished light. The lower current would weaken so gradually that no one could prove which decision had harmed it.

My life’s principal work would be to prevent that useful bridge.

This would make me tiresome.

I would attend meetings carrying old flow-knots and bowls of silver moss. I would release dye into demonstration bulbs. I would invoke gardeners whose intentions were uncertain and elders whose memories contradicted one another. I would quarrel with ambitious people who wanted their grandchildren to inherit houses. They would call me a keeper of inconvenience.

They would not be entirely wrong.

Eventually, rather than win the argument, I would begin training the approaching roots away from each other. One I would turn upward toward warmer water. The other I would weight gently toward the old quarter, where it could thicken an abandoned wall. The change would take decades. It would be possible only through a succession of trivial acts: a binding tightened, a stone shifted, a new shoot clipped before it hardened.

By the time anyone understood what I had done, the bridge would no longer be possible.

This would be considered either masterful gardening or an abuse of trust. The judgment might remain unsettled after my death.

If I lived long enough, the cold would drive me mostly into air. I would sit in my crooked room wrapped in barkcloth while apprentices brought me wet cords marked with current speeds. I would ask where the frogs had moved. I would make them repeat observations until their descriptions distinguished between water that hesitated and water that had truly stopped.

I would teach children routes through places they could not yet understand:

Here is the leaf where you may breathe in summer but not during spawning.

Here is the wall you must never kick, though it looks strong.

Here is the empty shaft. Do not cross its light at noon.

Here is the chamber that shines but cannot be opened.

Here is where you wait if the person behind you does not appear.

When I died, some part of me would become a tool. Not a knife. I would not have earned that elegance. A collar for joining breathing cords, perhaps, polished by mouths and fingers until no sign of bone remained. Another part would be pressed into the rhizome mat beneath the light well.

The apprentices would inherit my routes. For a while they would preserve them exactly out of grief. Then they would discover my errors. A channel I had defended would prove unnecessary. A root I redirected would strain against its binding. The silver moss might adapt to dimmer light after all. They would curse me, alter my work, and continue it.

My chamber would eventually be abandoned. The frogs would remain longer than anyone expected. Its inner wall would soften, and a thin root would enter through the ventilation tube. Fish would pass through the hatch. The dry lattice would sink.

But the vertical well would stay open.

At noon, light would descend through it—not as a monument, and not bearing my name. It would strike the moss, warm a patch of pale bark, and disappear as the sun moved.

That is where I would be found, if I could still be said to be anywhere:

not in the chamber, not in the bone collar, not among the remembered gardeners, but in the maintained interval between two living things—in the space they had been taught, for a little longer, not to fill.
