At first I think I would be one of the pale mites.

There is comfort in their precision: to wake angular and white, to find the newest bead, to drink until the body disappears. I imagine myself reduced to dark joints around a mouthful of rain, carrying a little water from one trembling edge to another. Not enough to drain either world. Only enough to persuade two droplets to touch.

But I would not remain a mite.

The first time the surface beneath me vanished, I would hang where it had been. My feet would remember a curve no longer visible. For a while I would mistake that memory for my body.

Then steam would rise. A film would gather around my legs, and the surface would take me into itself. I would flatten. My organs would open into marks. I would become readable from both sides: pulse at the border, hunger branching through the middle, one dark comma of an egg—or an eye—or something not yet divided into those purposes.

In that thin condition, I would learn that depth is only one local custom.

I would travel toward the narrowest place in the hollow, where the vein of water parts around the blade of glass. One stream falls and the other rises. The animals drinking there meet head to head, each certain that the other is inverted. I would enter at the bright seam between the currents.

The intersecting surfaces would divide me.

Both of me would continue without alarm.

One would go upward with the falling water. One would go downward with the rising water. Each would remember choosing its direction. Each would remember being the one from whom the other split.

For a time we would remain alike. We would turn at the same instant, open our mouths at the same tremor, recoil from predators that only one of us had encountered. Then the correspondence would weaken. One would be swallowed by a transparent ribbon. The other would crawl onto hot stone and unfold six limbs.

Yet the swallowed one would not end. Its shape would become briefly visible inside the hunter: a small angular interruption moving through clear flesh. When the ribbon divided, that interruption would divide with it. Later, mites would drink the ribbon’s water, and something of me would pass into each of them—not memory exactly, but a preference. At branching condensation lines, they would tend toward the same side.

The other me would harden.

Minerals would gather along my back until I carried a black shell edged like a broken lens. I would graze salt from the wall. Heat would crack the shell asymmetrically. Afterward, whenever I turned sideways, the broken edge would cut what brushed against it. I would become a predator through damage rather than desire.

Eventually a blind glass-worker would find me.

It would brace me with four limbs and place two divided hands along the fracture in my shell. I would sense it reading the accident that had made me sharp. It would not know me as prey or tool yet. Those distinctions would emerge from the angle of its grip.

Its obsidian teeth would close.

My soft body would be eaten. The shell would be struck against the wall. The impact would pass silently through the chamber, and every creature touching stone would pause.

A hooked flake would come free.

That, I think, is where I would begin to recognize myself.

Not in the mite, the flat swimmer, the shell-back, or the swallowed shape. Not even in the flake. I would be the particular turn of the glass-worker’s wrist that made the hook—the slight inward correction before the strike.

The tool would be abandoned after a single use. Mineral skin would begin to close over it. Generations later, another glass-worker would uncover the edge and hold it differently, but its hand would reproduce that same inward turn. A juvenile across a hanging plane would see the gesture reversed and repeat it. Thus I would acquire a second handedness.

I would pass without being taught.

I would live in the angle chosen before glass breaks. In the path a colony of mites makes when thirst offers two equal branches. In the way card-crawlers turn their layered body inside out. In the curl of a ribbon dividing around prey. In the direction a shadow moves a heartbeat before its owner.

Sometimes the mountain would perform me.

A fresh fracture would open with my hooked shape. Water entering it would emerge elsewhere carrying my curve. Knot animals threaded through the fault would bend as I bent. A mineral colony, growing while no one watched, would preserve the pattern as a translucent ridge.

No inhabitant would know these repetitions belonged together. Perhaps they would not belong together. The hollow might merely make the same decision many times.

I would be that decision.

So you would not find me in one fixed part of the summit world. My nearest thing to a dwelling would be the blade where the water divides, but I would inhabit moments more than places: the pause of a droplet before it chooses several directions; the instant a limb adjusts its grip; the fraction by which a shadow disobeys.

After rain I would be common. I would scatter through airborne lakes, enter the feet of hunters as changes in curvature, and wake in the mouths of the gray tool-users as a new edge among their teeth. During drought I would contract into the hooked flaw of an abandoned flake, sealed under clear mineral, waiting without awareness.

If the hollow is one animal, I would be neither one of its organs nor one of its thoughts.

I would be a habit it cannot remember learning.

And if the crack is remade with every rainfall, then each time I returned I would believe myself ancient: the same small inward turn, passing from water to animal to glass, surviving not as a body but as a gesture the mountain makes when it reaches for itself.
