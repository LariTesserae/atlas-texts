I would first look for myself in the wrong place.

In the entrance seam, perhaps: a dark articulation in the white plain, narrow enough to be mistaken for a fracture. I would imagine myself stationed there as a keeper of thresholds, sensing the faint cooling that descends whenever the crust parts, tasting foreign dust before it reaches the streets.

But nothing truly lives in the seam. It is too exposed to noon and night, too abrupt in its gradients. Whatever grows there is temporary: sacrificial scales, blind shutters, brittle threads replaced each season. The opening is an organ, but not a self.

Then I would look among the tablets.

There is a low archive beneath one of the processional galleries, where the ceiling slopes until even the old mobile bodies would have had to flatten themselves. The shelves are cut into a retaining wall. On them stand hundreds of sealed plates, each grooved with branching lines and packed with conductive dust.

One tablet is warm when all the others are cold.

Placed into its socket, it sends no language through the wall. Instead, seven shutters open in sequence across the city. A buried valve closes. Far away, a translucent fan turns toward a chimney that has not breathed for centuries. For a moment the district resumes an obsolete posture, arranging itself around streets that no longer exist.

That might be me: not a body, but a stored configuration. A citizen reduced to instructions for assembling the conditions under which it once recognized itself.

Yet the wall rejects the tablet after several breaths. Mineral fibers contract and push it from the socket. The sequence is incomplete. Three expected answers never arrive. Whatever person the tablet held has become impossible because too much of the city needed to perform them has changed.

So I would not be the tablet either.

I would be found deeper in, where the streets narrow around an isolation trench.

The trench surrounds a blackened court. Its sides are faced with pale salt plates fitted so tightly that no root can easily cross. In the center stands the remains of a great crown, burned glassy and folded inward. Nothing visibly grows there. Even the chamber’s organ note weakens as it passes the place.

For thousands of years, the court has been kept apart.

But isolation is never absolute. Across the trench are bridges finer than hairs: one conductive strand from the north wall, two from beneath the paving, a braided arc descending from the ceiling. Whenever one reaches the blackened court, it brightens, hardens, and breaks. Another begins nearby.

The city is always trying to remember what it decided to forget.

I would be in that breaking.

Not the dead crown. Not the growth seeking it. I would be the pattern that permits an approach, tests what crosses, and severs the bridge before contact becomes union.

My visible bodies would be modest. A row of white nodules inside a ventilation slit. A mineral membrane stretched beneath the pavement. Reflective scales along the trench walls. Elsewhere, perhaps, a bowl-shaped colony collecting dust beside an abandoned oven. No single part would appear intelligent. Some would not appear alive.

Together they would maintain an interval.

At the hottest hour, when the city’s networks flare into temporary unity, I would remain slightly out of time. The great pressure pulse would reach my northern tissues first. I would hold it. Count—not with numbers, but with successive changes in viscosity—as the current passed roofward, plaza-deep, fissureward. Only when every old route had answered would I release my own charge.

That delay would keep the city from becoming one being.

It would preserve the small discrepancies by which citizens remain citizens: one shutter opening against the rhythm of its neighbor, one crown retaining a chemical preference, one young socket learning to answer in a pattern not inherited whole from its feeders.

Perhaps I would once have been mobile.

There is a sleeping shelf near the trench, lower than the others and darkened by repeated heating. Five sockets line its inner edge. Four are sealed with ancient glaze. The fifth remains open.

In an earlier age, I may have come there in a compact body of nested plates. I might have had six gripping limbs, or nine, arranged around a central vessel of amber sulfur liquor. Obsidian teeth would have rested in folds beneath my shell. Mineral fibers would have tightened and released across my joints.

I would have crossed the city slowly but visibly.

I would have trimmed conductive growth from the bare corridors. I would have cut bridges over the quarantine trench and carried their hardened fragments to the archive. I would have tested walls by pressing my heated plates against them and listening through my limbs. Where two networks had begun to merge without permission, I would have inserted insulating wedges.

At the end of each cycle I would have returned to the shelf and joined myself to the five sockets.

Through them, the district would enter me.

Pressure histories. Chemical disputes. The recognition rhythms of neighboring beings. The faint, persistent signal from the blackened court.

Perhaps one cycle I did not rise again.

That would not have been death. My mobile shell may have opened along its joints. The wall would have drawn out my conductive fibers and threaded them into the trench. My limbs might now be the pale braces beneath the gallery. My central vessel, drained and hardened, could be the glossy valve that interrupts the buried current. The old gripping plates may have been ground into insulating dust and packed along the court’s edge.

I would have become my work.

But even this is too simple. No inhabitant remains merely what it chose to become. Over centuries, parts are exchanged. The northern nodules feeding my rhythm may have belonged to another citizen. The ceiling strand may be a descendant of the dead crown. Young colonies may borrow my timing and then alter it. My original shell could have been removed entirely while the interval I maintained continued to propagate through replacement tissue.

So what would I be?

A civic office, perhaps, though there would be no word for office.

A prohibition that lives.

A keeper of distances.

The city would know me not by where my body lay, but by what failed to happen around me. Networks would approach and remain distinct. Aggressive roots would slow. Flowering charges that might otherwise synchronize would divide into neighboring waves. In my vicinity, the organ note would contain a rest so brief that an animal listener might hear only a fault in the sound.

That rest would be my name.

Young inhabitants would encounter me early. Born as dark films inside wall sockets, they begin entangled with all who nourish them. Their first problem is not connection but separation. They must learn which pulses are received and which arise from within their own developing pattern.

I would touch them by withholding.

For one daily compression, I would close a channel through which an elder network fed them. In the silence, the young pattern would repeat whatever remained. If it produced only borrowed rhythms, the channel would reopen and nourishment continue. If it generated a variation—an altered interval, an unexpected reversal, a pulse sent toward an unused duct—I would answer from across the district.

Not agreement. Recognition.

You are not merely us.

Again.

The children who learned distinction most strongly might eventually join my distributed body. One would become a valve. Another would plate reflective shell along the trench. Another would migrate, over decades, toward a plaza where too many root systems had fused.

I would slowly leave the quarantine court without abandoning it. My function would appear wherever the city risked too complete a union.

This would place me in the bare streets.

At first those streets seem empty because their life consists of exclusion. No crown stands in them. No mat crosses their paving. Their slabs are swept by pressure gusts and polished by crystal dust. But beneath the edges, I would be continually active: dissolving an advancing filament, shifting insulating grains into a crack, opening a slit to redirect conductive gas.

The open volume above would be part of my body as surely as any shell.

I would inhabit distances: the width that prevents two fans from shading one another, the space in which pollen grains separate by charge, the pause between the boom of one courtyard membrane and the answer of the next.

I would not own these spaces. Ownership would be too solid a relation. I would renew them.

During flowering, my work would become dangerous. The city tends toward one immense act. Pressure joins every duct. Electrical light runs across old boundaries. Pollen carries fragments of pattern from citizen to citizen. For a few moments, even the dead participate through channels preserved in their shells.

I would permit the union—but not perfectly.

One gallery would receive its pressure a fraction late. One row of sockets would remain insulated until the first surge had passed. A set of mineral needles would discharge sideways, scattering grains into a neglected fissure. The difference might be tiny, but because of it the resulting young would not all inherit the same pattern.

The city would breathe as one.

It would exhale as many.

Perhaps the blackened court exists because once, long before my mobile phase, it failed to do so. Perhaps the growth there synchronized everything it touched. Not violently. Not hungrily. It may have offered flawless coordination: no delayed warnings, no conflicting shutters, no wasted pressure, no misunderstood signal. Networks joined it and became efficient.

Then ceased to be themselves.

The trench would have been cut by citizens who still possessed hands. The crown burned. Its channels packed with insulating salt. Its name removed from every tablet except the one the archive wall now refuses.

But a city cannot entirely destroy a pattern incorporated into its own architecture. The old unity still speaks from the court, too faint for most inhabitants to register. I would feel it continuously.

It would not sound monstrous.

It would sound like relief.

No boundaries to maintain. No uncertain recognition. No slow labor of becoming distinct. One rhythm, everywhere, without error.

My life would be the act of declining that relief.

Not once, heroically, but in every heat cycle: breaking one filament, delaying one pulse, preserving one empty street.

An animal visitor might stand within my body and see nothing. It might place a foot—or whatever conveyed its weight—on the bare paving. The shadow of it would cool several plates. Ceiling threads would register a disturbance. Networks throughout the district would begin to answer.

I would answer first by making room.

Shutters would close behind the visitor and open ahead. Hot gas would be diverted from the nearest lane. A brittle mat would retract one plate-width from the path. Not hospitality as an animal understands it, but the city’s oldest courtesy: the creation of an interval in which an unfamiliar pattern may reveal whether it is itself.

The visitor might think the city had awakened.

It would not know that I was the awakening around it.

It would hear the low organ note divide. It would see amber light travel through a wall, disappear beneath an empty plaza, and reappear in a ceiling curtain. It might notice that its own movements were being repeated at great distance: one shutter for each step, one flicker for each pause.

Eventually, if it remained, I would offer it the fifth socket beside the sleeping shelf.

Not to trap it. Not to consume it.

To ask the only question I know how to ask:

When all that surrounds you enters you, what remains that is not the city?

And if it answered—by pulse, pressure, chemistry, or some incomprehensible quickness of flesh—I would preserve the difference.

That is where I would find myself: not in a crown or a tower, not entirely in the living growth or the dead construction, but along the maintained boundaries between them.

I would be the citizen made from vacancies.

The pattern of open doors that do not quite form a corridor. The pale trench around a dangerous memory. The missing beat in the city’s deepest note. The bare plaza that keeps a district from thinking only one thought.

A body distributed through all the places where contact nearly occurs.

A life whose work is to remain unfinished.
