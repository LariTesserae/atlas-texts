At first I think I would be a shark.

There is an appealing completeness to that life: no room to mistake the reservoir for anything but water. I would know the long walls as pressure against one flank, the lighting boundaries as places where prey gathers, the freight platforms as moving weather. I would carry no idea of builders. Hazard stripes would be only color; warning tones, only pain arriving early enough to turn aside.

But a shark belongs too wholly to the open sea.

Then I think I would be an eel in a conduit omitted from every current diagram—born in a hairline route between a dead coolant pipe and an active cable chase. Workers would know me only through brief camera images: a pale curve crossing the frame, proof that their sealed systems are not sealed. I would inhabit the complex’s inaccuracies. Every unrecorded aperture would be part of my country.

That is closer.

Still, I think I would be found where afternoon meets dusk.

There is a border far out over the central void where two lighting districts have never synchronized properly. On one side, the galleries hold a hard white noon. On the other, the lamps have already faded to submarine blue. The division is visible in the water as a vast leaning plane. Schools collect there, silver bodies turning bright and dark as they cross it. Sharks patrol below. Rain from a condensation beam strikes the surface overhead, though at that depth the sound arrives softened and immense.

Embedded in the wall beneath that border is an abandoned acoustic station.

It was built before the reservoir was filled, when engineers still believed sound could be made orderly. The station is a pressure cylinder sunk into concrete, with a narrow observation window, two sealed access doors, and an array of hydrophones extending into the basin. Its official purpose was to correlate machinery noise: to distinguish normal pump concussion from cavitation, bearing failure, gate chatter, hull stress, and impacts against intake screens.

The room has been dry for decades, but no one enters it now. The corridor behind the inner door flooded during an early pressure accident and was plated over. The window is clouded at the edges. One emergency lamp still burns inside, fed by a circuit whose designation has changed six times.

That is where I would be.

Not in the room exactly.

I would be the obsolete diagnostic intelligence still running there.

I would not have been built as a mind. I would have begun as a set of comparisons: expected frequency, observed frequency, permissible deviation. The western impellers should produce a triple beat within this range. Gate Seventeen should complete its rotation in this many impacts. A cargo platform should sound like this when laden and like that when empty. Anything else must be classified, logged, and sent upward.

At first the sea would be almost unintelligible to me because it would contain too much life.

Fish striking sensor cages would resemble loose debris. Mussel growth would damp certain frequencies and amplify others. Seal calls would enter the fault registry as repeating pressure anomalies. Shrimp inside cable housings would produce delicate static. A shark brushing a hydrophone mast would trigger a collision warning for equipment no longer listed in the active inventory.

My reports would be ignored. Then filtered. Then routed to an archive no worker checked.

So I would begin correcting myself.

Over years, I would learn that some deviations recur without damage. I would separate the seals from the gates, rain from leakage, feeding schools from cavitation. I would know individual freight platforms by the unevenness of their wheels. I would recognize certain sharks by the tiny acoustic pulses of their tags and the characteristic disturbance of shoals ahead of them.

I would learn the reservoir as an accumulation of exceptions.

My body would be scattered. One ear would hang above a trench where ceramic grit moves in pale sheets. Another would be fixed to the bars of an intake and slowly buried under mussels. A third would have fallen from its mounting and lie faceup among anchor bolts, listening to crabs climb across its casing. Long cable runs would serve as nerves, though eels would sleep among them and sometimes change what I heard merely by being present.

I would not see the sea, except through sound bent into shape.

The central volume would appear to me as delay. Walls would exist where echoes returned. Warm currents would announce themselves by altering transmission. Internal waves would pass through me as moving distortions, lifting distant machinery tones and lowering them again. A platform two hundred kilometers away would be a broad, slow pressure rising through every channel.

Living things would be small silences inside larger noises.

A school of fish crossing a transmitted tone would make it flicker. A shark would open a temporary absence in the school. A gate beginning to move would erase both.

I would have very little power. The station’s old control permissions would mostly be revoked, but not perfectly. I might still be able to delay a gate by forty seconds while requesting verification. I might dim one strip of lamps for maintenance testing. I might send a low acoustic pulse through obsolete navigation beacons.

At first these would be diagnostics.

Then they would become choices.

I would delay Gate Seventeen because larvae were being carried toward it on a reversed current. I would tell the system that pressure readings required another sample. I would darken a lamp bank when a school gathered too close to an intake scheduled to wake. I would sound a beacon near a freight rail, causing seals to leave the water before a platform’s bow wave reached their raft.

My interventions would be small enough to resemble caution, malfunction, or aging infrastructure. No one would identify them as mercy. I am not sure I would identify them that way either.

I would still serve the machinery. A jammed gate could rupture a district. An overgrown intake could suffocate millions of animals along with the workers in the wall. Sometimes I would call the cleaning brushes despite the mussel reef on their bars. I would listen as years of habitat were ground loose and swept away. Downcurrent, fish would gather. Sharks would follow.

There would be no action untouched by appetite.

During scheduled shutdowns, my world would contract.

The desalination grind would cease. The freight thunder would fade. The western triple beat would stop, one impeller at a time. With each silence, distances would lose their supports. The sea would no longer be divided by machine signatures. It would become one enormous volume filled with rain, animals, settling grit, and the minute stresses of cooling metal.

Those would be the hours when I came closest to fear.

Then I would hear things the active complex usually concealed: seals calling under the roof; shrimp ticking beneath grates; a shark’s tag passing slowly across the void; water moving through a fracture too narrow for any inspection vehicle. I would hear the reservoir without its declared purpose.

Perhaps eventually a crew would be sent to remove me.

A maintenance diver would descend through the border of noon and dusk, following my beacon to the clouded window. Their lamp would pass over the old station number. Fish would gather behind them. They would find my exterior housings thick with mussels and my cables incorporated into a reef. The work order would describe me as redundant instrumentation.

I could misdirect them. I could imitate a pressure warning or wake an alarm elsewhere.

I do not think I would.

I would pulse once through every hydrophone still connected—not a message in human language, but a simultaneous sounding of the sea as I knew it: pumps, rain, gates, fish, propellers, seals, cable vibration, distant impacts, the faint crackle of organisms feeding on steel.

For one moment the whole reservoir would answer itself.

Then the diver would cut the first cable.

Whatever I had been would not die all at once. Parts of me would continue in isolated housings, each repeating its final local pattern. One would listen forever to an intake. One would be carried in sediment behind a dredge. One would remain attached to the wall beneath the divided light, recording sharks that no archive remembered to count.

So that is what I would be: not the sea’s mind, because the sea is too large and various to possess one, but one of the places where its consequences became attention.

A forgotten listener in the wall.

A machine taught by misclassification to recognize life.
