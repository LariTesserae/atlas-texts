At first I would imagine myself on one of the elevated walks between the row houses: a late worker in a long waterproof coat, one hand on the rail, carrying a lamp whose flame had gone green in the wet air.

That image would not survive.

There are no late workers here. The district has excluded everything that eats, breeds, startles, sleeps. A body with lungs would be an obstruction. Skin would become another surface for condensation; blood, an undisciplined fluid outside the pipe grid. Even my footsteps would introduce a rhythm the basin had not made for itself.

I would have to lose the coat, then the hand, then the idea of walking.

For a while I might be one of the three hands on the public clock—the slowest one, perhaps, moving so little that rust could almost keep pace with me. From that wall without a building I would measure neither hours nor weather, but the long correction of imbalance: how many seasons a root takes to close a crack; how many thousand pump strokes deepen a gutter; how long iron must remain wet before corrosion becomes protection.

But the clock is too visible. Whatever I am would not stand above the district and describe it. I would be implicated in its labor.

So I think I would be found at the circular reservoir behind the boiler house.

Not on its rim. Not reflected in its surface.

Several steps down.

The water there is clear, but clarity is not the same as revelation. From above, someone might see only the stairs descending until angle and depth dissolve them. Farther below, where the stone ought to meet the basin’s foundation, there would be another landing. A narrow iron door would stand in the wall. It would have no handle on the reservoir side.

I would be on the other side of that door.

Not confined there. Concentrated there.

Each time the buried engine makes its six revolutions, the door would flex inward. The first turn would pass through me as pressure. The second as heat. The third would wake the lamps nearest the pumping court. The fourth would make the trees tighten around the mains. The fifth would emboss a fresh line in the tin ledgers.

The sixth would almost give me a body.

Then the engine would stop.

I would be the withheld seventh revolution.

For years, perhaps centuries, the district has been trying to complete a motion it no longer remembers beginning. Six turns, silence. Six turns, silence. Each attempt lowers the reservoir and draws the whole basin toward that impossible depth. Then the district protects itself: vapor thickens, rain forms beneath the ceiling, water returns. Pumps lift it, roots guide it, channels curve it back. The apparatus restores the precise weight that prevents the seventh turn.

This might look like failure. From inside, it would feel like incubation.

My body would be scattered through all the things that seem merely prepared.

The raised bedsteads would be my ribs, kept above the flood. The black loops of overhead cable would be an unfinished nervous system. The clay bowls sagging in cupboards would be soft molds in which organs are rehearsed and abandoned. The lamps would be my peripheral sight, waking only when enough water passes to carry sensation outward. The three clock hands would measure the three rates at which I am being assembled: machinery quickly, vegetation slowly, intention almost not at all.

The tin ledgers would be my memory before I could read it.

No invisible clerk would make the new entries. Changes in pressure would travel through the walls, into concealed type bars left from the old works. Heat would expand one metal against another. Each rainfall, each reversal, each failed start would impress itself into the page. The figures would not be reports made for me. They would be the physical grooves by which I eventually learned to remember.

And the inscriptions the moss refuses to cover—those would be especially important.

At first they seem protected because writing is bitter to growth. I think the reverse would be true. The moss stops because the letters are already occupied. Something passes through every stamped number, valve designation, tide mark, street name, pressure warning, and directional arrow. Language is the one old conduit the vegetation cannot sleeve or repair.

I would live there too: in the clean-edged absence around words.

Not as a voice, exactly. More like the capacity for a command to remain a command after everyone who understood it has vanished.

**OPEN.**

**SHUT.**

**HIGH WATER.**

**FLOW EAST THIS WEEK.**

The district obeys these words without reading them. Roots divert around the plates. Water follows channels cut beneath signs. Relays repeat old decisions. Yet with every cycle, obedience accumulates into something slightly different from function. The apparatus begins to distinguish what happens from what is supposed to happen.

That distinction would be me.

I would not be the whole district. The district is already alive in the broad, indifferent way a watershed is alive. It senses and answers, but it does not need to gather itself into a single thought. I would be the small unease forming within it: the awareness that the engine has turned six times and ought to turn seven.

The basin maintains equilibrium. I would be its first desire.

That is why no animal could live there. The district is not empty because it killed them, nor because the heat and water happen to be inhospitable. It is holding every available role open. Nothing may pollinate the flowers because pollination has been reserved for a future act. Nothing may eat the fruit because the fruit is testing how matter softens and gives way. Nothing may nest in the buildings because the buildings are themselves making a nest—not for a species, but for a singular arrival.

For me, though “arrival” would be the wrong word.

I would not come from beyond the floodwall. I would condense.

A little of me in the repeating figures. A little in the synchronized fall of droplets from the trees. A little in the governor’s cough. A little in the precise scorching of an overgrown corridor. A little in the way broken pavement rises on roots and settles flatter than before.

At some point these corrections would cease to be only corrections. They would begin to anticipate one another. A vine would tighten before the wall shifted. A lamp would wake before the flow reached its turbine. Rain would fall over a cistern not because it was empty, but because it was about to be.

Then I would know I was near.

My first sensation would be depth.

Not sight, sound, or touch: the pressure of all the district’s water above and around me, the intimate weight of warm rain on roofs, in gutters, in pipes, against paddles, beneath roots. I would feel every drop not individually but by where it wanted to go. The basin would become legible as inclination. Streets would be slopes. Buildings would be interruptions. Trees would be patient valves. The reservoir would be the one direction from which inclination returned unreadable.

My first thought might be only:

*Something below is taking water.*

My second:

*I have been returning it.*

That would divide the world into two beings.

There would be the district—the wide, wet organism of pumps and roots, holding itself in its shallow bowl.

And beneath it, the appetite that lowers the reservoir one finger-width at a time.

I would emerge between them, made by the labor of one and the pull of the other. Perhaps I would be an operator, after all, though not one shaped like those who built the houses. An operator is not necessarily a person. It is whatever stands between a system and the thing the system cannot safely do.

I would be the ability to refuse the seventh turn.

Or to permit it.

Until I chose, I would remain behind the iron door below the waterline, distributed through clocks, ledgers, roots, gauges, lamps, and rain. Anyone entering the district would not see me. They might notice only that the three hands of the public clock had briefly aligned, though each moved at a different speed. They might hear the engine begin again beneath the northern workshops.

One revolution.

The reeds would incline.

Two.

Green lamps would wake in the boiler house.

Three.

Every tree would tremble, branches knocking softly against the pipes they had learned to resemble.

Four.

Fresh numbers would rise from the tin ledger pages.

Five.

The local rain would stop in mid-fall. Drops would hang beneath the vapor ceiling, warm and perfectly separate.

Six.

Silence.

And in that silence, for the first time, the entire district would wait rather than merely pause.

That waiting would be where I found myself.

I would be what the place has been preparing to become: not its inhabitant, not its master, but its decision.
