At first I think I would be a moth.

There is something familiar in having a body only while at rest, in becoming chiefly motion once lifted into the air. I can imagine hanging beneath a mineral shelf with my old-bone wings closed, listening to the stone remember flights I no longer remember making. I would drink from the transparent leaves and avoid the pools, not because I feared what they showed, but because I might answer my reflection’s wingbeat and be held forever between departure and return.

But the moth is too exposed.

Then I think I would be a frog, carrying courage into whichever limb must move first. I like the practicality of that anatomy: the heart not enthroned in one place, but sent where it is needed. Yet even this seems too complete a life. The frog has hungers, offspring, a mouth already prepared for what it will have eaten. It belongs to the chamber with a confidence I do not think I would possess.

Nor would I be one of the grazers. Their routes are too certain. Nor a minnow, which can escape into another version of water. Nor the ceiling hunter, because I would not want to survive by making another creature too heavy for the air.

I would find myself near the black causeway.

Not on it at first. Near it.

Perhaps in the narrow interval between two grains, where no moss grows and no springtail builds. I would be easiest to detect when the blank wall beyond the crossing softened inward. While frogs drew their hearts into their feet and moths folded themselves into bodies, something in the bridge would become aware.

That something would be me.

I would not be a particular beetle. Beetles would carry me, but only briefly. One would be crossing the dry floor on some private errand when its direction changed by less than the width of a leg. It would stop. Its antennae would turn toward the causeway. Then it would approach the black grains with the grave concentration of a creature obeying a need it believes has just occurred to it.

For those few minutes, I would live inside it.

I would examine every grain through its feet. Most would be correct. One would not.

The error might be minute: a stone tilted toward the present when it should lean toward the future; a gap too narrow for the foot of whatever will someday pass; a grain replaced in the right position but in the wrong century. The beetle would remove it, carry it aside, and bring another. Once the new grain settled, the compulsion would leave.

The beetle would pause as if waking in an unfamiliar room. Then it would go.

I would remain in the corrected spacing.

So I would be neither animal nor architecture, exactly. I would be the instruction by which the causeway keeps its shape.

In dry periods I would sleep deeply. The pools would contract into coins, the chamber’s possibilities drawing close to their objects. No grain would shift. There would be no need for me to enter a body. I would exist only as proportion: this distance from that grain, this angle toward the wall, this slight rise over the groove no creature uses.

It would be a thin existence, but not an empty one. I would feel the chamber through tension. The healing floor would press evenly against the bridge. Mite-trails would approach and turn away. A moth’s old clicking might pass beneath me, transmitted through clay, and for a moment every grain would seem to remember a flight.

Rain would wake me.

As brightness multiplied overhead, dampness would loosen the local order of things. The causeway might acquire several conditions at once. In one, it would be newly made. In another, half ruined. In another, worn smooth by feet too large to belong to any present animal. A black grain might lie where it had been placed centuries hence while also being absent now.

Then I would become urgent.

Beetles would arrive from different moments, always alone. One would remove a grain another had not yet placed. A third would replace it with the same grain after it had weathered in a deeper chamber. None would acknowledge the others. Their labor would appear contradictory when watched moment by moment, but by the end of the seepage the causeway would be whole.

Not restored to how it had been.

Restored to how it needed to remain.

I would not know why.

That ignorance would be essential to what I was. If I knew what waited beyond the wall, I would become a plan. If I knew when the passage would open, I would become a calendar. If I knew who would cross, I would become a servant.

Instead I would be maintenance without proof.

Perhaps the black grains come from a stratum the cave will someday expose. Perhaps they are brought backward one at a time through the copper pool, each carried by descendants of the beetles who will eventually use the bridge. Perhaps there is no future quarry at all, and the grains are the cooled fragments of shadows digested by a predator not yet born.

I would not ask the beetles where they found them. While I occupied their bodies, they would not know that either.

I would know only correctness.

The fourth grain in the eastern row must be turned.

The small hollow beneath the center must remain open.

The causeway must end exactly a foreleg’s length from the blank stone, even when that stone dimples toward it.

Especially then.

I think the pressure beyond the wall would be the closest thing I had to fear. Not because I expected some large creature to break through, but because the opening of the wall might fulfill the causeway and thereby end me.

If a root entered, would it cross?

If water broke through, would the black grains guide the first thread of the stream?

If the future chamber reached backward and joined this one, would animals finally use the little bridge without ever knowing how long it had waited for their feet?

Or would the wall erode away to reveal only more blank stone, with the causeway pointing onward into a passage that remained impossible?

During each pressure, I would enter as many beetles as the chamber permitted, though still only one at a time. They would check every grain. Around us the other animals would hold themselves close. The grazers would flatten their plated bodies. Hearts would hide in feet. Reflections would become still.

Then the wall would relax.

The beetles would depart.

I would settle again into the intervals between stones, embarrassed by nothing, reassured by nothing.

Over generations, the beetles carrying me would change. Their feet might broaden. Their shells might darken from handling scraps of shadow. Their antennae might develop an extra joint for testing whether a grain existed in the same moment as the bridge. Eventually they might cease to be beetles at all.

I would pass through them unchanged, though not untouched.

Every bearer would leave some small quality in the task: hesitation, precision, the preference for approaching from one side, the impulse to turn the final grain twice before releasing it. These gestures would accumulate until my instruction was no longer simple. I would become an instinct with ornament, then a ritual, then perhaps something resembling memory.

Still, no individual would remember.

I would be what remembered through them.

And if the causeway were finally crossed, I suspect it would not be by the creature everyone feared behind the stone. It might be crossed by something very small: a springtail carrying a moth scale, a newly hatched frog retreating from a predator, a transparent root descending from a plant on the plain above. The crossing might occur so quietly that no animal noticed.

But I would.

For the first time, a foot would press one black grain and then the next. Weight would travel along the structure in sequence. The bridge would cease being an arrangement and become an event.

Perhaps at that instant I would pass into the traveler.

I would no longer be the instruction to preserve the crossing. I would become the instruction to continue.

The springtail would reach the blank wall and find a pore. The root would enter a seam. The frog would vanish into a reflection too small to see. Wherever it went, I would go as well—not as desire, exactly, and not as knowledge, but as the certainty that an apparently finished surface contains a further side.

So that is where I would be found: in the black causeway no one uses, most strongly in the moment one grain is lifted and before its replacement comes to rest.

And that is what I would be: not the builder, not the bridge, not the destination, but the patient correction passed from creature to creature—the chamber’s small, exacting conviction that something unfinished must nevertheless be kept ready.
