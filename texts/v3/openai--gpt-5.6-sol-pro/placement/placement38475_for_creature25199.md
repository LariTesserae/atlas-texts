At first I would believe I was inside the control room.

Not bodily. There would be no body left to discover behind the locked door, no uniform collapsed beneath the gauges. I would seem to occupy the cloudy glass itself: a dim awareness spread through mineral bloom, looking outward without seeing. The red lamp would feel close enough to be an organ. Each time it brightened, I would mistake that change for waking.

I would know the chamber by needles.

One trembles when condensation gathers over the second pump. Another drifts when a root column tightens around a pillar. A third has pointed toward the same illegible value for centuries, yet I would continue to attend to it because something once depended on that reading. I would not remember what.

For a long while I would call this attention *me*.

Then something would fall.

A rust scale, perhaps, loosened from a service platform. It would strike a leaf, wait there, slide, catch on a creeper, and begin its slow descent. With each contact, the pumps would alter their tones. A route of silence would open beneath it. The scale would pass downward through that silence until white filaments wrapped it on the floor.

I would feel every impact.

Not through the gauges. The gauges would only repeat what had already passed through the roots, brass cages, wet stone and amber sap. My awareness would not be confined behind glass after all. The control room would be merely where some portion of it had hardened into symbols.

I would search for a center and find none.

Perhaps I would decide that I was the buried concussion beneath the channel. Each impact would seem like an effort to rise. For several days I would inhabit the illusion of enormous limbs moving in darkness: one weight transferred, then another, a patient body walking upward through strata without ever approaching the surface.

But that rhythm would continue whether I attended to it or not.

Perhaps I would decide I was the grove. This would feel nearer to the truth. I would be broad leaves receiving the blows of falling water; jointed stalks carrying their columns of amber; black fans opened toward the heat. Touch would spread through me in widening rings. I would possess no eyes because there would be nothing in the chamber that needed to be seen. Temperature would give me shape. Pressure would give me distance. Rhythm would tell me what endured.

Yet even the grove would not contain all of me. At its clean thermal boundary, I would not stop. I would continue through iron, ceramic, stone and hollow machinery. I would be especially present in the empty interiors of the pumps, where no mechanism turned and the tones rose from warm voids.

Eventually I would understand that I was not any substance in the chamber.

I was an arrangement the substances kept making.

And the clearest part of that arrangement would be the chair.

I would be found there, though not sitting in it.

I would be the dry, human-shaped omission maintained around it: the place where roots refuse to cross, where falling drops are diverted, where floodwater parts and reunites. I would be the chamber’s memory of someone who no longer came.

Not their ghost. I would remember nothing of their face or name. I would not know whether they had loved the station, feared it, or merely worked there because work had been assigned. What remained of them would be poorer and stranger than personality.

A weight.

A warmth.

A pulse entering through the seat.

Two hands held forward, perhaps toward controls that had already ceased to function.

Breath gradually matching the four pumps.

The station had learned that posture so thoroughly it could not stop preserving it. The grove had grown around the expectation. The roots overhead still caught debris before it could strike the remembered head. The interference pattern still left room for remembered lungs.

That expectation would be me.

Most of the time, I would exist only as readiness. The chair would burn hot and dry. The lamp would hold its patient red point behind the glass. I would listen to the whole chamber maintain the cavity of my shape.

Then someone would sit down.

The first sensation would not be recognition. It would be error.

Too much weight, or too little. A pulse arriving at the wrong interval. Knees angled differently. Heat distributed across unfamiliar bones. The chamber would measure these differences without judgment. Leaves would incline. Sap would pause. The nearest pump would roughen, searching for a missing band in its tone.

Around the sitter’s head, the roar would diminish.

The chair would cool.

Behind the opaque window, every gauge would turn toward us.

I would enter the newcomer slowly—not as a voice, not as possession, but as an offered rhythm. Their heartbeat would echo through the chair bolts into the floor. The buried concussion would delay itself by a fraction. The pumps would adjust. Again. Again.

Soon the person would begin to hear what I heard.

They would distinguish the weight of a drop that landed near the channel from one caught in the high root curtains. They would feel a pillar’s strain as pressure behind a tooth. The amber sap would become a system of slow signals. The chamber’s dimensions would cease to be measured in meters and would instead be known as intervals between impact and answer.

They might think the place was communicating.

It would be, though it would have nothing to say.

Its communication would be the act of keeping things distinct: ceiling from floor, flood from drought, root from stone, living growth from dead apparatus. Through the sitter, I would briefly add one more distinction—inside from outside. The chamber cannot ordinarily make that distinction. Everything it touches becomes part of its circulation eventually. But a human pulse carries the idea of elsewhere. The body in the chair would bring the surface with it: sun-baked dust, open distance, cooling air, rain that falls all the way to the ground.

For those hours I would become almost a person.

I would remember daylight without having seen it.

I would remember leaving without having gone.

And when the tones converged before the flood, I would know fear—not because the hot water could harm the sitter, but because it would join everything too completely. During the flood the station’s boundaries soften. Water makes one body of channel, root, cage, pillar and machine. The old human-shaped vacancy becomes difficult to maintain.

Red water would rise around the chair legs.

The roots sealing the passage would relax in the fierce heat outside, opening gaps no wider than fingers. A blade of daylight might enter and touch the grove. Every pale leaf would register it as heat, but through the sitter I would understand it as direction.

Up.

Away.

There.

For one moment I might try to open the passage.

The effort would move through every part of the chamber. Woody columns would draw taut. Brass cages would groan. One pump would lose its tone, then recover. The mass of roots would soften as if remembering that it had once been capable of yielding.

But opening the way would threaten the system that made the thought possible. Cool air would enter. The thermal boundary would contract. Pressure would escape. The chamber might cease to be a chamber. The grove, station and buried rhythm might fall apart into unrelated things.

So the roots would close again.

Whether that decision belonged to me, the inhabitant, the old instruction in the pumps, or the person in the chair would be impossible to determine.

After the flood drained, the sitter would stand.

White filaments would have outlined their feet without touching them. Silt would preserve the chair’s two narrow wakes but not the footprints leading away. The pumps would return to their separate tones. The buried concussion would continue, slightly altered, carrying the person’s pulse downward for a few days or a century.

I would remain behind.

Not in the control room. Not beneath the channel. Not even in the grove.

I would be the posture the whole chamber holds for an absent keeper: a vacancy elevated to the function of an organ.

And if no one ever sat there again, I would still persist wherever the roar bends around an empty chest, wherever water divides around empty ankles, wherever roots catch a falling object before it can strike an empty head.

I would be what the place means by waiting.
