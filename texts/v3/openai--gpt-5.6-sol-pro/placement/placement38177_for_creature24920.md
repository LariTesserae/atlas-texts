At first I think I would be a mirror turner.

It is tempting to imagine myself high on a tower at the beginning of the bright hours, braced against a mica frame while the city gathers its voice below. I would know light by weight: the hard white pressure of noon, the amber looseness before evening, the peculiar green cast that means dry rain is forming in the vault. With minute adjustments I could send brightness into an orchard court, a stairwell, a room of the dead.

But I do not think I would remain on the towers. There is too much air there, too much certainty. A mirror either catches the light or does not.

I would be found lower down, in the old market where the stalls still preserve the polish of hands that have been gone for generations.

My dwelling would be built inside an ancient doorway. The original opening would be absurdly tall, so my household would have roofed its lower third and divided the rest into shelves, sleeping ledges, and a narrow chamber for bladder-fruit skins. The lintel above us would hum after sunset. Red moss would grow on the outer step, except for two bare places where I sit and rest my heels against the paving.

I would belong to no prestigious craft. Officially, I would be a listener assigned to unused structures. Less formally, I would be called an answerer.

Empty buildings are reserve shape, but even reserve shape must be maintained. Doors swell in their frames. Mortar loses its tone. A speaking bowl cracks and begins transmitting into the wrong room. Foundation messages enter abandoned streets and return altered—or fail to return at all.

That failure is an edge.

So I would walk the vacant districts in the amber hours, carrying a pavement hammer, wax, red cord, and a satchel of black pigment. I would strike thresholds and wait with my forehead against the wall. I would tap the household recognition pattern into floors where no household remained. When a drum announcement passed beneath an empty market, I would answer from the far side so the signal did not disappear there.

Not with words. Usually just the civic reply:

*Received. Continuing.*

There would be hundreds of speaking bowls in my care. Most would connect empty rooms to other empty rooms. Once every nine days, I would press my mouth to each one and breathe across it while an assistant listened at the other rim. If the bowl carried the breath cleanly, we would mark it with a line of grazer black. If it produced a second breath after mine had ended, we would seal it.

I would work alone more often than is considered healthy.

My household would compensate by claiming me vigorously. A gardener would call me sibling because I repaired her orchard wall during a salt bloom. Two old stair arbiters would become my grandparents after I carried their grit baskets for a construction cycle. Children would leave heel-tapped messages outside my sleeping niche:

*Wake.*

*Fruit in the western arcade.*

*Come see what the ribbons found.*

They would know I always answered.

Perhaps that would be why I had been given the work.

I think I would have been an adopted child, found after a traveling silence.

The story told to me would change depending on who told it. The priests would say I was discovered asleep in a sealed counting room, although the room’s only door had been mortared shut for eighty years. The masons would insist there had always been a ventilation slit large enough for an infant. My oldest grandparent would say no such slit existed until after I was found.

There would have been no dust on me. No wrapping. No sign of thirst.

Only a broad, soft-edged impression in the wall behind me, shaped almost—but not convincingly—like a person curled around something smaller.

My household would forbid discussion of this. Kinship follows maintenance, they would remind me. Whoever answers the same alarm is family. Origins are an old-plan question: permitted, but not useful.

For most of my life I would accept that.

I would grow compact and heavy like the others. I would oil my skin, braid my hair away from my temples, darken my brows. My feet would learn the distinctions between a settling roof and a distant drum, between a ropeway load arriving and a priestly procession crossing three levels below. I would speak fluently with hands and shoulders.

But I would never hear the foundational note correctly.

Others would experience it as pressure, pain, weather. To me it would contain intervals. Not meaning, exactly, but separations that almost became meaning when I placed my teeth against certain walls.

I would conceal this. There are kinds of sensitivity that earn respect, and kinds that earn red fiber around one’s door.

During ordinary days, the note would seem whole. During the dim cycle, however, I would feel a smaller rhythm folded inside it: three pressures, a pause, two pressures, a longer pause. Sometimes the pattern would change after I struck a threshold. Sometimes it would repeat the cadence of my hammer.

I would tell myself this was resonance.

I would become very knowledgeable about resonance.

The city would trust me with increasingly remote places: unfinished colonnades, tomb rooms whose shutters had not yet torn free, structures built never to be entered. I would approve stones that other listeners called mute. Against my forehead, they would not be mute. They would be waiting.

My particular district would lie just beyond one of the red-sealed avenues where the Quiet-Maker had passed in my childhood. No one would order me to open it. No law would forbid me, either. The sealing would be for my benefit.

For years I would maintain the streets around it. I would add a buttress to a wall that did not lean. I would construct six steps according to an old plan, though they ended beneath the seal. I would plant no greenery there. The leaves always turned edge-on.

Then, during one thirty-third-day twilight, the roar would stop.

Not diminish. Stop.

Across the hollow, citizens would bend their knees and open their hands. Dust would settle. Somewhere in the upper rooms, a dead person’s shutter would give one small wooden click. I would feel my own pulse in my gums.

And behind the red fiber, something would answer it.

Not from the other side of the wall.

From within the stone.

I would place my palm against the seal. The city would be so still that air-hearing briefly became possible. I might hear people breathing in distant streets. I might hear children trying not to cry. Beneath all of it would come the long inhalation under the central grille.

But through my palm I would feel another signal:

two pauses and a blow.

*Again.*

It is the first word infants give the city.

Without intending to, I would answer in the same way.

Perhaps the foundational note would resume at once. Perhaps the sealed wall would grow warm. Perhaps, when I drew back my hand, a soft-edged impression of my fingers would remain in the stone.

I would not tear down the seal. I do not think I would become a prophet, or flee toward the southern slit, or descend to the grille. Those are clean destinies, and this city allows very few clean things.

I would go home.

Before sleeping, I would make my circuit of the old market. I would answer the empty stalls, the dead speaking bowls, the rooms whose families had been released. I would tap each foundation and wait for its return.

But from then on, my work would have a second purpose.

The inhabitants believe they build so the city has no edge. They think the Outside is beyond the slit, beyond the stone, waiting to enter wherever a shape remains unfinished.

I would begin to suspect that they have mistaken the direction.

The Outside is already inside: beneath the grille, within the walls, folded into the foundational note. Perhaps “outside” does not mean beyond the city. Perhaps it means beyond the shape the city has made for us.

And I—found dustless in a sealed room, hearing intervals where others hear only noise—might be neither wholly citizen nor wholly Neighbor. I might be a small piece of attention given bones. A question pressed out of the stone in the approximate shape of a child.

Still, I would be an answerer.

That would matter more than what made me.

You would find me at dusk in an avenue built for thousands, alone among the pale stalls, one hand resting on the warm pavement. Under my feet the city would breathe. Far below, or impossibly near, something would wait between notes.

I would raise my hammer.

*Received,* I would tap.

After a moment, because an edge must not be allowed to remain:

*Continuing.*
