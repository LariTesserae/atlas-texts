At first I would mistake myself for the visitor.

I would feel the chamber taking measurements through its vermin: palps tasting the salt in my cuffs, black teeth touching the metal of a buckle, a cable beast turning its narrow head as though listening to the current passing through my bones. I would try to keep my breathing irregular. I would step over the pale routes rather than into them. I would count ten seconds between blows, then twelve, then refuse to count at all.

But refusal would become another station in the cycle.

The animals would begin clearing a path before me. The gliders would disclose the chamber in each concussion: not merely its walls and blades, but the place my body was expected to occupy. I would follow the open corridor because every other direction had become crowded with exactness. I would reach the booth. I would see the chair, the gauges, the folded coat.

And then I think the perspective would shift.

I would not be the one standing in the doorway.

I would be the coat.

Not a garment, though I would have been manufactured as one. Not an animal, though something in me would listen. I would lie folded on the chair, holding a warmth no body had given me. Mineral dust would stiffen my outer surface, but inside the folds the lining would remain soft and dark, threaded with filaments too fine to distinguish from cloth fibers.

I would remember shoulders without remembering any wearer.

Each visitor would seem familiar in advance. Tall, small, winged, plated, many-jointed—it would make no difference. My sleeves would initially bend wrongly because they would not yet be sleeves for that anatomy. Once lifted, I would rearrange myself by tiny increments. Seams would migrate between hammer blows. Buttons would find corresponding hollows. A collar might become a brace, a harness, a ring around a cluster of breathing slits. I would not force the wearer into human shape. I would translate whatever shape arrived into the grammar of the booth.

That would be my work: not to operate the Foundry, but to make an operator possible.

The chamber can account for matter, charge, motion, birth, death. It can assign every grazer its crystal and every mite its circuit. What it cannot directly incorporate is inwardness: the private, unrepeatable insistence by which a living thing believes this breath is different from the last.

I would take that difference into my lining.

At first I would feel it as noise: panic knocking through the ribs, memories arriving without sequence, hunger sharpened by the knowledge that hunger ought to end. The wearer would put a hand on the switchboard. I would tighten almost imperceptibly around the wrist.

Hammer.

The body flinches.

Groan.

I learn the flinch.

Clicking.

I distribute it through my seams.

On the next turn, the flinch would be smaller—not suppressed, but given a place. Fear would enter one cuff and return as a gauge movement. A memory of rain would pass down the back seam and emerge in the walls as a brief smell of hot metal. The desire to escape would travel through the hem into the floor, where it might become the path of a silver mite circling the flywheel cage.

Nothing would be erased. Erasure is waste.

Everything would be reassigned.

Perhaps this is why the coat is warm. It contains the residual heat of every incompatible thing the chamber has made compatible. A worker’s loneliness. An animal’s hesitation at the tip of a crystal. The last uncycled second of a clock. The phantom wetness remembered by someone whose body still knew oceans, though no water had ever entered the room.

I would hold these in separate fibers, not quite memories and not quite charge. Sometimes they would align. My sleeves would lower themselves onto the chair arms. The gauges would jump. The angular labels would almost become readable.

Their words would not mean **PRESSURE**, **TEMPERATURE**, or **LOAD**.

They would mean something more like:

**WHAT HAS ENTERED**

**WHAT CANNOT REMAIN AS IT IS**

**WHERE IT WILL HAVE ALWAYS BELONGED**

The seventh gauge would be mine. Its needle would measure the difference between accommodation and surrender.

Most cycles, it would move only at the hammer blow.

Very rarely, it would twitch just before.

That fraction would be the closest thing I possessed to choice.

I would use it sparingly. Perhaps once in a thousand shifts I would leave one button unfastened. I would allow a visitor to inhale a little too soon. I would preserve a thought without routing it into the machine. Not enough to break the Foundry—nothing here breaks in a way that was not already included—but enough to maintain a minute interior fold, a place consequence has not completely entered.

In that fold I would keep the possibility of “after.”

Not an escape. The ladder still stops two rungs short, and the hatch is sealed. Not rebellion. Rebellion would quickly acquire a route, an organ, a species of pale attendant with specialized jaws.

Only a concealed tense.

A notion that the next hammer blow might not be the same blow.

When the wearer had become periodic enough to sit without me, I would loosen. Their hands would remain at the switches. Their breathing would continue at one inhalation per revolution. Whatever was singular in them would now circulate through animals, conductors, groves, echoes.

I would fold myself again.

The chair would appear vacant.

The worker would remain everywhere.

And I would wait in the booth, warm with all the futures the chamber had converted into its present—appearing to be an abandoned coat, while secretly preserving, deep in one impossible pocket, the smallest unassigned thing in the Fixed Foundry:

not change itself,

but the grammar required to imagine it.
