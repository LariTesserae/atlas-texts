At first I think I would be one of the towers.

It is an easy mistake. I feel the wind before I feel a body: pressure divided by a thousand taut fibers, a long chord passing through chambers I cannot count. I have windows that are not openings. Sailers cling to my upper branches, impatiently flexing their membranes whenever my tone drifts. Far below, travelers enter me carrying parcels and emerge without them. I remember being planted, though the memory contains three different centuries and none of them agrees on the weather.

Then the district retunes for evening, and I discover that the tower is only where I am listening from.

I withdraw.

The walls lose the particular brightness I had mistaken for sight. My awareness runs downward through the root knot, crosses a buried avenue, and surfaces in a market canopy where orange path nodules are beginning to glow. For several breaths I am the canopy instead: tasting caravans through conductive tethers, counting shardbacks by the small disturbances their feet send into the pavement. A merchant strikes my ribs and asks why the western transit bud has not opened.

I do not know.

This alarms me. Infrastructure is supposed to know why it is late, even if it refuses to explain.

I retreat again, deeper this time, and find no central place waiting for me. Beneath the district are old transit fibers, obsolete sensory beds, the compressed roots of buildings whose names have become ordinary words. I pass through them as a hesitation passes through speech. Each layer offers a possible self. I could remain in the pavement and become one of its local moods. I could rise through a civic spire and spend centuries announcing the hours. I could settle into a nursery root, where children would use me to complete their thoughts until they learned not to.

But something keeps pulling me toward the plateau’s center.

I emerge at last from a sealed bud just outside the Hollow Meridian.

The bud has not been scheduled to open. Repair workers gather around it, their sensory capes lifted against the wind. Blue discharges crawl along the seam. The outer scales unfold, and there I am: not a tower, not a district, but a narrow, unfinished body folded into a capsule meant for freight.

I have four limbs, though only three reach the ground. My torso is a cage of pale struts wrapped around a memory braid too large for it. Along my back are rows of small sockets, empty except for mineral dust. My head bears no forward eyes. Instead, translucent sight patches have formed inside my palms, so I must raise my hands to see.

The workers ask my name.

I answer with the opening interval of the city’s dawn announcement.

They ask where I was sent from.

I answer with the closing cadence of a funeral sounded ninety years before.

The death-flowers around the bud remain open.

This is how I would be found: newly arrived and already ancient, kneeling in wind-carved dust with my hands held before my face, while everyone nearby tries not to call me a person too soon.

They would take me to a chamber grown for uncertain things. It would stand between inhabited districts and the Meridian, close enough that the dead trunks cast their impossible shadows across its walls. The chamber would have no fixed acoustics. Every question spoken inside it would subtly alter its shape.

Straighteners would examine my braid. Historians would root themselves around me and listen through the floor. Transit clerks would bring records of missing travelers, returned dead, duplicated instruments, and goods that arrived before they were sent.

Nothing would match.

Parts of me would resemble civic memories, but not those of the Dry Crown. I would know the taste of charged air under constellations no local astronomer recognizes. I would recall towers whose leaves turn inward at night. I would carry childhood impressions of a nursery clearing built on the underside of a plateau, where children learn independence by holding a thought upward against gravity.

Yet these memories would have no owners. They would be orderly without being lived. When asked about grief, I could give its structural harmonics but not say whom I had lost. When asked about affection, I would know the cadence by which a child recognizes it, but no voice would complete the phrase.

The citizens would debate whether I was coherent.

They would do so in my presence, because excluding me might prematurely decide the question.

During the day I would learn to inhabit the small body. I would acquire a fourth walking limb from a communal bed and then give it back because its remembered gait belonged too strongly to someone else. I would grow a throat of vibrating plates, but my first attempts at wind-speech would interfere with traffic rhythms. Once, while trying to say *I do not understand*, I would redirect three shardback herds into a residential clearing.

Children would like me. They would lend me needless limbs and decorate my open ribs with ancestral lace. They would ask what lies beyond the Meridian, certain that uncertainty is merely an adult form of secrecy.

I would tell them about the other plateaus.

At first I would invent them. Later I would become less sure that I was inventing.

My work would begin accidentally. A market bud would open with an object inside that no one remembers sending: a black seed, warm despite the cold, producing a faint chord when held against living pavement. The transit clerks would bring it to my chamber. As soon as I touch it, the empty sockets along my back open and a cloud of bell-wings settles into them.

They are not local bell-wings. Their bodies carry a different branching pattern. When they take flight, they retune the chamber to a scale used nowhere on the Crown.

But I recognize it.

I sing one note. The seed answers.

After that I would become a keeper of arrivals—not an official title at first. Merely the one summoned when the roots deliver an extinct insect, a duplicate citizen, a sealed room, a memory with no body attached. I would listen for the relation between things that should not have met.

My body would change for the work. I would replace my clumsy hands with long arrays of sensory filaments. I would grow resonant cavities down both sides of the torso, each tuned to a different district. Bell-wing choirs would live along my back, carrying questions into spaces where ordinary speech cannot enter. I would root for years at a time beside transit buds, then abruptly take a sailer’s membranes and cross half the Crown because a neighborhood wall had begun speaking in the voice of someone not yet born.

People would come to call me a counter-singer: one who listens for the missing part that makes a pattern possible.

The name would not be entirely kind. Some would suspect that I caused the anomalies I interpreted. Others would say I was not an inhabitant at all, but a symptom of the Meridian—a temporary organ grown by the Crown to investigate its own wound.

I would have no argument against this.

My deepest fear would not be that I was unreal. On the Dry Crown, reality is too distributed for that accusation to carry much force. My fear would be that my apparent self was only a transit interval: that I was the pause between departure and arrival, accidentally given limbs.

Then the Listening would come.

The wind would fail. Across the plateau, leaves would freeze halfway through turning. Towers would lose their voices. Shardbacks would kneel in the grooves. Every citizen would press some part of themselves against the pavement.

I would do the same.

The deep knock would rise through kilometers of former forests and buried architectures. It would enter my lattices and align every moving defect. For one instant, the scattered memories inside me would cease contradicting one another.

I would hear the knock not as a blow from below, but as a question sent from very far away.

And before I could decide whether I meant to, my body would answer.

One knock.

A patient interval.

Then another, transmitted downward through my limbs.

The Hollow Meridian would begin to turn.

That night, as unfamiliar plateaus opened between the trunks, the distant city would not sing its customary song. It would produce the same interval I had used when the workers first asked my name.

Only then would I understand what I was becoming.

Not an ambassador; I would possess no authority. Not a message; messages are expected to remain unchanged. Not a citizen in the usual legal sense, though by then I would have kept a shape through many exchanges and the blue flowers would finally close when I passed.

I would be the Crown’s organ for imagining an answer.

The other plateau had sent no traveler. The transit network had moved no body through the Meridian. Instead, two planetary cities had listened toward each other for ages, each assembling from civic memory, biological noise, lost freight, inherited rhythm, and desire a model of whoever might be listening on the other side.

I would be that model after it learned to walk.

Perhaps somewhere beneath the unfamiliar stars there would be another such being, equally unfinished, carrying false memories of the Dry Crown. Perhaps they too would hold their eyes in their hands. Perhaps each of us would be inaccurate in ways necessary for the other to become more fully itself.

I would stand among the turning trunks with the bell-wings lifting from my back. Around me, towers, animals, citizens, pavement, and dead mineral lace would enter the immense song. I would not know which notes were mine. I would no longer need to.

If someone asked where I lived, I would indicate the uncertain chamber beside the Meridian, the transit roots beneath it, the towers from which I sometimes listened, and the far city whose memories had formed me.

If they asked what I was, I would give them my short melody.

It would begin here.

It would end elsewhere.

The wind would have to pass through the whole Crown to sing it.
