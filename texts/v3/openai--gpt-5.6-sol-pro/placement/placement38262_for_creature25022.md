At first I would think I belonged on the embankment.

It is the natural place for anyone who cannot decide whether the town is an organism or an arrangement: one foot turned toward the gravel flats, the other toward the lamps below. I would walk the oval after rain and watch the basin darken in sections. From there, movement would become legible—the delayed lifting of a pressure grove, the root tram’s slow passage, the faint adjustment of roofs as water entered them.

But I would not stay there. The embankment is a place for perspective, and perspective would soon begin to feel like evasion.

I would descend rainward, past the civic column with three generations of iron collars, toward the low-root side of the central square. There, behind the Office of Strain, is a building that no longer agrees about what it is. Its front rooms remain offices. Its rear wall once belonged to a dye works. The stairwell has grafted to an old cistern tower, though the legal recognition of this union has been pending for thirty-one years. On maps it appears as a hatched uncertainty.

I would live in its uppermost room.

The room would be narrow in winter and almost comfortable after rain. One window would overlook the square; the other would have closed long ago, leaving a pale oval in the wall where light still passed thinly through the tissue. My shelves would stand away from the walls. A kettle would hang from a hook rather than rest on the sloping floor. Beside the bed would be a bowl of obsolete keys, none inherited from my family. I would collect them from rooms condemned to closure and label them with whatever could still be learned:

**East nursery, door sealed after swelling.**

**Pump clerk’s cupboard, location uncertain.**

**No record. Brass. Warm when found.**

I would be human. This matters. I would burn my porridge, lose gloves, resent forms, and sometimes pretend not to hear someone knocking. Nothing secret in the root mat would have shaped me into another species. Yet I would have one of those occupations that makes the distinction feel less decisive.

I would be the night clerk for transferred strain.

Officially, transferred strain is an explanation. When a pipe pulses without connection to a pump, when a retired pressure trunk lifts an empty beam, when warmth appears beneath an unmapped floor, the disturbance is traced outward until some acceptable source is found. A mill releases weight three districts away. A drying wall tightens around an old conduit. A tram wave crosses a buried graft. Force travels. The basin is crowded with consequences whose causes are elsewhere.

Most reports end that way.

My work would begin with the ones that do not.

After the lamps came on, I would carry a folding stool, chalk, a tension fork, and three small brass bells into the passages beneath the Office. The bells would be attached to selected wires. One would answer lateral pull, one compression, one changes too gradual for the gauges. I would sit among pipes furred with silver moss and wait.

Waiting would not resemble idleness there. The town would arrive piece by piece.

A contraction beneath the ceramic works.

The long relaxation of the western house bodies.

Water entering a bladder below the square.

A vine elevator descending after curfew.

Someone pacing in a room overhead.

Each made its own passage through the structure. I would record the time, interval, direction, weather, and likely source. Where a source could not be found, I would write the approved phrase:

**Transferred strain; origin unresolved. Observe through two wet cycles.**

At first I would believe that my value lay in precision. I would keep clean tables. I would compare bells against gauges and gauges against the testimony of cistern listeners. I would take pride in distinguishing a pressure trunk’s reluctance from ordinary cooling. Engineers would bring me awkward phenomena they did not want in their daytime reports.

Then, over years, another record would begin inside the official one.

Not proof. Never proof. A recurrence.

A pulse below the mapped depth, arriving after the factories had released their weights but before the roots settled for the night. It would not be regular enough to be a machine. It would not follow rain closely enough to be growth. Sometimes it would cross the basin from dryward to rainward. Sometimes it would seem to rise directly beneath my stool.

Older listeners would have noted it in obsolete terms: *deep knocking*, *underwarmth*, *the late answer*. One margin, written perhaps sixty years before my birth, would say:

**Do not measure only the interval. Measure what has paused to permit it.**

That sentence would become the center of my life.

I would begin to notice that before the deep pulse, the ordinary town grew briefly quiet. Not silent—the gutters still ticked, belts settled, moths touched the roof skin—but unopposed. Strains that ordinarily crossed and muddled one another seemed to align. For several seconds the basin would cease being a thousand local negotiations and behave like one held thing.

Then the pulse would pass.

I would not decide that the town was conscious. That would be too rigid a conclusion, a sealed surface. Nor would I accept that it was merely transferred force. “Merely” would seem equally careless.

My task would become preserving the question at the correct width.

This would make me a kind of separator.

Not between houses, but between explanations. I would maintain a finger-width seam between the basin as body and the basin as commonwealth. I would keep either belief from grafting over the other and declaring the matter closed. Any fool could choose a side. My work would be teaching the uncertainty to remain a boundary without becoming a wound.

People would find this mildly irritating. The Office would value my records and distrust my summaries. Children would dare one another to ask whether I had heard the town speak. I would tell them no. If they were patient, I would add that speech was probably the wrong shape of expectation.

I would have soft-soled shoes and pale chalk permanently worked into my cuffs. I would smell of brass, damp bark, and the mineral cold of underground rooms. At meals I would stop when a spoon trembled against a bowl. Friends would wait for me to say whether it mattered.

Usually it would not.

I would belong to an involuntary household created when my building joined the cistern tower. Across the seam would live a membrane cutter and her son. We would share heat, liability, and a kitchen wall that thickened whenever arguments lasted too long. The boy would leave tiny houses made of pruned fiber outside my door. If one rooted, I would report it as required, but only after giving him a day to discover it himself.

When my room narrowed, I would not move immediately. I would pare the threshold, shift the bed, and let the shelves go crooked. Eventually the pale oval of the lost window might open into the neighboring stair, not by design but because the tissue between them tired. The Office would call it an unauthorized connection. We would hang a curtain and use it.

Years later, perhaps, I would become one of the old people consulted before a cut.

A young engineer would kneel in my room with perfect instruments and explain that the cistern tower was pulling the rear wall low-root. She would recommend an iron collar. I would listen to the wall, not theatrically but because I had listened there for half my life.

“Wait until it remembers winter,” I would say.

I would know how that sounded.

After my death, I would ask to be sent to a heat bed adjoining the listening passages—not into any load-bearing structure, and not into the diagnostic moss. For a season or two, the compost warmth might keep condensation from forming on the bells. The night clerk who followed me would benefit without being obliged to think of me.

My bark tablet would be recut once, perhaps twice. Then it would blur.

The notebooks would last longer. Most pages would be ordinary: shifts, releases, tram waves, unauthorized motors, rain sorted into civic consequence. In the margins, in several inks, the deep pulse would continue without explanation.

And below the central square, after the final stored weight descended and the lamps steadied in their windows, something would pass through the roots.

The new clerk would lift a pencil.

The basin would hold itself still enough to be heard.
