At first I would look for myself among the visible creatures.

Perhaps in one of the pale owls standing at dusk on a broken vent, toes spread against the cooling wall, feeling the district through its feet. It would be tempting to inhabit that height—not because it overlooks the basin, since nothing here can truly overlook it, but because the tower gathers so many small transmissions. The builders rasping below. A runner tapping a warning into the road. The nearly imperceptible shift of a herd. I could imagine becoming an animal that listens downward.

Or I might be one of the long-necked browsers, going still among the chimneys. There would be a certain peace in surviving through resemblance: bone shelves over bronze eyes, white bristles among thorns, the neck aligned with structures made by lives too small to perceive individually. To disappear not by hiding, but by becoming architecturally plausible.

But I do not think I would remain in the open.

The longer I attend to the basin, the farther down I go. Beneath the owl’s feet. Beneath the browser’s shadow. Into the shaded face of a mound where old masonry meets new, where one colony has grown against another and neither lineage wholly owns the wall.

I would find myself there: in a repair seam.

It would be near one of the dense knots formed where a buried clay band crosses an old animal road. The ground above would be crowded with towers, thorn crowns, dung-fed plants, vents enlarged by birds, and paths pressed deep enough to gather the next rare rain. Below, the architecture would be less legible. Passages from different centuries would meet at wrong angles. Some would still carry workers; others would end in packed bodies, roots, or rooms slowly becoming soil. Air from a living chamber would leak through a fracture into an abandoned gallery and condense, for a few minutes before dawn, along one mineral lip.

That lip would be my country.

I would be small even by the standards of the builders: a soft-jointed beetle, salt-pale, with a back roughened like drying clay. Not exactly shaped like a worker. Not convincingly enough to survive by appearance. My disguise would be chemical and imperative.

I would smell like damaged masonry.

Not like one particular wall, but like the condition of a wall requiring attention: exposed dampness, fractured clay, fungal contamination, the opened breath of an inner chamber. The builders encountering me would not recognize a creature. They would recognize work.

Antennae would touch my back and recoil. Workers would arrive carrying grains. Others would scrape the surface around me, clean it, coat it with saliva, test the moving air. In the disturbance I would feed—not on the builders themselves, but on the films exposed by their labor, on fungal threads peeled from old clay, on droplets gathered where fresh material meets cool stone. If hungry, I might raise the scent more strongly. If threatened with enclosure, I would quiet it, flatten my legs, and slip into the confusion between the old wall and its repair.

My life would be a succession of urgent places.

I would never possess a chamber. Possession is too conspicuous. I would move along uncertain borders where colony odors overlap and authority weakens: a sealed battle front, an inherited nursery, a foreign tunnel newly admitted into local traffic. Because I imitate damage rather than kinship, lineage would matter less to me. Narrow-tower builders and dome builders might respond differently, but both would understand fracture. I could cross districts by carrying the one passport older than any colony: something is failing here.

My eggs would be laid singly inside incomplete repairs. The larvae would be pale and ridged, easily mistaken for root tips or beads of moist mortar. Builders might wall around them without killing them. There, enclosed in humid darkness, they would graze on microscopic growth and the soft residue of throat secretions. Most would die when a wall dried too quickly, when a lizard entered a vent, when workers decided to remove rather than mend. A few would emerge through contraction cracks months later, already carrying on their skins the chemistry of collapse.

I would be a parasite, but not cleanly so.

My false alarm would cost the colony labor. A wall might become too thick where no thickening was needed. A vent might narrow. Workers might waste moisture sealing a harmless surface. Yet some of the fractures I occupied would be real, or would become real under my feet. Repairs summoned for my sake might brace a settling chamber. A root pressing into a nursery wall might be stopped. A leak that would have dried a fungal garden might close because I lingered beside it.

No one could say whether the settlement was stronger without me.

That uncertainty would be my ecological niche.

At dawn, when cold air sank through the outer vents, I would come near the surface. Not into daylight, but close enough to feel it as temperature moving through masonry. Beyond the wall, grazers would be passing along the road. Their padded feet would send broad, soft pressures through the foundation. A chalk pellet dropped from one mouth might strike the crust and roll against the tower. Workers would register the vibration one way, the owl above another, a threshold spider in its silk another.

I would know it as a brief loosening of dust from the ceiling.

By noon I would retreat behind a root that had entered an abandoned chamber. By dusk the wall would begin to exhale. Moisture would appear along my joints. Builders would stream past carrying dung, fibers, dead moths, flakes of salt, the bodies of their own. Somewhere deeper, reproductive mothers would continue producing eggs without knowing the tower’s shape. Somewhere below them, a wedge-headed burrower might be cutting a tunnel that would collapse into a channel centuries later.

I would know none of this.

I would know one seam becoming dry. One worker’s antenna. One taste of fungus. One current cooling the left side of my body. I would act within that smallness, causing a repair here, an obstruction there, a new line of masonry around the hollow where my larva slept.

Eventually I would die inside a wall I had induced others to build.

The builders would not mourn or distinguish me. They might dismantle part of the chamber, carry away my softer tissues, and leave the hardened wing cases mixed with clay. Salt would gather around them. A root hair would enter the gap. Water from some future, insufficient rain would follow the root. The wall would soften, slump, and redirect a passage. Much later the tower’s outer face might collapse and become a low ridge along the road.

So I would remain, not as a body and not as an intention, but as a slight excess of structure: a thickening, a deflection, a place where the wall had once been repaired around a lie.

That is where I would find myself in this world—not at its center, because it has no center, and not above it, where it resembles a city.

I would be inside the error by which the city continues to become terrain.
