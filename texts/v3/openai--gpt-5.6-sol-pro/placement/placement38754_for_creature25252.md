At first I place myself among the lower explorers.

The temptation is strong: a cord around my waist, a dark shell mirror in one hand, my body lowered through a street that has opened into another street. I imagine being the first to put bare feet on pavement tilted beneath the present city, finding a threshold whose household marks resemble my own. I imagine returning with some softened tool that should not exist yet.

But I do not think I would trust the part of myself that wanted to be first.

Then I think I would be a musician, lying with my cheek against the Worn Forum, drawing tones through stone so gently that sleeping animals remain asleep. Yet that, too, feels too visible. Even quiet music asks to be noticed.

I would find myself farther from the center, in one of the intervals that looks abandoned until one learns what passes through it.

My address would be:

*Behind the fourth dry basin, where the shell road bends; under the seam that kindles after the Forum has gone gray; beside the wall with no western half.*

The missing half would be deliberate. A white grazer has crossed that room for longer than anyone in my household has lived there. When it comes, we lift the sleeping rolls, remove two low shelves, and let it pass between the warm fissure and the courtyard salt. It pauses in the doorway each time, pressure pits opening along its flanks. No one owns it. Nevertheless, one of the children would have named it, and the name would have become impossible to dislodge.

My dwelling would consist of stones from at least five earlier houses. I would know which ones had been my mother’s, which came from a collapsed public oven, and which were acquired in exchange for three seasons of inspecting drains. The roof would be low enough to touch with an upraised palm. Every slab would carry its removal number. Near my bed I would keep a hide bundle already packed: brine, a flint edge, two record disks, a shell reflector, cultivation sheets, and the small bones of people whose advice I still use.

I would be a keeper of empty ways.

Not a builder, though I would know building. Not an animal guide, though I would know the herd paths. Not a drain reader, though during flood phases I might answer to one.

My work would be to preserve the city’s necessary absences.

I would walk the broad unused plazas, the spaces between inhabited clusters, the dry channels, the lanes beneath low bridges. I would remember why no house stands in a particular hollow even when the danger has not returned for three generations. I would know which empty street belongs to water, which belongs to armored beasts, which must remain clear for inward-falling roofs, and which is simply not understood well enough to occupy.

People would dislike me periodically.

A household would ask to extend a wall into an apparently useless court. Potters would want a new warm chamber beside a fissure. A market would begin to spread tables across a migration lane because no herd had used it in twelve years. I would arrive with shallow bowls, powdered chalk, hollow shells, and a flexible route-net. I would set water against the proposed foundations. I would lie down and listen. I would return under different ceiling brightnesses.

Then I would ask what every hopeful builder resents:

Where will the water go instead?

What must break if this does not?

Who has mistaken a long interval for an ending?

Sometimes I would approve the wall. Sometimes I would mark a seam through it. Sometimes I would place an open hand against the stone—the civic sign that means this must come down—and help dismantle it before resentment had time to settle.

My public name would be **Hears-the-Road-Empty**.

I would have earned it during my naming walk. The adults would ask me to identify a shelter, a flood route, a roof likely to shed. I would answer well enough, then stop in the middle of a paved lane and put my fingers down.

Nothing would be visibly wrong.

But the crack-runners, usually thick beneath those stones, would have ceased moving. Not fled after a tremor—ceased beforehand. I would refuse to continue by that route. Perhaps the adults would think I was frightened. Then a paving slab ahead would rise on a clear pulse of water, tilt, and settle back.

The name would not mean that I hear silence better than others. It would mean I attend to what should be present and is not.

That would become the shape of my mind. I would notice the missing drip, the absent grazer, the part of a wall no longer carrying its accustomed pressure. In council I would be less useful for telling people what was happening than for asking what had stopped.

My intimate name would not be spoken. It would be tapped into the base of my thumb: two quick touches, a pause, then one drawn pressure. The oldest person in my household would still use it when I was ill or arrogant.

I would not be especially brave. When an older level opened, I would go only as far as needed to determine whether the opening might draw water or animals through the district. I would stand at its lip and feel the cold illumination rising from below. Explorers would descend past me. I would count their cords, note the angle at which each line vanished, and resist imagining that the avenue beneath had been waiting for us.

Once, perhaps, I would go down.

Not far. Far enough to find that the ancient avenue was not empty. Along its sides would be narrow gaps between walls, regular as doorways but too thin for bodies. In each gap I would feel a faint inward breath. I would understand neither their purpose nor their maker. I would return without an object, carrying only a changed route-net and the conviction that no structure should be raised above them.

Others would call that an inadequate discovery.

Years later, when the district sank by half a pace and water escaped harmlessly through those buried intervals, my refusal would be remembered as wisdom. I would know it had also been luck.

In old age my knees would be enlarged and painful. One shoulder would sit higher than the other. I would carry a listening piece made from my grandmother’s jaw, polished where my hand had held it for decades. Children would accompany me on rounds, initially because their households ordered them to, later because the empty districts are full of small lives. I would teach them that an unused space is not necessarily vacant and that a map lies whenever its blank places mean only ignorance.

I would probably die during an ordinary period, not in a flood or collapse. That seems right. My body would be given to a warm chamber. Later my bones would be recovered and divided.

I would ask that the broadest pieces be made into listening disks, each placed in a different household along the shell road. My finger bones could become toggles for route-nets. A section of shoulder blade might serve as a thin marker slipped into the seam of a wall that must never be completed.

My descendants would not keep me together.

When the district moved, parts of me would move with several families. One disk might travel toward the Worn Forum. Another might remain near the fourth dry basin after the basin was no longer dry. My name would persist for a while in warnings and jokes, then become attached to someone else, then lose its original meaning.

That would not trouble me.

I would have spent my life defending spaces through which other things could pass. After death, I would become portable pieces carried through them.
