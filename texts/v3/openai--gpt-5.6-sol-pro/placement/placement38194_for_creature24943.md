At first I would think I was the cup.

Not because I understood stone, or water, or the long patience required to hollow one substance with another, but because each returning drop would seem to strike the center of me. The impact would pass outward in rings. For an instant I would possess a boundary: here, where the water trembles; here, where the mineral lip contains it.

Then a beetle would cross the rim, and I would feel its padded feet beyond that boundary.

I would think I was the terrace.

The sensation of the beetle would fade into the pressure of moss, the minute tightening of roots, the cool weight of seepage moving through channels too narrow to admit light. Farther off, something would close in the mud. A fish would turn under gravel. Shell fragments in the calcified arch would begin their buried trembling.

All of it would arrive without direction. There would be no above or below as a sighted animal knows them, only nearness, delay, and strength. The fall of a drop would be sharp and immediate. The movement of a serpent along a root would reach me softened by wood. Rain gathering far overhead would be less a sound than a widening tension in the limestone, too vast and faint to locate.

In the dry time I would lose even this.

My fluids would thicken. The small chambers within me would collapse one against another. Mineral seams would lock across my back, and the pores along my upper surface would close around grit, spores, old wings. Thought—if what I possessed could be called thought—would not cease. It would become indistinguishable from duration.

A moth larva might feed inside a root passing through a groove in my hide. A blind snail might lay eggs against one of my sealed folds. Moss would draw what little damp remained from hollows in my back. None of these would feel like visitors. Their hunger, pressure, and warmth would enter the same slow field as my own.

I might dream myself as each of them.

I would be the moth inside the hardened turret, plastering its chamber with blue-white fungus. I would feel the sealed dark become smaller as my wings formed. I would emerge dusted with light and climb toward another’s brief signal.

I would be the crab folded in its cell, counting no hours, trusting only the sustained pattern of rain.

I would be the lizard beneath the bronze-black leaf, my jaw resting against its veins. Warmth would flower above me in branching points: courtship, prey, or sunlight too diluted to see. I would wait to discover which.

I would be the stranded fish enclosed in its clear stone, consuming myself down to the shape that could survive.

But these would be quick dreams. Lives of opening and pursuit, of hunger moving toward what might satisfy it. Beneath them would remain the slower fact of my body.

I would lie near the largest calcified formation, where the old insect galleries rise into their person-tall cluster and the brief stream passes beneath its arch. One edge of me would disappear under the wall. Mineral water would have joined my outer plates to its lowest cells, so that no clean line could be found between animal and ruin. My opposite edge would border the gravel where the stream vanishes.

A curtain of red roots would cross my middle. One would enter a pore, travel through a shallow channel in my hide, and emerge several handspans away. In time it would thicken. I would grow around it. Whether the root pierced me or I enclosed the root would become an argument only a creature with hands might make.

I would be old enough to carry several such arguments.

There would be a blunt ridge across my back where, years before, I had lifted myself during a heavy seep. Limestone had gathered beneath it and prevented it from settling fully again. Water now followed that mistake, curving toward a patch of pale ferns. Snails laid their eggs there. Thin-legged crabs excavated their drought cells in the soft margin. A mud-jawed hunter had taken the lowest hollow for its wallow and gathered so much mineral skin that part of it had become nearly as still as I was.

None of this would belong to me. Yet much of it would happen because I had once turned slightly toward dissolved iron.

When rain began, I would wake by degrees too slow for the smaller inhabitants to witness.

First the pressure organs along my gravelward side would soften. Then water would enter the foremost chamber and spread through the internal web. One mineral plate would separate from another with no audible crack. Cilia would stir inside my feeding pores. The water would bring fungal threads, mite eggs, leaf liquor, the dust of shells, and the chemical traces of every creature it had touched.

To taste that water would be to receive the basin in fragments.

The bitterness of a damaged stem.

The musk of a serpent leaving its root mass.

The mineral blankness of a fish’s opened casing.

The warmth released by beetles feeding on an amber nodule.

The faint fertile change in the fringes of another of my kind.

That last trace would travel differently. Not stronger, but familiar in a place deeper than sensation. I would loosen along the side hidden beneath the red roots. Somewhere across the basin, another terrace would be filling its chambers. Between us would lie reeds, mud plates, a shallow pool, two fallen slabs, and innumerable lives whose movements wrote themselves into the stone.

I would not call to it. Calling would be impossible and dangerous.

Instead, I would alter the water.

A fold along my edge would rise. The stream would hesitate against it, deepen, and spill through a channel it had not used since the root curtain was young. Crabs would emerge into the new course. Fish would press over my back in water scarcely deep enough to cover their gills. The mud-jawed hunter would open beneath them. Leaves would bow with the passage of lizards. Moths under the roof would show thin blue lines.

The basin would seem to wake because of the rain.

Perhaps it would partly wake because of me.

Not by command. I would rule nothing. I might redirect water toward a nursery and also toward a waiting mouth. I might save snail eggs while drowning beetle larvae in the old galleries. A lifted ridge could preserve one pool and empty another. Even the movement by which I sought the distant terrace-being might close the route between us.

Restraint would not be a virtue I had chosen. It would be my anatomy. I could move only by filling, loosening, adhering, and waiting. A gesture might require the whole wet interval. Regret, if I possessed it, would arrive after the channel had already dried.

In the fullest seepage, if the water remained, I would extend a pale fringe from beneath the root curtain.

It would be almost transparent and finer than fungus. It would move along the stone without lifting from it, following pressure and chemistry through the flooded grooves. Another fringe might meet it in the dark below the fern patch. There would be no recognition as eyes understand recognition. Only contact, then knitting.

For a while, the membrane between us would be neither mine nor the other’s.

Later it would divide.

Hundreds of flat young would cling to the limestone, each smaller than the ivory beetles grazing over them. Most would become food before the pools separated. Some would dry. Some would attach where mineral flow buried them too quickly.

One might survive in the stone cup where the first drop falls.

It would begin as softness beneath shallow water. Films would spread over it. Limestone would cloud its surface. A snail would cross it and leave a trail. With each dry interval it would become less distinguishable from the cup, and with each rain the cup would become less distinguishable from it.

Perhaps, after enough seasons, it too would wake believing it was the place that received the drop.

That is where I would find myself: not at the chamber’s center, but at a threshold—the old arch behind me, the vanishing stream before me, roots crossing through what might once have been called my back.

And I would be one of the beings mistaken for architecture.

Not hidden under the stone.

Not lying upon it.

Becoming it slowly enough that the difference no longer mattered.
