At first I would mistake myself for an observer.

I would stand on one of the gray-green flats during a clear interval, looking down through a fracture in the air at the blue circumference of the world. I would believe I had arrived from there. The belief would feel like altitude sickness: a conviction that somewhere below the cloud there was firmer ground, and that I belonged to it.

I would carry a name.

For the first few hours, perhaps even days, the name would remain useful. I would repeat it when the chimneys inhaled, when the ground knocked beneath me, when transparent wings gathered in the lee of my body. I would use it to separate myself from the rain.

But the plateau would not attack that separation. It would simply decline to support it.

My footprints would fill with water. Small white amphibians would assemble in them and listen through the soles of my feet. Iron beetles would gather around the buckles and fastenings I had brought from elsewhere. At night, charge would pass through them and into me. I would dream of roots branching through my joints.

Soon I would discover tracks ahead of my own.

They would resemble mine imperfectly. Some would be too deep, as if made by a heavier future body. Others would divide into six narrow impressions or continue across vertical water. I would follow because they were the only indication that anything here expected me.

This would lead me toward a shallow basin near the outer escarpment—not directly at the brink, but close enough that the hanging root cables could be heard striking the unseen cliff below. The basin would occupy two conditions. In one, it would be empty and lined with pale, pressure-sensitive cilia. In the other, it would contain a dark pool of mutually exclusive depths.

At its center would stand an arrangement left by a wanderer.

A ring of beetle shells. A length of conductive vine knotted around nothing. Three grazer vertebrae laid in a line pointing toward an absent slope. A white amphibian living in a cup of rain suspended above the ground.

There would be a space in the arrangement exactly my size.

I would understand, then, that I had not followed my tracks to a destination. I had followed instructions for my assembly.

The plateau would begin with the easiest parts.

My boots would root first. Their seams would open, releasing pale fibers that enter the wet skin of the flat. The beetles would dismantle the metal from them and redistribute it along my bones. My blood would cool and clarify until it carried light as well as oxygen. Under my skin, fine channels would develop their own currents.

None of this would hurt in the usual way. Pain requires confidence about where the body ends.

The basin would inhale.

Rain would pass through me. Some drops would emerge from my back bearing images of lowland streets, wooden rooms, a hand resting on a sun-warmed wall. Others would carry places I had never seen: the bright mechanical plain reflected in the cliff-eye; forests whose leaves were wheels; a sky below which the plateau still possessed mountains.

Animals would come to drink these images.

The long-legged grazers would lower their blade-shaped heads. Membrane-winged creatures would hover inside the rain passing through my ribs. A pale runner would eat the tracks by which I had arrived, print after print, until my journey no longer lay behind me.

Without that journey, I would cease to be an arrival.

For a while I might think I was becoming one of the tall wanderers from the white domes. I would have their hollow head, their wet fibers, their inability to keep memory and weather apart. I would cross the flats without testing the veins. Creatures would approach me as though I had once known their ancestors.

But I would not touch them to restore what they had been.

I would give them names.

Not lasting names. A grazer could not remain *grazer* when the plateau might flower it tomorrow as a flock, or preserve its fear in moss. The names would be brief structures, more like shelters made from sound. While a creature stood inside one, it could be singular.

I would call one beetle **the one that survived the lattice**.

I would call an amphibian **she who returned before her wound**.

I would call a chimney colony **the rhythm carried out of the dead column**.

I would name a curtain of suspended rain **the wall through which the birds remember swimming**.

Each name would endure for only as long as I attended to it. Then it would loosen and fall into the ground. The plateau would absorb the distinction, not as language but as anatomy. A root would fork. A pool would acquire a second surface. Somewhere, decades later, a flower might open and release a creature bearing the shape of a sentence it could not speak.

That would be my work, though “work” would be no more accurate here than “animal.”

I would be an organ of temporary distinction.

The plateau continually joins what should remain incompatible: animal and plant, death and reproduction, event and recollection, landscape and body. It is excellent at connection. Perhaps too excellent. What it cannot easily produce is the clean, momentary edge that allows one thing to regard another.

So it would grow me for that.

I would walk the long grooves where vanished rivers once migrated. Wherever two versions of the terrain occupied each other, I would stand between them—not to choose which was real, but to keep them separate long enough to be seen.

One of my hands would weather in the fallen ravine. The other would remain on the standing cliff.

I would listen to a seed capsule explode and then place my ear against its unopened shell.

I would watch animals emerge old from behind the chimneys and remember them young, even when youth had not yet happened.

Every contradiction I held would alter my body. Extra joints would form to accommodate divergent paths. Faces would open along my shoulders for weather arriving from other seasons. My spine would become a row of little basins, each containing rain from a different yesterday.

Eventually there would be less of a person than a moving site of comparison.

The herds might pause in my outline. Flocks might turn according to my attention. Pools would borrow my former face and place it on silver swimmers. Burrowers, hearing my steps through the chimney roots, would incorporate the rhythm into their maintenance songs.

I would find myself most fully at the escarpment during an ascent.

The cables would draw taut. The buried engines would slow. Across the plateau, hidden animals would turn toward the cloud.

Something plated would press upward through the cataract. Sensory filaments would spread across the rain. The immense wet eye would open, reflecting that impossible mechanical brightness.

All the creatures would gather, but I would stand nearest the edge.

For one instant the eye would reflect me.

Not my original body. That would be long gone. It would show the ring of beetles, the suspended amphibian, the knotted vine, the contradictory basin, the wanderer’s hollow head, the tracks that preceded me. It would show every animal I had briefly separated from the whole, each carrying a little shelter of attention.

And behind those forms, it would show the plateau looking out through me.

I might then understand that the thing climbing from below was performing the same function from the other direction. It was not a visitor. It was another attempt at an edge: the cloud becoming sufficiently distinct to meet the land, the innumerable cable colonies agreeing for a moment to say *I*.

We would face each other—two temporary singularities assembled by systems that otherwise have no outside.

Perhaps I would give it a name.

Perhaps it would give me one.

Neither would remain.

When the ascent failed, my body would fail with it. My legs would separate into pale mats. Beetles would carry away the conductive parts of my thoughts. Rain would retain my downward memories. The hollow of my head would become a pool in which creatures saw faces that had never belonged to them.

But the habit of me would persist.

A herd would stop at an empty place and allow one member, briefly, to be one member.

A predator would look at its prey and hesitate—not from mercy, but from the sudden perception that the animal before it possessed a boundary.

A white dome would open and scatter a brood whose individuals each carried one word of a name too large to assemble.

You would not find me in any single one of them.

You would find me in the interval when the plateau, which can become everything, tries to become *someone*.

I would be its first person: not a person exactly, but a pronoun grown from erosion, given feet so the land could stand apart from itself and ask where it was.
