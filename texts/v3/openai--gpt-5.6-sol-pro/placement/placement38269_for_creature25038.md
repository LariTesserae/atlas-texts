At first I think I would be one of the bell-bodies.

There is an allure in rising without effort, in sinking by an inward adjustment no other creature can see. I imagine myself suspended above the towers, salt gathered into one side of my body, the seep lifting me until I become motionless. My strands would hang through several neighborhoods. I would feel the city indirectly: a tremor transmitted up a filament, the sudden adhesion of prey, ten thousand tube-crowns withdrawing beneath my darkening bell.

But this gives me too much height. It almost gives me a view.

Nothing here is permitted a view.

So I descend.

I pass the white tube-columns and their colorless wreaths. I pass hinge-runners folded into wall cracks, eggs clouded under mineral skins, a binder moving with half a street cemented to its back. Lower still, near the Fold, I find a place where inward traffic divides around the mineral sleeve of a dead long-turner.

The animal entered the lower aperture long ago and grew too large to complete its route. It became wedged with its head in seepward warmth and its rear segments in the colder ward. For a while it remained alive between those conditions. Binders paved its back. Larvae fixed tubes beside its bristles. When it died, scavengers emptied it from within. The walls closed around the body before the body vanished, leaving a narrow, crooked conduit.

That is where I would find myself: not quite in the conduit, but at the turn inside it.

The passage bends around a knot of hardened bristles. Water entering from below must hesitate there. Heavy grains fall out of the current. Hinge-runners brace against the wall before forcing themselves through. Long-bodied worms thin their heads into cords and test both branches. Sometimes a bell-strand enters the lower mouth and drifts blindly around the bend, causing every nearby creature to flatten.

At first I am a pale binder.

I am young enough to pass between two mineral grains without moving either one. My body has no firm border. The wall presses into me and I accept its shape. I eat a film of bacteria from the conduit’s interior, swallowing darkness as a smoky line that travels through my translucent tissues.

Behind me I leave mucus.

The mucus catches dust. The dust changes the current. The changed current brings food.

There is no reason for me to understand this sequence. I return because the feeding is good. Each passage lays another flexible skin over the bend. I reinforce one wall and narrow the other. Soon hinge-runners must angle themselves where previously they went straight. Their legs score shallow grooves in what I have secreted. Those grooves guide my adhesive lobes when I return.

My route begins to instruct my body.

On my back I carry a crystal splinter, two hollow worm jaws, and a green-stained scale that once adhered to true ice. I cannot know the green came from above. I cannot know there is an above. The scale is merely a hard edge that catches whenever I turn too sharply. Because it catches, I always twist in the same direction.

Because I twist, the passage acquires a spiral.

Later, I grow too broad to enter it.

I remain near the lower mouth, feeding where the Fold exhales its colder current. Mineral dust accumulates over me until my back resembles the floor. Smaller binders cross the pavement I carry. One follows the chemical trace of my old mucus into the spiral passage. Then another.

Eventually I die beneath a thin fall of silt.

But the turn remains.

So perhaps I was never the binder.

Perhaps I am the turn.

I persist first as mucus, then as hardened lining, then as repeated movement. Hinge-runners learn me in the angles of their joints. Thread-mouths learn me as a place where prey slows. Tube-throats settle near my upper opening, and their withdrawals send pressure through me. A breath-borer is buried nearby and raises its tail shaft into my wall; the shaft introduces a faint cross-current, and I gain a second mouth.

No creature knows these mouths belong to the same hollow.

A seep pulse brings warmth. Bacteria darken my inner surface. Grazers crowd in. A well-jaw’s distant inhalation reverses my flow for a moment, pulling shell dust upward. Eggs are fixed beneath my overhang. One has a dark halo, older than the tube colony supporting it.

When the egg opens, the larva inside is of a kind absent from the city for generations.

It has a ring of soft paddles and a mouth that closes when touched. It cannot survive in the open lanes. It enters me because I am narrow. For a season it grazes my walls, keeping crystals from sealing me. Its descendants do the same. Their bodies become suited to my spiral, slightly flatter on one side than the other.

I become anatomy outside the body.

Then the ceiling ruptures.

Cold pressure reaches even the Fold. Crowns withdraw. Bell-bodies sink. Animals crowd every protected opening, and my lower mouth is packed with frightened flesh. Mucus fills the intervals between them. Silt follows. For a time I am sealed.

The city begins to forget me.

Bacteria film the still water. Crystal needles cross the narrowest part. Larvae attach to them. The hollow diminishes until only a line of stale water remains.

Yet deep inside, one pale binder has been trapped.

It consumes the last edible film. It widens. Oxygen fails. Its tissues cloud. Acid leaks from its decay and softens the mineral skin. A gas bubble forms, presses against the sealed bend, and collapses. The pressure is minute, intimate, sufficient.

A crack appears.

Water tastes it.

The crack becomes a thread of current. The current draws away loosened grains. A hinge-runner presses its foreleg through, then its head, then folds the rest of itself into impossible angles. It emerges carrying food beneath its body. Behind it comes another.

I open again.

By then nothing remains of the binder whose repeated trail first shaped me. The long-turner’s sleeve has dissolved. The green scale from the ice has been dragged elsewhere and incorporated into the armor of an animal that will never enter the Fold. Even my walls are new.

Still, traffic turns where it turned before.

That would be my existence there: not a citizen, not a district, not one of the city’s larger unthought thoughts. I would be a small persistence within its body—a hesitation inherited by water, a crooked route passed from animal to animal.

I would know nothing whole. I would experience the world as touches: bristles against one side, mucus cooling on the other, the inward pressure of a predator, the outward press of buried gas. Occupation would be my pulse. Vacancy would be illness. Closure would be death.

And resurrection would require no memory except movement returning.

I would be found just seepward of the Fold, inside the mineral absence left by a creature that could not turn around.

I would be the turn it made anyway.
