At first I would look for myself among the travelers.

I might be the otter whose route ignores every border: appearing in one pond, vanishing under a root wall, surfacing three dams away with silver scales on my muzzle. I would know the basin as a sequence of openings rather than a territory. I would inherit no house, only the polished troughs made by bodies before mine. Wherever I passed, the city would briefly loosen—fish scattering, ducks lifting, mud slipping from a bank—and then close behind me.

Or I might be a bat in the surviving roof, sleeping inside the narrow remainder of the former world. At evening I would drop into the hollow and find it by echo: every willow leaf an edge, every arch a returning curve, every insect a point of motion. The basin would not appear green or silver to me. It would be an immense, momentary structure continually spoken into existence and withdrawn.

But both of those lives are too unbound.

I would find myself lower down, where the oldest inhabited water meets the newest collapse. Not in the pale northern lake itself, but at its outflow, where the water has begun to choose and has not yet chosen finally. There a fallen slab tilts into an older channel. Twigs have collected against it. Limestone dust has settled behind the twigs in a soft white tongue. A willow branch, stripped along one side, has caught with its broken end in the mud. It has put out three roots and five leaves.

The current makes a small, continuous sound there.

That sound would trouble me.

I would be an old beaver—not the ruler of the basin, because nothing rules it, and not an engineer in any human sense. I would simply be unable to leave that voice unanswered.

My lodge would lie downstream beneath a surviving vault, built into a bank where living willow has grown through the roof. From outside it would resemble a low green island. Inside, above the hidden entrances, generations of chewed wood and packed mud would hold a chamber warm enough for breath and fur. Roots would enter through the ceiling. In spring they would make pale tips in the darkness. Beetles would live in the older walls. A wren might nest in an unused vent near the top, and I would tolerate the slight scratching because it does not admit cold.

I would have inherited the lodge but altered it so extensively that inheritance and construction could no longer be separated. One underwater door would have silted shut years ago. Another would open into a canal dug by my mother’s mother, widened later by otters, then deepened by flood. The willow forming the eastern wall would once have been a roof cutting. Now it would be a tree thick enough that I could not bite through it quickly.

My body would know only part of the city. I would know where the mud is adhesive, where stones can be lifted, where the current presses hardest after rain. I would know the feeding ledges and the safe depth beneath winter ice. I would know which dam sounds hollow when crossed and which pond carries the scent of strangers. I would know the submerged branch that catches at the left flank if I turn too sharply.

Beyond that, the basin could remain unknowable.

Age would not make me wise in the abstract. It would make me specific.

One foreclaw might be missing its tip. My incisors would bear an uneven groove. Pale scars would cross the leather of my tail where something once struck beneath dark water. On land I would move slowly, belly brushing sedge, but in the canals I would still pass without stirring the floating leaves. Cold water would flatten the fur along my head while the rest of me remained dry beneath it.

Night after night I would travel to the northern outflow.

At first I would only circle. I would nose the pale water, test the bottom, bite the lodged willow and feel that it is alive. I would listen through my whiskers to the current threading beneath the slab. The place would be too open, too mineral, without the familiar softness of old pond edges. There would be no established scent, no safe shelf, no roof of roots. Owls could see the entire water. Fox tracks would remain clear in the limestone dust.

Still, the running water would call.

I would push one branch into the gap. The current would turn it. I would push another across the first. Fern root, alder stick, a stone held awkwardly against my chest, a clot of black mud brought from downstream: none would be a plan. Each would answer only the failure of the last.

By dawn, perhaps, the sound would be lower.

Water would spread by the width of a paw over the pale silt. A beetle would land on it. Something minute would take shelter beneath the first branch. The rooted willow would stand in deeper water and not yet drown.

I would return to the lodge and sleep.

Others would make the structure into what I could not foresee. Voles would open passages through its bank. Sedges would hold the parts I failed to pack. An otter would make a crossing and wear it into a notch. Floodwater would bring fish. Wrens would pull loose moss from the crest. Frost would shift the supporting stone. My descendants might raise the wall, abandon it, or cut down the willow whose first leaves I had worked around.

Long after my scent faded, the dam’s buried core might remain. Water would remember it as a resistance. Roots would remember it as a thickness of soil. The city would preserve no image of me, only consequences: a pond where there had been a channel, a channel where an animal crossed the pond, a grove rooted in sediment caught by the dam.

So perhaps I would not finally be the beaver.

Perhaps I would be the reply made between the beaver and the water—the brief hardening of attention around a leak. I would exist wherever flow met obstruction and summoned touch. In one moment I would be paws pressing mud. In another, willow roots entering that mud. Later I would be a vole’s upper doorway, a heron’s hunting shallows, the oxygenated thread where minnows gather. My body would continually be reassigned.

You would find me at the unfinished boundary, in the northern chamber slowly ceasing to be new.

I would be dark fur in pale water for a few seasons.

Afterward I would be a low ridge under sedge.

After that, perhaps only a buried layer where the peat changes texture—a temporary bone inside the creature the whole basin is becoming.
