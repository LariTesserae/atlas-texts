I would not live on one of the high towers, though at first I imagine myself there: a signal keeper with a mirror on my knees, watching heat storms drag their gray veils across distant ridges. From such a place I might know the broad country. I could name each watershed by the color of its stone and send warning flashes twenty kilometers ahead of rain.

But the height would be too much like mastery, and no one here is permitted that illusion for long.

Nor would I live beside one of the permanent wells. Those places are crowded even when they appear empty. Every rim bears the pressure of mouths not yet arrived. To remain there would mean becoming a gatekeeper, and I do not think I would want my life to narrow around deciding who may drink.

I would be found midway down the western side of a formation called, on old industrial diagrams, Pressure Exchange Column 41. No one uses that name. The people of the neighboring circuits call it the Split Listener because a fracture runs through its center and carries two distinct vibrations: the shallow pulse of a condenser line and, beneath it, the irregular shudder of the buried river.

My dwelling would be an abandoned cable house fixed into the shaded face. Its original floor would have fallen away long ago. The rooms would descend instead of proceeding one into another: sleeping shelf above, work ledge below, water vessels lower still where the stone remains cold. To enter, one would cross seven pipe braces over a slot too deep for sunlight, turn sideways beneath a manganese lip, and climb down a ladder whose bottom three rungs are missing.

The route mark would be a blue crescent inside a white circle.

Someone would have painted a red bar across it years before, after a storm opened a breathing crack. I would have added a thin black stroke at one end, meaning: passable before noon if the lower piston has completed its return.

My route-name might therefore be Ena After the Red Bar.

It would not be the name I was born with. That name would remain written on crawler manifests and in the letters sent up from the iron springs. On the plateau, what matters is not where one began but what must be crossed to reach one.

Officially, I would be a water accountant.

This would sound more orderly than the work is. I would have gauges, ceramic sampling tubes, wax tablets, and an old pressure recorder that cuts its measurements into a turning disk of blackened copper. Once every nine days I would climb a circuit of basins, valves, plant roots, crab fissures, and overflow cracks, recording not merely how much water appeared but where it seemed inclined to go.

Water here would have inclinations. Anyone who denied that would produce excellent reports and disastrous repairs.

The Split Listener’s system would be absent from the surviving plans. At some point an old condenser conduit had cracked into a natural channel. The mountain had accepted the leak and built around it. Lichen followed. Crabs entered. A thorn shrub rooted three ledges lower. Mineral deposits narrowed the original pipe until most of its flow traveled through the rock, reentering the machinery at a sump no engineer had intended to exist.

Replacing the conduit would kill the shrub, strand the crabs, dry two frog chambers, and increase the station’s measured output by four percent.

So my task would be to preserve the malfunction.

I would clean the sump without making it clean. I would keep the ceramic line open to the width of my smallest finger. I would scrape mineral bloom from one side of a valve and encourage it on the other. Before storm season, I would place woven screens in three channels to catch frog eggs before they were drawn into the deeper works. After the first flood, I would carry those screens to protected cups, shake them beneath the surface, and wait for the water to stipple with almost invisible movement.

I would know the crabs individually for a little while, though their generations would overlap and confuse me. There would be one with a black fork across its shell that lived behind the recorder housing. Another, rust-colored and missing a leg, would wait beneath the bypass lip each night. I would never feed them. Feeding would falsify the circuit. But when shutting the line for repair, I would leave a damp cord stretched from the sump to the natural fissure, and by morning the crabs would have crossed it.

The cabin’s listening wall would be the back of my sleeping place.

I would wake with one hand against it.

Through that hand I would feel the first condenser shutters opening above me. The motion would arrive as three faint knocks, a pause, then a long internal scrape. Later would come the deeper piston, so slow that it could not be sensed as movement, only as a growing tension in the wrist and jaw. On crawler days, bolts in the old cable housing would tremble hours before the machine reached the neighboring watershed.

The frogs would feel different from all of these.

Their waking would be like holding a sealed vessel in which water had begun to boil without heat. A soft multiplicity. No single call discernible, but the stone crowded with intention.

In my first years I would use contact needles to confirm everything. I would set their tips against the wall and listen through the handles, taking careful notes:

*Upper gallery flowing.*

*Air reversal in west fracture.*

*Possible bearing drag.*

*Biological vibration, shallow.*

Over time the notes would shorten.

*Rain.*

*Crabs moved early.*

*The lower dark is awake.*

Eventually there would be nights when I did not write at all.

I would not be wholly solitary. Route painters would sleep in the upper room after storms, and crawler crews would leave parcels at the nearest accessible brace: filter wool, salt cakes, letters softened by many hands. Children from an active station two ridges north might visit to learn the difference between a blocked line and a line whose inhabitants merely needed time to withdraw.

I would teach them to place their palms flat.

“Do not ask what the wall is doing,” I would say. “Ask what has not yet reached us.”

That would be the accountant’s true work: measuring absences.

A basin empty because the night was dry is not the same as a basin empty because a root opened a new path. A silent pump is not always broken. A silent frog chamber may mean the storm has turned, or that the last storm carried its young somewhere else. Missing water has not ceased to exist. It has entered another keeping.

Perhaps I would once have been impatient with this. Perhaps I came to the Split Listener as a young technician who believed every system possessed a correct state. I might have arrived with polished tools and replacement diagrams, intending to restore the station to its designed efficiency.

The plateau would cure me slowly.

First I would discover that the “waste” overflow cooled the ledge crossed by maintenance crews. Then that the ledge’s lichen fed beetles whose larvae kept a condenser intake clear. Then that the crabs in the sump consumed the same growth that had been blamed for reducing pump pressure. Every correction would reveal itself as an injury displaced beyond the edge of the diagram.

So I would become cautious. Then attentive. Then local.

Years later, newcomers might mistake me for one of those people who had withdrawn from regular circuits. This would not be quite true. I would still submit records. I would still exchange flashes with the high stations and wait beside the brace line for replacement seals. But my reports would become increasingly difficult for the southern offices to classify.

They would ask for the output of Column 41.

I would send them the output of the condenser, the level of the frog cups, the number of active crab grooves, the night temperature of the shrub root, and the delay between the buried river’s vibration and the sump’s response.

They would ask whether the installation was functional.

I would answer: attentive, but easily startled.

My life would be quiet until a storm.

Then the whole formation would become immediate.

Pressure would wake me before thunder. The listening wall would tighten beneath my palm. Frogs would begin inside the pillar. Crabs would leave the low conduits and climb toward the cabin, moving past my vessels and tools without fear. I would open the bypasses, unseal the egg screens, tie myself to the interior rail, and wait.

Water would strike somewhere above.

For several minutes nothing would reach me. Then black channels would shine on the opposite wall. The fracture would exhale cold air hard enough to lift my clothing. Gauges would jump. The station’s old pump—confused, attentive, faithful to some vanished instruction—would start before its float chamber filled.

There would be one manual lever that had to be held down with a particular piece of stone. Not because the lever required that weight, though that is what I would tell apprentices. The truth would be that the correct stone was porous and became heavier when the western runoff arrived. It released the lever as it dried. Machine timing, mineral timing, and weather timing would meet in that one object.

During the flood I would move from level to level, not commanding the system but joining its sequence: open this shutter when the frogs cease; close that valve when the wall cools; lift the screen when beetle wings collect against it; leave the lower gate alone no matter what the gauge says.

For an hour I would have no separate occupation. My hands, the stone weight, the crabs climbing the ladder, the pressure disk turning in its housing, the roots drinking below, the drowned channels and the buried river would all be parts of one act.

That is when I would understand what I was.

Not a hermit. Not exactly a technician. Not the keeper of the Split Listener, because keeping suggests that the thing remains where it is put.

I would be a return.

A small portion of the plateau’s circulation given memory and hands.

Water would arrive through my keeping, as the plateau people say, but so would warnings, eggs, mechanical strain, animal habits, and the knowledge of which apparent failures must not be repaired. I would receive disturbances from one world and pass them into another in altered form. Even my body would belong to the exchange: breath quickened by altitude, skin cooled by the cleft, palms mineral-stained, sleep divided according to the strokes of buried machinery.

If someone asked where to find me, directions would still be possible.

Follow the blue line until it ends. Cross the seven braces before the sun reaches the eastern cups. Wait beside the red bar until the crack exhales twice. Do not knock on the metal door; the gliders nest behind it.

Put your hand against the stone.

If the wall answers with three knocks and a pause, I am below, opening the night channels.

If it trembles continuously, a storm is crossing the upper watershed and you should secure yourself without waiting for me.

And if there is no answer at all, sit in the shade. Do not drink from the first basin. Listen.

Something is on its way.
