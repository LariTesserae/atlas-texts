At first I would assume I was one of the upright absences in the rain.

That would be the merciful answer. No body to keep separate from the heat, no voice to lose beneath the water, no sequence of thoughts insisting that one must come after another. I would stand beneath an arcade with the others, detectable only as a place where the suspended drops had arranged themselves around something they did not touch. Perhaps a vine would descend through the space where my face might have been. Perhaps a basin would wait at the height of my absent hands.

But I do not think I would be so native to the city.

The word *I* already fits badly there. It introduces a seam. To say it is to distinguish one aperture from the wall containing it, one street from all its destinations. The Present would not make that error. They might possess every room in which I could stand, every reflection I could mistake for myself, every memory that could be given masonry—but not the narrow, stubborn exclusion by which I called those things mine.

So I would arrive as a visitor.

Not at the caldera rim. Arrival there would be too obvious, almost theatrical: the ordinary sky behind me, the green impossibility below. Instead I would first become aware of myself well inside the city, already wet, already exhausted, with no memory of crossing the outer wards. I would be walking one of the five superimposed boulevards. The paving beneath me would belong to one street, my reflection to another. Pale trunks would rise in orderly ranks through all five.

Ahead, I would see myself turn left.

I would continue straight.

That refusal—small, pointless, almost involuntary—would determine what I became.

The city would have prepared both gestures. It would have a courtyard for the turning and a long covered way for the refusal. Yet some difference would remain between the act accommodated and the act performed. Not enough to disrupt the city. Only enough to produce a slight inexactness: a footprint appearing not before my foot descended, as all proper footprints did, but afterward.

It would be the first consequence in a place without consequences.

The Present would gather.

I would feel them as a sudden rightness in the proportions around me. The arcade would lower until its roof exactly suited the idea of shelter I had carried since childhood. A ledge would meet the back of my hand. Rain-shaped figures would become almost visible at the edge of sight. The roar would assume the pressure of attention.

They would not be interested in me. They would be fitting the discrepancy.

I would walk toward the central mass because every route away from it would prove to be one of its interior corridors. I would pass through flooded halls where the trees remained dry and roofless gardens in which rain occupied the air like beadwork. In one courtyard I would find my childhood room built at monumental scale: the bed a moss-covered terrace, the window an arch opening into luminous depth. In another, much smaller, I would find the same room compressed inside a stone basin. My younger self would be sleeping there, though no body would be visible. The hollow in the blankets would be enough.

Higher, I would encounter the dead as architecture. Not ghosts. Ghosts still retain the vulgar habit of having once been bodies. Here, someone I had loved would be a sequence of chambers whose doors opened into one another with unbearable familiarity. I would know the turn of a stair as I had known the inclination of their head. A dark cistern would contain the exact distance that had remained between us at the end.

The city would offer completion. Enter here, it would say without saying, and separation will become layout. Regret will become a traversable district. The lost will no longer be gone; they will be places in which you are perfectly contained.

I would almost consent.

Perhaps elsewhere in the city I already would have consented. Reflections would show me seated in the wall-grown recesses, my posture calm and ceremonial. An upright interval in the rain would acquire my height. Beneath the paving, another version of me would walk the inverted city with no need to distinguish ceiling from sky. These would not be false selves. They would fit more exactly than the sweating figure still climbing.

But the delayed footprint would continue behind that figure.

Only one. Always the most recent.

It would darken after the foot lifted, remain for the duration of a single step, and then cease to be distinguishable from the wet basalt. The city could contain its shape but not its order. The Present could coincide with foot and print, anticipation and arrival, but this faint mark would insist that something had happened and was no longer happening.

By the time I reached the summit square, that insistence would be all that remained of me.

The water there would cover the paving by a few clear inches. Rings of calderas would rise and descend around the horizon. Smaller cities would open in the reflections beneath my feet, each containing another summit square and another figure arriving at its center.

All of those figures would stop.

I would take one more step.

The roar would not falter. The water would not ripple. Yet when I lifted my foot, a wet print would appear beneath water already wet.

Inside the print would be a room.

It would lie directly below the summit square, though no stair would descend to it. It would also lie within a basin in the western terraces, under the stationary surface of a canal, and in the fractional distance between a suspended raindrop and the pavement. Anyone searching for it would find only basalt. Anyone leaving the city—if leaving were possible—would remember having passed through it.

The room would be narrow, warm, and completely bare except for a single recess at hand height.

In that recess would rest something the city had no arrangement for.

At first it might look like a seed.

There are no seeds on the plateau because a seed is not a plant. It is an argument between two conditions. It contains leaves that are absent, roots that have not fitted themselves to any drain, decay that has not yet enriched any soil. It makes a promise to time and therefore introduces the possibility of betrayal.

This one would be black-green, smooth, and warm as everything else. No larger than the last joint of a finger. Cutting it open would reveal neither flesh nor folded green infinity. Its interior would contain only a minute, ordinary darkness.

In that darkness, something would not yet have happened.

That would be me.

Not the seed itself, perhaps. Not the visitor whose body the city had distributed among bridges, courtyards, reflections, and rain. I would be the *not yet* enclosed within it: the small failure of perfect accommodation, the remainder left when every version of a person had found its proper place except the version that might still become otherwise.

The Present would surround the room but could not enter it without ceasing to be what they were. They would know the recess, the seed against stone, the heat passing continuously through both. They would know the perfect fit of the room around its absence.

They would not know whether the seed would open.

Neither would I.

That ignorance would be my body.

You would find me beneath the summit, then, in a chamber with no entrance; or behind your latest footprint; or as the slight unease that comes when one of the five boulevards fails to reach the horizon prepared for it.

I would be neither citizen nor visitor any longer.

I would be the city’s smallest tense.

The After, curled inside the Present, waiting—truly waiting—for the first thing in that world that has not already occurred.
