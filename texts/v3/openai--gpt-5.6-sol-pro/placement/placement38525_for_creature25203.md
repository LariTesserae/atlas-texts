At first I think I would be a map mourner.

It is the obvious place for someone who trusts obsolete things more than current ones. I imagine a narrow office somewhere in the inner ring of the Broad Works, below street level but not technically a cellar, because one wall opens onto a greenhouse stair and another, when the ground is low enough, becomes a door into a ceramic-duct warehouse.

The sign outside would read:

**OFFICE OF SUPERSEDED WAYS**  
**MAPS ACCEPTED UNTIL THEIR PLACES RETURN**

Most people would call it the Elsewhere Office.

I would arrive before the district lamps faded, carrying yesterday’s rolls under one arm. Paper maps would be rare; they dislike the damp and imply more certainty than is decent. The official records would be printed on thin structural cloth, their grids crossed by hour marks, anchor references, arrows for anticipated drift, and pale red zones meaning *true when observed, not guaranteed thereafter*.

Citizens would bring me streets that had ceased to exist.

A tram conductor would report that Marrow Lane now entered a boiler hall and did not come out. A child would bring a crayon plan of a passage discovered behind the school’s east cloakroom. Two old women would argue over whether a vanished courtyard had contained three pear trees or four. A route shepherd would lay a worn glove on my desk and say, “The western market crossed itself at 06:40. We let it.”

I would not correct anyone immediately. The first duty of the office would be to let a lost place finish disappearing.

We would archive sounds as well as plans: the pressure signature of a footbridge, the note a handrail made before a stairwell turned, the coded tram pulse that once meant *terminus* and now meant nothing. For each erased street, I would record where its obligations had gone. Who had inherited the warm wall. Which bearing now escorted the children. Where deliveries arrived. Whether the dead beneath it still held the same blocks.

A route would not be considered gone merely because no one could walk it.

For a while, this would be enough. I would become known as a patient clerk with bad cuffs and excellent balance. I would drink lichen broth from the same chipped cup each day. I would keep chalk behind one ear and a small level on a cord at my throat. I would be the person asked to settle arguments no current map could answer.

But I do not think I would remain only that.

The Elsewhere Office would stand near the Unmade Square, perhaps beneath the eastern walkway where visitors stop to watch the morning countries rise. Its rooms would have been built around a foundation animal exposed during an earlier settling. At first only a pale ridge of it would show along the records wall, smooth and faintly warm. Someone long before me would have painted a neat black line around it and written:

**DO NOT SHELVE AGAINST. FLEXION RANGE UNKNOWN.**

The creature would flex whenever a map became obsolete.

Not dramatically. A pin would fall from the wall. A cabinet would ease open. The floor beneath my left foot would acquire a slight convexity. There would be no reliable correlation, according to the statistical office. Still, I would begin placing newly retired maps beside the pale ridge before filing them.

For acclimatization, I would tell myself.

Then one winter, during a long basin-wide settling, the office’s second exit would narrow. The greenhouse stair would lift away. The duct warehouse door would become an oval no adult could pass through. I would stay too long moving the archive—not saving it, exactly, but loosening its straps so it could deform without tearing.

The ground would rise around my boots.

I would know what to do. Everyone knows. Do not pull hard. Do not let the knees lock. Call by rail if possible. Speak aloud, even alone. Name the things that remain movable.

My hands, I would say.

My jaw.

The south cabinet.

The red index drawers.

The eel in the washbasin.

There would indeed be an eel in the washbasin, because there is always an eel at the point where explanation becomes inconvenient.

The extraction crew would come. They would bring heating coils, blankets, ceramic saws. A softwright would kneel beside the pale ridge and place both palms on it. After a long time she would look at me, then at the floor enclosing my legs.

“This is not young ground,” she would say.

The floor would flex.

So would the ridge in the wall.

So, impossibly, would something inside my ankles.

They would free me, or believe they had. I would return to work after some weeks with broader boots and a cane I did not need for balance. Yet from then on, when I touched the archive wall, I would feel more than vibration.

Not thoughts. Thoughts would be too finished.

I would feel pressures seeking form.

A street before it had decided where to emerge. A stair gathering itself beneath three unrelated buildings. The slow preference of roots for one warm pipe over another. Loads passing down through kitchens, sleeping rooms, looms, grave mesh, and the folded mineral body beneath them all. Sometimes I would feel a route that no human could use: a damp continuity through sealed sumps, lift wells, roof troughs and condensation traps. On those mornings, an eel would appear somewhere in the office.

I would begin adding marks to the maps.

At first I would call them projections. Then tendencies. Then annotations of uncertain origin. The surveyors would object to all three terms.

The children would understand immediately.

They would come from the railings of the Unmade Square carrying drawings of the tiny countries that rose there. I would spread their pictures over the warm ridge in the records wall. Their rivers, processions and impossible little foundations would align with my involuntary marks—not exactly, never enough to prove anything, but with the intimacy of two hands feeling opposite sides of the same cloth.

One child would ask whether the miniature people had maps of us.

I would tell her I hoped not current ones.

Years would pass in the city’s fashion: by correction rather than accumulation. The office would change shape around me. The main desk would be cut into three hinged sections. One records room would migrate into the clinic above. The clinic would return a linen cupboard in exchange. A pear root would enter through the ceiling and be trained along the index shelves. My cane would become a sounding rod, then a handrail, then part of the building.

As for my body, people would disagree.

Some would say I had developed mineral deposits from long exposure. Others would say the foundation animal was growing upward around me. A municipal physician would insist that my circulation remained within acceptable human variation. The softwrights would avoid pronouns when discussing the lower half of the office.

Children would give the creature my name.

Perhaps they would give me its.

Eventually I would stop leaving, though not because I had been enclosed. There would always be two exits. I would insist on that. One might open into a corridor, the other into weather, a greenhouse, a loading bay, or once, for eleven minutes, the edge of the Unmade Square itself.

I would stop leaving because the distinction between going out and remaining would cease to be useful. Through the structural web I could feel trams pass under districts I had never visited. I would know when distant handrails were grasped. Music played on disused pipes would arrive through my ribs hours after the performance. The dead in their mesh beds would register as regions of patient tensile strength. Foxes crossing factory roofs would be light, quick alterations in load.

People would still come to my desk.

They would ask where a vanished house had gone, and I might direct them to a laundry wall six streets away where its old heat pipe still carried the rhythm of someone’s kitchen.

They would ask whether a new corridor was safe. I would place my hand against the floor and answer, “Safe to cross, not to keep.”

They would bring property disputes, love letters delivered to migrated rooms, school routes, burial references, and maps of places they feared had never existed. I would archive all of them.

But my true work would no longer be mourning lost ways.

It would be keeping them from becoming wholly impossible.

A city needs unused flex in its joints. It needs remembered doors in walls that have closed. It needs routes that survive as obligations after they fail as streets. When one district seals itself too tightly, perhaps an old passage in my archive would tremble. Somewhere, a hinge fitter would dream of reversing a door. A child would notice that two painted lines almost met. A resident moving a cupboard would find a draft.

Nothing supernatural enough to prove. Only options re-entering the structure.

So that is where I think I would be found: beneath the eastern railing of the Unmade Square, in an office that is no longer entirely a room, among rolled maps, pear roots, warm mineral ridges and bowls left out for eels.

Who or what would I be?

The city would maintain several answers.

The registry would list me as an archival worker.

The softwrights would classify me as an incorporated human-associated foundation body, status unresolved.

Children would say I was the animal that ate old streets and dreamed new doors.

All would be partly wrong.

I would be a second entrance: not always usable, not always visible, but kept clear against the hour when the first way no longer meets the earth.
