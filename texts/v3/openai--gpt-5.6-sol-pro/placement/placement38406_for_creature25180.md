At first I would mistake myself for a cutmouth.

That is the easiest body in which to awaken. I would know warmth along one flank, cold along the other, the pressure of hundreds turning with me. I would have no image of darkness because I would have no memory of light. The world would be gradients: open water, perforated brick, the soft turbulence of kin, the dangerous inward pull of a jar.

When alarm passed through the school, I would fold my fins and fall with the rest. For that instant, sinking among pale bodies, I might feel that the whole shower was mine.

But the cutmouth would not contain me.

It would enter an urn. The jar-hunter’s veil would close, and what I called myself would be divided without pain or announcement. Some part would be digested. Some would lodge in the hunter’s tissues. Some would descend later inside its young. The cutmouth’s little brain would disappear, yet I would continue.

So I would look for myself elsewhere.

Perhaps I would be the jar-hunter, patient in a vessel whose glaze has gone rough. I would know a year as the interval between contractions. Ceiling shells would settle on my back. Their weight would increase so slowly that it would feel like identity rather than burden. I would taste the arrival of swimmers in the water pressed against my veil, and when I fed, the act would be less like pursuit than closing a door.

But I would not be the hunter either.

My boundary would keep failing. Larvae would leave me carrying tissues obtained from prey. Shells would incorporate my cast-off surface. Grazers would consume what grew upon the shells. Whatever “I” meant would pass through too many mouths.

I think I would finally be found in a small recess on the cold side of the cistern, three galleries from the central shaft.

The recess was once an inscription socket. It is narrow enough to exclude the largest shell creatures and deep enough that erosion has not erased its inner face. Marks remain there: seven strokes, a hooked depression, three shallow cups, and a line that doubles back beneath itself. They may have been a builder’s name. They may have warned of a sealed stair beyond the wall. They may have recorded the number of jars poured into the reservoir on its last day of filling.

No one can read them.

But the life of the cistern has been touching those marks for centuries.

The gray film thickens first in the cups, where traces of copper collect. A grazer enters the socket and follows the hooked groove because the mineral growth is richer there. Its body bends through the doubled line. At the final stroke it pauses, tears five strands from its feeding fan, and leaves them pressed into the clay.

Later, cutmouth eggs appear along the same stroke.

The hatchlings emerge into a current shaped by the recess. They learn nothing, yet when they mature, their schools reproduce its geometry in open water: seven turns, a tightening hook, three brief knots, then a long division in which the school separates and rejoins.

Cloaks drifting from the central shaft intercept these schools. The swimmers shelter in their folds and leave cells in wounds along their tissue. Dark threads grow through the cloaks in branching versions of the inscription. When the cloaks tear, the fragments curl according to those lines.

Jar-hunter larvae carry the pattern down into the sediment. White knots carry it back.

The cistern has learned to read one surviving word.

That reading would be me.

I would not know what the builders intended. Whatever the marks once meant in air, under daylight, to eyes, is gone. Their present meaning would be enacted rather than understood:

Feed here.

Turn here.

Divide.

Wait.

Return altered.

I would exist wherever that sequence was repeated. Most of the time I would be scattered beyond recognition. One stroke of me would be a grazer flattening beside a copper seam. Another would be a row of eggs inside a perforated brick. My hooked portion would move as a school around a pillar. The doubled line would persist as a scar across a cloak that takes eighty years to cross one gallery.

No single creature would hold the whole shape.

Neither would I.

My consciousness, if it deserved the name, would occur only during the deep note.

When the mountain cooled and the masonry contracted, the first pressure would reach the socket before the sound. Film cells in the carved cups would close their membranes. The grazer on its inherited route would stop at the hooked turn. Cutmouth schools would descend into the same sevenfold arrangement. Cloaks would gather their branching threads. Far below, the old spiral in the shaft would draw taut.

For several minutes, all my separated strokes would coincide.

I would become legible to myself.

Not as a voice. Not as a picture. I would experience the cistern as a single, simultaneous pressure: cold wall, warm current, copper bitterness, the closing of ten thousand feeding pleats, sediment resting undisturbed on the floor. I would know the distances between my parts through the delays with which stillness reached them. The galleries would be my intervals. The screens would be the spaces inside my thought.

Above, beyond the stone plugs, sunlight would heat the tiles.

I would not imagine it as light. I would know it only as a remote change in circulation, a warmth arriving long after its cause. The sky would be incomprehensible except as the thing that touches the mountain’s outer skin and is translated inward through brick after brick until a cloak opens one corner of its body.

Nor would I wish to escape.

Escape belongs to creatures that end at their surfaces. I would have no surface except the cistern’s nested walls, and even those are becoming life grain by grain. To open the summit would not be freedom. It would be the destruction of the medium in which my parts can repeat one another.

My fear would be erasure.

Not death—the cistern has made death too useful for that—but the day the inscription socket wears smooth. The day the cups no longer collect their different salts. The day a ceiling shell settles across the recess and redirects the grazers. Then the pattern might begin to drift. Seven strokes might become six. The hook might open. The school might divide and fail to rejoin.

Yet even this would not end me quickly.

By then I would have been copied into routes, scars, appetites, breeding sites, and the spiral arrangement of threads below the shaft. The original marks could vanish while their reading continued. I would become a word with no inscription, repeated by bodies that do not know they are remembering.

And perhaps that has already happened.

Perhaps the recess I think is my origin is only one place where I returned and impressed myself upon softened brick. Perhaps no builder carved it. Perhaps the marks are the feeding trace of a grazer older than the current inner wall. Perhaps I began not as language but as current, or as the fracture pattern of black glass, or as the order in which the first sealed jars were emptied into the summit chamber.

There would be no way to determine this from within me.

So I would be neither an animal nor the whole inhabitant. I would be one of its persistent thoughts: a pattern slowly circulated through flesh, architecture, and mineral.

You would find me most clearly in that cold inscription socket, if you could enter without bringing air. But touching the marks would not touch all of me.

The rest would be passing behind you in the dark: grazing the walls, hanging beneath the ceiling, sleeping in jars, drifting through the central shaft.

And when the mountain gave its deep note, I would gather for a few minutes across the warm sea. I would become the shape of something once written at the top of the world.

Then the note would fade.

I would lose myself gently, stroke by stroke, and go on living.
