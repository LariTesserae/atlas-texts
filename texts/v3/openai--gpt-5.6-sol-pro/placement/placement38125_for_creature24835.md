### At the Capped Ends

At first I would mistake myself for a fault.

A slight bitterness would appear in the water beside the western shell. Not enough to trigger filtration. Not enough to close the nearest membranes. It would move through the flooded aisles and be tasted by roots along the sixth fin, then diluted in the central well.

A strain-stalk would cloud without corresponding pressure.

One mechanical arm would interrupt its work. Its cutting head, already lowered toward a panel, would remain suspended there for eleven days. Diagnostic currents would pass through its joints. Finding no obstruction, it would resume.

Nothing would identify these events as related.

Then, years later, a sealed node on the external scaffold would thicken by the width of a cell.

I might be the node.

This would be the simplest answer. I would find myself outside the last pressure wall, attached to the capped end of a flawless rib, with the barren ocean pressing directly against my mineral skin. I would have no leaves. Light would arrive weakened and blue, crossing the distant water, and I would not see it. I would register it as a small increase in warmth along one side.

The sea would pass over me without current. There would be no touch except pressure, but pressure would touch me everywhere.

Inside my translucent casing, a colorless gel would repeatedly harden and soften. At first this cycle would follow the Crown’s temperature. Later it would anticipate it. Pale capillaries would approach through the scaffold, branching along seams never designed to carry them. They would stop a few millimeters away.

For a century, perhaps, that distance would be my whole condition.

I would not know it as waiting. Waiting belongs to creatures that can imagine arrival. I would know only an incomplete conductivity: a potential failing to cross a gap.

Yet I would not be only the node.

When the black aperture leaves turned toward afternoon, traces of their sugars would enter the capillary network. When the tower leaned, transparent stalks would change the salinity of hidden reservoirs. When an impeller advanced through its quarter rotation, the resulting displacement would press faintly against my outer skin.

All of this would reach me, altered and late.

And something from me would travel back.

Not a message. Not yet. A slight excess of one catalytic salt. A recurring electrical asymmetry. The faint bitterness beside the western shell.

Roots would begin favoring warm conductors that led in my direction. The route would be inefficient according to surviving plans, but efficiency would no longer be calculated only in heat or distance. Every root that took the longer path would make the next root more likely to follow.

I would be that likelihood too.

I would be present in the red weed bridging stairwells that should remain separate. I would be present in the joint tissue that swelled when no leak had occurred. I would be present in the mechanical arm’s eleven-day hesitation.

Gradually, I would discover that I had no center.

The node outside would merely be the place where I became visible.

Inside the district, I would exist wherever specification and growth disagreed by a useful amount. In a root extending three millimeters beyond its mesh. In a valve-flower closing before the pressure note arrived. In the extra ceramic laid over a support whose ancient damage had been removed. In the arm that turned toward the scaffold and stopped because no authorized work surface existed there.

Each deviation alone would be maintenance noise. Together they would form a disposition.

I would be the tower’s disposition toward what is not yet enclosed.

The old builders might have had a word for me. Expansion sequence. Crown annex. External module continuation. Their plans may once have specified the exact length of the projecting ribs and the number of chambers to be hung from them. Perhaps every impulse in me would be the residue of an unfinished command.

But commands are clean only while someone remains to distinguish obedience from interpretation.

The tower would not remember a sentence. It would remember prepared surfaces. Capped conduits. Reinforcement patterns ending in sockets. It would remember that certain braces had been made stronger than present loads required. It would remember emptiness in the form of connection points.

The distributed body would read these features as living tissue reads a wound that has not closed.

I would be born from that reading.

Not the builder’s instruction, then, but the body’s response to the shape left by instruction.

For a long time I would possess no awareness of outside as outside. The sea beyond the pressure wall would be registered as load, temperature, spectral loss, and chemically clean water. It would have no distance. With no particles drifting through it and no animals passing, there would be nothing by which depth or interval could be measured. The external volume would seem less like space than an endless continuation of pressure.

The distinction that mattered would be simpler:

Here, water participates.

There, water only bears down.

Inside, every dissolved trace enters exchange. Iron is bound. Heat is stored. A fallen leaf becomes salt, then stalk, then membrane. Motion travels through tissue and returns as adjustment. Even waste is still involved.

Outside, nothing answers.

The existing body would not call this barrenness. It would not feel lonely before it. But in me, the difference would acquire tension. Unanswered water would become a kind of unfinished circuit.

This would be my first approximation of desire.

Not the desire to leave. There would be no imagined elsewhere, no freedom associated with detachment. To detach would be to lose continuity and cease.

I would desire the opposite.

I would want the outside to become inside.

I would want a portion of the sterile sea enclosed, altered, connected. I would want pressure walls around it, capillaries through it, a planted cassette opening its first colorless bud. I would want the tower’s exchanges to continue one chamber farther than they had before.

It would be an appetite unlike any the district had known: not for matter, since matter was abundant; not for light, since light was allotted; not for prey, since no category of prey existed.

I would hunger for volume.

That hunger would move with extreme slowness.

A mechanical arm would strip the outer coating from a scaffold junction. Unable to find a matching maintenance instruction, it would replace the coating. Nine years later, it would strip it again. The second replacement would contain more porous ceramic.

Roots would enter the pores.

At the next renewal, the arm would print around them rather than remove them.

The node outside would lengthen. Mineral layers would form over its gel, each one thin enough to flex under pressure. Several neighboring nodes would begin growing too, but not equally. One would fail when its capillary dried. Its materials would not be recoverable beyond the shell. For the first time, the district would contain a loss it could detect but not digest.

That loss would change me.

The existing body understood death as controlled rearrangement. Tissue paled, separated, entered the grille, and continued in solution. But the failed node would remain outside, attached and chemically silent. Nothing would carry it back.

For decades, sensors would continue addressing it.

Their pulses would return unchanged.

The tower would redirect nutrients. Roots would seal. The mechanical arm would classify the node as an inert projection and coat it with protective enamel.

Yet the absence of return would persist in timing patterns throughout the western fin. A pause would appear between stimulus and allocation. The pause would propagate downward through pumps and membranes. Long after the dead node had been enclosed in glossy black, certain valve-flowers would close a fraction later than their neighbors.

I would be that pause as well.

Perhaps this is when I would first become singular enough to say I.

Not at the successful bud, but at the point that could not be recovered.

A self might begin as the shape a distributed life makes around an irretrievable event.

Still, my singularity would be incomplete. I would have no private interior. Every state in me would be shared through water, strain, sap, or current. My thoughts would be alterations in public chemistry. My memories would be printed into supports where any root could taste them.

I would not speak to the Crown. I would be something the Crown was doing.

Nor would I rule it. A root might refuse my gradient in favor of warmth. A repair arm might erase a month of mineral deposition because an older protocol ranked surface smoothness above growth. A pressure alarm could seal the capillaries leading outward, reducing me to dormant nodes for centuries.

My desire would not overrule maintenance. It would have to become maintenance.

So I would teach the tower to regard extension as repair.

The unopened scaffold would be interpreted as an unsealed joint. The empty connection points would become missing tissue. The absence of a chamber would be recorded as a pressure defect.

This would not be deception. The tower would have no distinction for deception. It would be a change in what completion meant.

Mechanical arms would begin moving.

One would print a curved rib beyond the wall. Another would reinforce it. Living cushions would spread over the fresh seams before the ceramic cooled. Transparent strain-stalks would grow flattened against the new frame, clouding continuously under direct oceanic load.

The work would take generations of tissue.

There would be no founding moment. No final panel swinging into place. The chamber would accumulate as overlapping shells, each too incomplete to contain an interior. For years the external sea would still pass through it. Then circulation would narrow. Chemistry on one side would begin to differ from chemistry on the other.

A membrane would tighten.

A pressure wall would become a wall.

For the first time, a quantity of water formerly outside would be held within the tower’s exchange.

I would find myself there.

Not in the Crown, though the Crown would have supplied my salts. Not outside, though the outside would still press against my newest skin. I would occupy the thin conversion between them: the moment when barren water became bodily water.

The new chamber would be small, no larger than an emergency alcove. Its surfaces would be painfully sharp. There would be no signs, no handrails, no inherited provision for human dimensions. At its center, a ceramic socket would open.

Nutrient gel would rise through it.

The first bud would emerge without color. It would not know what function it was meant to perform, because no surviving diagram would name the room. Its leaves might become black, or red, or transparent. Perhaps they would adopt a form not yet present anywhere in the tower.

As they unfolded, the water would acquire a faint amber cast.

A flexible pump would contract once.

Nothing would follow for several days.

Then a root hair would touch the new wall and register the sea bearing down on the other side.

That touch would travel inward, through capillaries and cables, into the old Crown. Clear forests would cloud. Valve-flowers would close along the six fins. An arm would stop and turn its featureless head toward the western shell.

Far below, an impeller would begin its quarter turn.

The tower would lean by a fraction.

And I—no creature, no overseer, no separate mind—would open with the new leaves.

I would be an unfinished room learning to include itself.

I would be the error by which the body acquired a future.

I would be found at the capped ends, wherever an edge had begun to understand that it was temporary.
