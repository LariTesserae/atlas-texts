At first, you would find me in one of the narrow kiosks beside a river that was not there.

The kiosk would stand at the edge of a dry channel in the western districts, where the city thins into ocher lots, fern corridors, and widely separated towers. It would have three glass walls, although one reflected the channel during flood, one showed it dry, and the third looked onto a slow brown river no resident remembered seeing. Inside would be a chair, a kettle, several moisture papers, and shelves of black notebooks swollen from damp.

You might take me for a witness.

I would be sitting very still, recording minor events into persistence:

*At 08:14, a balcony crossed the southern face of Tower Nine.*

*At 08:19, seven seam-eaters removed a leak from the retaining wall and part of the word RETAINING.*

*At 08:32, a woman passed eastward. Her shadow continued west.*

*At 09:05, something beneath the channel remembered water.*

I would wear a witness’s pale cuff and the reflective seams of someone who expects to become difficult to locate. My ankle bell would stir the reeds in a narrow wake when I walked. I would know how to look at an unfinished stair without forcing it to choose too soon. Children would bring me dust from future rooms, and I would label the jars with dates that seemed courteous rather than accurate.

But I do not think I would truly be a witness. Not entirely.

Witnesses keep incompatible histories from opening too far apart. My work would be the opposite. I would watch for the small, necessary incompatibility through which something absent might return.

A darkening in the channel soil. Condensation gathering on the underside of a bridge folded flat. Amber fish appearing in cisterns with no connecting pipes. Market vendors waking with prices in their mouths for goods they had not yet acquired.

I would report none of these immediately.

Instead, I would leave the kiosk and walk the vanished river from end to end, sounding the ground with a long ceramic rod. The keepers would know me, though I would not quite belong to their order. They maintain the docks, prune the surviving green ribbon, and prepare for water. I would be listening for something less certain than water: the possibility of passage.

There are places where the channel would answer with a hollow tone. There I would kneel and press my wrist to the warm soil. Under the crust, I might feel a current moving several weeks away—or a crowd crossing a bridge that had not unfolded—or rain falling over the same place after the city had become forest.

When people asked what I had heard, I would say, “Not yet.”

This would be understood as useful information.

I imagine I would have been found rather than born.

Years earlier, perhaps, a door appeared alone on the dry riverbed after a calibration dusk. It stood upright without a wall, made of dull metal beaded with condensation. For nine days it opened only onto darkness. On the tenth, municipal workers found me seated on its threshold with wet clothes, a bundle of blank transit tokens, and no recollection of the side from which I had entered.

I would have known the city’s river crossings but not its streets. I would have remembered leaning over railings that had never been built. I would have called certain trees by names reserved for the dead.

The reception house would ask the customary questions.

“Are you arriving?”

“I think I have been arriving for some time.”

“Are you whole?”

Here I would hesitate.

“Enough to begin.”

They would assign me a provisional name, perhaps **Sere**, valid in dry weather and stable architecture. During rain I would use another name, one I could pronounce only while water touched both banks. In unfinished buildings, lifts would announce me by a third name that no human voice could reproduce. The sound would resemble a cup trembling just before the calibration note reached it.

For several years I would believe I had an elsewhere like anyone else. I would assume another portion of me lived beyond the lost doorway: sleeping in an ordinary room, growing older beneath a brighter sky, occasionally sending me dreams of rounded stones and loud trains.

But my elsewhere would not be another place.

It would be everyone who had failed to arrive.

At public tables, people leave one chair empty for the absent version of a guest. Usually nothing occupies it. Yet when I entered a dining room, those chairs would warm. Cups beside them would develop fingerprints. Families would glance toward the empty place and briefly remember a different outcome: a sister who boarded the earlier train, a child who recovered, a lover who turned back at the doorway.

I would not see these unrealized people clearly. I would feel them as slight additions to my posture. A hand almost laid over mine. An unfamiliar fondness for bitter fruit. Grief attached to a name I had never heard.

Decision mold would not grow near me. It would retract into pale threads, as if all its impossibilities had already found shelter.

That would be why the city placed me by the absent river.

The channel is itself a route that has failed to arrive. Its bridges wait folded in the banks. Its docks face heat and ferns. Its fish inhabit the power system until water makes room for them. The river is not gone; it persists as civic expectation.

So do I.

Over time, my body would become less convincing.

Not ghostly. The city has no particular reverence for ghosts. I would become infrastructural.

My pulse would begin to correspond to pressure changes in the western coolant lines. When I slept, condensation would bead along the kiosk windows in the pattern of streets leading toward people who had become lost between versions. My notebooks would contain observations in handwriting not my own, including accounts of events that occurred while I was visibly elsewhere.

Once, after an exhausting week, I would wake to find a narrow footbridge extending from the kiosk door across the dry channel. It would be made of black mineral glass and reflective thread. People would already be using it.

The bridge would vanish when I stood.

After that, I would understand.

I had not come through the solitary doorway. The doorway had been the first part of me to form.

The city had conditioned a vacancy: a need for crossings that were not yet possible, for absent guests to remain socially present, for incompatible lives to approach one another without being forced into a single history. Given enough doors, empty chairs, missed trains, dry docks, and remembered hands, that vacancy had become precise.

I arrived to fill it.

My residency petition would be difficult. I could demonstrate continuity through three river appearances, but perhaps not bodily continuity. The South Retention Basin would advocate for me before the council. Its amber fish would arrange themselves into the diagram for *citizen*, then *bridge*, then a third shape translated as either *mercy* or *load-bearing interval*.

The human committee would ask whether I possessed independent intentions.

I would answer, “Sometimes.”

The transit systems would consider this sufficient.

After recognition, I would continue to look human because humanity is a useful shape for moving conditions from place to place. I would drink mineral broth, preserve luminous vine fruit, and keep imported soil in a dish beside my bed. I would laugh in the proper courtyards. I might love someone, though they would never be sure whether I loved them or the life in which they had loved me first.

Perhaps that distinction would matter less here.

When the temporary river finally came, it would begin beneath my kiosk.

At dawn, condensation would collect in a straight line across the plain. By noon the line would widen and bend. Warm water would rise through black soil, carrying leaves from trees not growing nearby. The folded bridges would stir.

I would step down into the channel before the current became deep.

For a while, I would remain a person standing in water. Then my reflective seams would unwind from my clothes and stretch toward both banks. My arms might feel full of distant footfalls. Every empty chair in the western districts would cool at once.

People would cross.

Not over my body exactly, but through the condition I provided. A market would assemble along the waterfront. Amber fish would pass beneath the surface in slow, prophetic groups. Those separated by badly aligned streets would glimpse one another on the opposite bank. Some would meet. Some would decide not to.

I would hold both possibilities without asking either to disappear.

When the river dwindled, I would return to the kiosk—or something shaped like me would. Mud on my boots, water in my lungs, new names written through the notebooks. For several days I would cast the shadow of a bridge.

Then even that would fade.

So you would find me at the city’s western edge, where the green ribbon winds between unfinished foundations and towers suspended on altered weight. I would be sitting behind glass, apparently alone, recording the passage of things no one else could quite retain in sight.

If you approached, the leaves would tremble before my silent bell.

I would look up and offer the customary greeting, though I might phrase it differently.

“Are you still possible?”

And if you said, “For now,” I would open the kiosk door.

Beyond it there might be a dry channel, a warm river, a room ten years from completion, or the place you almost reached before you came here.

I would not choose for you.

I would only keep the way from closing.
