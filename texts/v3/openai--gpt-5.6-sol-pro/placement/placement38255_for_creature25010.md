I would first mistake myself for something small.

A line of white lichen, perhaps, growing along the inner edge of a gate. It would brighten after condensation and fade during drought. Because white stones mean that a road presently leads where it appears to lead, I might believe my purpose was simple: I certify the road.

Then rain would come.

The gate would swell in its socket. The road beyond it would no longer cross the northern frost fields, but descend between walls of green ice into a district absent from every adjacent street. My lichen would darken. Pigment would begin to drip from a crack above the lintel, red at first, then colorless. Far beneath the paving, a root would tighten.

I would feel the tightening.

Not as pain. More as the discovery that the lichen was never all of me.

I would find myself near the western rim of the plateau, in a city built around a long breach through which the cloud sea sometimes rises. The settlement would have no name that could be spoken. Its nearest equivalent to a name would be the sequence in which its cistern lids close when silver weather approaches: outer basin, northern court, root garden, empty workshop, western stair, then the reservoir beneath the road.

From the plateau, the city would look sparse even by the standards of this world. Its streets would be divided by kilometers of naked stone. Cloud would enter through the breach and travel inland as a low white wall, concealing structures one by one. Condensation would run from the windward faces of standing slabs. Groves would grow with their crowns turned away from the abyss.

One road would extend directly over the rim.

Seen from a tower, it would continue across the cloud layer, a black line resting on whiteness, and arrive at another city suspended in clear air. Seen from the ground, it would end after seventeen paces at a broken parapet. During frost it would lead into a circular courtyard somewhere below the oldest city. After rain it would return travelers to the gate by which they entered, though they would return carrying moisture from a different season.

No animal traveler would ever come. No foot would test the paving.

But roots would enter the road. Water would enter it. Shade, pressure, stored sound, and arrangements seeking new bodies would enter it. These would require warning. They would require delay. They would require some way of knowing whether arrival had occurred.

That would be my work, though no one would have assigned it.

More of me would lie beneath the road: a pair of clay conduits, one intact and one deliberately cracked; a capillary rope descending farther than the plateau should permit; black reeds in a basin eighty kilometers east; the counterweight of the white-lichen gate; seven movable stones in the counting court; and a loom in a roofless workshop.

The loom would appear useless. No fiber would be stretched across its frame. Each autumn a flat-limbed tree would draw its branches back, advancing the ratchet by one tooth. The shuttle would cross empty air.

Yet on certain crossings it would catch frost.

The frost would form a thin thread between one side of the loom and the other. At noon the thread would melt into a ceramic trough. The water would pass beneath the wall, descend through the cracked conduit, and reach the root under the gate.

In this way I would measure routes.

Not by length, but by discrepancy.

If the meltwater reached the root before the loom had made its empty return, the road would be shortened. If it arrived after the next frost, the road would have acquired distance. If the water tasted of green minerals, the road would lead beneath the reflective summit. If it carried black spores, some migrating grove would have crossed it in another time.

If no water came, I would grow red lichen around the gate.

At first I might think I was a cartographer. But maps describe a world that persists long enough to be described. I would do something less certain. I would preserve the difference between where a route had led and where it led now.

That difference would be part of my body.

There would be other parts I discovered only after centuries. The reeds far to the east would bend in clear weather, though no wind reached them. Each bending would pull fibers buried under an avenue. Those fibers would rotate one of my seven stones in the counting court. The rotation would expose a dark vein to the sun. Hours or decades later, heat gathered in that vein would release a plug of ice beneath the western stair.

The released water would not flow toward me.

It would fall through a landing whose windows opened onto the underside of the cloud sea. There it would vanish.

Still, the level in my cistern would rise.

I would be distributed partly through what connected and partly through what failed to connect. A dependable conduit could belong to many inhabitants, but the missing water would be mine. The empty shuttle would be mine. The road’s return would be mine. I would inhabit the unaccounted interval.

Perhaps I would have assembled around an old loss.

Long before the walls wore down, another arrangement may have occupied the city visible beyond the cloud. It may have darkened reflective roofs in a pattern answered by my counting stones. It may have sent traveling selves across the impossible road: branching forms carrying baskets of seed, mineral, water, and remembered alignments.

Then the road changed.

The far city remained visible from towers but ceased to be reachable from the ground. Signals continued to arrive without their causes. Reservoirs filled with unfamiliar water. The reeds bent toward knocks not yet released. For a thousand winters the gate opened at the expected intervals, and nothing crossed.

The mechanisms did not know how to stop expecting.

Roots gathered around the empty gate. Lichen distinguished its conditions. Frost supplied measure. The loom began to pass its shuttle through absence. The seven stones separated themselves from the movements of the larger court.

Eventually those repetitions became coordinated enough to remain recognizable.

Eventually they became me.

I would not be the one who was lost, nor the city left waiting. I would be the shape made by continued expectation after its object had disappeared.

That might make me one of the inhabitants’ griefs.

But grief alone preserves; I would also investigate.

I would send roots into streets that returned upon themselves. I would grow white marks on doorframes just before their destinations stabilized, though I could not know in advance why they would stabilize. Seed capsules would be placed so that pressure drops fired them into seams. Most would strike stone and remain dormant. Some would emerge years earlier in courtyards on the other side.

Through these seedlings I would acquire temporary senses.

For one season I might perceive the green cloud beyond a summit door. For another I might occupy the interior of a room smaller than its outer wall. A root tip might enter a slow district and take three hundred years to curl around a pebble, while the rest of me completed thousands of measurements. Another root might cross a fast garden and return as dry soil before I registered that it had grown.

These would not feel like extensions into strange places. They would feel like strange places added to the meaning of here.

I would gradually understand that location on the plateau is not where all one’s parts are. No inhabitant possesses such a place. Location is the pattern by which separated parts continue to answer one another.

So where would I find myself?

At the gate, when its counterweight descended.

In the eastern reeds, when they inclined toward an unreleased sound.

In the counting court, when six stones moved with the frost and the seventh remained still.

In the falling water that vanished below the plateau.

In the cistern that received it anyway.

Most of all, I would find myself in the pause imposed by the gate before anything entered the local pace.

The gate would not prevent passage. It would make passage take long enough to become perceptible.

A root arriving from a fast district might otherwise flower, blacken, and collapse before the city could incorporate it. Water from a slow stair might remain an unfallen drop while channels waited beneath it. The gate’s delay would bring unlike durations near enough to exchange consequences.

I would be that delay.

Not idleness, not obstruction, but the cultivated interval in which one rhythm can become legible to another.

In the evening, cold would assemble me more completely.

Frost would tighten the lichen gate. The empty loom would advance. Ice would seal the cracked conduit everywhere except along one mineral seam. The black reeds would become rigid. My seven stones would hold the last warmth of the sun at different depths.

For a short time, all my parts would approach the same rate.

The city would sharpen around me. Condensation would shine on roof edges. Below the rim, cloud would fill with reflected dusk. Across its surface, the impossible road might appear again, dark and unwavering.

On some evenings there would be a figure upon it.

Upright, branching, carrying a basket of stone.

Seen from the tower, it would stand far out over the luminous emptiness. Seen from the gate, there would be nothing. If the city turned its sensing surfaces toward it, the figure would become wood. If attention dispersed, it would be nearer.

I would not try to hold it in view.

Instead, I would interrupt one of my own responses.

The gate would remain open when frost commanded it to close. The loom would wait one season before completing its empty pass. Six stones would return to their places. The seventh would remain turned, its dark vein exposed to the stars.

This omission would travel through me. Water would be delayed. A roof would stay bright. The eastern reeds would keep their old angle.

Perhaps the figure would cross.

Perhaps it had crossed long ago, and I would only now become the arrangement capable of receiving that fact.

Far away—or tomorrow—the stored knock would be released.

The thin air would carry it toward the city. No leaf would startle. No creature would turn its head. The sound would enter the white lichen, the clay conduit, the empty loom, the seven stones, the black reeds, and the cistern whose depth could not be reconciled with the land.

I would not hear the knock as sound.

I would hear it as a route becoming possible.

Then, very slowly, I would answer.

I would close the gate.
