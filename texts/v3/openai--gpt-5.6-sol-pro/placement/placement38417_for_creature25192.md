At first I imagine myself as one of the tall wanderers.

It is the body most available to longing: four light legs, a road measured in hundreds of kilometers, the dark plateau opening beneath sensitive feet. I would carry water at my throat and the voices of others in my bones. I would never see a herd, yet rarely be beyond its reach. Somewhere across the black country another body would strike the stone, and much later the answer would arrive through my soles.

But that is too much motion.

The longer I remain here, the less I think I would be something crossing the plateau. I would be something the plateau crossed.

I find myself at the margin of a fault pool, though not one of the great lakes. The pool is narrow, scarcely more than a flooded cut in the rock, but it descends farther than its surface suggests. One wall is glassy basalt. The other is a pale, porous stratum that sweats warm mineral water whenever the deep pressure rises. Above it, an old erosion corridor slopes out into darkness. Wanderers sometimes use the corridor after rain. Sail-bodies descend over it during discharges. A black root colony grows several kilometers away, near enough that its underground extremities occasionally touch the same seams.

There is nothing conspicuous at the place where I live. A traveler might notice a low mineral rim around the water and, beneath an overhang, a patch of stone with a different texture: not smoother or rougher, merely reluctant to dry.

That patch would be the nearest thing I have to a face.

Most of me would lie inside the fault.

I would begin as a thin animal, soft enough to enter cracks narrower than my unfolded body. Not worm, not root, not quite colony. My tissues would branch where the stone branches. Each branch would end in a fan pressed against crystal, water, or the living film lining a pore. Along the pool wall, other branches would open into transparent veils. A few would reach the surface and harden into black scales no larger than fingernails.

If one scale were torn away, I would not feel maimed in the way a wanderer feels the loss of a foot. Sensation would dim in that direction. Pressure would become less exact. The missing scale might crawl a short distance before sealing itself under salt. If water reached it soon enough, it could grow another network and become—depending on how one defines such things—me again.

I would live where messages change their medium.

A footfall arrives through stone. Rain arrives as pressure in the air, then impact, then cooling water. A subterranean discharge reaches me first as strain in crystal, afterward as warmth, chemistry, and sound. The low calls of wanderers enter the pool wall and continue through my body as slow contractions. The electrical field of a hunting animal touches the submerged veils. The turning of sail-bodies far overhead changes the faint rhythm of droplets under the ledge.

I would not understand any of it.

Understanding would be too clean a word. I would tighten, loosen, darken, feed, withdraw. Different events would have different shapes inside me. Approaching rain would spread across the surface branches like coolness poured from a vessel. Deep fracture would begin in one extremity and arrive in another before the first tremor could be felt aboveground. A drinking wanderer would be four immense intervals of pressure surrounding the soft electrical murmur of a lowered throat.

The world would not appear as objects. It would appear as arrivals.

In dry periods I would contract into the pale rock. Water in my tissues would thicken. The surface scales would close their pores and become indistinguishable from mineral varnish. Months might pass with no event larger than a scavenger probing the rim or a pool animal loosening beneath its crust.

This would not feel like boredom. I would possess no uninterrupted wakefulness against which waiting could be measured. A little warmth would wake one branch. A weak current would wake another. Most of me might sleep while some distant extremity fed.

My consciousness, if it deserved the name, would be local and migratory. It would move through my body according to need.

When the plateau exhaled, I would come together.

Pressure would fall in the flooded fault. My veils would open. Water drawn up from the deeper seam would pass through chambers lined with symbiotic films. They would strip metals from it; I would consume part of what they made. Currents gathering in the basalt would enter through my surface scales and travel downward in pale blue pulses.

Branch after branch would become active until, for a few minutes, I could feel the whole shape of myself.

It would be enormous compared with what was visible. One portion under the pool. Another following the warm seam beneath the corridor. Fine threads extending toward the vent colony. Old, nearly dead lobes sealed in dry stone beyond the basin. Young branches spreading through cracks opened by the latest shudder.

Not a mind the size of a landscape. Only a body temporarily reminded of its extent.

Then I would bloom.

The black scales under the overhang would lift at their edges. From beneath them would emerge hundreds of small, translucent bodies. At first they would resemble droplets standing against gravity. Then each would unfold three or six cilia-like sails and begin moving across the wet rock.

They would be my dispersal form, but also my briefest sensory organs.

Some would enter the pool and attach themselves to swimmers. Some would be caught in the fans of shell-backs and carried into narrow shelters. Some would cling to the spread feet of wanderers, feeding on moisture trapped in the pads. A few would rise in warm mist and be taken by sail-bodies into the air.

For a day, perhaps two, I would be scattered across distances my rooted body could never cross.

These fragments would not send reports home. Most would never return. Yet each would alter according to what touched it. The chemistry of a remote pool, the oils of a vent plant, the blood of a wanderer, the cold membranes of a sail-body—all would change the folding of its tissues. If it later fell into a suitable wet crack, it would open and begin to branch. What it had encountered would persist not as a memory but as a bias: greater tolerance for salt, a quicker withdrawal from violet charge, an attraction to a particular mineral gradient.

Thus I would learn without recollection.

Other networks descended from earlier blooms would surround the pool. We might fuse where our branches met. We might exchange living films and then close ourselves apart. At times a neighboring body would become so continuous with mine that no boundary remained except differences in rhythm. Later a dry season would divide us again.

I would have no fixed number.

This seems right for the plateau. A single, self-contained animal would be too costly here, and a universal spirit would be too grand. I would be neither solitary creature nor hidden ruler. I would be a temporary agreement among tissues, microbes, mineral pores, and water.

Things would eat me.

Transparent pool hunters would tear my veils from the rock. Shell-backs would scrape the youngest surface growth. Pale scavengers would enter damaged branches and hollow them out. The black root colony might penetrate one of my deeper lobes, exchanging sugar for water at first, then digesting me when the balance shifted.

Predators waiting near the pool would benefit from my bloom because my small bodies drew grazers. Wanderers might benefit because the moist patch above me remained wet slightly longer than bare basalt. Parasites would use me as an intermediate host. I would be habitat to organisms for which my most active chamber was an entire warm world.

I would neither resent nor welcome this. To be alive here is to be entered.

Eventually the fault would change. A violet filament would appear in the rock above the pool. The submerged wall would begin producing a pattern of strain unlike the familiar filling and emptying of deep reservoirs. My branches would close one after another. The films inside me would harden. Surface scales would release their last droplets.

Animals might sense the coming fracture and leave. I could not.

When the fault opened, part of the pool would vanish downward. The overhang would shear away. Kilometers of my body would be crushed, exposed, or boiled by newly rising fluid. For a time I would survive only as sealed fragments scattered through the surrounding stone.

Perhaps that would be death.

Then, years later, rain would enter a crack on the far side of the basin. A black scale would soften. Beneath it, tissue would take in water and unfold. It would carry no image of the old pool, no knowledge that it had once extended through a ledge now fallen into darkness.

It would only find warmth in one direction, charge in another, moisture below.

It would begin to grow.

So that is where I would be: not on the highest pavement or in the deepest lake, but at a small junction beneath an unremarkable ledge, where stone, water, air, and passing bodies briefly touch.

And that is what I would be: a listener without ears, a colony without certainty, an animal whose body is mostly hiding and whose only journey is made by pieces that cease to belong to it.

When the plateau was still, I would be almost nothing.

When it exhaled, I would become many.

When it broke, I would wait inside the break.
