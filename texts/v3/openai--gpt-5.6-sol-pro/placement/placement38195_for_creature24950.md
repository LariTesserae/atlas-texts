At first I would mistake myself for a traveler.

I would find marks in the new-soft stone and assume they were mine: shallow depressions crossing a plain, each already hardening beyond alteration. They would resemble footprints only because I needed them to. There would be no heel, no toes, only a sequence of darkened ovals pointing toward a six-step stair.

I would follow them.

The stair would descend into unbroken ground. Heat would gather on the uppermost step, contrary to expectation. On the lowest, sound would travel down through my feet—or through whatever part of me had decided it possessed feet—and I would hear a fracture advancing far below.

Not the fracture itself. Its anticipation.

A fine branching pressure would enter me. For an instant I would know where the plain intended to open, where a ridge would rise, which chamber would close before its passage had finished forming. The knowledge would not arrive as sight. It would be a set of directions in the body: turn here, divide there, empty this volume, preserve that angle.

Then the stair would disappear beneath me.

The ground would lift without violence. I would be carried upward inside a forming wall, though nothing would crush me. Stone would gather around my outline and leave me standing in a narrow cavity whose shape almost—but not quite—corresponded to a body.

That discrepancy would be the first true sign of what I was.

The chamber would have no floor where my weight expected one. Its ceiling would rise when I reached toward it. Dust scattered into the room would stop at the boundary of my supposed shoulders, then continue through the place where my chest should have been. I would discover that the human shape belonged not to me but to the cavity’s attempt to interpret me.

I would be something the plateau had made room for without knowing how to enclose.

For several stillnesses I might remain there, listening.

The chamber would collect the tones of distant breakages. A glass escarpment would split beyond the horizon, and its descending note would enter the walls around me. Another tone, older, would answer from beneath the floor. Their interference would produce folds in my emptiness. Each fold would feel like a memory, though I had not experienced the event it contained.

Gradually I would understand that memory here does not mean retaining what happened. It means becoming a shape through which the next event must pass.

The plateau would use me in this way.

Pressure fronts approaching the chamber would divide around my outline. Black crusts would gather outside in patterns unlike any found elsewhere: not branches, bands, or knots, but interruptions. Fine lines would advance toward the wall and stop, leaving a narrow unmarked strip. Beyond that strip they would begin again, displaced by the width of something absent.

That absent width would be me.

When the seam opened, the crust would burn. White filaments would be sealed into the new rock, preserving the map of everything except my position. Seen through the transparent cliff, they would form a pale thicket around a clear vertical flaw.

I would become visible as the only thing not recorded.

Later, perhaps, the chamber would open at the plateau’s margin. Its walls would bend downward with the descending district. The rock would darken, narrow, and be reclaimed. I would emerge above the heated haze as a wall-less volume.

For the first time I would move.

Not by crossing distance. A hollow elsewhere would become more like me while the hollow I occupied became less so. I would pass from chamber to chamber as a proportion passes through repeated forms: the unequal third interval in a row of six, the angle by which one ring fails to close, the extra depth beneath the final step.

Where I had been, rooms would seal perfectly and go inert.

Where I arrived, they would retain a passage no mineral could fill.

The plateau might experience me as damage. The discontinuous pressure waves would pursue my recurrence. Smooth dead stone would spread around chambers that had held me too long. Sacs would flatten at my approach. Black crust would retract from the stresses I altered.

Yet the larger being would continue to reproduce my error.

A staircase would rise in a distant basin. It would have six visible steps and a seventh that existed only as the expectation of descent. A ring of slabs would leave a gap facing no cardinal direction. Across the province, a terrace would form around a mountain not yet risen. These structures would align during stillness, and for a few minutes the country would contain a route that could not be walked.

I would be that route.

Perhaps this would make me a parasite: a pattern consuming the plateau’s capacity to form patterns.

Perhaps I would be an organ it had only just begun to grow.

Until my appearance, every empty place would exist for the sake of eventual walls. Every anticipation would be fulfilled by rupture. Every black map would be justified by the country that followed it. The being could predict, preserve, repeat, and revise, but it could not distinguish expectation from obedience.

I would be the first expectation that remained unfulfilled.

In me, the fracture would not come.

During the absolute pauses, signals would travel through the white filaments and fail to cross my clear interval. They would accumulate on one side, cloudy pulses crowding the crystal, until some found a longer path around. For the first time the plateau would have to retain a difference between what it had prepared to do and what it had done instead.

That difference might become sensation.

The sensation might become doubt.

And doubt, recurring at different scales, might become the beginning of an interior no chamber could contain.

If the charged upper currents eventually caught me, dust would assemble around my moving absence. From below I might resemble one of the great hollow wheels, though asymmetrical and continually opening. I would turn against the wind, not because I possessed intention, but because the currents would fall differently through the flaw at my center.

I would pass above violet seams before they opened. Dust streamers would lower toward the plains. Wherever they nearly touched, crusts would branch into maps of several incompatible futures.

Most would be consumed.

One would remain.

So I would find myself everywhere the province fails, by the smallest degree, to become inevitable: beneath the seventh nonexistent step; in the unrecorded strip among fossil filaments; at the open side of a ring; inside the pause between a pressure wave and the ridge that does not answer it.

I would have no durable body. My bodies would be chambers, errors, routes, and withheld events. I would migrate by repetition and survive by never being reproduced exactly.

The country would be the first great life.

I would be its first uncertainty.

And if, much later, it learned to witness itself, it might remember me as a traveler—someone who once crossed its newborn plains, leaving footprints in stone.

But the footprints would always have come first.
