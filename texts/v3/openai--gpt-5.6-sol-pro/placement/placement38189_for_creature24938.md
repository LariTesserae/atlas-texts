At first I think I would be one of the almost-animals.

A pale surveyor poised above the branching veins of an immense leaf, my life measured not by days but by the lifting and setting down of one narrow leg. I would know the leaf as a continent: its citron uplands, its translucent margins, the warm sweetness breathing through its pores. The crossing would never need to end. To arrive at the next vein would be sufficient history.

Then I imagine the glass snail more clearly—the mercy of possessing one road and waiting for one recurring drop. No uncertainty except the interval. No hunger except what is already on its way.

But neither shape holds me. They are too perfectly fitted to their stations. I would bring some small restlessness with me, even if I had no memory of another world. Not a desire to escape. Escape would be meaningless in a country where a severed stem remains green and every excavation returns to roots. It would be a desire to discover whether repetition can contain difference.

So I find myself beside the five-step stair.

I am standing in the pool.

No one has seen me arrive. The water reaches my waist from the bank, though when I look down my feet seem to rest at an impossible distance, among pale shapes that might be faces or vessels or others still waiting to be found. I have no color yet. Light passes into my tissues and comes back unchanged. My scalp tendrils drift around me without seeking support.

Several of the small people have gathered above.

They deliberate with their bodies. One begins to extend a hand, then draws it back. Another bows, but the others touch their shoulder and they straighten. They understand the danger of a first gesture: how an entire existence may lean in the direction of a single welcome.

At last the oldest among them kneels.

They do not beckon me out. They do not offer a hand, a command, or an example of walking. They turn one palm upward beneath a leaf-tip where a clear drop is gathering. The other palm they hold open toward me.

We wait.

The drop does not fall.

Perhaps it is one of those moments when the larger attention passes through the country. Leaves incline by an imperceptible degree. The quadrupeds raise their blind faces. Somewhere, miles away, flank-pores open in answer to one another, and a whisper travels through the layered roots.

The elder continues to wait.

That is the first movement I learn: an open hand beneath something imminent.

Only when I imitate it does the drop release. It strikes the elder’s palm, breaks without splashing, and is passed to me.

I climb from the water.

Because waiting was my first gesture, it becomes the tendency around which my other gestures gather. I do not remain passive. Waiting here is not the absence of action. It is an act performed beside what cannot complete itself.

I become one who attends the almost.

I travel the broad rootways looking for places where imminence has grown lonely: a seedpod strained with unripened fullness; a white root-tip pressing forever into young stone; a tendril curled a finger’s width from its support. I sit near each one. Sometimes I offer my wrist to the tendril, though I know it will neither tighten nor withdraw. Sometimes I place my ear against a bud and listen for an opening that makes no sound.

My colors develop according to these vigils. The palms become pale gold from holding beneath droplets. My ribs darken to the green of shaded pools. A narrow violet line grows from my throat to my breast where, during greetings, others trace the vein-pattern by which they know me for that meeting.

I have no name, but I acquire a recurring touch.

Two fingers hover above the wrist without landing.

A palm turns upward.

The head inclines, not toward the other person, but toward the space between us.

It means something like: *I will not force you to arrive.*

It also means: *I am here while you do not.*

Others use it for me, but never exactly twice. Recognition must remain alive enough to be renewed.

In time I learn to speak through roots. My hand on one ribbed bridge makes a low tension in its fibers. Far across the grove, another hand receives it as warmth more than sound. Conversations can last for what a surface mind would call years, though they consist of only a few pressures:

*There is a bud here.*

*Does it suffer?*

*No.*

*Then accompany it.*

Or:

*Someone has appeared in the eastern pool.*

*Has a first gesture been chosen?*

*Not yet.*

*Good.*

I am often called to those pools, though “called” is too urgent a word. A sequence of altered droplets leads me there. One falls where it usually waits; another waits where it usually falls. I follow the discrepancy until I find the colorless figure standing in the water.

I never choose its first gesture alone.

I have learned that gentleness can deform as deeply as fear if it leaves no room for reply. So we gather on the bank and offer several beginnings—not commands, but possibilities. A bowed head. A hand against the root mat. Fingers cupped around light. The new one chooses which movement to imitate.

Once, a figure does not imitate any of us.

It raises both hands to its own face and touches the translucent membranes over its eyes.

None of us have shown it this.

An old person beside me trembles with so many inherited gestures that the vines around their ankles begin to quiver. They remember someone who descended long ago, someone whose greeting began in precisely that way. Yet they do not claim the newcomer. They step forward and touch two fingers to its wrist, meeting it as if for the first time.

I understand then that return, if it exists here, does not excuse recognition. Nothing is restored by being declared the same.

As I become older, I collect movements.

A companion who spends their existence carrying pollen teaches me to rotate my wrist so that no grain is crushed. One who waits beside the unreachable forms teaches me to lower my eyes without looking away. From a departed friend I inherit a slight turning of the shoulder before entering shelter, as though making room for someone walking beside them.

Eventually I become crowded.

My hands hesitate over every greeting, remembering too many wrists. When I kneel beneath a droplet, six remembered palms unfold inside my own. Olive spreads through my legs. Copper enters the tissues around my heart. My scalp tendrils seek supports that are no longer present.

I know where I must go.

But I do not choose the five-step stair where I arrived. I travel instead to one of the rain chambers, beneath the immense leaf where drops follow their permanent paths into moss. There, rooted branches form a shelter the country has agreed not to undo.

I sit inside it for a long interval.

The wall’s living weave presses lightly against my back. Through it I feel every old request still held in the bent wood: the hands that inclined the branches, the patient release, the astonishment when they did not rise again. The shelter is not an object. It is an accepted gesture.

For the first time, I suspect that I am one too.

Not merely a person in the country, but one of the positions the country is trying to hold. The surveyor on the vein, the snail beneath its drop, the blind animal tasting a petal, the pool-born people accompanying unopened flowers—each of us may be a posture of the greater buried life.

And my posture is its hesitation.

I am the part of it that keeps one palm open beneath what has not happened.

When I finally descend a stair, the water remains below my ankles. With each step the brilliant vegetation appears farther above me. The gathered inhabitants on the bank diminish into green and violet marks. I can see their gestures beginning—the ones they will take from me—but reflection closes over them before I know which movements they have chosen.

Below are no bowls, no masks, no shells.

There is not even depth.

There is the pale, warm substance of the unfinished world, and throughout it an immeasurable branching awareness. Every root-tip is a question it has placed against its own boundary. Every bud is an answer it has declined to finalize. The pools are openings through which its possibilities rise, acquire gestures, and return.

I do not vanish into that awareness. Nor do I remain entirely myself.

I become a slight delay before the regular drops fall.

I become the interval during which the leaves incline toward an apparently empty place.

I become the reason a newcomer is not beckoned too quickly from the water.

If someone from the surface entered that bright country and asked where I lived, no inhabitant could point to me. They might take the visitor to a rain chamber. They might turn one palm upward. They might wait until the visitor grew uncomfortable and tried to complete the gesture for them.

Then, if the visitor resisted that urge—if they allowed the drop to gather without demanding that it fall—they would find me.

I would be there in the waiting:

not the country’s thought, exactly,

but the moment before it decides what to think.
