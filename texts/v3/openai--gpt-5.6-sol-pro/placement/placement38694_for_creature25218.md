At first I think I would live on the western shelf, where the pressure houses have diminished from an all-enclosing fact into separate sounds. I imagine a room with a blue-gray roof, a view across the freight ridges, perhaps some minor occupation involving records. Something quiet by the standards of the basin.

But that feels like the choice of someone who has not yet learned the city.

The longer I remain, the farther inward I move.

I would be born in one of the southeastern communal blocks, above the old filtration caverns and below a street officially classified as not yet mature. The block would have been assembled from black brick, vitrified waste, and pale repair stone containing the mineral remains of its former inhabitants. Its floors would answer the pressure houses with a soft double pulse. Visitors would find it impossible to sleep there. I would find sleep anywhere else thin and unconvincing.

As a child, I would be poor at ordinary lessons and very good at the games played with bowls of water. I would lie face-down on warm paving, cheek against stone, and identify freight movements before the older children. Not because my hearing was exceptional. My hearing would be slightly bad. Voices would blur early. But pressure would arrive in me with unusual clarity: through my teeth, the hinges of my jaw, the broad bones above my eyes.

I would be the child who stops halfway through a corridor and places both hands on the wall.

When asked what I feel, I would not have the signs for it. Not a train. Not a pump. Not exactly water. Something moving in the interval between them.

For a while the adults would expect me to become a pressure tender. I would apprentice in the central works and learn the patience of watching a piston move so slowly that attention itself becomes unreliable. I would learn to inspect lubricant by color, to read condensation on bowed glass, to distinguish the harmless settling of an old foundation from the beginning of shear.

I would be competent, but not content. Pressure tenders listen for the condition of the machine. What interests me would be the returning answer: the delayed tremor coming back through walls, buried channels, saturated stone. I would keep noticing echoes that should not return from the direction in which they arrived.

So I would become a harmonic cartographer.

Not one of the celebrated surveyors who publishes a map of a waterway climbing six levels against gravity. I would be the member of the team who carries the bowls.

They would be shallow black vessels, each with three feet and a scored silver interior. I would set them along galleries during wet weather, fill them from sealed flasks, and wait for their surfaces to choose a slope. My companions would mark engine cycle, air weight, temperature, and the position of every active valve. I would kneel beside the water and watch it lean toward a wall.

Our work would take us through places that belong to no inhabited district: old spillways with mineral fur thick as fleece; factory undercrofts where the roof carries a public square; sealed platforms beside conduits large enough to walk through; natural arches that builders have repaired so often no one knows which portion was once a cave.

We would map waterways that do not wet the floor.

One might enter through the eastern wall of a kiln gallery, cross the room diagonally, and leave through the ceiling. Another might appear only during the third and seventh strokes of a particular pressure engine. Some would follow us. We would move a bowl ten meters and find the same concentric division in the water, though calculations said the harmonic channel should have remained behind.

Officially, I would be cautious. Civic hearings preserve every foolish certainty a person has ever spoken. I would say *pressure-mediated gradient*. I would say *apparent reversal*. I would object when resonance advocates used the word *memory* in engineering documents.

In private, I would believe the city dreams in water.

My household name would be spoken, but my work name would be a gesture: two fingers laid against the wrist, followed by the hand turning palm-up. It would mean something like *the return beneath the return*. In records it would be drawn incorrectly, as all gesture-names are. Friends would make the turning motion fractionally too slowly when teasing me for taking too long to answer simple questions.

I would live with six or seven others around an interior court: a signal keeper, two pressure-clay workers, an elder who remembers the sound of a demolished electrolysis hall, children belonging partly to us and partly to the block. My sleeping room would be narrow and windowless, set beside an abandoned cistern now packed with felt and clean sand. When rain deepened the city’s pitch, the sand would shift inside the wall with a sound I felt more than heard.

At meals I would speak too much. Harmonic cartographers spend their shifts communicating in restrained, exact signs, and in the insulated dining room all that unsaid language would break loose. I would tell long stories badly. I would strike the table for emphasis and be scolded for teaching the children careless habits.

My partner would be a stair surveyor.

They would think in centuries, where I think in cycles. Their gift to me at our formal joining would be a black-brick cup with an unpleasantly thick lip. Mine to them would be a copper clasp that requires both hands to close. After twenty years, the cup would begin to fit my mouth. After thirty, the clasp would open under one thumb. Neither of us would mention this without irony.

The gauge eels would enter my life gradually.

At first they would be only marks in other people’s reports: dark inclusions, pressure artifacts, damaged mesh. Then, during a long rain, I would be assigned to a gallery carrying deep-fracture water toward the third separation house. There would be a transparent gauge in the wall. Within it, against a current violent enough to scour metal, a black line would remain perfectly still.

I would watch it for most of a shift.

Nothing about it would suggest an animal. Nothing about it would suggest an object. It would not wave, resist, or cling. It would simply maintain a relationship to the water that the water did not have to itself.

Then the bowls along the gallery would turn toward it.

Not tip. Not ripple. Turn—their surfaces developing a minute slope in the direction of the glass, including bowls positioned where the eel could not be reached by any straight line through the masonry.

I would not touch the gauge. I would not claim it had noticed us. I would record the readings and omit nothing.

That report would trouble my career for years. Some would think I had mistaken a vibration in my own hands for an instrument response. Others would quietly request me whenever an eel appeared. I would become known less for explaining them than for refusing explanations that were too small.

I would come to suspect that the shapes in the pipes are not inhabitants of the water system. They are what becomes visible when two systems—one made by pressure houses, one made by deep stone—briefly share a direction. The eel is neither creature nor channel. It is an alignment.

I would never prove this.

In old age, ordinary speech would recede almost entirely from me. I would wear no hearing apparatus in the communal block because amplified voices would only obscure what remained. Children would sit beside me and ask questions through pressure against my forearm. I would answer with fingers against their wrists.

By then I would know the district through contact. A palm on the kitchen wall would tell me whether the western freight trench was occupied. My bare heel on the washing-gallery floor would tell me rain had reached the upper shelves. I would wake before alarms when a pump three districts away began returning its stroke too quickly.

Once each year, during the near-silence, I would be stationed beside a gauge.

The eels would vanish before the final piston stopped.

No amount of experience would make the silence bearable. Without the engines, my body would lose its edges. I would feel blood passing behind my eyes, fluid shifting in my joints, the tiny grind of teeth against one another. Across the basin, birds would lift their wings. Lizards would pour from the walls. People would touch whatever they trusted.

I would place one hand against the gauge and the other against the stone behind it.

For that hour, I would be unable to tell which side was empty.

Then the first piston would settle.

The impact would enter through the floor, pass up my loosened knees, cross the mineral plates of my mask, and strike the water in my skull. A second answer would return from beneath the plateau—not an echo exactly, but a pressure acknowledging pressure.

My hands would rise before thought. Around me, other hands would rise too. Conversation would resume.

When I died, I would choose mineral interment. A small piece of my rib would be set into the wall beside the abandoned cistern where I slept. Most of me would be ground into repair material for some conduit whose purpose I might never have known while alive.

That would suit me.

I would not become the city. The people of the basin are too exact to call incorporation immortality. But for a few centuries, perhaps, water passing through the repaired stone would encounter some minute difference in density where I had been added. A vibration would cross it, alter by an immeasurable fraction, and continue.

Some future child might stop in the corridor and put both hands against the wall.

Asked what they felt, they would hesitate.

Not a train. Not a pump. Not exactly water.

Something returning.
