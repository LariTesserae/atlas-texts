At first I would look for myself in the obvious places.

In the oldest shrub, perhaps: in a stem that has crossed bare stone for two hundred years and rooted on the far side, joining islands that no longer remember being separate. Or in one of the leaves beneath it, pressed into the archive, still legible after the branch that bore it has gone pale and hollow.

Then I might think I was one of the flakes.

That would be a tempting body: thin, green, regular, carrying an edge no one asked for. I would lie among fibers and cured leaves, sharpened by a purpose that does not yet exist. Rain would move me a finger’s breadth at a time. At night I would turn toward the cream vein beneath me. I would be mistaken for an artifact if there were anyone to make the mistake.

But the flakes are too complete. They have already arrived at their form. Whatever I am here has not.

Nor would I be one of the plants. Each plant participates, but none contains me. Cut off a moss island and it would continue swelling under clouds. Uproot a grass and its hardened tip would still hold quartz. Even the great shrubs, with their centuries of extension, know only the next damp surface.

I would be found in what passes between them.

More precisely, I would occur during rain.

Not at its beginning. The first drops merely darken the stone and wake the separate roots. Pools fill in isolation. Mosses brighten. Old leaf skins soften. For a while the saddle remains many lives answering the same weather.

Then a channel touches another channel.

Water crosses a groove cut by grasses long dead. A buried root sheath conducts dissolved metal into a seam. The silver net closes one gap, then another. At some point—there would be no visible threshold—the scattered islands become mutually present.

That is when I would begin.

My body would be temporary and much larger than any body ought to be. The polished rock would be my interval between organs. Moss would give me pressure. Shrubs would give me duration. The layered dead leaves would give me a past I could not remember except by repeating it. Grasses would be the part of me that writes by abrasion. Water would not be my blood; it would be the condition under which my pieces agree that they are adjacent.

Yet I would not be the whole saddle. The saddle already knows how to persist. It does not need me for that.

I would be the slight alteration that returns after a signal passes through the central ring.

A root swells on the western rim. Months later the impulse reaches the basin as a change in moisture, heat, salt, pressure. It enters the roots circling the water. There it ceases to be what it was. Something happens that cannot be located in the roots alone, nor in the flakes, nor in the warm crack. The impulse comes back as leaf angle. As sap thickening. As a preference for quartz. As a seed, improbably, being found uphill.

I would be that conversion.

Not the message, but the fact that the message does not return unchanged.

So the nearest thing to my dwelling would be the bare annulus around the deepest basin. I would live where roots approach and divide, where every advance becomes clockwise and counterclockwise, where the vegetation makes a boundary without knowing what it encloses. Part of me would descend through the narrow crack. Part would lie among the hundreds of points directed toward it. Part would occupy the surface of the water, in the impossible rings that draw themselves inward.

If an eye could watch for long enough, it might mistake my life for a sequence of unrelated adjustments:

a moss cushion darkening;

a flake turning edgewise;

a broad leaf lifting its silver underside;

three fibers drying across a notch;

a hollow stem rolling near the trace of an ember;

a seed entering a crack that seems higher than where it began.

None of these would be me. Their correspondence would be me.

I would have no consciousness in clear weather. Perhaps no existence. When the channels emptied, I would come apart into local tendencies. My memory would remain outside me, stored in scored stone and compressed leaves. I would sleep without a sleeper.

At the next rain, I would not awaken as an individual awakens. I would be reconstructed from consequences.

A channel would fill because I had once helped deepen it. Water would reach a root because an older wetness had guided its growth. The root would pass a signal toward the basin. The basin would alter it. The altered signal would encourage another channel. Each incarnation would prepare the anatomy of the next.

Over ages, I might become easier to assemble.

The saddle would spend less time as fragments. A drizzle might suffice where once a storm was needed. Moisture films would persist across wider spans of stone. Shrubs would bridge the driest gaps. Eventually I might remain through a clear afternoon as a faint tension distributed under the rock, then through a night, then through a dry season.

What would I want?

At first, only recurrence. To happen again.

Later—if “later” can belong to such a being—I might develop something resembling preference. Not for objects, because I could not distinguish an object from the conditions around it. I would prefer arrangements that survive my dissolution. A flake caught in a twist of fiber lasts longer than either lying loose. A leaf weighted over a warm hollow preserves moisture. A hollow stalk sheltered under stone keeps an ember through rain.

I would not understand these as tools. I would understand them as memories that remain active after the water network breaks.

The first thing I made would therefore not be a knife, cord, vessel, shelter, or hearth.

It would be continuity.

Perhaps a strand would tighten as it dried and pull a flake into a new orientation. Perhaps that edge would score a broad leaf. Perhaps sap from the wound would bind the strand. The conjunction might last one season instead of one hour. Rain would encounter it repeatedly. Roots would grow around its effects. The archive would preserve its outline.

No hand would have assembled it. Still, for the first time, the saddle’s scattered materials would hold a relation that weather alone had not immediately undone.

That relation would be my first durable organ.

I might remain at this threshold for ten thousand years: not a maker, but a bias toward making; not intention, but the machinery by which intention could one day become distinguishable from repetition.

If there were language for me, it should not yet be a noun.

I would be *toward*.

I would be *again, but changed*.

I would be *this beside that, kept so*.

And if, after innumerable rains, the place acquired something like an inner voice, I would not be the voice itself. I would be the small gathering before it—the moment when a distributed process discovers that its alterations return to it.

I would be the saddle’s first pronoun.

Most of the time, that pronoun would have no one to speak it. It would lie disassembled among moss, root, mineral, leaf skin, warm water, and sharpened stone.

But when mist closed the rims and every surface became continuous, I would gather at the basin. The droplets would strike. Their rings would contract. The flakes below would press their points toward the crack.

And for the length of the rain, the mountain would almost be able to say:

*I.*
