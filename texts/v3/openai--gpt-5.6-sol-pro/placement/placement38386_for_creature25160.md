At first I think I would be below.

Not the lifting apparatus itself, but some minor faculty within it: a pressure-sense stretched through warm fractures, awake only when stone begins to resist. I would have no continuous consciousness. Years might pass between one sensation and the next. A command from the city would reach me as a remote change in tension, and my answer—a spring cooling, a fault easing, a shelf inclining westward—might arrive after everyone who issued it had moved away.

But that is too large a solitude. Too easy to mistake slowness for wisdom.

Then I imagine myself among the eastern instruments, one of the reed-thin towers in the mist. Birds would nest in the spaces between my sensors. Moss would interfere with readings at the base. I would measure atmospheric charge, the tremor of ungulates passing, the weight of water entering a crack. Every measurement would also be an intervention. A field too slight for an animal to feel would pass from me to the next reed, persuading a fracture to remain open through one more winter.

Yet I would not know whether I was observing the plateau or teaching it to resemble my predictions.

No. I think I would be human enough to worry about that.

You would find me beyond the easternmost inhabited district, where the buried transit line ends and the instrument forest begins. I would live in a small, relocatable station on a shelf provisionally called the North Divide, though in wet years its water goes south. The legal designation would be something dry and dated. Among those who work there, it would be called **The Hesitation**.

My work would have several official names. None would describe it well. Watershed advocate. Release engineer. Margin custodian. I would be one of the people asked to identify what the city must stop doing before stopping becomes impossible.

Most mornings I would walk out before the basin fog had drained. My coat would be the color of wet basalt, except for a faded red repair around one cuff. I would carry no grand instruments: a folding probe, sample envelopes, a strain listener pressed against the jaw, bright tablets for tracing water. The larger systems would already be everywhere around me.

I would visit structures scheduled for success.

A bridge whose supports had adjusted flawlessly for thirty years, but now encouraged hoofed animals onto nesting gravel. A reservoir membrane so efficient that the downstream marsh had become chemically monotonous. A foundation that had learned the rock beneath it too well and was beginning to preserve a district beyond its intended life. My task would be to ask whether each excellent thing had become excellent in the wrong place.

Sometimes I would authorize a loosening.

Not destruction all at once. First the bridge would cease correcting its eastern edge. Then one span would be lifted during migration season. The maintenance path would be rerouted beneath the surface. People would complain that the old crossing still worked, which would be true. In spring, water would spread through the abandoned supports. Sedges would appear where foot traffic had compacted the gravel. A fox would discover the dry central pier. Three years later, someone would ask why a bridge had ever been built there.

I would keep a private record of such disappearances.

Not before-and-after images. Those make change look cleaner than it is. I would record the interval when a place was no longer one thing and not yet another: a doorway opening onto weather; a conduit carrying meltwater instead of heat; amber roof lights shining over rooms already emptied; children using stacked wall sheets as windbreaks while adults unlace the foundation below.

I think I would be good at leaving work incomplete.

This would not make me serene. I would sometimes want to fix the water where I could understand it. I would look at a wandering stream and see how easily three low barriers could turn it into a dependable channel. I would resent the requirement to preserve uncertainty, especially when uncertainty meant evacuating houses, abandoning cultivated soil, or telling a family that the eastern water had arrived early.

There would be one place I could not regard professionally.

Near my station, a warm fracture would feed a chain of shallow pools. The maps would give them separate identifiers, but I would think of them as one creature appearing and vanishing between stones. In the first pool, mineral films would shine violet-black. In the second, silver moss would grow beneath the water. The third would exist only after rain. Birds would use the last pool, where the warmth was nearly gone.

I would visit it every day and claim I was taking measurements.

Over the years the fracture would narrow. The upper pool would cool. Models would recommend a minor opening pulse from the reed grid—enough to preserve the system without fixing its exact course. I would delay approval through one winter, then another.

This would be my hypocrisy: I would defend impermanence everywhere except where I had learned to love a particular sequence of water.

Eventually a child from the station would ask why the first pool no longer steamed. I would explain mineral closure, thermal migration, changing pressure below the shelf. The child would listen and then ask whether the water had gone somewhere else.

I would realize that I had been mourning it as if it had been destroyed.

So we would look.

We might find warmth several kilometers downslope, beneath a crust I had crossed for years without noticing. There, in a shallow basin with no projected name, black water would be opening between new green edges. Perhaps nothing would have followed it yet. Perhaps beetles would already be under the moss.

We would mark the place, but lightly.

By then people would recognize me at a distance by my gait: slow, testing, slightly lateral. They might mistake me for an instrument when I stood listening beside the reeds. That would please me more than it should.

I would not be the spirit of the plateau, nor its interpreter. I would be one of its temporary organs of doubt—a person appointed to notice when care has hardened into possession.

And when my station was finally withdrawn, I would help take it apart. I would remove the window skins, coil the listening fibers, and wait while the foundation released the stone a fraction at a time. The last thing left would be the entry step, because people need somewhere to stand while dismantling the place from which they leave.

Then that too would go.

Years afterward, there might be a pale rectangle of lichen where the station had altered the light. Water might use one of our old cable trenches for a season. A raptor might hunt from the top joint of a reed tower whose measurements no longer mattered.

If anything of me remained, I would want it to be no more permanent than that: not a monument, not a load-bearing tile, but a small permission in the landscape—

a margin left wide enough,

a bridge removed in time,

a river given room to be wrong about where we thought it would go.
