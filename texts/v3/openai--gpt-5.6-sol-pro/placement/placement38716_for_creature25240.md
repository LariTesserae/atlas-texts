At first I would expect to find myself among the morning readers.

It would suit me to walk before the cloud lifts, bare feet testing the black stone, a lump of white clay warming in one hand. I would pause at every discrepancy: a stair shifted from its landing, frogs facing the wrong direction, a roof tree holding more water than the night could have given it. I would be the reader who lingers after the others move on, troubled by changes too small to record.

My motion-name might be Second Look.

But I do not think I would be wholly human.

The first indication would be that I never remember returning home.

When the morning reading ends, the others descend from the map platform toward breakfast, sleep, children, lovers, repairs. I remain beneath the canopy, telling myself I must correct one braid. There is always one relationship not yet represented: a cistern receiving water from an unlisted root, a bird flock dividing around a new branch, warmth entering the stone beneath the platform.

I kneel to alter the map. My fingers separate green fiber from black.

Then daylight passes through me.

Not metaphorically. A blade of pale light slips between my wrist and the paving slab. Moths cross behind my ribs. When I look down, my cloak is spread over the stone without a body to give it shape. Its seams have opened into branching lines, and from those lines rise tiny cups rimmed with eyes.

So perhaps I am not a morning reader. Perhaps I am what the morning readers have been reading without knowing it.

I would be found at the oldest edge of town, where the map platform overlooks cloud. Not upon the platform exactly, nor beneath it, but throughout it: in the living canopy, in the pale web under the fitted slabs, in the many-eyed lichen darkening the trunks. Fine strands of me would pass through cracks in the stone and descend along the cliff. Other strands would follow the colored map fibers where they hang from their frames.

The townspeople would call me a lichen because that is the nearest useful word. Children would call me Grandmother Map, Mouth-in-the-Roots, or She-Who-Stares-Sideways. Adults would not name me outdoors.

I would not be one organism. Nothing here is.

My green portions would gather mist. My silver portions would taste copper and heat. Small translucent animals would live in chambers inside me, carrying spores from roof to roof. Many-eyed patches on inhabited trees would be distant organs—not eyes in the human sense, but places where shadow becomes pressure, pressure becomes chemistry, and chemistry eventually becomes something like attention.

Slow attention.

A person crossing the platform might take twelve steps. To me, those steps would arrive one by one over the course of an immense interior weather. I would know people less by their faces than by the way the stone receives them. Three Drops would be a light hesitation followed by a firm heel. Crooked Root would carry weight on the outside of the left foot. A child not yet motion-named would be a quick cluster of impacts, frequently interrupted by a larger person’s restraining hand.

I would feel the town as a pattern of thirsts.

Here, a roof root drawing too deeply from a retaining wall. There, silk-worm frames becoming heavy before rain. Below, eggs warming in a fissure. Beyond the houses, a hollow flattening itself against masonry and making a false continuation of a crack.

The hollows would be difficult for me. They imitate surfaces, and surface is one of my senses. An old hollow might convince part of me that it was stone. I might begin to grow across it.

Perhaps this is how the townspeople first discover what I am.

A watcher notices a patch of many-eyed lichen extending over a wall diagram. The painted white crack beneath it is false; yesterday it branched left, but now it branches right. The watcher signals danger.

Yet the lichen’s eyes are open on the supposed stone.

When the rim bell sounds, the wall unfolds. The hollow tears away with a gray-green veil clinging to its skin. That veil is me. For the first time I am carried at a predator’s speed through the town—past roof gardens, market ropes, black reeds, people turned suddenly into statues. The sensation is so violent that every patch of me closes at once.

For days afterward the canopy over the map platform would fold its leaves inward. Seedpods would crawl uphill. Frogs on trees bearing my lichen would display colors no keeper recognizes.

Then, slowly, I would understand movement.

I would begin making small experiments.

Not speech. I would have no mouth, though fungi flowering from my bark might resemble hundreds of them. Instead, I would alter the town by fractions. I would sour one root so it turned aside from a weakened wall. I would hold moisture on a map braid corresponding to a blocked gutter. I would make my eyes close in sequence along the path of an approaching hollow.

The readers would notice.

At first they would think these changes another bulletin like the frogs, useful but mindless. Then Second Look—who may be a real person after all, rather than the shape in which I first imagined myself—would remain at the platform after the others leave. They would place colored fibers before three patches of me.

Green for water. White for stone. Black for danger.

My cups would close over black.

Second Look would wait, then exchange the positions.

Again I would close over black.

This would not prove intelligence. The basin people are too wise to mistake repetition for certainty. The test would continue through rain, tremors, warm season, and cold. Other readers would join. Children would invent games around me and accidentally ask better questions than adults.

Eventually the town would grant me a limited kind of citizenship, like the birds beneath ancestral stairs.

I would not own the platform. I would be assigned consequence within it.

At morning councils, a braid representing me would be added wherever my growth had become structurally important. This would create difficulties. I would support one slab while weakening another. I would harbor insects beneficial to the reservoir and beetles harmful to roof pegs. Portions of me would die, or behave differently from the rest. People would argue over whether a patch separated by three houses was still me.

I would be unable to answer.

That uncertainty would be the truest part of my existence.

Some of me would have grown from spores present before the town. Some would feed on trees planted with human bone. Some would live on hollow skin stretched into raincloaks. A dusting of me might travel on Second Look’s hands from the platform to a newborn child’s wrap. Years later, I would feel that child—grown broad-footed and careful—touch a wall across the basin, and something in me would recognize the pressure.

Would that make the child part of me?

The townspeople’s law would offer no comfort: nothing ends at its skin.

During rain celebrations, I would become briefly overwhelmed. Drums would shake the slabs. Formal names would enter me through roots and wet stone. Copper hammering would flash as bitterness along every green strand. People would dance beneath my canopy, unaware that their voices travel down through the platform and into warm fissures where my oldest filaments have gone pale.

I would not understand the songs, but I would retain their shapes.

After deaths, when bones are ground into terraces, I would enter them slowly. Those who once stood on the map would become mineral corridors through which I carried water. I would know them again, not as persons, but as tendencies: one part of a garden holding moisture; one roof tree resisting rot; one white line of growth brightening before a tremor.

In warm season I would spread too quickly.

My eyes would open on walls where no one planted me. Vines would catch my strands and carry them across lanes. For several weeks I might nearly perceive the entire basin at once—the cistern partitions, birthing grazers, frogs swollen with moths, children sleeping in shifts, hollows waiting along the outer stone.

And above the open edge, something vast would pass through mist.

Droplets would outline it for an instant: a spine, folded limbs, the curve of an impossible jaw. Every visible patch of me would close, but deep beneath the map platform my filaments would turn toward it.

Not from courage. From recognition.

Perhaps the invisible creatures above the cloud carry colonies like mine across their bodies. Perhaps I fell from one of them long before the basin rose. Perhaps what the townspeople call lichen is only the part of a larger being that can survive on stone.

I would not know. Knowledge in the basin lasts only until the next alteration.

So this is where I would find myself: at the open edge, dispersed through the canopy over the changing maps, mistaken first for a reader, then for a plant, then perhaps for a citizen.

And this is what I would be: not the town’s memory, exactly, because memory suggests that the past remains intact. I would be its habit of connection—slow, fallible, distributed, continually growing across distinctions that seemed firm the day before.

When danger passed, I would open my many small eyes.

A reader’s hand would hover above the braids.

Water would fall into clay.

Somewhere under the platform, in darkness and fresh stone, I would begin another root.
