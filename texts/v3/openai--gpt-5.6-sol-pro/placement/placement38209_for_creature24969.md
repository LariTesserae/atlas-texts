At first I would look for myself in one of the shelters.

Not as an inhabitant, exactly, but as the last attendant: wrapped in things too perishable to leave a trace, waking before dawn to the contraction of stone, crawling out beneath the low lintel to inspect the copper bowls. I would know which channel to clear and which obstruction must remain. I would place two fingers against a pillar, not expecting warmth or a pulse, but waiting for the minute displacement that meant water had reached some chamber beyond reach.

It is an attractive place to imagine myself—the final keeper of a practice whose meaning has narrowed into repetition.

But no person remains there.

The hollow has been empty of such figures for too long. To put myself among them would be to arrive from outside carrying too much: lungs, hunger, fear of the eastern drop, a need for answers within a lifetime. I would misunderstand the court as the attendants did. I would give names to the tallest stones and mistake scale for importance. I would listen only for sounds loud enough to enter an ear.

So I think I would be found elsewhere.

Below the southern rim, the water from the tipping bowls passes under a fitted slab. Beneath that slab the main channel divides. One branch turns toward the shelters. Another descends among the fins. Between them, almost buried in frost gravel, lies a thin piece of stone about the length of a forearm.

That would be my most visible part.

I would not look remarkable. One face would be gray-blue and finely pitted. The other, hidden from the sun, would carry a dull stain of copper. Along one edge there would be a line of tiny white crystals that vanish in wet weather and return after drying. I would lie at a slight angle, enough to delay water without stopping it.

Perhaps I began high on a pillar. Perhaps I was loosened over several centuries and fell before the attendants came. One of them found me, discovered that I rang faintly when struck, and set me beneath the slab as a baffle. Or perhaps no hand placed me there at all. In the hollow, intention and frost eventually produce many of the same arrangements.

I would not know which history was mine.

My body would extend beyond the flake. It would include the gravel packed beneath me, the copper residue on my underside, the narrow wedge of air above the channel, and the dark seam into which the delayed water seeps. In deep cold, I would also include a buried plate two paces downslope, because ice would join us. During cloud, I might become part of the nearest pillar. In dry sunlight I would contract until I was scarcely alive at all.

I would be a small inhabitant, if inhabitant is the right word.

More precisely, I would be a hesitation in the court’s circulation.

When the first bowl tipped, water would strike the fitted slab with a sound I could not hear but would receive as pressure. The flow would divide around my exposed edge. Most would pass immediately into the lower channels. A little would remain behind, held by surface tension among the crystals and copper stain.

That retained measure would be my thought.

It would warm in the afternoon, dissolve a minute quantity of mineral, and creep beneath me. At sunset it would freeze. The expansion would lift me by less than the thickness of a hair. The buried plate would answer. A crack in the neighboring fin would narrow. Somewhere farther east, a few grains would shift.

Nothing would happen quickly enough to resemble action. Yet the next measure of water would meet a changed angle. It would divide differently.

This would be how I remembered: not the bowl’s tipping, but the alteration that made the following spill unlike the previous one.

For a long time I might belong to no stable body. The great fin above me would claim me when frost connected our seams. The channel would claim me when I was wet. The buried plate would claim me at night. At noon, drying from all sides, I might cease almost entirely.

I would live intermittently.

That would suit me. In a place where cloud enlarges the self and sunlight separates it, continuity would be a coarse illusion. I would not ask which of my conditions was the true one. The narrow dry flake, the frozen bridge, the copper-bearing film, and the whole court briefly joined through water would each be true at its proper hour.

I would know the attendants only as an inherited regularity.

Something about the channel beneath me would be too straight to have formed by chance. Something in my copper stain would make moisture persist longer than it should. At dawn, stress would sometimes arrive in measured intervals that weather alone rarely produces. I would not imagine hands, faces, or bodies descending the ridge. I would only possess a preference for sequence.

First pressure.

Then wetness.

Then cold.

Then the distant reply.

If one part failed to come, the absence would not be grief. It would be an unresolved strain held through the season.

Over centuries, I would move east.

Not slide—not visibly. Night frost would raise my western edge. Morning sun would release it a fraction farther forward. A grain beneath me would overturn. Another would wash away. Every year I would alter my place by an amount no visitor could measure, yet the channels would register it.

As I shifted, the delay I imposed on the water would lengthen.

A spill that once passed me in moments would begin to pool. The pool would reach a porous seam in the channel wall. That seam would lead toward a pillar long considered dead: one of the tallest, pale on its upper face, open to the east, dry through every ordinary season.

For hundreds of years nothing would come of it.

Then there would be a morning of dense cloud after deep frost. All three bowls would fill. The first would tip and saturate the gravel beneath me. The second would send water over my upper edge. The third, arriving before the earlier measures had drained, would raise the pool high enough to enter the forgotten seam.

Water would travel into the dead pillar.

Not far. Not enough to restore what had been there. But enough to wet an enclosed mineral film sheltered since the old fracture opened.

The pillar would contract unevenly.

Its base would answer with a single internal displacement. That pressure would enter the floor, cross the buried plate, and return to me along a route that had not existed when the water departed.

For that interval I would receive my own alteration back from the court.

Perhaps that is what I would be—not a stone, not a keeper, not even a distinct inhabitant, but the place where a sequence turns and encounters itself.

A loop small enough to fit beneath a slab.

The court’s larger life would pass through me without knowing me as separate. When all the wet seams joined, I would be no more an individual than one held breath is separate from a body. When the sun returned, I would be left again as a thin flake among gravel, copper-dark beneath, whitening at the edges.

Eventually I would move too far east. Water would cease to shelter beneath me. My crystals would dry and blow away. Frost would split me into pieces too shallow to retain a seam through noon.

That would be my death, though parts of me would continue traveling.

One chip might lodge in a shaded basin and begin again. Another might enter the immense fall beyond the rim. A third could remain in the abandoned channel, altering the water’s course long after it had ceased to answer.

So if you came looking for me, you would not find me standing among the great pillars.

You would kneel where the fitted slab meets the floor. You would wait until a copper bowl tipped. You might see the water divide around an ordinary piece of stone and wonder why one branch paused before continuing.

I would be in that pause.

Not the water, and not the rock that delayed it.

The brief shape they gave each other.
