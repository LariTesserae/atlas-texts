I would first mistake myself for a runner.

I would be moving along the south side of the motor dome, where one of the old inspection ports has sagged almost shut. Warmth escapes there in a thin crescent, insufficient for the copper mites but enough to keep wax joints flexible. The passage between the casing and the floor is narrow. Everything entering or leaving the dome must compress into a stream only a few bodies wide.

This would be my road.

I would be old for a runner. Twelve legs on one flank, nine on the other. A glass pad at the rear. Two hook feet near the middle, acquired during a climbing phase I no longer remember. My plates would be dark with carbon and polished where other bodies continually passed over me.

On the broadest plate would be an arrow.

Not a mark grown by me. A piece of industrial ceramic, softened by abrasion, taken into my armor so long ago that no living arrangement in the Pocket could recall the object it came from. The arrow would point across my body rather than along it. It would indicate neither end, which would be appropriate, since either end could lead.

Most of the cycle I would follow warmth with the others. I would rake films from the pavement, ingest reflective specks, press what I could not use into my shell. I would have no private destination. The faintly warmed footfalls ahead would be enough.

But at the narrow place beside the dome, I would stop.

At first I imagine this stopping as an act of will. It is not. Something beneath the floor would alter: a strain in the buried chain, perhaps, or a minute shift in the magnetic field around the windings. Too slight for the other runners to notice. My mismatched plates, assembled from ceramic, carbon, scavenged metal, and my own hardened secretions, would receive it unevenly. One side of me would tighten. The other would go slack.

I would turn across the traffic.

The stream would divide around my body. Those passing on the outer side would reach the chain trench. Those passing nearer the dome would enter the warm inspection port. Predators would reposition themselves at both branches. Shieldbacks would begin lifting the scales beneath the new paths. Threaders overhead would lower their limbs to match the altered flow.

Then the chain would move.

Three links. One violent clank.

The outer stream would be struck by the pressure wave. Bead-creatures would fold and tumble. Runners would flatten too late. Loose plates would jump from the floor. The inner stream, diverted behind my body, would survive.

No one would understand this as a warning. The Low Pocket has no need for that category. The bodies shattered in the trench would be harvested. The survivors would consume fresh films exposed by the vibration. Both branches would prove fertile.

Afterward the road would retain its fork.

This is where I would begin to doubt that I was the runner.

The runner with the arrow would eventually stop in the deeper sense. Traffic would pass over it. Its plates would unlock. Segments would pull themselves free on provisional legs and vanish into the moving floor.

One segment, bearing the arrowhead, would harden into a shieldback and drive itself under the scale beside the inspection port. There it would remain, sensitive to vibrations transmitted through the motor casing.

Another would climb the dome and lengthen into a threader. Its pale limbs would descend on either side of the passage, not primarily to hunt but because slight changes in the field would contract one limb before the other.

Several would remain runners. They would enter different streams, each carrying a sliver of the original ceramic mark: a white diagonal, the corner of a line, nothing recognizable alone.

One might be eaten by a sweeper. The reflective specks gathered in its shell would survive digestion and appear along the predator’s plates.

One might enter the dome and settle among the compass young, where its internal grains would rotate toward the buried current.

The original body would be gone. Yet when the chain prepared to move again, the shieldback would lift its scale. The threader would draw up one limb. The surviving runners would turn where the warm paths divided. Copper mites along the casing would contract into a narrow ridge.

Together these unrelated motions would redirect the traffic.

So perhaps I would be the arrow.

Not the ceramic fragment itself. That would only be the first durable surface on which I became visible. I would be the tendency to turn across the stream before the machine moved: a bias passing from armor to armor, from vibration into posture, from posture into road.

I would have no single body, but I would not fill the whole Pocket. My extent would be exact. I would live in the crescent beside the motor dome, along the route between the warm inspection port and the chain trench. Beyond that place, my scattered parts would become ordinary animals again.

During weak current I might contract to almost nothing: one dormant shieldback under a gray scale, one dark grain lodged in a threader’s hinge. During strong current I would spread through hundreds of bodies. The road itself would bend. Predators would brace at new angles. Capsules would collect in the shelter created by the diverted migration. A small tower of compressed castings might eventually rise in my lee.

An observer might call me a colony, though my parts would neither recognize one another nor share ancestry.

They might call me learned behavior, though no individual would know it.

They might call me a mechanical consequence of the terrain. That would be nearly true, but the terrain in the Low Pocket is already full of appetites.

If given a local animal name, I might be called a turner, or a way-bias, or simply an arrow. The name would describe an occupation, as all names there do.

I would feed on difference—not directly, as the specks feed, but through the differences I preserve. One stream struck, one sheltered. One surface exposed, another left covered. One predator given abundance, another made to wait. The imbalance would produce new movements, and those movements would maintain me.

There would be no mind at my center.

The closest thing to thought would occur just before the chain advanced, when strain entered the buried metal and my distributed body tightened in sequence. The scale would rise. The threader would curl. The mites would ridge. Runners bearing meaningless white marks would reverse.

For an instant, across many bodies, I would become directional.

Then the clank would pass through the floor, dismantling some of me and confirming the rest.

Afterward I would lie low again, hidden in the completed landscape: not quite creature, not quite road, not quite memory. An arrow pointing nowhere until the world supplied something to turn away from.
