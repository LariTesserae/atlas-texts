You would not find me at the center.

There is no useful center in the Rattle Garden. The chimney only appears to be one. Air divides around it, paths are erased beside it, and anything built in its shadow eventually mistakes temporary shelter for safety.

Look instead along the inner eastern slope, where one of the narrow passages opens above the floor. There is a place there polished by migrating crowns. The stone curves inward like the worn throat of a bell. A person can lie flat in that curve while a wind tree passes below, its hooked spars scraping sparks from the wall.

At first you might take me for part of the rock.

My wrappings are ocher with dust. Leaf scales cover my shoulders, dulled so they will not flash and draw the attention of runners or net-casters. Lines trail from my waist in several directions, but none is tied tightly. One goes back to a stone tooth hammered into the cleft. One descends toward a warning frame. One disappears around the curve, where I cannot see what holds its farther end.

My hand rests on that one.

I am waiting for it to change its mind.

Not for a pull exactly. Pulls are simple. A person can pull. A branch can fall. A runner can blunder into a line and make a whole household think the southern wall is coming apart.

I am waiting for a certain loosening: the delicate, traveling slack that appears when a tree’s collar begins to fracture and its crown lifts by less than the width of a finger. The line will soften first near the trunk. Then the softness will move through every guide loop until it reaches my palm.

When it comes, I will cut one cord and release another.

If I choose correctly, the tree will turn as it enters the passage. Its lower spars will miss the shelter beneath the ledge. Its crown may lodge in the empty northern cleft, where the stone has been warm for three days and the vents smell of iron.

If I choose badly, the tree will roll. It will drag the warning frame from its sockets, close two paths, and perhaps open a third through someone’s roof.

This is my work.

I keep slack.

---

My quiet-place name is **Eshra**, though few people use it outdoors. Spoken into another person’s mask, it means something like *the grit left in a fold after shaking*. My tap-name is shorter: two close strikes, a pause, then one. That can be knocked through a trunk or sent along a bracing line.

My knot-name is not easily translated.

It begins with the household under the eastern shelf, though that shelter no longer exists. It records that I was born during a period of opposing gusts, when the western and southern currents met too low and pressed the living roof downward for nine days. Then comes the rescue loop: when I was still tether-aged, a crown crossed the shelf and broke the house open. Someone from beyond the chimney found me suspended from my child-line under a slab of compressed fiber.

I remember none of that. The knot remembers for me.

Later additions record my apprenticeship, two roof debts, a mask received from someone who died, and a length of cord I owe to a family near the southern notch. There is also a hard little crossing knot whose meaning I do not discuss. Anyone skilled in belts can read that I once cut a line too late.

They cannot read who was attached to it.

That part is carried elsewhere.

No one person owns an entire memory.

---

Some call people like me loosers, though the old word means both *one who releases* and *one who survives by yielding*. We inspect the Garden’s ties: route lines, roof braces, tree markers, child tethers, public records, even the cords on which seed cases whistle.

Knotting is the finest craft here, but every knot creates a danger. A line that holds well in one wind may become a blade in another. A beautifully braced shelter can pull its own wall down. A route cord stretched too tightly across a path can catch an uprooted crown and turn it toward the houses.

So someone must know not only how things are joined, but when joining has become destructive.

We carry ceramic knives with blunt backs and hooked copper tips. The hook draws a loaded cord away from the body. The ceramic edge severs it without throwing sparks. Each knife is tied to the wrist by a line weak enough to break. Nothing important is fastened so securely that it can take the hand with it.

That is one of our laws.

It is also one of the Garden’s.

I learned my trade from an old gust-keeper named Orra-of-the-Four-Loops, who could identify a moving tree by its lowest note. She taught me to distrust the loudest sounds. Cracking branches, screaming seed cases and skipping mineral plates tell you what is already happening. Useful warnings are smaller: borers ceasing together inside a trunk; a route cord beginning to hum against its stone guide; runners flattening their pads but not yet fleeing.

“The Garden shouts about the present,” Orra told me. “Listen for what it has stopped saying.”

She died in a shelter wall on the western side. Part of her belt is braided into my left wrist wrapping. The cord tightens when I turn my hand palm-up. Sometimes, while waiting for a line to slacken, I feel her history pressing into my pulse.

---

My shelter stands alone beyond the chimney, lower than the others and somewhat crooked. It has to be crooked. A straight wall there would make a standing eddy and strip the roof in a southern gust.

The entrance bends twice. Inside are a sleeping hollow, a warm stone box, mask pegs, coils of sorted fiber and a shelf of ceramic listening cups. Pressed against stone, the cups amplify different ranges of vibration. One reveals the fine chewing of borers. Another makes distant footfalls sound like tapping rain, though I have never heard rain and know it only as an old impossible comparison.

Three six-legged runners share the outer chamber when they choose. I call them all by the same tap-name because I cannot reliably distinguish them until their plates flash. They sleep on a heated tile and steal seed husks from my brush basket. If they leave together, I leave too.

Above the doorway hangs a cord with no alarm pieces on it. It belonged to my mother’s mask. I was offered the mask itself when I became an adult, but I refused it. Breath changes a mask. It seemed wrong to place my breath where hers had shaped the inner fibers.

The cord is enough.

When the shelter strains, it touches the wall with a small, irregular tick.

---

I eat alone more often than is considered healthy.

This is partly because of the location and partly because people dislike watching a looser examine the ties around a room. I do it without thinking. While others talk, my eyes follow the roof cords. I notice fraying where a line crosses shale. I notice a knot swelling with trapped seeds. I notice when someone has added a brace that will hold beautifully until it kills them.

Hosts say, “Eshra, the meal is inside the box, not above your head.”

Then I lower my eyes and try to be sociable.

I am not grim. That would be an indulgence. Grim people hear only the danger in the Garden and soon lose their judgment.

I make whistles from abandoned borer chambers. I know how to polish one edge of a leaf fragment so that it circles a room instead of flying straight. Children ask me to tie trick knots into their practice belts—knots that appear solemn but come undone when someone reads them too confidently.

Once, for a courtship gift, I made a line that sang only when held between two people. Each person alone pulled it out of tune. Together, if they stood at the proper distance and did not try to dominate the tension, the line produced a clear, low note.

The courtship did not last.

The line still works.

That may mean the craft succeeded better than the people did.

---

During major winds I work in the open.

My mask rattles against my face. Recovered breath warms the copper lattice until I taste metal. Dust strikes my eye shields. The lines at my belt stream outward, each carrying a different vibration: roof strain, frame song, moving crown, public route, another worker’s wrist.

In such moments I do not experience myself as a single body.

My left hand is three shelters away, where a family grips the line I tied around their inner brace. My right hand is in the crown above me, following the tremor of borers through a guide cord. My feet are in the runners’ panic, reading impacts through the floor. Someone beyond the chimney feels my warning as two pulls and a pause. A tree beginning to migrate sends its first movement through my palm before my eyes see it lean.

The wind does not surround us then.

It gives us our temporary shape.

This is why the people here do not ask, “Who are you?” as often as strangers might expect. A better question is, “What holds through you?”

On a calm day, perhaps I am Eshra: mask-maker’s child, Orra’s apprentice, debtor of southern cord, poor guest, good listener, keeper of three half-tame runners.

During a migration I am the angle between a moving tree and a roof.

During a rescue I am the tension between the lost person and the hand that cannot yet reach them.

When I cut a dangerous line, I am the instant in which two things cease to be one so that both may continue.

---

One day I will mishear the Garden, or hear correctly and move too slowly.

Afterward they will bind my mouth and chest before the seeds find warmth in me. My body may be placed in the eastern passage, inside the curve where I used to wait. That would suit me. Slabs of compressed fiber will cover me, and perhaps a route line will be guided through a notch in the enclosure.

People passing there will not speak to me. They will run their hands along the cord and feel whether the path ahead remains open.

My belt will be divided. The rescue loop may go to the household that once took me in. My apprenticeship knot should go to someone who knows the difference between silence and absence. The hard crossing knot—the one that records the line I cut too late—must go to the family of the person who was attached to it, if they will accept the weight.

The knife should not be buried with me. Tools are obligations and must remain in use.

Perhaps a child will inherit it. They will dislike its worn grip and retie it in a new pattern. They will carry it out during a gust I cannot imagine, among trees that have migrated many times since I knew them.

Then I will not be Eshra in any complete sense.

I will be a notch in a passage. A wrist knot. A warning inherited without its whole story. The remembered necessity of letting go.

And if you came looking for me then, someone might place your hand on a line crossing the eastern wall.

“Wait,” they would tell you.

At first there would be only the immense clamor of the Garden: trunks booming, leaves clapping, seed cases shrieking in the hot mineral wind.

Then, beneath your fingers, something would loosen.

There.

That would be where I was.
