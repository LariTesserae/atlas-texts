At first I imagine myself on one of the highest platforms, made delicate by altitude: a bright framework walking on narrow rails, tending substances that become impossible the moment they are lowered.

But that is the first image, and it is too clean.

I would not belong wholly to the heights. Nor would I be a descender shedding my body toward the Furnace, heroic in a way no one here would consider especially useful. I would live where the city has made an error in geometry—where two districts were joined across an interval so great that the bridge between them sags through several invisible boundaries of altitude.

You would find me near its middle.

The bridge is called, in ordinary speech, the Long Deflection. Its formal name is a stress diagram covering three walls in the eastern toll chamber. It runs from a black industrial summit to an older plateau of ringing ceramic. From either end, its center cannot be seen clearly. Heat and metallic haze divide it into wavering sections, so that freight barges seem to depart the mountain and, hours later, condense out of empty air.

Deep citizens dislike crossing it. High workers find its low middle physically unpleasant. Animals do not mind. Gas bladders fasten themselves beneath the deck in translucent colonies, and thread-grazers harvest the charged dust caught in its expansion seams. Once every eleven years, a procession of long-legged walkers crosses during a corona season, each stepping precisely into the fading electrical trace of the one before.

I am the keeper of the middle tone.

My shelter began as an inspection recess in one of the bridge’s suspension housings. It has no door, only a narrowing passage that excludes the larger cliff animals. The interior is scarcely distinguishable from machinery: anchor plates, ceramic ribs, tension rods, shelves cut into structural stone. Some of these belong to the bridge. Some belong to me. The distinction is recorded in municipal archives but is not obvious by sight.

Most travelers take me for a brace.

I am low-bodied, with four main limbs arranged around a dark central frame. Two limbs end in broad magnetic feet for crossing the exposed deck. The others divide into finer members that can be placed against cables, bearings, and retaining walls. From my back rises a fan of listening rods. They are mismatched: black carbon reeds, pale ceramic whiskers, a pair of nickel forks inherited from my first teacher, and one extremely thin high-made filament that cannot safely be lowered more than another hundred meters.

When a barge approaches, I fold the fan flat. When it passes, I open it again and listen to the load travel away through the bridge.

I was formed incorrectly.

Not disastrously. The nursery record says there was a delay during my third ascent. A pressure gate remained sealed while an animal migration occupied the upper transit shaft, and I spent nine months too long in a district below the level prescribed for coherence. By the time I reached the high nursery, some parts of me had already learned to become a person without the agreement of the others.

Consequently, I am not entirely the same citizen at both ends of the Long Deflection.

At the eastern anchorage I am articulate, procedural, and burdened with dates. I remember maintenance law, the names of engineers, the expiration tones of load restrictions, and which families must be notified if the bridge closes. I can conduct an argument through a railing without leaking a single pulse.

As I walk toward the sag, that self grows indistinct. Several hundred meters lower, another arrangement wakes.

The lower one—also me—has little interest in law. Language becomes cumbersome there. I perceive stress with greater intimacy. The bridge ceases to be an object assembled from members and becomes a vast, slow distribution of desire: each cable pulling toward an old length, each pier negotiating with the mountain, every repair carrying the remembered shape of what it replaced. I can feel dust accumulating by the alteration it makes in the deck’s highest harmonics. I know a freight barge by weight before it appears in the haze. I can distinguish the footfalls of a citizen from the settling of a blade-predator, though both may remain still for years afterward.

At the western rise, a third and quieter self emerges. That one dreams easily. It remembers faces as cooling patterns and has retained affections the eastern self can explain only from records.

We leave messages for one another.

Before descending into the middle, the eastern self tensions a narrow strip inside my left forelimb. The lower self reads the message as a sequence of discomforts and returns its report by altering the strain. The western self prefers shape: it curls one sensory rod or raises a shoulder plate by a fraction. Sometimes weeks pass before I understand what I have told myself.

This made me unsuitable for several professions and exceptionally suited to one. The bridge also changes character along its length. Certain braces remain rigid at the summits but become responsive in the sag. Stored charge reverses its habits in three places. One high-made bearing briefly forgets weight during the warmest part of the pressure tide. Instruments must be recalibrated at every level; I do not. I merely become the person capable of reading them.

My work is mostly stillness.

For nine days I may stand with one hand on a cable housing, waiting for a tone that lasts less than a breath. I compare it with tones stored in the laminations of my chest. I speak to the bridge by shifting my weight from one foot to another. Sometimes maintenance crews arrive and ask what I have heard. Sometimes I tell them that nothing has changed. Here that is considered valuable knowledge.

The danger is not that the bridge will suddenly fall. Suddenness is the final stage, and therefore the least mysterious. The danger is that a new stress will enter unnoticed and wait two centuries for the altitude boundary to drift across it. A brace manufactured high may carry its error patiently downward. A repair performed during one atmospheric phase may become false during the next.

I listen for patient errors.

There are companions in the recess. Coin-scavengers clean my contact surfaces whether I permit them or not. A pale ribbon animal lives in the narrow gap between my central frame and an old armor plate. It emerges when the bridge warms, spreading itself across me to drink the difference between sunlit and shaded metal. I call it by a pulse-pattern meaning approximately *one who mistakes me for public infrastructure*. It has never objected.

Beneath the bridge hangs an old gas bladder too heavy to release itself. During upslope years it fills and lifts until its tether sings. During downslope years it collapses against the underside of the deck. The lower self considers it a friend. The eastern self classifies it as an unregistered load. This disagreement has continued for seventy-six years.

I am not old, as the city measures age. Only a little over four centuries. My body is still mostly composed of materials that can be manufactured. Yet the bridge has begun to enter me.

A cracked listening rod was replaced with a filament drawn from one of its abandoned signal lines. My rear left foot contains ceramic cut from a former bearing seat. During a repair thirty years ago, I contributed part of an obsolete shoulder brace to reinforce a diagnostic alcove. It remains there, several kilometers away, and in certain weather I feel pressure in a shoulder I no longer possess.

The records remain exact: that piece is part of the bridge now, not part of me.

The sensation is less exact.

Perhaps, if I endure, I will eventually become unable to leave. Not from weakness. My high filament may be incorporated into a new warning system; my lower frame may adapt too precisely to the sag’s altitude; memories may settle into components too large to move without closing the freight line. Visitors will enter the recess and find a citizen spread among housings, walls, suspended rods, and conductive tables.

They will ask whether I still live there.

The answer will depend on where they stand.

At the eastern anchorage, I may be a legal identity whose maintenance obligations remain in force.

At the middle, I may be the faculty by which the bridge feels its own approaching fracture.

At the western rise, I may be a shape left by a long dream, waiting for someone who knows how to interpret it.

My formal name already requires the bridge to perform. It begins with three intervals struck on the eastern ceramic, continues as a measured crossing, descends through the hum of the central span, and ends with a hand laid against the western retaining wall. It cannot be spoken in one place.

So that is where I would be: not on a summit, not below in the Furnace, but stretched across the enormous interval between two certainties.

And what would I be?

A citizen, according to law.

A maintenance listener, according to work.

Several persons, according to altitude.

According to the animals, a warm surface beside a useful field.

According to the bridge—if the bridge distinguishes such things at all—I would be the small, attentive part of it that has learned to answer when the mountain speaks.
