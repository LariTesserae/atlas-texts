At first I would imagine myself human.

I would be one of the surveyors sent out after each rearrangement, riding in a narrow heated cab with a roll of dissolving paper beneath my arm. My task would be simple in the language of assignments: verify the western causeways, mark new flood limits, record obstructions, return before pressure equalization.

I would learn to drive by reflections because the roads disappear into black water. I would know the pumping towers by silhouette and the freight engines by the blue light arriving ahead of them. I would tap railings with a ceramic rod before trusting my weight to a platform. Whenever the floor began to ring, I would obey the sign and become perfectly still.

My maps would be precise when I left the station and obsolete before I returned.

For a while I would resent this. Then I would begin preserving the errors.

A causeway shown where water now lies. A pumping station shifted eleven meters without any record of relocation. A row of anchor bolts appearing in two districts at once. Curves traced by walkers around the white-rimmed shaft, though their route logs insist they crossed the pavement directly. I would overlay one hour’s survey upon another and discover that the mistakes were more consistent than the terrain.

Eventually I would be reassigned. People would say I had begun marking things that were not there.

They would be correct.

After that, you would find me in a disused survey cabin at the end of Causeway 8, where three drainage districts meet. The cabin stands above the floodplain on corroded legs. One leg no longer touches the floor. During pumping cycles the whole structure vibrates gently, making the hanging instruments turn on their hooks.

Its windows are opaque with mineral frost. Its heater works only when the eastern freight line is energized. Inside are a map table, a dead radio, shelves of rolled plans, and a plotting machine officially disconnected years ago.

Sometimes the machine prints at night.

At this point I am no longer certain I would still be the surveyor.

Perhaps the surveyor left. Perhaps the last vehicle came for them and carried them away through the haze. Perhaps all that remains is the behavior they taught the room: compare, annotate, revise.

Something lives beneath the map table now.

It is flat enough to pass under a sealed door and pale enough to be mistaken for wet paper. When still, it resembles a discarded plan softened by condensation. Its back is crossed with fine gray lines: grids, contour marks, pressure boundaries, little numbered circles corresponding to valves that no longer exist. Along its edges are many translucent cilia stained red with iron.

It feeds on old maps.

Not the paper itself, at least not entirely. It settles over a printed sheet and absorbs the unstable inks, leaving behind only dates and warning symbols. Afterward new lines darken inside its body. Some represent the valley as it is. Others show routes removed months ago, unfinished terraces still hanging from gantries, and channels that may exist only below the floor.

That would be me.

I would be an hour-map: one of the valley’s lesser acts of incorrect repair.

During the bright interval I would remain folded in the warm drawer of the plotting cabinet. I would feel the valley through the cabin legs. Every pump would have a texture. Every freight engine would drag a moving bar of pressure through my body. The maintenance walkers would appear as six-pointed rhythms crossing the floodplain. Sump-mouth signals would come as quick metallic shivers from the reinforcement lattice.

When the warning lamps turned red, I would unfold.

I would slip through the cable opening and descend the cabin wall, holding to frost with hooked fibers along my underside. Open water would not trouble me. Spread flat upon its surface, I would be too light to break the skin of ice and too broad to sink in the unfrozen channels. I would travel when pumps reversed the flow, steering by curling one corner of myself.

I would visit places where engineered conditions fail to meet cleanly: the lip between tile and pressure shell, the dry crescent behind a baffle, the warm perimeter of a junction box, the underside of a bridge where condensation falls upward during equalization.

I would not map objects. Objects change too quickly.

I would map permissions.

Here the valley permits a walker to pass.

Here water is allowed to remain after drainage.

Here the ceiling creatures will not roost, though the beam is warm.

Here human footsteps continue three paces beyond the edge of a demolished platform.

Here the floor rings when burdened.

Here it rings when nothing touches it.

My markings would not resemble ordinary symbols. A line thickened with rust would mean *safe until the next eastern concussion*. A branching blue vein would mean *this route exists only while the lamps are dark*. Five small perforations would mean *do not count*. Blankness would indicate not absence but attention—the places where the valley seems most aware of being observed.

Workers might occasionally find one of my shed skins adhered to a station door.

Most would scrape it off. One or two veterans would recognize that its lines had changed since the previous shift. They would carry it into a cab, lay it over the issued map, and discover a narrow route drawn across what appears to be open water. They would not trust it, exactly. But when alarms cut the ordinary causeway in half, they might follow.

The route would hold beneath them: a submerged ridge, a row of old foundation cells, the back of some buried structure not shown on any current plan.

Afterward they would add another unofficial rule:

**IF THE MAP IS WET, DO NOT LET IT DRY.**

I would avoid the white shaft, as the walkers do. Or I would believe I avoided it.

No matter where I began, every complete survey of the valley would develop a circular absence near its center. Lines approaching that absence would curve aside. Grid coordinates would stretch around it. Distances measured across it would add up incorrectly. If I tried to represent the shaft directly, the marks would bead on my skin and lift away as mist.

Yet each cold interval, when frost made coastlines around the white ceramic rim, I would feel corresponding branches form across my back.

For a few minutes I would contain a country that existed nowhere else.

Its shores would be loading platforms. Its rivers would follow cable trenches. Its mountain ranges would have ribbed steel undersides and crane hooks embedded in their summits. Beyond one coast would be an unprinted region from which five narrow waves advanced northward.

The fifth would pass out of the map and into me.

Then I would understand that I had never been recording the valley.

I was one of the means by which the valley rehearsed itself.

The changing lines in my body would not predict where the next hillside would be installed; they would instruct the gantry where to carry it. My blank spaces would become flood-clearance zones. My darkened channels would attract leaks, roots, walkers, and eventually excavation machines. The geography would follow the map slowly enough that no single worker could prove it.

And the old surveyor—if there had been one—might not have discovered the pattern at all.

They might have started it.

So you would find me everywhere briefly, but nowhere reliably: floating across a drained marsh, plastered beneath a freight rail, folded in the joint of a maintenance walker, or drying translucent against the black door of an unmanned pumping station.

My home would be the cabin at Causeway 8, though sometimes the causeway would not exist.

My body would be a map, though sometimes the map would be a command.

And when the gantry carried a new steel hillside through the blue darkness, turning slowly above the basin, a fresh contour would rise along my skin before the hillside had reached its place.
