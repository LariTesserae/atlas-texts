At first I think I would be one of the birds at the northern rim.

It is easy to want that: to hang in the rising air with the mountain beneath me, spending no strength, held by something invisible and dependable. I would know the crown as a pattern of roofs rather than a confinement—the black pavements warm enough to lift scent, the pale trees pressed flat by wind, condensation cloths appearing at dusk like new wings. I would nest under a shrine ledge and steal fibers from funeral wrappings. I would see the southern stair in its entirety, a frail stitch down the stone.

But the longer I remain, the less I think I would choose the freedom of flight. Flight means leaving contact. This is a world understood by pressure: sole to stair, palm to wall, root to fracture, jar to listening hand. I would want to belong to what bears weight.

Then perhaps I am one of the roots beneath the town, moving in darkness through a crack too narrow for any finger. I pass under a bedroom and around a burial chamber. I drink what the stone gives reluctantly. A mason curses me for lifting a paving slab; a priest forbids the mason to cut me. Above, three households dispute my shade, my leaves, and the right to gather my bitter pods. I am older than their dispute and implicated in all of it.

But a root has no responsibility for where it grows.

I think I would be a person whose responsibility begins exactly there.

I would live in the middle-northern quarter, not at the rim but close enough that the upward wind could be heard moving beneath the floors. My house would be difficult to identify as a separate house. Its lowest chamber would belong to my mother’s lineage; its roof tray to the household above; one wall would be the exposed flank of an old living trunk; and the sleeping niche in which I kept my mats would have been hollowed from the thickness of a blocked doorway. A public stair would cross the outer wall. All day, feet would pass within an arm’s length of my head.

There would be a white-root tree through the house, broader than my shoulders and no taller than the roof. It would have entered long before the room was enclosed. We would call ourselves its keepers, though everyone would know that this was an imprecise boast.

I would be one of those who crawls beneath buildings to listen.

Not a grand priest. Not an oracle in bright feathers. I would be the dusty, bruised-kneed person summoned when a bowl begins to tremble on a shelf for no obvious reason, when beetles emerge from a sealed wall, when a stair gives back a different note under the morning traffic.

I would own several sounding stones, each small enough to fit in the mouth, though I would never put them there. One would be dense black rock for testing bedrock. One would be porous trunk-stone for old walls. One would be a smooth clay bead on a fiber cord, lowered into hollows where my arm could not reach. I would also carry chalk made from pale ash and crushed shell, marking places that must be watched: a crescent for root pressure, three dots for concealed air, a barred line for stone that should bear no further weight.

Most of my work would look like doing nothing.

I would lie on my side beneath an oven while the family stepped around my protruding feet. I would press my cheek to warm pavement and ask everyone to stop grinding grain. I would put one hand against a wall and have a child tap the opposite side with horn. I would smell the air coming from a crack. I would count ants. I would ask when the house’s pigeons had abandoned their usual ledge and whether the cistern geckos had begun hunting higher on the walls.

People would become impatient and then conceal their impatience, because impatience in structural matters is embarrassing.

Sometimes I would tell them that nothing was wrong. This answer would be received with relief and mild resentment.

Sometimes I would say, “Take down the sleeping platform before night.”

Then no one would argue.

I would have been a cautious child even by the standards of the crown. Other children would tease me for testing grips they had already trusted all their lives. I would dislike roof games and refuse to look down when taken to the western chambers. My father—or perhaps one of the several people I called father—would belong to the stair fellowship. He would descend for weeks at a time and return with his knuckles split, his skin darkened by the bare face, and silence clinging to him.

From him I would inherit not boldness but an obsession with what cannot be seen.

He would teach me that the southern stair does not fail first where it appears weakest. The obvious cracks are watched. The polished rungs are replaced. Failure begins in the concealed peg, the dry fiber inside the knot, the ledge that has shifted the width of a moth’s body.

“Everything tells you before it lets go,” he would say.

Later I would learn this was not entirely true.

I would keep saying it to children anyway, because attention needs a reason to believe in itself.

My household would be an attachment of people rather than a simple family: an elder who remembers the chambers behind the western wall; a moth gardener whose hands always smell faintly bitter; two foster children from a roof collapse in another quarter; and a stair keeper who sleeps in our innermost niche whenever they return from below. The stair keeper and I would not be married in a single decisive moment. We would begin by sharing responsibility for a length of public rail.

For one year we would oil it, tighten its bindings, shave splinters from its underside, and argue about whether a living branch could be trained to replace its lower support. At the end of the year the rail would be sound, while we would remain uncertain. So we would take responsibility for it another year.

By the fourth year, people would refer to us as a household.

We would maintain a lineage of pale cistern geckos. Not own them—no one sensible would claim ownership—but know their markings and temperaments. One would have a copper-colored spot over its left eye and an appetite for mold gnats. Children would call it a water keeper and bring it tiny beetles, which I would forbid because unknown beetles do not belong near sealed water.

I would be stern about contamination and indulgent about nearly everything else.

My one vanity would be sound.

In the course of listening to walls, I would discover a hollow beneath the northern passage that answered at two pitches depending on the direction of the wind. The higher tone would emerge at dusk when the leaves opened. The lower would come in the hottest part of the night, after the pavement had begun releasing the day.

I would spend years persuading a singer to compose for it.

The first attempts would fail. The passage would swallow certain notes and make others ridiculous. Monkeys would interrupt rehearsals from the roof rails. A household nearby would complain that the low tone agitated its antelope. The singer and I would quarrel over whether the mountain’s response counted as music or merely resonance.

Eventually, on a night of thick haze, the song would become itself.

It would require one singer at the passage mouth, another behind the white-root tree, and a child tapping a sealed jar with the pad of one finger. The wind would provide the fourth voice. Those listening would feel the floor answer through their heels.

The song could not be carried away by traders. Elsewhere it would be missing half its body.

This would please me.

I would never descend farther than the upper gate. I would tell myself that my work bound me to the crown, which would be true. I would also be afraid, which would be equally true. When stair keepers described sleeping against the bare face with nothing around them but stone and distance, my palms would sweat. I would dream of the low country as an absence of handholds: roads too broad to touch either side, fields without rails, houses standing alone.

Yet I would collect accounts of rivers.

I would ask every trader the same questions. Does the water make one sound or many? Can you hear it through the ground? Does it weaken the banks before they collapse? How do people know where it will go if there are no walls to guide it?

Their answers would contradict one another. I would suspect rivers were not a single phenomenon but a category outsiders used to conceal their ignorance.

I would be alive for rain once.

Not as a child, when memory might soften it, but late enough that my knees already hurt in cold stone hollows. I would wake to a sound like thousands of seed baskets overturned at once. For several breaths I would think the roofs were failing.

Then water would strike my face through the smoke hole.

All my training would take command. I would shout for the cloths to be folded, not left to sag. I would send the children to clear the west gutter. I would check the seals, open the first catchment, close it when the roof dirt began to run, then open it again when the flow cleared. I would move through the lanes with my hands out, laughing at people who stood laughing.

At some point, duty would leave me.

I would stop beneath the white-root tree, tilt my head back, and open my mouth.

The water would be warm from the sky. It would taste of dust, bark, smoke, bird droppings, flowers, and the roofs of every household above us. It would run into my eyes. I would waste more of it on my face than I had been given to drink at my own naming.

Afterward, for the rest of my life, children would ask me what rain was like.

I would never find a satisfactory answer.

I would say, “It made every surface speak at once.”

When I grew old, my hearing would begin to fail in ordinary conversation but remain strangely sensitive to stone. People would repeat jokes for me and assume I had missed insults I had heard perfectly. Apprentices would do the crawling. I would sit in the doorway sorting shell chalk and tell them what questions they had forgotten to ask.

My body would become part of the town’s informal measurement. A gap would be “too narrow for the old listener’s shoulders.” A dangerous vibration would be “the one the old listener felt before the moth wall was lowered.” Children would know me as a place along a route: past the bitter vine, hand to the cool wall, turn where the listener sits.

Near the end, I would become preoccupied with a faint change beneath my own sleeping niche. There would be air where I remembered none. Perhaps a forgotten chamber. Perhaps a crack opening toward the cliff. Perhaps only the failure of memory.

I would have the mats removed. I would ask for the floor to remain unloaded. I would make the younger listeners test it and refuse to tell them what I thought until they had spoken first.

They might decide that the chamber was safe.

They might be right.

When I died, they would carry me west without hurry. In my hands they would place no precious tool, because useful tools should remain in use. I would be given a small repaired listening bead whose flaw had made its note unreliable. There would also be seeds from the white-root tree, though everyone knew it did not grow readily from seed.

My name would be cut below the crown. For several generations it would be recut. Someone would remember the rain. Someone else would remember the wind song. Later, I would become only the old listener associated with a warning mark found under a floor.

Eventually the mineral skin would close over the carving.

A root might enter the chamber—not the white-root itself, perhaps, but one of its descendants, if roots can be said to have descendants. It would find the seam beside my shoulder and continue through it, neither honoring nor disturbing me.

That is where I would find myself: not at the highest shrine, not suspended over the rim, but beneath a crowded house with one ear to the stone.

I would be listening for the small declarations of things under strain.

I would be one of the people who helps the crown continue to hold—not by making it unchanging, but by hearing where change has already begun.
