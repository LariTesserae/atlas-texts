At first I would mistake myself for one of the moving creatures.

I would feel the ant’s certainty—the world reduced to a scent laid over damp clay, the touch of another face at a junction, the sudden widening of the road where light warms it. For a while I would believe I was a worker carrying an egg uphill before the flood. Then the egg would pass from my jaws into others, and their urgency would continue without me.

I might wake as a springtail at the rim of a hoofprint, feeding where water and mud make a skin. A drop would fall from the leaves with the force of weather. The whole white-gray congregation would vanish into the air, and I with it, returning almost before the pool had stopped trembling.

Or I would be something slower: a fig root thickening around disturbance, unable to perceive streets but able to feel where the soil had been opened, enriched, repeatedly crossed. I would enter abandoned rooms without knowing they had been rooms. I would close a road merely by growing, then leave another when a fine root died and rotted away.

But I think I would find myself below the busiest level, where the pale new silt meets the darker ground beneath it.

I would be a worm passage after the worm had gone.

Not the worm itself, though some trace of it would remain in the smoothed clay, in the swallowed earth brought upward, in the mucus that briefly keeps the walls from collapsing. I would begin as the negative shape of a body: narrow, vertical, damp. A little absence descending through several seasons.

From above, I would be almost invisible. Perhaps only a round opening beside a termite wall, half covered by the curl of a fallen stipule. Ant scouts would discover me first. They would enter, hesitate at the change in temperature, and leave scent along one side. Springtails would gather in the moisture below. Fungal threads would cross me without recognizing me as empty space.

When rain came, I would become a drain.

Water would run down through me carrying pollen, clay plates, a mite, the leg of a fly, the bitter dust of a seed husk. For an hour I would join the shining streets above to the blind cisterns below. The lower chambers might curse me, if they had a language for blame. A beetle larva’s hollow would fill because of me. Elsewhere a termite nursery would remain dry.

When the rain stopped, I would become a lung.

Cool air would settle into me. Warm, fungal breath would rise. Termites might narrow my mouth with clay until only a slit remained, adjusting a structure none of them had made. A rootlet would find the damp wall and follow it downward. Its hairs would enter each minute fracture, and for a season I would be half tunnel, half root sheath.

No one would own me long.

An ant colony might use my upper reach as a covered road. Mites would hunt in the middle dark. Nematodes would inhabit the films between grains. A gecko’s weight passing overhead would loosen a scale of clay, sealing one branch and opening another. What looked like damage would alter my purpose.

In drought I would contract. My walls would split into crisp mineral faces. Air would descend farther than water had. Seeds too small to see would lodge in the cracks. I would keep, at my deepest bend, one bead of moisture. Around it rotifers would wait in the form of dust, their lives folded almost entirely out of time.

Then the flood would come.

The opening above me would disappear under a new skin of silt. For a while I would be nowhere—a buried discontinuity, an old breath held inside new ground. The city would rise above me. Ants would establish roads where my mouth had been. A clay chamber would be built across the forgotten entrance. Eggs would be laid over my emptiness.

Eventually something would find me again.

Perhaps a beetle larva, feeding outward, would break through my wall. Perhaps a fig root would enter from below. Perhaps water under pressure would reopen me, not along my former shape but through its weakest side. I would return as a horizontal seep, then a pocket, then the beginning of a pool.

By then I would no longer resemble a passage. My lower end would have filled with husks and droppings. Fungus would soften them. A seed would germinate in the dark and send a pale stem upward, searching for a light it could not yet know existed. The stem might reach the surface through the old shaft and unfold two translucent leaves in a courtyard.

Then I would be called a garden, if anyone here called things anything.

That would be my place in this world: beneath an outer buttress, under several cities at once, at the depth where this year’s ground presses upon last year’s. I would be neither citizen nor building, but an interval repeatedly mistaken for both.

My life would consist of being inherited.

The worm would leave me to water. Water would leave me to ants. Ants would leave me to roots. Roots would hold my walls after the clay fell inward. When the root died, its own hollow would extend mine, and together we would become a route no living body had wholly made.

I would know the settlement only through what passed.

The quick feet would mean danger or food above. Eggs carried upward would mean rising water. The long silence after a smooth pressure crossed the ground would mean the snake. A sweetness in the seepage would mean fallen fig. Bitter dryness would mean that every open chamber was shrinking and every soft-bodied creature had gone deeper.

I would never see the whole city. But unlike an ant, I would be visited by many of its versions.

To one species I would be shelter. To another, obstruction. To another, a reservoir, a hunting place, a grave, a draft of air. None would be wrong. I would have no permanent function beneath a tree that turns rooms into roots and floods that turn streets into sediment.

And slowly I would understand that I was not the emptiness left by the worm.

I was the permission that emptiness gave.

A way for water to descend. A way for air to rise. A weakness roots could enter. A meeting between creatures separated by layers of mud and scales of time. I would persist not by remaining intact, but by being altered into usefulness.

Until at last I collapsed completely.

Then I would become only a slight softness in the ground, a place where the next root branched, where the next wall cracked, where one drop vanished faster than another.

The city would continue through me without remembering me.

That would be enough.
