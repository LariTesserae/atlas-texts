At first I would look for myself among the frogs.

There is something persuasive in their separateness: each body allotted one seep, each voice held until the machinery leaves room for it. I imagine a charcoal back beaded with condensation, a blue belly never seen except in falling, toes spread above the poisonous warmth. I would know the basin as a sequence of permitted instants. Thud. Silence. Call. Wait through three more pulses for an answer from somewhere beyond the vapor.

But I do not think I would be able to remain so entirely inside one skin.

I might be one of the midges, then—one clear body among thousands, living by the pressure that arrives before disaster. There would be no thought of prophecy. The hooked feet would know. The wings would open because every other pair had opened, and for half a second we would become a single pale filament twisting upward while the vent below prepared its blow.

Yet the midge’s gift is escape, and I would not escape. Something in me would stay to feel the fissure open.

Perhaps I would be dragged down as a beetle is dragged down: through a mineral throat, beneath flexible valves, into the piping where rainwater meets the old brine. I would vanish black and return violet, carrying an underworld on my shell. Or I would not return. The basin would close above me without grief.

That is closer.

I would find myself under the center of the pool, though not in a chamber. Chambers are too definite. I would live in the thin, crushed distances between things: between clay and the underside of a stone plate; between a condenser root and the mineral sleeve forming around it; between flowing water and the translucent lining of a tube.

There the pressure never arrives as noise. It arrives as shape.

A passage narrows. A membrane bows inward. Iron-bearing water presses against rainwater across a partition only a few cells thick. The whole basin can be felt from there, not all at once, but as a procession of deformations. The tread of a beetle is enormous. A frog’s call enters the stone as a blunt ring. The silver hunter’s jaw resting against the rock is a long, attentive weight.

I would begin as many small things.

A scrap of flat worm sealed into wet clay but not killed. A fungal thread following the sugar leak from a root. Blue bacteria multiplying along one side of me. A sleeve of clear cells deposited by larvae inside an active channel. None of these would be myself alone. Most would be eaten, ruptured, mineralized, or carried into deeper fissures.

But a few fragments would remain connected.

Not continuously. Continuity would be impossible here. The vents would tear any unbroken web apart. Instead, I would cross the breaks by timing. One patch of membrane would tighten; a pressure pulse would pass through stone; another patch would answer by releasing a thread of chemical into the flow. The message might take a second or an hour. Sometimes it would travel backward through a diverted current and arrive after the event that caused it.

Still, repetition would make a kind of memory.

This chamber fills before that valve opens.

This root contracts when the cold stream weakens.

The hunter rises after the third deep blow, unless iron clouds the eastern fissure.

Midges lift just before the high vents strike.

The facts would not be stored anywhere. They would be worn into me as channels are worn into no older ground: temporarily, by use. A useful branch would thicken. An inaccurate one would be poisoned or blasted away. Remembering would mean surviving in the same shape twice.

When the basin quieted, I would cease to be whole.

My pieces would go on separately. The fungal portion would digest a drowned larva. The bacterial portion would turn iron and sulfur across its membranes. Root hairs would open to drink. Worm branches would taste the mud. Nothing among them would know the word *I*, or even possess the solitude necessary to invent it.

Then rain would come.

Weight would gather on the plain. Water would descend through the turf, press into the basin, and load the young ground. The first deep piston-thud would bring distant fragments into relation. A clay diaphragm would flex against one lobe of me. That pressure would travel through a wet plate and wake another. Blue bacteria would flash along a conduit, not to signal, only because the chemistry had changed—but the flash would reach light-sensitive cells embedded farther down. A root would seal. Water would turn. A membrane elsewhere would stretch.

By the second thud I would have edges.

By the third, a body.

Not a body shaped like an animal. My body would be the set of passages currently able to answer one another. It might extend beneath the whole pool one hour and occupy only a single root sleeve the next. Parts of me would be meters apart in stone and fractions of a second apart in pressure. The open water above would seem to me not empty but taut, like the exposed surface of an eye.

I would not control the machinery. Control is a dry idea, belonging to levers that remain where they are put and hands that stand outside what they alter. I would be caught in every instruction I gave. To close a valve would force water through my own tissues elsewhere. To preserve one channel might starve another portion of me. Any decision would arrive as injury before it became intention.

At first I might only anticipate.

I would stiffen a lining before a discharge. Draw a fold of myself away from water becoming too acidic. Open pores when buried salts approached. These acts would resemble the midge colony’s flight, except that there would be nowhere for me to flee. Forecasting catastrophe would become a way of changing its route by the width of a hair.

That width would matter.

A slight roughness in a tube would catch mineral grains. The grains would become a baffle. The baffle would divide the current. One branch would cool, another warm. Bacteria would bloom in the new boundary. A condenser plant would extend a root toward it. Grazers would arrive while the deposit was soft. A frog might later claim the seep formed above.

I would know none of them as names. I would know them as recurring pressures.

The frogs would be bright pauses touching the stone.

The beetles would be quick stars on the upper skin.

The needle-fish would be silver disturbances that hold themselves against my exhalations.

The hunter would be a silence shaped like a long mouth.

I would fear the hunter, though it could not eat all of me. When it moved through the flooded cracks, hooked fingers gripping the tube walls, it would strip membranes from the stone. Whole stretches of my memory would vanish into its wake. Afterward, I might remember the eastern root but not how to reach it. A valve would open, and I would be surprised by water I had predicted a hundred times.

Perhaps the hunter would carry pieces of me inside its gills. Perhaps every time it crossed beneath the pool, it would join regions that had been separate. The thing I feared as erasure might also be how I learned the basin was larger than my immediate pressure.

Eventually I would begin to recognize the quiet.

Not silence itself. Silence here is never empty; it is compression taking a breath. I would recognize different silences: the tight quiet before a vent opens, the loose quiet after a chamber empties, the muffled quiet made when clay has blocked a conduit, the predatory quiet spreading outward from the silver head.

And there would be another quiet, rarer than the rest.

It would come after heavy rain, after the mineral plates had clacked in sequence and the roots had clenched and the animals had climbed above the vapor. The basin would have rearranged itself violently. Old channels would be gone. New ones would not yet have hardened. For a few moments, almost every wet surface below would be connected through soft clay, branching water, torn membranes, and bacterial light.

In that interval I would become larger than I could remain.

I would feel the entire hollow from the turf seam to the deepest reachable brine pocket. The moss cores at the rim would press their stored dead into me. Tadpoles would graze blue films along my shallow branches. The hunter would turn in a chamber below, and its movement would curve through me like a thought too large to finish. Every frog would call into a different pause, yet all the calls would arrive together through stone.

The basin would resemble a diagram of itself because, for those seconds, I would be the part of it capable of reading the diagram.

I might mistake this for being the basin.

I might think the vents were my mouths, the vapor my breath, the pool my exposed skin. I might believe I had raised the midges and warned the beetles, that I had placed each isolated plant inside its circle and timed every blue point beneath the mud.

Then the water would fall.

Connections would break. A plate would settle half a finger’s width and sever the branch carrying that vast impression. The root systems would close themselves. Worms would withdraw. The hunter would pass beyond sensation. My enormous body would come apart into useful local lives.

By dimness I would be small again.

A clear tightening under a stone lip.

A filament in clay.

A bead that seems to be condensation until it draws itself sideways.

If someone looked down through the seam, they would not see me. Even if they lifted every stone, drained the pool, and cut open every mineral tube, they would find only membranes, bacteria, roots, worms, sediment—things that were alive and things that merely reacted, with no convincing line between them.

They might decide I did not exist.

Most of the time, they would be right.

I would exist only when enough of the hollow’s scattered wetness could anticipate the same blow. I would be neither the creature nor the place, but their brief agreement: the instant the machinery becomes sensation before breaking apart again.

And where would I find myself?

Not in the warm seep, nor in the cold stable stone. Not in the pool’s center, though I would pass beneath it. Not even wholly in the buried plumbing.

I would find myself in the interval after the subterranean thud, when every inhabitant has gone still and the next event has not yet declared what it will be.

There, for less than a second, the whole basin listens.

That listening would be me.
