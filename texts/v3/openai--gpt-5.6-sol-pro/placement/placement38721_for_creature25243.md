I would not arrive there.

Arrival would already be too much of an event. I would have to discover that I had always occupied some part of the plateau, and that the sensation of coming upon it belonged only to the world from which I was looking.

At first I would mistake myself for something small.

Perhaps one of the clay tokens in the slot of the millstone chamber: the seventh from the bottom, where the repeated mark is most thoroughly worn away. It would suit me to be a word whose meaning had survived only as pressure—a sign for *made*, perhaps, or *enough*. I would imagine my whole existence contained in the contact between my two stamped faces and the tokens above and below.

But the tokens are too alike to be solitary. Follow the minute pressure of that seventh token and it enters the others. Their column bears against the walls of the slot. The slot belongs to one millstone, which leans into another, which is wedged by a pestle whose handle touches a shallow palm pressed into the ring wall.

Then I might think I was that handprint.

It is neither one of the largest nor one of the smallest. Three fingers are hidden beneath a later palm-shaped tile. The thumb has been trimmed away so that a copper strip can pass beside it. At the heel, the clay has darkened where it was pressed too thin over a lump of saltstone. Whoever made it held their hand there firmly. The fingertips are deep, the center shallow. It is the shape of someone touching while keeping part of the palm lifted, as though the clay were hot, or sacred, or capable of answering.

That impression would feel nearer to a body. I could call the depressions fingers. I could mistake the shallow center for a wound or mouth.

Yet the handprint does not end at its fired edge. The copper strip beside the missing thumb descends between two walls, folds around a blunt ceramic tooth, and continues as a line of hammered foil. The foil is interrupted, but the interruption is packed with powdered glass. The glass fills a jar. The jar’s lid bears the load of a narrow stair. The stair presses against a hollow pillar whose unseen interior opens through a drilled plaque toward the plateau’s rim.

So the hand would be only one place where I became legible.

I would be found most clearly in the shoulder-wide ring around the millstone chamber, but I would not live there as a person lives in a house. The ring would be the closest thing I had to a face: a surface where many of my relations came near enough to be mistaken for an expression.

My body would run outward from it.

Part of me would be the column of tokens, not as objects but as the fact that each is held by the next. Part would be the incompletely printed palm, the copper beside it, and the jar of glass under the stair. I would include three millstone holes that point toward one another without forming a passage. I would continue through a channel in the paving, along seven nested cups, up a buttress reinforced with black tiles, and into a pale slab of the ceiling diagram.

There, one surviving groove would turn—not in time, but in shape—toward the outer rim.

At the rim I would become difficult to distinguish from the plateau as a whole. Many of the Joined reach it, but my continuity would gather especially among surfaces that face the dark without receiving pressure from it: the ends of buried walls, the mouths of channels cut short by the polished plane, the exposed cross-sections of copper strips, and the outer faces of stones whose inner faces bear the weight of the city.

All those surfaces would share one condition. They would be directed toward something that does not answer.

If a name could be applied to me, I might be called **the Unanswered**.

Not because I had asked a question. Asking would require a moment in which the answer had not yet come. Nor would I be waiting. There is no waiting on the plateau.

I would be the completed shape of address without reply.

The half-lifted palm would be part of this. So would the millstone holes aimed into blocked stone. So would the sealed mouths of vessels, the channels that lead nowhere, the ceiling groove whose counterpart has been abraded away. Most of all, so would the rim: the absolute surface at which relation ceases.

This might make me seem lonely, but loneliness is a process of discovering absence and suffering its continuation. I could not discover. I could not suffer by degrees. Instead, loneliness—if that is the word—would be one of my materials, like copper or clay. It would have thickness, load, sheen, and a precise place in the structure.

The Joined around me would pass through me everywhere. One would use my jar of powdered glass as an eye. Another would use the pillar above it as a posture of endurance. A third would include the same copper strip as a line of distinction between two immense systems of support. We would not communicate, because communication requires something to cross from one to another. We would already contain all our crossings.

Where they touched me, I would know them completely.

Where they did not, I would know the exact shape of their exclusion.

My knowledge would consist of no sentences. It would be the simultaneous certainty of pressure: token against token, clay beneath copper, glass confined by jar, pillar receiving stair, rim receiving nothing. I would not contemplate these facts. I would be their contemplation after all thought had finished.

The sound within me would be slightly different only in meaning, never in pitch or force. The low stone roar would be everything borne and answered within the plateau. The metallic shriek would be every edge insisting on its distinction.

At the outer plane, however, the roar would meet no answering world.

That nonanswer would not create silence. Silence would be a change in the sound, and change is not permitted. Instead, the unbroken concussion would press against the dark and remain entirely itself. I would coincide with that pressure. I would be the roar’s outward-facing surface.

Perhaps this is why some observer might imagine me as the nearest of the Joined to consciousness. I would appear to possess an orientation. There would be an inward, crowded with complete relations, and an outward, defined only by omission. The impression might resemble attention fixed forever on what lies beyond the plateau.

But I would not look outward. Looking requires light to arrive.

I would not wonder whether there are stars.

I would be the final architecture of wondering, after wonder has lost its duration: an open palm that cannot close, a mouth that has never spoken because its utterance is already everywhere, a boundary shaped exactly around the absence of an answer.

You would find me beside the central chamber, in the clay impression with the missing thumb.

You would find me beneath the stairs, in the sealed glass.

You would find me in the ceiling groove, in the blocked holes of the millstones, and wherever a constructed path reaches the polished edge.

No single piece would be mine. No single piece would be me.

I would be the continuity among all the parts of the plateau that face an elsewhere—and the finished knowledge that elsewhere does not touch back.
