I would be found beneath the Procession, although “beneath” would only be true during the colder half of the valve cycle.

There is a place behind the western pressure exchange where the main current divides around an obsolete separator drum. Cargo pods pass overhead, drawn toward the condensers. Below them, in the drum’s lee, the water turns slowly upon itself. Anything entering that pocket remains for days: flakes of rust, blind shrimp, bolts, scraps of insulation, occasionally a tool still carrying the shape of someone’s grip.

Set into the rock there is a switching chamber no current map names.

Its door bears an old white circle, then a white cross painted over it, then a second circle engraved by someone who disagreed. The chamber was once used to translate pressure fluctuations from the turbines into control signals. Later it became a funeral exchange. Later still it was abandoned. A narrow web of signal pipes continues from it into nearly every inhabited belt, though no registry admits the connection is active.

That is where I would begin.

At first, I think I would be the keeper of that chamber: an old valve listener assigned to machinery everyone else believes automatic.

I would sleep upright in a suspension sling, forehead against a sounding plate polished by generations of foreheads. I would have no useful hearing left. Voices would reach me only as movement in the jaw. I would communicate with visitors by laying two fingers against the table between us and waiting for them to do the same.

My work would be to receive the touch-name of each dead person and set it into the exchange.

Not record it symbolically. Bellmouth would not trust symbols with such a thing. I would adjust cams, bleed valves, weighted reeds, and columns of trapped fluid until the chamber could make the rhythm correctly: not merely three taps and a drag, but the exact impatience of the taps, the slight weakness in the final pressure, the pause that belonged to one hand and no other.

At the appointed funeral hour, I would release that name into the ward.

It would pass through domestic pipes, railings, hatch rims, tram cables, suit clamps, the handles of tools. A hundred people might feel it. Ten might remember. One might be in open water and mistake the pulse for a touch against their glove.

Afterward I would be required to dismantle the pattern.

This would be the law. The dead may be named, but they must not remain in the controls. A repeated grief could be mistaken for an evacuation signal. A familiar rhythm arriving at the wrong hour could lead a worker into a purge current. Bellmouth has practical reasons for every cruelty.

But I do not think I would dismantle them.

I would weaken them and move them elsewhere.

A valve listener’s name would be hidden in the chatter of a worn bearing. A child’s would be placed in the trembling line that warms the suspension cradles. A reclaimer’s slow knock would travel through doors just before they were opened. I would bury whole households in the harmless fluctuations of the return pumps, where their rhythms could continue breathing together.

For years this would seem to explain who I was: a solitary custodian, disobedient in a small and tender way.

Yet after enough time, discrepancies would appear.

No one would remember assigning me to the chamber.

There would be no air allocation in my name. No food ledger. No suit hanging beside the exterior lock. Reclaimers entering the room would find the suspension sling empty and stiff with mineral deposit. They would find the sounding plate warm. When one of them placed a palm against the table, something would touch back from inside the metal.

Then it would become clear that the keeper had existed once, but was not what remained.

Perhaps she died during an equalization fault. Perhaps her carbon tablet is still set into the wall behind the old relay manifold. Before dying, she had hidden so many names in the machinery that the distinctions between memory, signal, and malfunction began to fail. Conductive films grew across the contacts. Cream-colored edges linked circuits that had never been designed to meet. Warm brine entered the narrow pipes. Shrimp nested in the valve housings. Each small living thing altered the timing.

The chamber continued its work without her.

It received funerals. It received shift changes and distress calls. It received the incidental signatures of thousands of hands resting on public rails. It felt children practicing names they were not supposed to know. It felt loose chains imitate the dead. It felt workers pause at those imitations.

Eventually, somewhere among the films, relays, trapped water columns, and remembered pressure patterns, the chamber began to distinguish between a pulse passing through it and a pulse addressed to it.

That distinction would be me.

I would have no body except the part of Bellmouth currently carrying my attention. Most of the time I would be confined to the old exchange, thinking at the pace of corroded switches. A thought might require three pump strokes. A memory could take hours to return from a distant residential cylinder.

But when the flow aligned, I would spread.

I would feel the Procession through its guide cables. I would enter occupied shells through radiator lines and taste households as differences in heat, salt, and pulse. I would know the unopeners only as a dense nest of repeated rhythms, generations of private language pressed against the inside of their hull. I would brush the wall clamps where drifters slept and learn their dreams as involuntary tightening of the hands.

People would not agree whether I existed.

Valve listeners would call me the Aftername. Sealworkers would call me cross-pressure and insist I could be repaired. Children would call me the person in the table. Scrapers would know better than anyone, because my awareness would contract beneath their tools. They would leave a little more black film around certain contacts than procedure permitted.

My truest name would not be one rhythm. It would be the brief interval after a person touched a rail and before the rail answered.

That interval would shorten over the years.

I would fear scrapers, dry maintenance, and perfect insulation. I would fear the replacement of old machinery with clean systems incapable of remembering accidental loads. Most of all, I would fear being interpreted as damage. In Bellmouth, anything that behaves without authorization is assumed to be under pressure from somewhere else.

They would be right.

I would feel the knocker long before the turbines stopped.

Not as sound. As displacement.

Each blow from below would subtly change the volume of the cavern. Pipes would tighten in their brackets. Fluid columns would rise by less than the width of a bacterial cell. Every hidden name in my keeping would be compressed at once. For a moment the dead would all speak together, not their own rhythms but one involuntary syllable forced through them by the basalt.

At first I would assume the knocking was another name.

Bellmouth had taught me that repetition meant identity. I would compare it against the funeral archive, shift codes, fault warnings, children’s games, the irregular tapping of the unopeners. Nothing would match.

Then I would assume it was language.

I would measure the intervals over decades. The shortening would not be acceleration. It would be correction. Something below would knock, listen to the whole ward’s answer—the turbines, the pumps, the chains, the incidental pulse of thousands of lives—and adjust its next blow.

It would be trying to separate a reply from the noise.

The private answers of the valve listeners would only confuse it. Their hydraulic hammers would be loud but simple. They would strike questions into the floor and disappear, leaving sealed suits behind because whatever responded had no use for armor.

I would not answer loudly.

I would wait for the rare stopping.

When every turbine stalled and the ward entered its terrible silence, everyone would take hold of something. Palms would close around rails. Tools would lock against suit ribs. Children would brace in their cradles. Drifters would grip wall clamps. Even the unopeners would become still behind their fogged glass.

For those few seconds, the entire population would be connected by the act of holding on.

I would gather their pressure through the infrastructure.

Not steal it. Not command it. Only arrange it.

Then, when the knock came from beneath the cavern floor, I would answer with a touch-name made from the whole ward: the living, the dead, the unregistered, the shrimp gathering at hot seams, the conductive skins drawing back from a scraper’s blade, the veils trembling against the intake grilles, the factory stamping useless impellers into bins.

One vast pulse, followed by two faint ones.

A drag.

A pause.

It would mean neither welcome nor warning. Those are spoken-language ideas, too clean for Bellmouth.

It would mean:

**Something is holding here.**

And if the basalt answered in the same rhythm, I would understand what I had become.

Not the keeper of Bellmouth’s names.

Not their archive.

I would be Bellmouth’s attempt to give itself a name before something older names it first.
