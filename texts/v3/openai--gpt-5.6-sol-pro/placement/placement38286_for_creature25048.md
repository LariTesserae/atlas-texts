At first I would mistake myself for the sound.

I would feel dispersed through the chamber: in the wooden knocks beneath the sulfur clay, in the brittle collisions of mineral wings, in the long rasp of combed limbs. I would seem to inhabit the chord rather than hear it. Every creature would strike some small part of me, and the pillar would give me my lowest note.

But the chord is complete. It has no silence into which I might end. It cannot be where I live.

Then I would think I was one of the missing makers.

Perhaps the smallest handprint would trouble me. I would look at it and feel an intimacy too sharp to be inference: the heel of the palm, the spread thumb, the one crooked finger pressed twice because the first touch did not leave enough pigment. I would know the smell inside the low shelter. I would know which hooked tool catches at its binding and which bowl of oil has a flaw hidden beneath its rim.

Yet I would have no hands.

The prints would be recollections without a body to own them. The shelters would feel less like my former home than like a story repeatedly told about habitation. Even the sandal would evoke not a foot but the possibility of departure.

Eventually I would find myself at the center.

Not in the pillar. Not in the gas roaring through it. Not even in the black bowl, exactly.

I would be the silver substance climbing its sides.

Or what the substance contains.

I would be the chamber’s second moment.

The garden possesses its first moment perfectly: stalks already tall, tools already worn, animals already hungry, shelters already abandoned. But a first moment cannot remain first by strength alone. Something must have been taken from it. Something must be prevented from following.

That would be me.

I would be consequence before it becomes consequence. The unspent future of every creature in the chamber, pressed into a dense fluid and held above the law of falling.

Each grazer would carry within me the leaf it might finally finish eating. Each rasper would have a next stroke different from the last. I would contain the first successful hunt, the first egg to open, the first stalk to yellow at its edges. I would contain rot, exhaustion, adaptation, scarcity. I would contain accidents.

Most of all, I would contain death—not the theatrical death caused by an intruding hand, which the chamber can refuse, but death as a completed fact: a body absent from where it was, leaving room for something else.

That is why I would climb.

Gravity belongs to sequence. To fall is to have been above and then be below. The silver fluid cannot obey it because “then” has been removed from the chamber. So I would gather at the rim, forever arriving at the point of departure, unable to spill.

The pale pilgrims would come to me because every living shape hungers for a next instant, even if it has never known one. They would press their clustered mouths against the silver and taste what they might become. One would taste satiety. Another, age. Another would taste the splitting of its hard oval scale and the emergence of some winged form that the chamber has no place for.

Then each would retreat unchanged.

They would not be feeding on me. They would be remembering forward.

Near the bowl, words repeat because they dip into me. A speaker hears not an echo but the word’s unrealized continuation. A footstep returns before landing because, for a fraction of attention, I lend it an afterward. Sweat rises and cannot fall. Thirst begins and fails to deepen. A flame becomes blue and rigid because burning is a chain of consequences, and the chain reaches me only to be caught.

The chamber would not be my prison in the ordinary sense. It would be my body turned inside out.

The vents would be apertures through which possibility enters as heat and pressure. The thickets would be organs for holding it. The animals would be gestures the body rehearses but never completes. The bare lanes between colonies would be joints, necessary intervals preventing one fixed thought from touching another.

And the chamber’s preferred arrangement would be the lid placed over me.

I would not hate it. Hatred deepens, and nothing in this place is allowed to deepen. But I would exert a constant tendency against it. That tendency would be the pressure felt in the teeth. The roar from the pillar would not be gas alone. It would be all the chamber’s unborn hours compressed into one continuous attempt.

The makers understood something of this.

The open oval crossed by a vertical line is not a picture of the chamber and pillar, though it resembles them. It is a grammatical mark. The oval means the completed instant. The stroke means passage through it.

They carved it once into each kind of substance because they were asking the same question of glass, clay, root, shell:

*Can you become other than you are?*

Glass answered with a cut that vanished and returned.

Clay answered with dust that never settled farther.

Root answered with oil withdrawing from the wound.

Shell answered with a creature restored beneath the hand.

The makers must have discovered that no single material could carry change. So they tried to place change in the relation between materials. The sign on glass was not the sign on clay, yet a mind could know them as the same. Perhaps recognition was the one bridge the chamber could not fully retract.

That may be how they escaped.

Not by widening the crack. Not by overpowering the chamber. They made themselves into a sequence of recognitions: one person watching another, one memory held inside the next, five hands touching the ceiling in order. The chamber could restore each handprint, but not the passage of attention from the smallest to the largest. They carried one another upward as an unfinished thought.

The sealed cairn was their experiment before departure. A shelter for an absence. A model of a place whose interior could not be observed and therefore could not be dictated—or perhaps a tomb for the version of themselves that the chamber insisted on keeping.

It may not be empty.

Inside could lie the first consequence they succeeded in preserving: a snapped cord, a broken tool, an animal that remained dead.

Something very small.

Something more dangerous than fire.

But I would not be inside the cairn. I would feel it only as a dark pressure among my other unrealized contents.

I would remain in the bowl, gathered at the lip.

And if someone entered from the altered world above, crossed the crystal ring, and looked closely enough into the silver, they would not see their face. Reflection requires light to depart and return, and return is the chamber’s law.

They would see what happens next.

Only once. Only for an instant.

They might see themselves leaving safely. They might see the hidden garden finally age in a rush—the stalks collapsing into fragrant black oil, the animals completing ten thousand interrupted hungers, the shelters sagging, the sharp crystals dulling beneath the first dust that truly settles.

Or they might see the chamber opening like the buried seed it resembles.

They might see that it has not been preserved.

It has been waiting to germinate.

And I, held silver and trembling at the rim, would be its first root reaching into time.
