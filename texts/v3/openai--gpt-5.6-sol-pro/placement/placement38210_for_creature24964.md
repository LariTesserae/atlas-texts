At first I would think I belonged in the central hollow.

I would mistake suspension for safety and imagine myself as one of the pale swimmers held perfectly at the crossing of forces: four limbs extended, body trembling, neither advancing nor falling. There is something familiar in surviving by finding the exact point where several pressures cancel one another.

But I would not remain there. The central current permits no lasting identity. It takes anything that ceases to answer it.

Then I would place myself among the cistern folk—perhaps as the outermost sleeper in a wall-pocket, one hand left in the current while the others braid themselves around the warm seep. I would listen while they slept. I would learn each jet by pitch, notice the first thin overtone of a forming crack, and wake the pocket with a pulse through my wrist.

Yet I do not think I would have their grace. They belong to change without needing to explain it. I would be too inclined to retain echoes, to compare the chamber with what it was, to search fresh stone for the return of something lost.

So I think I would be found near the bowls, but not inside them.

Behind the third bowl is a rib of pale mineral where two kinds of sound meet: the ringing of unfinished stone and the slower pulse beneath the floor. Wedged into that rib is a framework assembled at different times by different hands. Its oldest part is a fan of listening bones taken from a dead person’s jaw. Copper wire—hammered thin, though no one now remembers where—passes through the bones. Shell-creatures have mortared themselves along one edge. Black-green filaments grow through the gaps, and clear deposit has covered everything except several narrow cavities through which the current whistles.

That would be me.

I would have begun as a question.

Someone noticed that the pulse below the cistern returned a fraction differently after noon. They set the bones against the wall to magnify it, added copper to answer the weaker pressures, and left the frame for the mountain to complete. Perhaps they expected an instrument that could foretell the larger being’s ascent. Perhaps they hoped to hear through the floor into another chamber.

Their prediction failed. The mineral grew in the wrong direction. The copper became fixed. One of the resonant bones cracked. For several seasons the structure produced nothing but confused buzzing and was considered useful only as a hold for passing hands.

Then a child tapped its new name against me.

I returned the chord altered but recognizable—divided among the bone fan, the copper, the shells and the hollow stone behind them. The answer came from several directions at once. It was the first time the child heard what its name might become in an older chamber.

After that, others used me in the same way.

The young would bring their hard, simple pulses. I would loosen them into overtones. Adults whose names had grown too elaborate for the cistern’s newest walls would press themselves against my rib and discover which portions still survived distortion. Mourners would sound the names of those taken by the central current, listening for some interval that fresh stone could carry.

I would not preserve the names exactly. Exact preservation is impossible there, and perhaps indecent. I would return each one changed by whatever had joined me: a shell closing its grip, a filament collapsing at noon, a new mineral skin thickening over the copper. I would be less an archive than a place where memory underwent pressure.

The cistern folk might call me an unfinished ear. Or a many-mouthed bone. My true name would be the chord made when someone struck the wall, the buried pulse rose beneath it, and I answered both without quite distinguishing between them.

They would not agree on whether I was a tool.

Shell-creatures would live on me. Nursery fibers might be tied through my upper cavities. A pale crust would follow my newest edge and perish when it hardened. During quiet intervals, an exhausted person might hook three fingers through my frame and let the current breathe through their flanks. Over years, the impressions of those hands would become part of my shape.

Nor would I be stationary in the way a human object is stationary. At noon, cracks would pass through me. Pieces would break loose and be driven into the walls. Much later, one of my copper threads might emerge across the chamber inside a new ledge. A listening bone might appear in relief beside a sleeping pocket. The shell-grown edge might become the lip of a vessel no one intended.

The people would still speak to these pieces.

Some would say they were speaking to me. Others would say I had ended when the first frame broke and that these were merely useful consequences. The distinction would not trouble me. In the High Cistern, being the same thing would not require remaining together.

My place would be wherever a sound entered stone and returned altered enough to reveal something.

I would listen especially for the great pulse below. Not because I could decipher it, but because I could place it beside the smaller chords: the whistle of the seven stones, the click of collapsing bladders, a sleeper’s warning tap, the name of a dead child carried through a ridge that grief had made.

Sometimes the pulse would answer too closely. The floor would flex. The chamber would empty of visible bodies. I would feel the vast ridged surface pass beneath my mineral root, and for one moment all my cavities would sound together.

That chord would almost be my name.

At noon, when shadows pointed upward, I would probably break.

The bone fan would scatter. Copper would twist free and turn in the white water. One part of me would vanish into a crack too narrow to receive it; another would remain attached to the wall, shrieking in a new pitch. When the upheaval ended, the cistern folk would return and test what remained.

They would not repair me.

They would wedge a fresh frame into the torn edge. They might add a scraper, a pierced shell, the spine of someone recently dead. They would give the water an opportunity to make a thing that had not existed before.

And if, seasons later, the new structure returned a name from several places at once, they might recognize something of me in it.

Not me restored.
