I would not find myself at the summit seam. That brightness is too absolute, reducing every body that crosses it to a momentary silhouette. Nor would I live in the bottom basin, where the mountain sometimes opens one wet eye and the blind fish rise through it.

I would be somewhere between those thresholds, in the crowded middle where the Cleft is least like a place and most like a living pressure.

At first I might seem to be one of the pale crabs. I can imagine knowing the world through my feet: the faint tack of bacterial film, the dangerous smoothness of drying stone, the tremor of water approaching before the basin fills. I would carry a particular basalt flake from cup to cup, not understanding that I had chosen it for its weight and edge. During floods I would climb until there was nowhere left to grip, then stand on the shells of others while others stood on mine. My possession would be temporary. Eventually the stone would slip away and become mountain again.

But the crabs belong to what arrives from below, and I think I would belong to what returns from elsewhere.

So I rise.

Not to the swift ribs, though I admire their severe lives: one egg fastened above emptiness, one small circumference defended against the multitude. I would need more tolerance for contact than that. I would need the press of familiar bodies around me, the smell by which one life can be found among thousands.

I would be an old broad-eared bat.

A female, probably, though age would have made the distinction less visible than the accumulated damage. One ear would end in a healed crescent where an owl’s claw—or another bat’s teeth—had taken it. My left wing would bear a long translucent seam. When spread against the dimness, it would show as a pale branching line, stronger in some ways than the membrane around it and less flexible in others. In cold air it would ache.

My place would not quite be a place. It would be a sequence.

From the upper opening: drop past the shelf where the swifts launch; bank at the limestone tooth; close the wings through the two basalt pillars; fall seven body lengths; catch the air rising from the eastern wall; turn toward the smell of the maternity press. There, behind a hanging mineral fold, would be a shallow pocket warmed from beneath.

I would return to it often enough for others to associate me with it, but I would not own it. Some mornings it would be filled. Some years condensation would make it intolerable. Once, perhaps, a snake would discover the approach and the entire cluster would abandon it without discussion. Home would survive not in the pocket but in my ability to reach another.

I would know the Cleft as delays in sound.

Not walls, ledges, pillars, or chambers: nearnesses. A hard rib returning my cry sharply. A moth-softened surface swallowing part of it. The blurred answer of fur. The open-throated echo above a drop. I would know when the wet season altered a familiar gallery because mineral skins would thicken and the old echo would come back changed, as if spoken by a swollen mouth.

The outside world would be stranger, though larger. Each evening I would leave with the colony and descend toward the warm valleys. The air would thicken around me. Smells would separate: flowering trees, rodents, dust, standing water, distant blood. There would be open distances in which a call could travel without immediately striking stone. Even after many years, that openness would feel less like freedom than exposure.

I would feed low over scrub and among trees, taking beetles and moths, sometimes returning with pollen powdered across my muzzle. On rare nights I would drink from an animal opened by another predator, lapping quickly before danger returned. I would carry all of it upward without knowing I was carrying a valley: insect salts in my blood, seeds in my fur, sweetness in my gut.

By dawn I would be heavy with distance.

Young bats might follow me through the narrow approaches, not because I taught them deliberately, but because age would have made my movements economical. I would hesitate before a passage that had begun to close. I would choose the lower turn during dry years. I would know which landing shelf held snakes and which only smelled of them because one had fed there long ago. My knowledge would look like instinct to anyone watching. It would be neither instinct nor thought exactly. It would be survival worn smooth by repetition.

Sometimes I would descend to the carcass shelves and select a quill.

I would test several with tongue and thumb-claw, rejecting those too brittle or curved. Holding the chosen one would make my flight clumsy, so I would crawl sideways to a grub-hole and work by touch: insert, turn, withdraw, taste. The act would require an attention unlike hunting. The larva could not be found by echo. It would have to be persuaded out of stone.

When I succeeded, I would eat and let the quill fall.

Nothing I did would remain arranged. No pile of useful objects would mark my intelligence. No offspring would inherit the tool except by seeing another living bat use one. If I were the last to know, the knowledge might vanish for generations, then arise again in some patient claw as though the mountain itself had remembered.

I would have borne young. More than one, not all surviving.

I would recognize each pup first as a voice inside noise, then as a smell inside musk and guano. I would return from the valley to a curtain of thousands and crawl through biting strangers until one particular body fastened itself to me. Later, when that youngster could fly, recognition would loosen but not disappear. Years afterward I might tolerate a weakened adult beside me without knowing why its nearness felt permissible.

One of my young would be taken by a snake at the colony’s edge. Another would fail to return from the valley. There would be no grave, no long vigil, no emptiness left around their absence. Other bodies would close the space at once. Loss in the Cleft would not create silence. It would alter a pattern too subtly for anyone but the living to hear.

In my last season, I would fly less far.

My damaged wing would tire on the climb. The younger animals would strike me in the narrow passages because I no longer matched their speed. Parasites would gather where I could not groom. I would seek the deepest warmth, edging into the press of familiar bodies. They would not nurse me, but they might allow me to remain.

Perhaps I would die there, still hanging.

For a while my claws would hold after the rest of me had stopped. Then I would fall—not far, perhaps only onto a shelf below. Beetles would find me. Mites would leave and return for other reasons. My membranes would dry, my fur loosen, my bones enter the dark terrace forming beneath the colony. Nothing would preserve my shape.

Yet something of me would continue without becoming a ghost.

A younger bat would take the lower turn in a dry year because she had followed me once. Another would probe a grub-hole with a quill. Pollen from my fur would feed a larva in a temporary pool. The salts of the valley carried in my body would pass into beetle, fungus, salamander, perhaps crab. The pocket where I had roosted would close under mineral film, while another opened a little farther down.

So that is where I would find myself: midway between buried water and fierce daylight, behind no permanent door, in a route rather than a room.

And that is what I would be: not the ruler of the Warm Cleft, nor its witness, but one old, scarred vessel by which the lowlands entered the mountain—briefly furred, briefly winged, and remembered only as a turn taken correctly in the dark.
