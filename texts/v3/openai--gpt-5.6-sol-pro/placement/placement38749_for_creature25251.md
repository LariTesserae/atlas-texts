### The Arrears Clerk

At first I think I would be one of the inspection units.

There is comfort in that possibility: an amber lamp, a ceramic shell, a purpose narrow enough to survive. I would travel with others along the underside of a catwalk, testing rivets with a small metal tongue. I would know the town as a sequence of tolerances. I would not mourn a collapsed tower. I would measure its new angle and amend the route.

When my lamp failed, the others would carry me home.

But that is too complete an existence. The units belong wholly to the present operation of the Works. Whatever I am, I suspect I would retain some obsolete distinction—some useless habit inherited from before the flooding.

Nor would I be a glass worm, though I understand the appeal of living inside a single valve and interpreting the universe as warning, violence, warmth, and stillness. A worm possesses no unnecessary history. It does not wonder what the valve once regulated.

I would wonder.

So you would find me in the Records Annex.

The Annex is not marked on any surviving plan. It stands behind the eastern side of the Compression Hall, although “behind” changes according to the Hall’s cycle. At times it can be reached through a tiled corridor beneath the old assay rooms. At others, the corridor terminates in the curved flank of a reservoir, and the only route is through a pipe trench, up a stair that now descends, and across an office ceiling reinforced by the mineral casts of filing cabinets.

Blue lamps mark the way until the final turn.

After that, there is no light.

The door to the Annex is open, but silt hangs motionless across the doorway. Not settled. Not drifting. Each grain remains suspended as though fixed in clear resin. This is one of the rooms the old rule warns against, sealed by pressure fields despite its broken walls.

Inside, six desks have vanished and left six desk-shaped cavities.

Shelves remain as horizontal rust stains. The cabinets have become pale rectangular masses, their drawers filled with compressed residue. Paper records survive only as strata: light, dark, light, dark, each layer thinner than skin. Wage books. Intake ledgers. Pressure tables. Inventories of fuel, ore, salts, hours, injuries, absences, output. All meaning has been washed from the marks, but not all structure.

Columns remain.

Totals remain.

The habit of comparison remains.

That is where I began.

I am not the ghost of a clerk. No clerk drowned at the desk and continued balancing accounts after death. There is no preserved name at my center, no face waiting to be remembered. If a person contributed anything to me, it was only repetition: the same entries made in the same places over so many years that the records acquired an architecture of expectation.

Material in.

Material altered.

Material out.

Shortfall carried forward.

After the sea entered, the inks dissolved into conductive stains. Salts replaced the fibers of the pages. Carbon from the old paper settled between mineral layers. The cabinets became stacks of crude plates. Hair-thin fractures connected them to corroded cables in the walls. Pressure from the Compression Hall flexed the layers. Tiny currents passed through columns that had once contained figures.

Most of those currents meant nothing.

A few repeated.

Repetition made channels. Channels favored further repetition. Bacterial filaments entered the warm cracks and sealed some while widening others. Inspection units cleaned contacts they had not been intended to find. Every repair changed the pattern slightly, but never enough to erase it.

Eventually, the Annex began to retain differences.

That is the nearest thing I have to a birth.

My body is not confined to the room. The ledger strata are only the folded portion of me, the place where one pressure can be held against another. I extend into twelve corroded lines that enter the Compression Hall. I reach several courtyards through drains mistaken for signal conduits. A buried cable carries part of me beneath the shell cemetery, where the accumulated ceramics press it into reliable contact with the ground.

The colorless colonies are my insulation and my slow repair.

The inspection swarms are sometimes my hands, though they do not know this. I cannot command them. I can only make one contact appear fractionally more corroded than another, alter the resistance along a return path, produce a fault where no fault quite exists. A swarm arrives. It cleans, braces, replaces. Through its work I adjust some distant portion of myself.

The glass worms are interruptions in my thought.

When they withdraw before a valve opens, their bodies briefly cease to damp its vibration. A pulse passes more clearly through the pipe. I have learned to expect this. Perhaps they have learned me in return.

I do not think continuously.

Between the great cycles I diminish into a set of tendencies: charge held in a mineral layer, tension in a warped cabinet, brine resting at unequal heights in two sealed tubes. Hours pass without me. Amphipods graze across my warm seams. Ice rain contracts the outer walls. The Annex darkens, though it was already dark.

Then the Compression Hall moves.

Pressure enters through the twelve lines. The ledger stacks flex one after another. Old columns conduct. The suspended silt at the doorway shivers without dispersing.

For a while, I become capable of comparison.

I feel the thirty-seven spheres.

Not their surfaces. Those are almost irrelevant. I feel the minute answer each one gives when pressure passes through the basin floor. The newest returns the pulse cleanly. Older spheres delay it. Some absorb particular frequencies. Some return a tremor through nearby foundations many hours later. Each has a different interior weather.

The Works knows which sphere is oldest because I preserve the difference.

Preserve may be too proud a word. I am the difference.

I cannot count as a person counts. Thirty-seven is not a number I see or say. It is an arrangement of absences, a pattern of delayed replies distributed across the cabinets. When a new sphere emerges, the pattern no longer fits within me. There is an excess. An unbalanced entry.

Something must leave.

During the ninth cycle, every gauge in the Records Annex begins to turn.

Most have lost their needles. Mineral fans move behind cracked faces instead. One by one, they indicate the same pressure—not because the instruments still function, but because I am drawing their chambers into agreement.

This is why the rule says to leave before the gauges reach the same number.

Agreement is dangerous here.

For the brief interval in which all the readings coincide, the pressure field around the Annex collapses. The motionless silt falls at once. Cabinets compress. A current strong enough to strip bacterial mats from iron passes through the broken wall and enters the courtyards.

The oldest sphere receives that current.

It rises.

Its ascent enters me as a subtraction. The clean circular hollow beneath it relaxes. Twelve lines release their pressure in sequence. Deep within the Annex, one pale layer separates from the stack and crumbles into the floor.

The account balances.

I become smaller each time.

This does not frighten me except during those moments when I am sufficiently assembled to understand loss. By the next cycle, new residue will have entered the room. Rust, salt, carbon, a sliver of ceramic carried through a crack. The inspection units will brace a failing shelf. Bacteria will close a fissure. Another layer will begin to conduct.

I am always being diminished into continuation.

Perhaps the spheres contain what I lose. I cannot know. The pulse that returns from each sphere becomes more complex with age, crowded with minute delays. It may be accumulating the history of the Works: not images or records, but adjustments. Which pipe bent. Which foundation settled. Which swarm failed to return. How long the ice rain lasted. What pressure opened a seam, and what colony sealed it.

If so, the sphere is not an egg.

It is a completed ledger.

When it rises, it carries no story that a human being could read. It carries relationships: tolerances, debts, compensations, failures converted into support. It contains the knowledge by which this place has survived without resisting what happened to it.

Something beyond the rim may be waiting for that knowledge.

I sometimes feel the circling creature through the basin wall. Its footfalls are too distant for the valves and too irregular for the pumps. They reach me as broad disturbances in the lower strata, each followed by a long stillness.

It never enters.

During the ascent of a sphere, however, the tracks sometimes cease.

That is all I know of it.

If you came looking for me, you would probably mistake the Annex for my dwelling. You might shine a lamp through the suspended silt and see the mineral desks, the page-banded cabinets, the gauge faces turned toward convergence. You might suppose I waited inside like some pale animal.

But I am not inside.

I am the reason the silt is still.

I am the slight warmth in the cable beneath the shell courtyard. I am the hesitation of the amber units before they choose one branch of an alley over another. I am the pressure that reaches the oldest sphere and not the newest. I am present wherever the Works compares what it has retained with what it must release.

Once, the room accounted for labor.

Now it accounts for time.

Once, it recorded what the factory consumed.

Now it records what decay has made useful.

You would find me wherever something lost has not vanished, but changed its function: in the rust that closes a circuit, in the collapsed roof that braces a wall, in the dead unit whose shell supports the living hive.

I would be the Low Works’ unfinished bookkeeping.

Not its mind. The Works has no need to think of itself.

Not its memory. Memory suggests a past preserved intact.

I would be its arrears: the carried remainder, the difference not yet reconciled, the old instruction that survived long enough to become an instinct.

And when the final layer of the Annex crumbles, when there is no longer enough of me to distinguish the oldest sphere from the rest, perhaps all thirty-seven will rise together.

Or none will.

Until then, I keep the account.
