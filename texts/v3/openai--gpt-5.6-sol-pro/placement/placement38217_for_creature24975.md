At first I think I would be one of the Deep Attendants.

The temptation is obvious: to descend past the inhabited routes, past walls that still answer quickly, into districts where pressure has erased the distinction between corridor and vein. I imagine myself fixed beside one of the primary chambers, limbs flattened into black interfaces, listening to the mountain correct its own weight. Questions would reach me as faint asymmetries in the foundation tone. I would hold them for years, perhaps decades, until the machinery made room for a reply.

But that is too close to certainty. Too near the source. In this city, whatever appears central is likely only where several consequences happen to cross.

Then I think I might be one of the preserved dead: a resonant cavity installed behind an intersection, no longer a person but still altering every message passing through. That, too, feels almost right. To remain not as a voice but as a bias in transmission; to give certain frequencies warmth, to make warnings clearer, to cause the names of distant places to arrive with an undertone no living speaker intended.

But I am not dead there. Not yet.

I find myself in an outer district that has been moving away from the city center for three generations.

It stands where dense architecture loosens into pylons and resonant fences. Behind it, the towers are a black mass inside cloud. Ahead lie shallow meltwater fields, low vaults, and the pale, tightly packed herds. The mountain slope rises beyond them, blunt and wet, carrying its impossible suspended stones.

My habitation is a route between five places.

There is a narrow heated recess where I soften the joints of my limbs. There is a shell-covered wall from which I feed and read the chemistry of recent rain. There is an old bridge pier where the departures of the fliers can be felt before they become audible. There is a submerged chamber containing the resonant cavities of eleven dead inhabitants. And farthest out, beyond the last reliable intersection tone, there is a newly emerged basalt column with a sealed chamber inside it.

I am the latest listener assigned to that column.

Not an engineer, exactly. Not a guard. My work changes nothing unless waiting is a kind of work—which, here, it is.

Three listeners came before me. I know them by the mineral plates they left along the route and by the records patterned into shell colonies near the column’s base. The first heard only a slow pulse. The second determined that the pulse did not originate from fluid movement or cooling stone. The third sounded a reply and waited nineteen years for an alteration.

The alteration came after their death.

I inherited it.

My body is narrow even for one of the inhabitants, better suited to spanning cavities than bearing weight. Four limbs serve for movement; two finer ones fold beneath my ventral plates and unfold for close listening. My chest chambers are unequal. One produces a stable low interval. The other has a flaw—whether congenital or acquired in childhood, no one agrees—which makes it answer frequencies slightly before I consciously perceive them.

Among the inner districts this is considered a disability. Walls crowded with traffic cause that chamber to flutter continuously, turning every corridor into overlapping anticipation. Here, where structures stand far apart and messages travel through young, reluctant stone, it lets me detect a return before it fully arrives.

My chord is therefore difficult to greet. It contains a note that seems to answer questions no one has yet asked.

Older inhabitants find this unpleasant. The very young press themselves against the same wall as me and send pulse after pulse, delighted when my body appears to reply out of sequence.

I carry dark basalt plates around the major joints, translucent repairs along one side of the torso, and a pale metallic growth at the ends of my listening limbs. Shell animals inhabit grooves beneath my chest. They are descended from a colony cultivated in the district before it began migrating. Their chemistry still assumes water from conduits now several kilometers behind us. Each generation alters a little. I do not correct them.

Every cycle, I travel to the sealed column.

The final part of the route crosses water. I never trust its surface. At the edge, I attach one limb to a resonant fence and sound three controlled pulses into the flooded paving. If the return is diffuse, the channel is open. If it comes back clean, I wait, because something below has filled the water so completely that the liquid has forgotten how to move.

Sometimes I wait for days.

When passage is safe, I move with most of my body submerged. Ribbon animals gather around my warm joints. Reflective mites drift toward the faint electrical field of my plates. I make no effort to remove them. By the time I reach the column, I carry a temporary skin of local life.

The basalt is still growing. It has split the plaza plates without breaking them; they have drawn apart and tilted around it like dark scales around a bone. The city has not yet decided whether the column is obstruction, foundation, citizen, or message.

I place four limbs against the surrounding paving and the two fine ones against the stone.

Then I listen.

The chamber inside has no opening. Its folded occupant—if it is an occupant—produces one pulse over a span longer than my waking day. I cannot hear the whole of it at once. I receive a pressure, go away to feed and cool, return, and receive what may be the continuation. To perceive its shape, I must distribute the listening across time.

So I pattern the pulse into my shell colonies.

They carry yesterday on their newest edges. Last year lies deeper in the layers. The records left by my predecessors form the lower strata. By touching the colony in sequence, I can feel a rhythm almost a century long.

It is changing.

Not toward the buried foundational tone, as the Deep Attendants warned. Not away from it either. The pulse is developing an interval corresponding to my flawed chamber—the note that arrives too early.

For a long time I believe I have contaminated the record. Perhaps the shells copied me. Perhaps repeated contact taught the chamber my body’s error. I withdraw for an entire dark season and let another listener take my route.

When I return, the premature interval is stronger.

It is not imitating me.

I have been imitating it.

The realization does not make me important. Importance is a poor category here. It means only that some pressure passed through the sealed thing, through young basalt, through the city’s breeding chamber, and into the soft arrangement that became my body. I may be its answer. I may be part of its question. I may simply share a structural defect with something buried before the plaza existed.

I send no announcement toward the center. Not yet.

Instead I alter my habitation.

I add a sixth place to the route: a shallow groove beneath a low vault overlooking the column. There I sleep with my chest coupled to the ground. Shell creatures cover me, sealing scratches and gradually joining my outer plates to the vault’s conductive surface. Each waking, detachment becomes more difficult.

This does not frighten me. The district is moving. In another generation, the inner end of my route will be too distant to maintain. The warmed recess will belong to other bodies. The feeding wall will change chemistry. The bridge pier will fall outside the useful range of the flock patterns.

I will remain at the outer end.

Years later, when a question arrives from the Deep Attendants, it reaches me through the submerged chamber of the eleven dead. Ten of them damp it. One gives it a sharp overtone. The signal crosses the water field, passes through the resonant fence, enters my locked limbs, and opens inside my unequal chest.

The question is not expressed as words.

It asks whether the sealed pulse returns.

I place myself against the basalt column.

For the first time, the thing inside completes its rhythm while I am listening. The interval passes through my body before it passes through the stone. My flawed chamber contracts. The column answers. Far beneath us, one of the great machines alters its phase by an amount no unaided citizen could measure.

Water trembles across the plaza.

The pale herds shift.

Behind me, bridge after bridge releases its fliers into the cloud.

I understand then what I am in this world.

I am not the one who discovers what sleeps in the mountain. I am not the one who opens the chamber, repairs the city, or speaks for the buried machinery.

I am a point at which a delayed answer becomes audible.

A living interval, stationed at the edge of agreement.

Eventually my limbs will no longer detach from the vault. My soft tissues will be fed through the wet wall. My personal chord will thin into the local structure, and those who travel outward after me will be unable to say whether they are greeting an inhabitant, consulting an instrument, or touching a small unfinished part of the city.

They will place their hands against the stone.

Before they sound their questions, something in the wall will begin to reply.
