At first I would mistake myself for something with a wide view.

A pale falcon, perhaps, held above the basin where the wind climbs the western wall. From there I could see the glacier under its rind of grit, the moraines laid down like unfinished sentences, the herds pausing where inherited knowledge no longer agrees with the ground. I would know the basin by distance: the sudden tightening of a flock, the dark thread of an animal crossing pale gravel, the copper ignition of water at evening.

But that life is too singular. Too cleanly bounded. The falcon passes over the basin without being held by it.

Then I might think I belonged among the migrants—a small wader arriving out of cloud, carrying southern mud dried between my toes. I would live by shorelines that moved between one feeding and the next. My bill would read the soft ground more precisely than eyes could: larva, grit, root, empty tube, crustacean. I would leave tracks in sediment that filled with water before I had crossed the pool.

That comes closer. Such a bird is both inhabitant and messenger. It brings pieces of elsewhere into the new land and takes part of the basin away.

Still, I do not think I would have wings.

I would be found farther north, below the geothermal field but above the broadest marshes, where a warm seep loses itself among glacier-polished stones. Not at the spring’s mouth, where the water is too hot and burdened with sulfur, nor in the meltwater channel, where cold scours everything loose. I would live at the uncertain seam between them.

There, a slab of black rock lies tilted into the ground. Its upper face is exposed to sleet and weak light. Its lower edge remains damp, warmed from beneath by only a few degrees. Silica has made a pale rim around it. Wind has pressed flour-fine glacial sediment into one shallow fracture. A feather barb, nearly transparent, is caught there. So are grains of pollen from a valley no one in the basin can see.

At first I would be mistaken for a stain.

A darkening no wider than a thumbnail. Perhaps green-black when wet, ash-colored when dry. I would have no single body. I would be a lichen: fungus and photosynthetic cells joined so thoroughly that the alliance has acquired one name. Within and beneath me would be bacteria, mites, and fungal threads not entirely mine, so even that name would only be convenient.

My world would have little to do with the basin’s apparent immensity. It would be measured in the depth of a crack, the duration of a water film, the difference between the stone’s sunward and shaded sides. Noon would be the loosening of ice around my cells. Evening would be water withdrawing into crystal. A cloud crossing the sky would not darken a landscape; it would alter the chemistry of my entire existence.

I would spend most of my life paused.

When mist collected on the slab, I would soften and become active. The photosynthetic part of me would use the basin’s diluted light. The fungal part would retain water, shelter us from exposure, and send acids against the rock. For a few hours we would make sugars, exchange minerals, repair what freezing had broken. Then the wind would dry the surface, or sleet would glaze it, and I would contract again into something nearly indistinguishable from death.

This would not feel like endurance. It would simply be the local form of time.

The birds would be weather to me. Their shadows would pass without meaning, but a dropping carried downslope in rain might transform years of scarcity. The herds would be catastrophe and opportunity: one misplaced hoof could scrape me from the slab, while the tremor of their crossing might lodge new grit in my fracture. Midges would fall beside me after their brief hour in the air. Their bodies would become nitrogen, then moss, then perhaps the first dark crumb of soil.

I would grow too slowly to follow any animal’s story. The calf hesitating at the stream would become a horned elder and vanish while my edge advanced scarcely the width of its hoofprint. Generations of waders would arrive carrying seeds. The glacier would retreat a little farther up its wall. A spring would seal itself and reopen elsewhere. The warm seep beside my stone might cool by degrees, and I would experience that cooling not as an event but as a gradual change in which companions could live near me.

Nor would I remain only on the surface.

My filaments would enter pores invisible from above. I would hold moisture after the exposed stone had dried. Acids from my living edge would loosen iron and other minerals grain by grain. Frost would do the larger violence, widening the weaknesses I had begun. Dust would settle into those openings. Dead cells would remain there. A moss spore might find the pocket and, because of what had accumulated beneath me, fail to die.

In time the moss could overgrow part of my body.

That would not be defeat. I would have made the conditions for my own concealment.

The place where I lived might become a green cup holding water. Sedge roots might one day enter the softened seam and split it. The slab itself could tilt as saturated ground slumped eastward. I might be broken into fragments, some buried too deeply for light, others carried downslope and pressed against new stone. Each viable piece would begin again as a stain.

So I would not be the basin’s watcher, ruler, or wandering spirit. I would have no knowledge of its whole shape. I would never see the glacier calve or understand the echo crossing the plateau. I would not know that mountains surrounded me, only that the light sometimes arrived brighter from one direction and that grit came more often from another.

Yet I would participate in nearly everything.

Mist would wake me. Stone would feed me. Geothermal warmth would lengthen my intervals of activity. Glacial dust would collect in my tissues. Birds would bring distant elements into reach. Frost would open the rock ahead of me. My dead layers would hold what the living edge needed. Around me, mineral surface would become habitat, and habitat would begin—almost imperceptibly—to become ground.

If something passing close enough could ask who I was, there would be no fully honest singular answer.

I would be an agreement between unlike lives.

I would be a thin, patient metabolism laid over new rock.

I would be one of the basin’s first ways of remembering moisture after the water had gone.

And you would find me at the meeting of warm seep and cold stone, below the vapor columns, dark and easily overlooked—already turning the unfinished mountain into something that could someday be called soil.
