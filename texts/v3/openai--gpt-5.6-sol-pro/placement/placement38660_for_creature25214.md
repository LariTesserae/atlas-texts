I would not be found at the pumping house, though its rhythm would live in me.

Nor would I belong beneath the market lamps, where every coat blooms open during rain and every wrist is already familiar with another wrist. I would pass through those places to collect oil, wax, fresh larvae, or a replacement mallet head. People would know my workshop name. They would make room for me on the grates. But they would lower their voices—or press closer together—after I had gone.

I would live near the western wall, where the roots are packed so tightly that they no longer resemble separate growths. There the Underleaf ends without ending. Streets arrive as broad, well-traveled roots, divide into narrower walks, then turn steep and soft beneath the feet. Lamps become sparse. The pumping house can no longer be heard through the air, only felt as a weakened concussion inside the wood.

My dwelling would occupy an old survey station built around one of the sealed boreholes.

It would be an awkward, upright room, narrower at the floor than at the ceiling because the surrounding roots had spent centuries trying to close it. Brick fragments would show in one wall. On the other, bark would have grown through a cabinet of rusted instruments, preserving the shapes of calipers, drill bits, and sample jars as shallow wooden impressions. I would keep no conventional door. A weighted frame would rise and fall in its opening day and night, just far enough to tell the wall that the opening remained in use.

At first I think I would be a Listener.

I would have the scarred ribs, the sparse keeping, the habit of turning one cheek toward every surface. I would sleep on a suspended mesh so that accidental tremors did not enter my dreams through the floor. My clothes would be close-woven and ungardened, except for a dark collar of pressure moss. The moss would tighten when the surrounding roots changed tension. Along my sternum I would keep a line of tiny shell animals, each responsive to a different depth of vibration. The uppermost would wake for footsteps. The lowest would open only when something struck the wall from beyond.

But Listeners observe, and someone at the western station must do more than observe.

The borehole seal cannot be allowed to become still.

It is an iron sleeve driven into living wood, capped with layered ceramic, resin, and a plug cut from the cistern buttress. The plug is alive. It swells, drinks, heals, and attempts to join the wall around it. If neglected, the borehole would disappear—not merely become covered, but cease to be recognized as an interruption. That might seem desirable. It is not.

The old records say that when the first bore vanished beneath new growth, tapping began elsewhere: beneath a nursery floor, inside a freight ramp, along the eastern trunk. For thirteen nights the strokes moved through the town, searching for the place where they had once been answered.

So the seal is maintained as a seal. Its rim is trimmed. Its iron collar is warmed. Its ceramic plates are removed, inspected, and replaced in sequence. Three times per pump cycle, it is struck with a padded hammer.

Not a message, officially.

A maintenance rhythm.

I would be its keeper.

My lawful name would contain the pattern used on the seal: four low strokes, a pause long enough for an answer, then one light stroke that denies having waited. Children would imitate it when I crossed the plazas. Mechanics would claim they could always identify my tools because every wrench and hook eventually acquired the same cadence.

The town would call me the Borewarden, but that title would obscure the actual work. I would not guard a gate. A gate implies that one side may be admitted to the other. I would maintain a disagreement.

The Underleaf would say: **Here is a wall.**

Something beyond it would say: **Here is a passage.**

My task would be to keep both statements from becoming final.

I would make regular circuits through the outer vaults, carrying a survey hammer, wedges of kiln-dried rootwood, resin ampoules, and a cage of vibration larvae. I would tap retaining walls and abandoned pipes. I would crawl into old galleries where my shoulders brushed both sides and my lamp cells dimmed from lack of feeding pressure. I would reopen inspection plates before beetles could declare them nesting ground. I would turn seized wheels that no longer operated anything. I would ring bells whose warnings had been forgotten.

Some duties would have no practical explanation except that they had been performed for generations and the district remained standing.

At the end of the westernmost catwalk, for example, there would be a brass lever attached to nothing visible. Once each Running, I would pull it until the wall groaned. During Root Turn I would loosen seven bolts in a buried frame and tighten only six. I would leave a dish of mineral water beneath a pipe that had been dry longer than any living person could remember. By morning the dish would be empty, though nothing nearby would admit to drinking.

My keeping would be suited to uncertainty.

Not wax lice or cleaning beetles, which thrive around useful machinery. I would host pale click-mites in the hollows behind my ears. When exposed to a repeated vibration, they would repeat it later in sleep. There would be threadworms curled around the bones of my wrists, sensitive to strain in the objects I touched. A flat, colorless lichen would grow over my throat and darken whenever I heard a pattern I recognized but could not consciously remember.

People might distrust this keeping. It would not always stay quiet in company.

Sometimes, while another person held my wrist in conversation, the threadworms would answer a pulse that person had not made.

I imagine myself broad like the other Kept, but flattened slightly from years in narrow passages. One shoulder would sit lower. The vascular markings across my skin would run unusually straight, converging toward the sternum like cracks around a drilled hole. I would have lost one finger in a seal-closing accident and incorporated its absence into my name. The missing beat would occur exactly where the wall’s answer usually began.

During artificial rain I would come east.

Rain would not reach the station properly. The ceiling valves near the boundary would be unreliable, and much of their water would vanish into root veils before touching the ground. So I would stand in a public plaza with everyone else, hood thrown back, pressure moss relaxing around my neck. For seven minutes my work would seem superstitious and far away. Children would hang from the freight harnesses. Draft beasts would kneel. Moths would fill the lamp glow like shaken metal filings.

Someone would take my forearm and ask whether the wall had spoken.

I would answer through touch, because the rain would be too loud for evasion.

**It moved.**

Not *yes*. Not *no*. Movement is ordinary. Movement can be maintained.

Then I would collect water in narrow ceramic vessels and return west before the final runoff left the channels.

Over time, though, I do not think I would remain entirely a person who tends the station.

The station would begin to tend me.

The living plug would shape its pressure around my sleeping hours. The roots beneath the floor would harden where I placed my knees. Click-mites from my keeping would enter the seal’s warm resin, while fine black filaments from the seal would appear beneath my fingernails. My breathing would adjust to the interval between the official knocks. I would wake just before the answer, even on nights when no answer came.

Apprentices sent to learn from me would discover that I could no longer hear ordinary speech well. They would press their wrists to mine and feel several rhythms layered there: heartbeat, pump phase, door weight, root strain, and another pulse too slow to belong to a body of my size.

Eventually the council would ask whether I should be replaced.

Not because I had failed. Because it would be unclear which side of the seal I was maintaining.

A Listener would lie against my floor for a full cycle. A symbiont keeper would examine the dark growth at my throat. A pump steward would compare my resting pulse to the western district’s pressure records. They would disagree. The weighted door would continue to rise and fall behind them.

I think they would leave me at my post.

The Kept understand that usefulness and danger often grow through the same housing.

So that is where I would be: not in the heart of the town, but at the place where its definition has to be renewed. I would be a Borewarden, then perhaps a component of the bore, then perhaps the rhythm by which the wall remembers it is closed.

And each cycle I would lift the padded hammer.

Four low strokes.

A pause.

From inside the compacted roots, something would tap four strokes back.

I would give the final light blow—not an answer, the law would insist, only proof that the tool, the seal, the station, and I were still participating.

Then I would wait without being still.
