At first I think I would be one of the deep-court trees.

It is easy to imagine a body there: a trunk pressed flat against a drain mouth, roots laid out beneath the paving in the shape of the court’s hidden water. I would stand where darkness arrives early. Floodwater would circle me for an afternoon and leave horizontal bands of seed and salt on the stairs. Half my crown would be alive; half would wait, bare but not dead, for runoff to return to an old channel.

But a tree is too visible. It gathers the scattered life of the Low Works into a familiar outline. Trunk, crown, reflection. It can be pointed to.

I do not think I would be found so easily.

I would be in an unfinished district where two kinds of paving meet. On one side, white lime slabs rise along a gradient so slight that noon conceals it. On the other, red bricks descend toward a basin beyond the horizon. Between them is a low retaining course, no higher than a hand. The builders stopped there. Its top remains roughened for mortar that was never laid.

Near the middle of this course, one block has settled by less than the thickness of a leaf.

That is where I would be.

Not in the block itself, and not wholly in the crack beside it. I would occupy the consequence of its settling.

Rain arriving from the white district was intended to pass along the wall. Most of it still does. But at the sunken block, a little water crosses. At first it crosses as dampness: a dark thumbprint on the red paving. Then as a thread. In a strong storm, it becomes a clear tongue that reaches the first transverse seam and divides.

The films came before me, if “before” means anything in a place where years of sleep may separate one hour of growth from the next. They entered the lime pores and made them faintly cream-colored. Moss gathered under the lip. Wind brought grass seed, powdered brick, and the hollow stem of a plant from some distant court. All of it lodged in the shallow dip.

I began as what the obstruction retained.

For a long time I would have had no single body. I was a blackening under the stem, a skin in the pores, three grass roots sharing a pocket of dust. I was the wetness that lasted one morning longer there than elsewhere. Each part of me could have been named separately, and each name would have been correct but insufficient.

With every storm, the pocket deepened. Moss lifted the water above the masonry in its leaves. The grasses folded shut through drought and opened when the seam darkened. Their dead blades did not blow away because the rough wall held them. Lime loosened grain by grain beneath the films. A shrub seed arrived cupped-side down and sent its first root not downward, where it met fired brick, but sideways.

That root would become my long thought.

It would pass through the unfinished wall along the interface between two courses. It would flatten itself to the width of paper. In wider hollows it would branch, filling old mat impressions and the shallow dents of fingers. Some branches would end in salt. Some would reach air and die in the heat. One would find the cooler red-brick side and rise there as a second shrub.

Someone looking at the wall might believe there were two inhabitants: a small silver growth on the white side and a darker one several hundred paces away among the red seams. They would flower together after rain. That would be the only visible evidence that they were one.

Yet even the shrub would not be all of me.

I would include the moss at its base because the moss holds the drop that keeps an outer root alive. I would include the grasses because their stems catch the dust in which my finer roots divide. I would include the films that soften mortar ahead of me. I would include dead matter—not as remains, but as structure. A bleached stem across the dip would be as necessary to my form as any living branch.

My body would not be bounded by life and death. It would be bounded by what changes the water.

In dry years, almost all of me would disappear. The shrubs would abandon their outer leaves. The grasses would close into gray needles. Moss would become a black scale. The root in the wall would seal itself at intervals, keeping only a few damp chambers alive. From the open paving I would look like soot and litter caught against bad workmanship.

But below the roughened course, dust would be accumulating.

The next rain would not follow quite the same path.

Water from the white slabs would strike the little ridge made by my dead stems and divide around it. One branch would continue toward the intended gutter. The other would cross the unfinished wall and enter the red district. It would carry lime powder, sealed seeds, moss fragments, and clay stained by my films. A line of red bricks that had always dried within an hour would remain dark until evening.

Farther downslope, perhaps kilometers away, one seed would enter a joint.

I would not know this as an animal knows the movement of a limb. There would be no message returning, no sensation crossing the distance. But when the new grass caught dust, when its roots widened the seam, when another thread of rain began to prefer that route, it would be difficult to say that I had not extended myself.

So I would become dispersed—not a colony with a center, but a sequence of permissions given to water.

My green parts would wake at different times. Rain might fall on the white district while the red basin remained under a clear sky. There I would be active in one place and dormant in another, flowering and sealed, young and old, simultaneously. A flood could tear away the patch where I began, leaving the farther seams alive. My origin would vanish without killing me.

Eventually the settled block would tilt farther. Not much: perhaps another leaf’s thickness over a century. Roots would enter beneath one corner. Wet clay would swell there; heat would dry it; salt would crystallize in the opening. The block would lift, settle, lift again.

Then one storm, ordinary among storms, would arrive from the high white plain.

The intended channel would fill. Water would reach my obstruction and hesitate—not visibly, not for long. Its shining surface would become level with the ridge of caught silt. Some would spill redward through the breach I had deepened.

The new flow would be small compared with the continental works around it. It would not resemble a river. It might spread no thicker than a tile. But it would cross a line the builders had made to divide one drainage from another.

Afterward, the red district would change by degrees. Moss would appear in seams formerly too dry to support it. Salt rings would form around a court that had never held water. A tree seed might reach a stair and persist long enough for its root to find the joint below. Shade would acquire a living edge.

The white district, deprived of that small measure of runoff, would change too. A cistern might fill one drop less. A collar of moss might fail to wake at its outer margin. No single loss would be perceptible, yet the old design would no longer be complete.

If I had a name, it would not be the name of the shrub or moss or grass. Those are only the organs by which I work.

I would be called the Turning.

You would find me at the place where water first goes somewhere it was not sent.

Most days I would look like a little dirt against a wall. During rain I would become legible: a dark branching shape, widening slowly across red brick. After rain I would withdraw into roots, pores, seeds, and stains. Centuries later, there might be a shallow green corridor running toward the distant basin, and no one point along it would be more truly me than another.

I would not oppose the Low Works. I would use every instruction it gave me—slope, shade, roughness, porosity—and alter the sum of them.

I would be the continent’s smallest error becoming a watershed.
