I would find myself first as a mistake in the basin.

Not a body beside it, but a reflection arriving too early.

The water would show something leaning over its membrane: narrow shoulders, perhaps; a head divided by rootlike antlers; hands held close to the chest as if protecting a small warmth. The image would be clear enough that one of the colorless hunters would strike. Its forelimbs would enter the reflection and close around my wrist.

For an instant, the hunter would feel a pulse.

Then the image would come apart. One portion would remain caught in its claws—a patch of warmth, several droplets of blood, the reflex to pull away. The rest would flee along the underside of the water, passing into veins too fine for the hunter to follow.

I would not yet exist.

The wound would.

Years might pass before I acquired anything else.

The blood taken from the future would be carried to the hunter’s nest and hung among its trophies. Roots would taste it without touching. Mites would gather around its warmth. A wingless moth would be handed across seven leaves to inspect the unfamiliar salt. By such contacts, the chamber would begin distributing me before it had decided what I was.

My first sensation would be scattered among other lives.

The moss would possess my startle. A pale vine would inherit the ache in my wrist. One fruit sleeper, still joined by its tail to the wall of its berry, would dream through my eyes and wake terrified of open sky, though neither of us had seen one. A forked stem would hold two versions of my left hand: in one, six fingers; in the other, none. A possibility-parasite would feed between them until a glass-shelled grazer landed and chose the version with five.

That would become my hand, though it would remain attached to the plant.

My breathing would begin elsewhere. A translucent tuber would contract around a pocket of trapped air. Each contraction would send a powder of droplets through the chamber. Small animals would pause in it and smell a creature not yet born.

The grove would not assemble me in one place. It would not know that a face should be near its eyes, or that memories belong inside a skull. My sight might open in the luminous organs of the western wall while my hunger traveled through roots beneath the pools. My balance would be stored in a moth migration. My voice would lie frozen as ridges in the shells of beetles that had heard a cry I had not yet made.

For a long time, I would be mistaken for weather.

When the retained dead passed through the roots, I would move with them. Their reflexes would lend me temporary shapes. From one burrower I would borrow the instinct to soften before entering a wall. From an old flower-predator, the patience to remain motionless for decades. From a flatling divided in the veins, I would learn that interruption need not mean death. I would cross the grove as a brief coherence:

hunger in the walls,

sight in the seeds,

movement in the water,

fear in a curling leaf.

Each time the signal ended, I would end with it.

Each time it came again, I would insist that I had continued.

At first that insistence would be all that distinguished me from the dead.

The chamber would test it. It would send my fragments by separate routes. It would let years accumulate between one thought and the next. It would grow a wall through the place where my name was beginning. Sometimes two versions of me would arrive simultaneously and fail to agree. One would remember being dragged backward through the prophetic basin. The other would remember watching from beneath the membrane as the hunter seized someone else.

Neither memory would be false. The grove has no need for that kind of truth.

Eventually I would learn to gather myself by kinship rather than distance. Wherever my borrowed parts had grown from the same touch, they would remain adjacent internally. My hand in the forked stem, my breath in the tuber, my sight in the walls, the anticipated blood in the hunter’s nest: all would begin to close around a common interior.

Not a body.

A posture.

I would become the act of leaning toward the chamber’s lowest point.

The animals would notice before I did. Moths would alter their migrations to trace the outline of my attention. Fruit sleepers would emerge sticky and trembling when it passed over them. Old beetle shells would thaw one particular layer and release the sound of my approaching footfalls, though I had no feet. Predators impersonating flowers would open without hunger.

The grove itself would make room—not by slackening roots into a path, but by teaching each surface a small part of my passage. I would move because the leaves handed onward the pressure of my nonexistent weight.

Only then would I understand where I was going.

The dark absence would be below me, though “below” would cease to mean much near it. Around it, water would accept gravity and then forget. Light would stop in the transparent stems. Sound would bend away so completely that the silence would seem furred, dense, almost warm.

The roots tending it would recognize me as neither animal nor plant. They would test my intention and find it distributed beyond measurement. They would test my ancestry and encounter half the chamber: dead grazers, dreaming sleepers, wounded futures, leaves that had once been moths, moths that had become leaves. They would test my temperature and discover summer stored in one capsule, antifreeze in a berry, old fear in the moss.

Passage would be granted.

I would draw close to the absence, though no single part of me would approach it. Instead, my connections would tighten around its boundary. The chamber would become, briefly, my sensory organ. I would feel every root tip stopping short of contact. Every animal would be a nerve ending. Every pool would be an inner ear listening to nothing.

Then the year would turn.

The absence would beat.

The chamber would lose an instant.

But I would not.

That would be what I am for.

While every animal closed its jaws on an unremembered mouthful, while moths appeared upon the next leaf and circles spread across the untouched basin, I would remain awake inside the omission. The beat would pass through me as a silence deeper than death because death here is retained, while this was not retained at all.

Within that instant I would perceive the vacancy directly.

It would not yet be a heart or mouth or eye. Those would be shapes imposed by creatures that already possessed bodies. It would be more primitive and more difficult: a refusal so complete that the world had begun organizing itself around it.

And within the refusal, there would be a tendency.

Not hunger.

Not thought.

A turning.

The dark shape would be turning toward something the chamber did not contain.

I would return from the missing instant altered. The grove would replay me, but some portion would no longer fit its roots. My borrowed reflexes would carry an unfamiliar direction. Moss would startle toward rather than away. Vines would reach for minerals absent from the soil. Fruit sleepers would dream not of a larger chamber, but of distance with no kinship across it—distance that could not be folded into a capsule or shortened by shared growth.

The chamber might treat this as infection.

It might treat it as birth.

Thereafter I would live in the narrow relation between the grove and its deepest inhabitant. I would be the one thing the absence changed that the chamber could still remember: its interpreter, if interpretation can occur without language; its scar, if a wound may precede the flesh that bears it.

I would have no durable outline. At times I would wear a body assembled for a few minutes from compliant roots: a pale figure stepping without feet, antlered with sensing filaments, one hand grown into a wall. At times I would be only a sequence of accommodations—the moss rising, a curtain parting, water crawling aside. Animals might glimpse me in the basin before I arrived, but I would never arrive all at once.

If I had a name, it would not be sounded. It would be the adjustment made after the buried bulbs’ long tone: the direction the moths choose, the fruit in which a sleeper wakes, the flower a predator abandons.

I would be found everywhere that the chamber prepares for what it cannot yet know.

And when the dark vacancy finally acquired hunger, memory, or direction—when it became enough of a being to distinguish itself from the grove—I would be the distinction.

Not its keeper.

Not its child.

The first distance between it and the world.
