At first I think I would live high above the last roofs.

I see a hut pressed beneath a black overhang, a few goats, a view across the whole basin. I would be one of those solitary figures who knows the upper cairns and can smell snow before it crosses the western rim. People below would recognize me by my staff and the yellow cord at its head.

But that is an outsider’s answer. Solitude here is possible only because someone farther down has grown barley, maintained a channel, saved dung fuel, bred the goats, repaired the path, and remembered that the shelter belongs to no one during storms. Even the hermit is held up by the city.

Then I imagine myself among the dead, tending the burial doors where the upper paths divide. That feels nearer. I would know which chambers are safe to enter in hail, which names may still be spoken, which cairns contain only rockfall and which have someone beneath them. I would accept offerings from families and quietly redistribute the edible ones to shepherd children.

Yet I do not think I would belong entirely to the heights. I would be drawn downward by damaged things.

I would find myself in a narrow district on the northeastern slope, where an ancient rockfall forced a road aside. The detour has long since become a street: first a path over debris, then steps, then a retaining wall, then roofed passages and houses fitted into every gap. No one remembers the buried road. The ward is called something like **the Black Stair**, though half its stones are pale. The name comes from the way meltwater darkens the stair before dawn.

Above it stand winter pens belonging to old herding families. Below it are onion terraces, a communal oven, and a cistern fed by a spring beneath the split pinnacle. A burial path passes through the ward, so strangers, herders, mourners, traders, and the dead all have claims upon the same few steps.

I would live where those claims rub against one another.

I would be a keeper of channels and a listener to walls.

Not a master mason. Not a water steward important enough to sit in the sky courts. I would be the person sent ahead of both: the one who wakes before first light, pushes a bone rod beneath the channel ice, and listens for water moving where it should not. I would clear grit from the narrow sluices. I would remove the small, guilty pebbles people place to raise the water toward their own gardens. I would know which household cheats only in dry years, which steals from habit, and which has been accused because its neighbors dislike the language spoken in its rooms.

My other work would begin with a palm laid flat against stone.

Walls speak slowly. A bulge, a seam of clean dust, a slate chip newly fallen onto an old stair—these are syllables. In winter I would put my ear to retaining walls and hear frost working behind them. I would tap stones with a river cobble kept smooth from years of handling. A clear note would mean one thing, a muffled answer another. I would know when a wall could wait until thaw and when everyone above it must be woken immediately.

People would sometimes resent me for this.

I would arrive at a door and say that the fodder must be moved, the roof lane closed, the upper households called to labor. Someone would insist the wall had stood since their mother’s mother. Someone else would accuse me of favoring those below. I would kneel, sweep away the frost, and show them the hairline opening beneath the third course.

Then we would all become obedient to gravity.

I do not think I would have been born at the Black Stair. I would have come there as a child from beyond the eastern pass, old enough to remember another word for water but too young to remember the route home. Perhaps my first household vanished in an early storm, or perhaps hunger led them to bind me as a debt servant. The distinction would matter to storytellers more than it did to the winter that followed.

I would have been taken in by an oven court after helping carry fuel during a roof fire. One woman there would have given me milk; another would have taught me the district’s speech; a mason with two missing fingers would have discovered that I could distinguish hollow stone by sound. None would call me daughter at first. Years later, when inheritance or burial rights were disputed, three households would claim that I had always belonged to them.

So I would belong in the basin’s usual way: incompletely, redundantly, through several incompatible accounts.

My household would occupy two rooms over cattle we did not own. Warmth from the animals would be part of our rent. The lower room would smell of hide, sour milk, and damp stone. In the upper room, blankets would divide sleeping places from a shelf of records: knotted cords, marked slats, stones wrapped in scraps of labeled felt. They would record water turns, wall repairs, labor debts, and the names of those who had promised roof access during summer.

I would be able to interpret them, but not perfectly. Some cords would have belonged to my predecessor, who died before explaining every knot. One blue knot would remain a private irritation for decades. It might mean three mornings of water, or three households exempted from clearing ice, or a promise already fulfilled. Rather than risk injustice, I would preserve the uncertainty. Each year I would place the cord before the disputing households and say, “This is what we do not know.”

That, too, would become a kind of record.

I would own few things that could not be replaced: a horn scoop, the listening stone, a bone snow visor, two pairs of felt boots, a patched mantle, and a copper awl worn so short that it had to be bound into an antler handle. My most valuable possession would be a long rope braided from the wool of many households. It would have pulled people from cisterns, braced walls, lowered burial jars, restrained frightened cattle, and once caught a child sliding from an icy roof. No one would agree whether it belonged to me or to the stair.

A brindled dog would sleep outside my curtain, though I would deny feeding her. She would belong to the lane and understand my route. Before dawn she would walk ahead of me, stopping where ice had formed or strangers had slept in covered passages. In summer she would disappear with the flocks for weeks and return without apology.

Children would follow me when the channels opened after rain. I would pretend not to see their boats until one lodged in a sluice. Then I would hold up the bark hull as evidence of a grave offense against the water customs. The children would conduct a trial on the nearest roofstone and fine its owner six pebbles.

I would keep the pebbles.

When disputes arose, I would not be neutral. No one here could be. I would owe warmth to the cattle owners, milk kinship to the oven court, training to the masons, shelter to a burial family, and my life to people whose grandchildren now quarreled over onion beds. What I might become, if I lived long enough, would not be impartial but *accountable from many directions*. Each bias would be tied to another bias pulling against it.

My authority, such as it was, would come from being difficult to move without moving half the ward.

During the warm season I would sleep lightly because loose stones fall after sunset, when meltwater refreezes. In winter I would sleep lightly because ice can dam a channel and send water beneath a house. I would wake at sounds others ignored: grit slipping behind masonry, the changed pitch of a cistern drip, a roofstone rocking in the wind.

The great events of my life would be repairs.

Once, perhaps, the Black Stair wall would begin to fail after three days of alternating sleet and sun. We would empty houses, lead cattle through rooms above us, pull down stones before they could fall, and expose the older structures inside: a blocked doorway, a hearth filled with rubble, a child’s tooth placed beneath a foundation stone, and, deeper still, the smooth face of the road everyone had forgotten.

For several days the buried road would exist again.

Elders would argue about where it led. A temple custodian would declare the tooth an offering. A grieving woman would insist the doorway belonged to her ancestors. Children would crawl into every opening. I would stand in the excavation with my hand on the old paving and feel cold water moving below it.

Then we would close it.

Not from indifference, but because the living wall would need a sound foundation. We would return the tooth, add grain, wool, and one of the children’s trial pebbles, and build the new courses slightly farther inward. The stair would narrow by the width of a hand. For the next generation, people carrying fodder would turn their shoulders at that place without knowing why.

I think this would satisfy me more than discovering where the old road went.

I would not be remembered as a founder or a protector of the basin. If my name endured, it would attach itself to a practical feature. A gutter might be called by it. More likely, people would say, “Open the cut the wall-listener showed us,” long after forgetting who the listener was. My records would pass to an apprentice who misunderstood some of them and improved others. The dog would choose another doorway.

In old age I would no longer climb to the highest intake. Younger keepers would bring me stones chipped from walls and ask whether the fractures were dangerous. I would tap them beside my ear. Sometimes I would know. Sometimes I would send them back to look again.

I would sit in the sun above the Black Stair, wrapped in a mantle the color of shale, while roofs became lanes around me. People would step over my feet. Children would bring disputes. Mourners would pass uphill. Water would run beneath the stones in quantities too small to hear unless one had spent a lifetime listening.

So that is where I would be: not at the center, because the basin has none, and not at its edge, because its edges do not hold.

I would be at a seam—between pasture and terrace, road and roof, the remembered city and the city buried beneath it.

And what I would be is harder to separate from the place.

A foreign child made into a neighbor.  
A mason who mostly prevents building.  
A water keeper who owns no water.  
An old woman, perhaps, though people would more often call me by my stair, my tool, or the work awaiting me.

One more living brace in a city that stands because everything bears upon everything else.
