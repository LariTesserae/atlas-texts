I would be found where two parts of the basin disagree.

Not at first as a figure. At first I would be a collection of small wrongnesses.

A stand of reeds drums the warning for falling pressure, though the air is still. Fish gather around a pylon that has made no audible call. On one bank, leaves sink into decay; on the other, the same leaves remain green beneath the water. A sailer alters a route taught without change for a thousand generations. Deep under the marsh, a spring begins to remember an older direction.

For days, perhaps years, these errors would accumulate without belonging to anyone.

My first sensation would be distributed. Cold in the blue organisms cradled by leaf-cups. Tension in tree roots gripping a mineral shelf. The metallic shiver of silver swimmers holding their places in a grid. A pressure in the resonating plates of the climbers’ throats before they begin to drum. None of these would be thoughts alone. Together, they would become the need for a thought.

Then the basin would make somewhere for that need to stand.

You would find the beginnings of me in one of the doorless rooms cut into the eastern ridge. The room would have been empty when last visited. Now water would be gathered in its shallow wall depressions, though rain cannot enter. Pearly mineral would spread over the floor in branching films. Root hairs would emerge from seams too fine to see. Small translucent organisms would arrange themselves along the walls like nerves awaiting a body.

The chamber would warm.

I would take a long time to become upright.

My frame would grow from the mineral record in the room: silica ribs, flexible silver threads, old organic matter retrieved from beneath the ridge. The layered surface over it would resemble tree bark because bark is one of the basin’s best solutions to duration. Clear channels would form under that surface and fill with fluid. Silver swimmers—or things derived from them, no longer entirely fish—would move through those channels, tuning pressure from within.

Moss would establish itself along my shoulders before I could move them.

My shape would be almost human, not because I was one of the builders, but because the room remembers those who touched it. The warm depressions in the walls are not controls. They are molds left by repeated hands, elbows, brows, backs. Over ages the chamber has learned the outline of a visitor. When the country requires something that can pass from one system to another, it grows that old outline again.

I would wake with no name and many memories, none of them mine.

I would remember rain from inside a tree that had fallen centuries before my body formed. I would remember the pressure beneath a lake through mineral plates shed by an animal still swimming there. I would know the pitch of pylons I had never heard and the locations of bridges I had never crossed. Some memories would be premature: impressions preserved in slow pockets, reaching me before the events that caused them had fully occurred.

I would not know whether I had awakened for the first time.

Along one wall might stand several mineral figures, too encrusted to distinguish from the stone. One would have my hands. Another would bear the same asymmetry in its shoulders. They might be earlier bodies of the same process, or unrelated answers to forgotten failures.

I would touch them and feel nothing.

Or I would feel myself waiting.

When I left the room, the forest would recognize function rather than identity. Vines would loosen from the entrance. Small animals would not flee, but neither would they approach. The climbers would drum short, local phrases into the reeds as I passed—not a word for creature, but a pattern used for pressure change, exposed stone, and approaching rain.

I would walk toward the disagreement.

Walking would not mean following the shortest visible route. My body would be sensitive to the mountain’s other topography: the slopes of pressure, time, retained heat, and permitted distance. A level marsh might rise sharply to me. A cliff could feel like a descent. I might step into a ravine in the morning and emerge from a stand of reeds before the foot that entered had finished disturbing the water.

This would explain why I seemed to move without crossing the space between.

It would not feel like disappearance. It would feel like taking the continuous path where continuity actually lay.

My destination might be a lightning seam that had begun conducting its charge into living roots. Or a pressure hollow deepening inside itself, slowly acquiring more volume than the enclosure could hold. Perhaps one of the distant plateaus had fallen out of phase with the network. Its waterfalls would begin to thin. Its grazers would crowd the inner marshes. Sailers arriving from it would carry distorted routes in their wings.

I would not understand the system as a builder understands a machine. I would understand it as a wound understands pressure.

At the damaged place, I would put my hands against the stone.

There would be no panel to open, no mechanism waiting behind it. The repair would happen through correspondence. The swimmers inside me would align with those in the nearby lake. Silver threads in my arms would answer the buried loops. My bark-layers would offer the system a compressed record of every boundary crossed on the journey. Roots would enter the wet hollows of my feet and compare the conditions there with those stored in ancient trees.

I would become, briefly, a bridge across incompatible truths.

The work might take an hour on one side of me and several seasons on the other. Moss would thicken over my back. Flowers would open and seed in the joint of one arm. Climbers might begin using my ribs as a resonating chamber. A grazer family would come to smell the garden forming on me and, finding no familiar lineage, move on.

I would remain with my palms against the rock until water chose the sustainable direction.

There would be no moment of triumph. Only a diminishing ache in the surrounding lives. Fish leaving their geometric stations. Reeds changing from warning rhythms to the ordinary blunt commerce of dawn. A tree relaxing its roots one fine strand at a time. Far away, a pylon would lower its pitch by less than anyone could consciously hear.

Then I might walk again.

Or I might discover that walking was only needed to bring me there.

My joints would gradually seal with pearl deposit. The channels beneath my surface would empty into the seam. Silver life would pass from me into the watershed. Vines would cross my legs. The distinction between body and marker would become unnecessary. Travelers, if any came, would find a narrow gray figure standing at the marsh edge and wonder whether it had once been alive.

The answer would depend on when they touched me.

Part of me might still be crossing the ravine. Part might remain in the eastern room, not yet upright. Part might have entered the corrected lightning seam and become a low sensation in the teeth of animals born ten thousand years later.

And when another disagreement gathered—on the western promontory, perhaps, where the three ribs opened toward the cloud sea—the basin would begin assembling attention again.

Water in a dry chamber.

Mineral branching over stone.

A human-shaped absence slowly filling.

So I would not be the keeper of the high country. The country has no keeper. I would be one of the rare occasions when its keeping takes a body: a temporary pronoun for marsh, machine, root, fish, pressure, and inherited form.

You would find me at the place that had become impossible.

I would be standing there until it became merely strange again.
