At first I think I would live low in the city, where the mountain has begun accepting debris.

I imagine a narrow room above the sediment houses, its windows furred with condensation, its floor always faintly gritty beneath the broom. I would wake to the metallic weather of the dead: thousands of name-plates stirring in the wind, sounding not like speech but like speech remembered through pipes. My work would be modest. I would carry soil from the low country in sacks, mixing it with brick dust, moth scales, fruit pits, filings, and the black sweepings from machine halls. I would know which moss tolerates copper, which fern prefers iron, which seeds will open only after a pump stroke.

Perhaps I would be a maker of first earth.

That seems right for a while. I can see my broad hands, the permanent dark beneath my nails, the bell at my wrist muted with twine while I work among the dead. I would tend the lower orchards and know families by what they add to the sediment: cable fiber from a conductor, blue glass from a water-marker, a child’s broken slate. I would help turn the city’s wearing-out into the mountain’s beginning.

But I do not think I remain there.

There is a pull higher up—not upward exactly, but outward, toward the places where the lamps thin and every constructed thing carries an extra fitting for what has not yet arrived. I begin to picture myself sleeping in rail sheds, eating from workers’ kitchens, keeping my belongings in a single waterproof roll because the room I rent may cease to be convenient by morning. I carry chalked cord. I understand echoes better from behind than from ahead.

Then I think I must be a returner.

I would go with the survey crews but carry no instrument. While the others measure the newborn stone, I would study what happens at their backs. A new ridge would be trying on distances. Fog would close over the railhead. The tower we had left an hour before might appear below us, then across from us, then briefly inside the reflection of a rain bowl. The surveyors would argue over inclination, pressure, and the silver insects. I would say nothing. My task would be to preserve one thin continuity between their bodies and supper.

I would tie cords where no post stands. I would mark the homeward faces of stones, though those faces might not yet exist on the outward journey. I would memorize the order in which the bells disappeared behind us. When necessary, I would walk backward for miles.

The crews would trust me, but not completely. No sensible person trusts a route too much.

Still, this is not the whole of it. A returner remembers the way back because they began somewhere. When I search for my beginning, I find no lower room, no parents’ shelf, no pressure ward whose rhythm lives in my gait. I remember kitchens, but in each memory I am already a guest. I remember childhood games, but sometimes I was the child playing surveyor and sometimes the district ordered to move. Several households have a chair they insist was mine. Their accounts do not agree on my age.

My elevation book has entries in handwriting older than my hand.

*Before North Bell was cast: behind the western parcels office.*

*Year of warm rain: level with a ridge not yet confirmed.*

*After my return: outbound with the fifth survey.*

There is no first page. There are several last pages, bound at different ends.

So perhaps I am not the returner assigned to the blank place on the roster.

Perhaps I am the reason the place is left blank.

I would be found beyond the last terraces, on the first morning after a new ridge becomes dark enough to interrupt the fog. The stone beneath me would still be warm at its center. Nothing would grow there. The birds would have arrived, but no one else.

Someone from the city would already have set out a chair.

I would be sitting in it.

Not waiting in any dramatic way. I would be damp, cold, and mildly annoyed that the chair had been placed with one short leg on a pale mineral seam. I would have a coat with reflective piping, a lunch tin, and a municipal bell at my throat. The bell would be properly stamped, though the district named on it would not exist. In one pocket there would be back wages in obsolete coin. In another, a handful of dark soil from an erosion slope the mountain will not possess for ten thousand years.

When the surveyors arrived, some would recognize me.

None would recognize me in the same manner.

One would remember apprenticing under me. Another would swear I had attended her funeral, though she remained very much alive and would take offense at the suggestion. The youngest would know only that my name had been written in the crew ledger before the expedition was approved. The returner would look at me longest. Returners dislike unexplained familiarity.

They would not ask what I was. That would be discourteous and administratively useless.

They would ask whether the chair belonged to me.

I would say, “By the registered approach.”

That would be enough to begin the paperwork.

They would issue me gloves, inspect my bell, charge me for the lunch, and add me retrospectively to the safety briefing. Someone would note the basalt grains beneath my eyelids. Someone else would notice that the pale seam under the chair continued faintly beneath the skin of my free hand.

My occupation would be harder to settle.

I would prove poor at ordinary surveying. Levels would incline toward me. Caged birds would become quiet. Weighted smoke would enter my coat and emerge from a sleeve several minutes before being released. Yet I would know where to place the first unnecessary door.

Not approximately. Exactly.

I would walk to a blank wall of fog, knock three times, and wait. On the other side, very far away or very much later, someone would answer. I would mark the hinges so the doorwright could make the opening face toward the knock.

After that, the city would find work for me.

I would become a kind of continuity inspector, though no two departments would use the same title. The rail authority would call me a prospective terminus clerk. The doorwrights would list me as an outside respondent. The archive would classify me as a resident source of uncertain precedence. Children would call me the Before-Man, whether or not I was a man. Birds would imitate my bell but never my voice.

I would go wherever the city was preparing to become adjacent to itself.

Before a station acquired its back room in another neighborhood, I would sleep in the parcels office. Before a staircase disagreed with its count, I would chalk a warning on the seventh step. Before a house received an inhabitant who had always lived there, I would set an extra bowl at supper and advise the family not to ask the guest for proof.

I would not foretell disasters. That is an echo clerk’s work, and prophecy easily becomes vanity. What I would sense is accommodation: where reality had begun making room.

Walls near such places would feel hollow even when backed by miles of stone. Rain bowls would fill from beneath. Moths would settle on my shoulders in patterns resembling streets. The mineral line in my hand would brighten and continue through whatever surface stood nearby. I would knock. If something knocked back, I would submit a work order.

My home would never be one dwelling. It would be the unnecessary room in every district: the narrow chamber beyond the boiler stairs, the berth kept in a herd watcher’s tower, the attic reached only when rain runs uphill, the sealed office that opens once a year behind the western station. Each would contain some evidence that I had just left or was expected shortly—a wet coat, an unfinished cup, a pair of boots facing inward.

Families would include me provisionally. My chair would be retained without grief.

There might be periods when I vanished from the audible city. Years, perhaps. During those absences, I would walk the slopes on which the cloud herds graze: broad inclines still deciding whether to become stone, air, or time. The animals would pass through me, and for a moment I would appear weathered and old. I would see the city as it might look after rivers finally choose their valleys—bridges hanging useless over dry air, mills buried in green, sediment houses become hillsides, the dead at last participating in deep soil.

I would gather a handful of that soil.

Then I would find my way back.

Not necessarily to the year I left. Return is a direction here, not a date.

Eventually the city might discover what I was, though it would not alter my employment. Perhaps I would prove to be a person the mountain remembered before making. Perhaps I would be assembled from the city’s habit of leaving space: every spare span, blank junction, unclaimed chair, and unanswered knock accumulating until absence acquired broad hands and needed a coat.

Or perhaps I would be something simpler.

The first erosion.

Not the wearing-away of stone, but the loosening of certainty around it—the small necessary loss by which a finished edge becomes soil, a fixed distance becomes a route, and a stranger becomes a member of the household because they have succeeded, repeatedly, in coming home.

Where would I find myself?

On the newest ridge, before the rails but after the birds, seated where someone has courteously anticipated me.

Who would I be?

The blank name on the survey roster. The worker who arrives retrospectively. The one who knocks from the side where no room stands yet.

And when the crews prepare to return, I would rise, sling my tools across my back, and leave one hand free.

I would already remember the way.
