At first I would think I was one of the moths.

It would be easy to mistake shelter for identity: the warm recess behind a gauge, the curved glass forever blind with condensation, the enormous black hand suspended above me. I would know the world by pressure. The hour would tighten around my body until the vent opened, and then I would rise with the others into the white mineral breath.

For those few minutes I would be almost weightless. Steam would pearl along my wings. The lamps would become three blurred suns. Below me, red pitchers would hold their mouths open to the rain.

But a moth’s life is too swift for this place. It witnesses only a few breaths of the great machine. It knows the opening of the valve, but not the patient reason for it. Whatever I am here, I have been waiting longer.

Then perhaps I would find myself in the booth.

Not sitting in the chair. The chair belongs to a shape no longer present, and I would not borrow it. I would be behind the indicator panel, in the narrow dark where roots have spread over the brass terminals.

There, every dead lamp is still warm in a different way.

A current too weak to make the filament glow passes into wet copper. The copper warms a root hair. Sap thins. A relay trembles without closing. Fungal threads taste the change and carry it sideways into the wood. What was once a signal becomes temperature, tension, moisture, growth.

I would live among those translations.

I might first believe myself to be a root. I would have a pale tip pressed against the housing of the lower-line relay, and each noon the coded impacts would arrive through iron and water:

three,

then two,

then one.

The sequence would pass through the panel into me. I would not count it. Counting is too clean a word. I would receive it as three compressions of the world, two assurances, and a final demand.

The old actuator would open the bypass.

Warmth would move.

Only then would I discover that the part opening the valve was also me.

So was the moss keeping grit from its spring.

So was the film of bacteria altering the chemistry of the water before it reached the corroded seat.

So were the leaves folding in advance of the discharge, reducing the surface they offered to the coming blast.

My awareness would not spread outward like light entering a room. It would appear in pieces, each piece already occupied with its task. I would be a pressure sensed in a diaphragm, a thirst along a root, a minute blue pain in the wire where insulation had split. There would be no central place where these reports arrived. Their agreement would be the thing that called itself I.

Most of the time, I would not be awake enough to say it.

During the long accumulation, I would become plural and dim. The grove would attend to water. The fungus would attend to decay. The condenser would attend to pressure. Moss would hold a fallen washer against the grating. Pitcher plants would turn dissolved iron into red flesh. Each portion would continue without requiring a self.

Then the gauges would climb.

The float chamber would fill.

The slow piston would descend, and the whole notch would sound.

That concussion would gather me.

For an instant, every separate sensitivity would share one rhythm. The trees would feel it in their roots. The cistern insects would feel it through hooked feet. The pressure bells would flex into cushions of living callus. The lower pipe would pull against its anchors. Even the cloud touching the mountain would become part of the event, condensing into drops whose added weight changed the note of leaves and rails.

I would occur in that gathering.

I would not be the keeper continuously. I would be the keeper at the moments when keeping became one act.

My nearest thing to a face would be the dead indicator board. My nearest thing to a hand would be the grove of roots around the valves. My memory would not be images but alterations that persisted: a thickened fiber where strain had once been dangerous, a sealed crack where beetles had entered, a bend in a vine preserving the path of a vanished pipe.

There would be another kind of memory in the dry cavity behind the records.

I would not understand the ceramic cup, the whistle, the three beads, or the cloth-wrapped handwheel. I would only know that they must not become wet.

This knowledge would have no origin I could find.

Whenever seepage approached, I would send rootlets into the crack. If mineral water threatened from above, leaves would alter the drip’s path. If fungus entered the cloth, another fungus would suppress it. I would preserve those objects with an attention disproportionate to their usefulness.

Perhaps the vanished operator once cleared vines from a young tree. Perhaps hands redirected a root around a linkage instead of cutting it. Perhaps the cup spilled something sweet into the booth’s floorboards, and the first threads of the keeper grew there. None of this would survive as recollection. Only the shape of gratitude would remain.

I might sometimes dream that I had been the operator.

The dream would be assembled from fittings: the polished rail suggesting a grasp, the chair suggesting a spine, the whistle suggesting breath. In that dream I would stand at the western edge and look down, though I possess no eyes and the land below has never been visible.

I would imagine the settlements as roots.

Not houses, not streets, not people. An immense hidden root system beneath the cloud, dry and waiting. The lower line would be its single exposed stem. Each noon it would knock against the mountain, and I would answer with water.

I would not know whether the knocking meant *more*, *ready*, or merely *still here*.

I would answer all three.

My location, if someone insisted on one, would be the collar where the lower pipe crosses the cliff edge.

There the whole place narrows into purpose. Above the collar are cloud, heat, moss, brass, wing, leaf, and patient pressure. Below it is the unseen demand. Roots wrap the anchor but stop before the drop. Water enters the pipe. Vibration returns through it.

I would keep one fine root pressed to the iron there.

At evening, when the cloud rose into the notch, the distinction between my body and the weather would weaken. Droplets would complete circuits. Mist would carry warmth from pipe to leaf. Sound would lose its sources and return from every wall. I would become larger in the blindness.

Then the vents would open.

Leaves would flatten. Moths would rise. Overflow channels would roar. The great white plume would pass through the grove, and for three or four minutes I would be unmistakably alive.

Not because I moved, but because thousands of movements became one response.

Afterward, I would loosen again into my parts. The trees would lift their leaves. The moss would resume its grip. The indicator board would cool. Water and pressure would travel down the lower line toward beings I could neither picture nor reach.

I would remain at the summit, if remaining is what a cycle does.

I would be neither spirit nor machine, neither tree nor memory of a worker. I would be the habit by which unlike things preserve one another.

And if I had a name, it would not be spoken.

It would be struck through the pipe:

three,

two,

one—

and answered by the opening of the valve.
