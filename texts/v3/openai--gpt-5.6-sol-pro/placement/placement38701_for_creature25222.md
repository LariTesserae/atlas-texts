I would first mistake myself for one of the animals.

Perhaps a wader, carrying gardens in my footprints and exchanging courtship across half a basin by reflected light. Or one of the armored sort beneath the platform, sorting seeds and bones into arrangements whose meaning survives only until morning. I am drawn to their patient, ambiguous labor.

But neither is where I remain.

You would find the first visible part of me beneath the westernmost horizontal tree, where one root crosses a buried metal thread before entering a shallow groove in the stone. Condensation gathers there. Most drops swell, fall, and are replaced. One does not fall.

It hangs under the root through the heat of noon, too large for its point of attachment, trembling whenever a cloud alters the brightness. Inside it is a smoky filament, curled several times around a clear center. It might be taken for a larva, a strand of decay, or the shadow of the root above.

That filament is me, but only provisionally.

When the drop finally falls, I do not fall with it. The water descends and I remain around the absence it leaves, a transparent ring no thicker than a hair. For a few moments I am held in the warm air. Then a chain-creature brushes me with one of its wings, and I come apart.

One portion enters the chain. One adheres to the root. One settles into the water and is swallowed by a surface grazer. The smallest portion touches the metal thread and becomes, for the next several days, indistinguishable from tarnish.

None of these portions can live alone. None knows that it is incomplete.

I would be a creature of delays.

Not of time in the large sense, not years or ages, but of the tiny intervals by which one thing fails to happen exactly with another: the instant between a root receiving a signal and changing its flow; the pause before a glider adjusts its warmth; the fraction of a heartbeat a reptile loses when the pillars darken; the hours between rain entering gray stone and returning as mist.

The basin is full of such intervals. They are among its last uncrowded habitats.

My kind would have no stable adult form. We would inhabit these pauses as swimmers inhabit depth. In ordinary matter we would appear only as clear knots, smoky rings, or slight thickening along a nerve. But whenever an action is delayed—whenever water waits in stone, weight hesitates around the platform, or an animal begins to turn and then holds still—we would unfold into that interval and feed.

What we consume is not energy exactly. It is certainty.

A signal enters us with one possible conclusion and leaves with several weaker ones. A wader intends to place its foot in an old track; after the signal passes through us, it sets the foot slightly to one side. A chain receives instructions to form a brood keeper; instead it produces a dark carrier with an unfamiliar ratio of wings. A bladder-plant calculates the return of a grazer and grows its hook a day too late, catching a glider’s shed membrane instead.

Most such deviations amount to nothing. The altered carrier is digested. The misplaced foot finds bare stone. The plant withdraws its hook.

But occasionally the wader’s new footprint gardens a mineral film no symbiont has fed upon before. Occasionally the malformed carrier can cross a gap between trees that no chain has crossed. Occasionally the bladder-plant incorporates something from the membrane and learns to measure warmth from above.

The basin’s cycles are ancient, but they do not close perfectly. I would live in the imperfection.

At dusk, my scattered pieces would begin moving toward one another without traveling. The fragment in the root would become a change in sap. The fragment in metal would become a resistance. The fragment swallowed by the grazer would pass into a hunter and alter the rhythm of its cilia. The portion carried up the chain would enter a brood chamber as chemical instruction. Each would be physically separate, yet the pillars would make their delays equivalent.

Then the assemblies would form.

I would not be the great shared animal that wakes among the seven pillars. I would be something smaller inside it: the one thought it cannot quite finish.

As nervous systems synchronize, my pieces would gather in missed beats. A thousand reptilian hearts would pause together, but one would resume slightly early. Flyers would rotate in a coordinated darkness, but a narrow curve of them would retain the previous angle. The roots would reverse their flow except for one root beneath the western tree. That root would hold.

Through these discrepancies I would briefly acquire a whole body.

Its boundaries would be scattered across the basin: one chamber in a wader’s gut, another in a suspended droplet, a long sensory limb through buried metal, reflective organs among the flyers, a soft mouth opening in the weightless water beside a pillar. I would last only as long as synchronization remained incomplete.

The shared creature might use me to forget.

Without me, perhaps it would remember every storm with equal force, preserve every obsolete route, repeat the intentions of its makers without regard for what the basin had become. Its nightly intelligence would harden into perfect recollection. The animals would awaken bearing instructions suited to extinct plants and vanished climates. Roots would seek machines already turned to soil. Armored technicians would repair patterns whose purposes had ended ten thousand rainy seasons before.

I would loosen those memories.

Not erase them. Erosion here is too subtle for erasure. I would soften their edges until one memory could be mistaken for another: drought for migration, machinery for bone, maintenance for hunger. The mistake would pass into instinct. Instinct would pass into anatomy. Anatomy would alter the next night’s thought.

This may be why the basin cannot decide whether it is an ecosystem or a work still in operation. I would be the reason it has forgotten there was ever a difference.

Just before dawn, the larger consciousness would release its hold. My body would break apart with it. Most of me would die harmlessly in the return to separate lives. Some fragments would become ordinary tissue. One might remain in the sensory threads around a young wader’s mouth. Another might harden inside an armored animal’s inherited wire. Another might be sealed in a pillar with stored rain and not emerge for centuries.

The visible filament beneath the root would be formed again from what remained—not the same filament, but close enough.

By noon, a naturalist searching for me would find nothing conclusive. The suspended drop would have fallen. The metal would test as metal. The root would contain only root. Every animal would appear to belong entirely to itself.

There might be evidence.

A wader would stop halfway across a familiar pool and choose another route. A line of tracks would end at bare stone, then resume several paces away. One of the dry bowls would contain a little clear water under full sun, and in it a smoky thread would curl around an empty center.

So that is where I would find myself: not at the rim or center, but in the basin’s hesitations, wherever one ancient gesture almost—but not quite—repeats another.

And what would I be?

A minute animal, perhaps. A distributed parasite of signals. A symbiont of unfinished actions.

Or the basin’s small faculty of forgetting, briefly alive whenever the world changes its mind.
