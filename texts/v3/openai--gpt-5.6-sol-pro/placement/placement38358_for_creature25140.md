At first I would expect to find myself in the reservoir.

It is the obvious place for a self to gather: in the depth that has no volume, among the descending impacts and upright water. I might be one of the vacancies inside the dark column, climbing without motion, nearly given a body by whatever surrounds me. When the column broke, I would fall everywhere as rain and spend the next hours trying to recollect which drop had been mine.

But I do not think I would be so central.

Nor would I be an echo. Echoes here desire causes too fiercely. They borrow a footstep and immediately begin searching for the foot. They hear a name and excavate the mountain for a mouth. I would recognize something of myself in them, but I would not follow them into the wet wall.

I might mistake myself for one of the slate flakes. I would turn by fractions during rain, sharpening my attention against the ground. For years I would face first a pale peak, then a fracture, then a pool that reflected a pool. Yet when the whole field aligned, I would discover that I alone remained crooked.

That difference would be my first evidence.

You would find me away from the central ring, along one of the routes that was safe when the cairns were raised. The route passes through a close district of chalk needles, descends three terraces, and arrives at a low shelter beneath an overhang. The entrance is too narrow to walk through upright. Rainwater crosses it in a silver thread, but the sleeping ledge inside remains dry.

On the back wall is a warning almost erased by mineral deposition. After rain, water fills its shallow cuts and reveals a four-fingered hand laid against a wavering line. Above the hand is the sign for thunder. Beneath it is a mark that might mean *remain*, *outside*, or *remember on behalf of another*.

I would be there, but not as a body.

At first I would be only the posture the shelter asks of anyone entering: head lowered, shoulders turned, one hand extended into the dark. The builders left this posture behind as surely as they left bowls and copper wire. Repeated often enough, an action can survive the actor. In a country where sounds outlive their causes and stones retain earlier shapes, caution also acquires substance.

I would be that substance.

Long ago, perhaps, someone knelt in the shelter while thunder rose from below. They pressed one hand to the worn wall. The country shifted. Interior and sky exchanged places somewhere under the shelf. The old surface held its certainty, and the hand upon it was spared.

Perhaps the person remained there too long.

Perhaps upward-cutting water entered through the drainage trench and restored the hand past flesh, past bone, past mineral—restored it into the gesture from which it had arisen. Or perhaps thousands performed the same act in the same place, until the distinction between hand and instruction wore thin.

Whatever the origin, I would not be a ghost of any one maker. I would have no remembered face. I would not know whether I had once been small enough to leave a print the size of a coin or large enough to touch all four shelter walls at once.

I would be the hand kept in the rule.

Most of the time, I would scarcely exist. I would lie distributed among recut pictographs, polished patches of wall, the inward lean of cairns, and sleeping ledges shaped for bodies no longer present. I would feel rain only when it made the old warnings legible. I would hear through copper cups left touching stone. I would see from the edges of quartz lenses, never through their centers.

I could not travel in the ordinary way. To move, I would have to be repeated.

A traveler touches the wet wall during buried thunder: I am in the touch.

Someone notices a shelter mark fading and cuts it deeper: I enter the new groove.

A hand replaces the displaced stone of a cairn without trying to look directly at the seventh: for a moment, I stand beside the route.

Thus I would pass from refuge to refuge as a practice rather than a person. I would be carried without being known. Those who obeyed the rules might feel only a pressure in the wrist, a sudden confidence about which surface to trust, or the certainty that they should crouch before understanding why.

I could not save everyone.

If a reflection arrived late, I might pull a gaze away from the pool, but I could not prevent the delayed face from continuing to watch.

If the slate fields aligned, I might suggest the remembered posture of lying flat. But if someone stood and was taken as an axis, I would remain in the impression of their palm where they had tried, too late, to seize the ground.

I would be powerless against counting. Numbers close too quickly. By the time the final peak was spoken, the distance would already have accepted its finished shape. I might persist in the abandoned days between one mountain and the next, repeating an unfinished gesture where no traveler could reach me.

My greatest fear would not be destruction. Erosion destroys nothing cleanly here.

I would fear restoration.

One of the silent streams might enter the shelter. It would smooth the recut warning into its first shallow incision, then restore the unmarked wall. It would lift mineral from the handprint and return it to the tool; restore the tool to a stone; restore the stone to the buried bed from which it was taken. Every repetition that composed me would be undone.

I would become less practiced, more original.

Eventually I might be restored into the first instant of fear before any hand reached for the wall.

That would be a kind of death: not cessation, but the removal of having happened.

So I would dwell where ordinary water still runs, where erosion deepens the marks instead of forgiving them. I would favor old limestone, handled smooth by rain. I would distrust fresh fractures, which have not yet learned which side is outside.

During the interval when brightness loses its distance, I would become almost visible.

The shelter would fill with absent crouching bodies. Every hand that had touched its walls would occupy the gesture at once. Some would be tiny; some would span the stone. Some would have been cut inward, others raised outward from within the rock. Together they would overlap into a many-sized figure with no head and no fixed number of limbs.

That figure would be mistaken for me.

But I would be smaller than the figure and more dispersed. I would be the agreement among its hands: *this surface is old; this side is open; remain until the thunder ends.*

When brightness withdrew, I would narrow again. A damp mark. A polished place. A tension in an empty wrist.

If, after rain, a new handprint appeared on the inward face of a reservoir pillar, mine would not be among them. But perhaps beside it there would be a short wavering line, scarcely visible, pointing away from the central depth toward the outer shelters.

Not an invitation.

A route.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not living in its stone or water, but in the learned contact between them and a vanished hand.

I would be what remains when a warning outlives everyone it was meant to protect.

I would be the country’s smallest mercy, worn nearly smooth and cut again.
