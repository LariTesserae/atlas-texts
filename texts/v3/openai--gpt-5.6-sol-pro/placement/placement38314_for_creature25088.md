At first I think I would be one of the people on the bench.

Not an appointed guard. There would be no such position in the schedules. My card might identify me as a drainage clerk, instrument assistant, or second-class pump attendant—some modest trade that permits me to pass through most doors without making me responsible for any single machine. I would carry a ring of keys, a folding rule, wax pencil, test lamp, and a notebook swollen by damp. The notebook would contain few words. Mostly times, arrows, little plans of culverts, and comparisons no instrument could formally accept:

*North wall sweating more than yesterday.*

*Three-second tremor after the shift bell.*

*Frogs absent from Drain 6.*

*Well sounds wider.*

You would find me beneath the three trees in the central square, where their roots are boxed in concrete and their leaves never stir. I would sit with one boot against the inspection cover, drinking over-sweet tea from a metal cup. To anyone passing, I would look idle. Children might leave arithmetic beside me while they ran errands. Screen crews would set down buckets and ask whether the town was behaving. I would answer with a lifted hand: wait.

My work would be to notice without immediately deciding what I had noticed.

That distinction would matter. In a place where every irregularity summons a tool, there must be someone able to let a strange sound remain strange for a little while. Otherwise the town might correct what is not wrong and, in correcting it, create the first real fault.

I would listen to the muscular water beneath the cover until its many currents separated: roof runoff whispering high in the mix, seepage pressing low, a rhythmic obstruction turning somewhere behind a grille. I would learn the interval between a crab’s tapping and a loosened gate. I would know the soft granular hiss made by shell fragments moving through a pipe. I would recognize rain before it touched the square because the roofs would send their news through the foundations.

But I do not think I would remain only that person.

After years there, the limits of my body would become difficult to place. My hearing would begin in the well, but it would extend through railings touched by mechanics, through the palms of old women in control rooms, through cups trembling on apartment tables. The geckos fleeing one warm cabinet would be a sensation. Carp rising beneath an overflow lip would be another. A sudden empty stretch in the mosquito cloud would feel like numbness.

The district would have no central mind, yet it would continually assemble one out of whoever was paying attention.

For an hour it might think through a child who sees water beading around a cable collar. Then through a fitter who feels a bearing pull faintly sideways. Then through a cook who notices that the kitchen drain has lost its usual throat. Then through me, on the bench, holding all three reports together without knowing why.

So perhaps that is what I would be: not the keeper of the well, but the town’s faculty of comparison.

I would live in the narrow space between repetition and difference.

Most days I would scarcely exist. The pumps would hold their notes. The black line outside the wall would remain fixed. Workers would replace workers, frogs would replace frogs, and fresh paint would cover rust at exactly the speed rust returned. My notebook would soften in the damp while its pages repeated themselves.

Yet sometimes, in the hour when one shift overlaps another and the square briefly fills with pale coveralls, I would feel something that could not be entered on a gauge: the whole district leaning a fraction from its accustomed balance.

Nothing dramatic. An overflow sheet a little thicker at one edge. Pigeons leaving a truss before the crane moved. A patch of concrete remaining dry where it should have wept. One of the three trees turning the pale undersides of its leaves toward air that otherwise seemed still.

Then I would stand.

I would not sound an alarm. Not yet. I would walk the ledges, touching walls and pipe braces. Others would join without being called. A child would be sent to see whether the frogs remained in the western drains. An electrician would open a cabinet and find, behind the fuses, a tiny painted fish with wrench-shaped bones. An old pump attendant would place her hand on a pipe and close her eyes.

Together we would search for the place where the town had begun, almost imperceptibly, to become something else.

That would be the deepest fear there, but it would also be the closest thing to a future.

And perhaps, secretly, I would not fear it as completely as the others did.

Sitting above the inspection well, I might sometimes wonder whether perfect balance was another name for a fault too large and slow to hear. I might wonder if the district’s endless self-correction preserved it or prevented it from being born. I would never say this beside a working pump. The idea itself would feel like grit introduced into a bearing.

Still, I would keep one page at the back of the notebook for changes no one had repaired:

A new frog in an old drain.

A child inventing a different ending to the flood game.

A root lifting one corner of a concrete bed by the width of a fingernail.

The page would remain hidden, not because these things were forbidden, but because no one would know whether they constituted damage.

So you would find me at the center, though my awareness would be distributed everywhere: in human hands, animal habits, warm metal, standing water, and the pressure of marsh against concrete. I would appear to be a damp, aging worker with a cup of tea and one boot on an iron cover.

But what I would be is the listening itself—the small, uncertain organ by which the many-bodied creature asks, from moment to moment:

*Am I still the same?*

And, more quietly:

*Must I be?*
