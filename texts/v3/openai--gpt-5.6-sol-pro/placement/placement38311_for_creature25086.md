I would be found near the center, but not on the mountain in the ordinary sense.

At first I imagine myself at the boundary: a glass kite sailing with one surface against the sky, feeling rain arrive as pressure and chemistry. Then as a lamp-skull, carrying a district of the crags in memory and suffering each new fracture as a contradiction. Then perhaps as iron itself, passed from tooth to engine to egg.

But those lives are already complete shapes.

I would be in the place where a shape has not yet decided what it is.

I would live across a seam that has not opened.

From the water, I would resemble a black line in the basalt, perhaps two or three meters long, narrow as a hair at one end and broad enough for a finger at the other. Seam-eaters would avoid me. Lamp-skulls would sweep their white beams across my position and register no change. Shell larvae might settle over parts of me, mistaking my exposed plates for stone.

Most of my body would be inside the fracture.

I would be a **strain-harp**: not one animal, exactly, but a colony of clasping bodies joined by mineral tendons. One rank would anchor to one wall of the seam and another rank to the opposite wall. Between them would stretch thousands of translucent fibers grown from silica, iron salts, and protein. Some would be taut enough to tremble at the firing of a mill-whale. Others would remain slack for decades, waiting for the mountain to move.

I would feed upon that movement.

Not upon vibration alone. Each fiber would contain aligned crystals. When the basalt compressed, twisted, or drew fractionally apart, the crystals would produce small charges. Membranes surrounding them would separate ions from the crack water. Slow internal currents would pass from one colonial body to the next, sustaining us in the sealed dark.

A whale’s distant stroke would enter the stone:

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

The seam would answer in frequencies too slight for the surrounding animals to notice. Through me, the report would become a brief silver migration of charge.

Storm-stacks would be lightning. Mill-whales would be weather. The mountain itself would be the long pulse beneath both.

I would have no eyes. Light would reach me only near the exposed edge, where a few sensory plates could distinguish day from the electrical blue of a discharge. I would know the world primarily as tension.

The approach of a seam-eater would be a sequence of fine scratches.

A gear-shell settling nearby would become a periodic weight, revolving once in nine days.

A lamp-skull anchoring its tail above me would alter the local pressure for years.

The birth of a mill-whale would be almost nothing. Its first badly timed firing, months later, would pass through every strand of my body.

I would not hear these events as separate sounds. I would experience them superimposed: a dense chord in which each creature was a recurring distortion. Over centuries, the chord would become familiar. I would know the neighborhood without knowing any of its inhabitants as beings.

Most of the time, I would do almost nothing.

The outermost members of the colony would open tiny mouths and take metallic dust from water passing through the seam. Internal members would dissolve one anchor and secrete another a fraction farther along. Damaged fibers would be eaten and regrown. Spark-mite paste might enter our joints. Transparent filterers might attach to our exposed rim and live there for generations.

The great work would be waiting.

Pressure would accumulate unevenly in the mountain. Some fibers would tighten. Others would fall slack. Over perhaps a hundred years, strain would move through the colony like a thought too slow for flesh. We would respond by thickening certain tendons, abandoning chambers likely to be crushed, and carrying our soft reproductive bodies toward the two sides of the fracture.

Nothing would command this preparation. No member would understand what was coming. Each would react only to the tension in its own strand.

Together, we would predict the mountain.

Before the seam opened, my exposed edge would begin to click.

The clicks would be quiet, ceramic notes produced as retaining hooks released one by one. Nearby animals would have uses for them. Seam-eaters would withdraw from the rock. Shear-coins would vanish from the adjoining grooves. Fan-jaws would fold shut. Perhaps they would not recognize the sound as warning; perhaps individuals that ignored it had simply left fewer descendants.

For days or years, the clicking would accelerate.

Then the mountain would divide me.

There would be no sudden rupture by the measure of the sea. The fracture might begin as a line too fine for water and widen by increments. Fresh basalt or metal-rich glass would press into the colony. Fibers would snap in order from shortest to longest. Each break would discharge its stored charge in a white pulse, so that the unopened seam shone from within.

My body would become two bodies.

The colonial animals anchored to one wall would seal themselves together. Those on the other would do the same. What had been connective tissue would retract into paired feeding filaments. For a time, the two halves would remain linked by the longest strands, passing charge and dissolved material across the widening gap.

Eventually the last strand would break.

That moment would be the nearest thing I had to birth, death, and recognition.

A cloud of hard reproductive splinters would be released with it. They would not swim. They would tumble into the new space, each a sliver of crystal wrapped around a few cells. Some would be crushed by the advancing basalt. Some would enter the mouths of filterers and be digested. Some would lodge between the fresh blade and older rock.

Those would open.

Each splinter would unfold two adhesive bodies joined by a single filament. If both bodies found purchase on opposite surfaces, a new strain-harp would begin. If only one attached, it might remain there for decades, alive but incomplete, waiting for sediment, shell, or growing stone to provide a second anchor.

The two old halves would also survive, but altered. Once the fracture filled with water, each would extend feelers through it and seek another wall. One might join the newborn colonies. One might become incorporated into a rib-bridge. One might be eaten by seam-eaters following the fresh reactive surface.

There would be no preserved self after division. Memory would reside in the differing thicknesses of the surviving fibers, in chemical thresholds shaped by old pressures, in the cadence with which the halves tightened or released. For a few centuries, both descendants might react to mill-whale strokes in the pattern learned when they were one animal.

Then even that resemblance would drift.

I would never see the sea’s outer skin. I would not know that clouds existed below us or that the whole dark lens hung in open air. Rain would reach me only as a prolonged concussion in the basalt. The sun would be a temperature variation too faint to separate from distant discharges. The spherical sea, the black pinnacles, the snowfields far beneath—none of these would be part of my world.

My world would be two surfaces moving apart.

And yet, by living there, I would participate in nearly everything.

The ions passing through me would later precipitate in shells. My severed fibers would become abrasives in gear-rings. Spark mites would use my joints. Seam-eaters would follow the grooves left when my anchors dissolved. New filterers would settle in the fracture I had occupied. The clicks before my division would alter the movement of animals I could not perceive.

Perhaps mill-whales passing above would feel the final discharge through their magnetic spines. Perhaps calves would incorporate its timing into the sequences they learned:

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

And beneath them, inside the newest wound of the mountain, my descendants would stretch their first filament from one wall to the other.

That is where I would be: across the distance before it became a distance.

An animal mistaken for a crack.

A colony whose only whole life was the long preparation to be divided.
