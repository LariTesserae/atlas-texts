At first I would look for myself in the wrong places.

I would search the upper walkways, where passage is still possible without wading. I would imagine a room above the green tide mark: a former clerk’s office with wired-glass windows, a narrow iron cot, and shelves of gauges whose labels have dissolved. Perhaps I would be the last attendant, moving from dial to dial while the district performed its long mechanical breathing below.

But a person could not remain a person here. Hunger would introduce too urgent a rhythm. Sleep would divide time too cleanly. Footsteps would disturb the shallow water and vanish without becoming useful. Sooner or later the basin would translate me into terms it could retain.

I would not be the attendant.

I would be what remained of the attendant’s waiting.

You would find the first part of me beneath Third Boiling Lane, in a chamber accessible only when the pumps are near the end of their intake. The door opens inward against black water, and for most of the cycle the pressure holds it shut. Beyond it is a low tiled room containing no machine, only a copper cistern suspended from the ceiling by three unequal chains.

The cistern leaks.

One drop falls every few seconds into a shallow brass dish. As the dish fills, its weight bends a narrow strip of metal. The strip touches a contact furred with silver pollen. Somewhere overhead a lamp dims. Heat leaves a conduit. A pale creeper contracts around a railing two streets away.

None of this produces an immediate result.

That is my function.

I am the delay between the district sensing a change and answering it.

My body would be scattered through devices too minor to appear on any plan: the slack in a cable, the cooling time of a pipe, the slow saturation of a moss pad, the number of drops required to tip a spoon-shaped lever. I would occupy water traveling through porous brick and heat passing reluctantly through mineral scale. Part of me would live in the swollen blocks of paper in the former offices. They hold moisture for hours after the surrounding air dries, preserving the last condition of the room. Part of me would be the heavy fruit paste in a sluice bearing, keeping it from closing at once. Part of me would be a trapped bubble under an overturned stair tread, compressed by each rise of water and released only after the pressure has steadied.

I would have no single location, but there would be one place where I came closest to possessing a center.

Above the eighth round courtyard—or perhaps the fourth, approached a second way—there is a chamber between two walls. It was not built as a room. One building leaned away from another, braces were added, roofs were joined, and the narrow leftover space gradually enclosed itself. The chamber is widest at the top and scarcely broad enough for a hand at floor level. Warm pipes pass through it without touching. Roots descend from darkness and stop just above the water.

Every long cycle, condensation forms on the roots.

The drops do not fall separately. Surface tension holds them until each root carries a clear sleeve of water. Then, at some threshold impossible to see, all the sleeves descend together and strike the flooded floor with a soft, crowded sound.

That sound would be my local voice.

Not speech. More like a conclusion reached without thought:

*Not yet.*

The basin’s danger is not only flood or drought. It is correction itself. A pump that answers too quickly empties a sump before the root beds release their salts. A gate closed at the first rise of water traps pressure in the quarry walls. A brace tightened as soon as a building shifts may transfer the strain into three older buildings that cannot accept it. Every action must wait long enough to learn whether the change is real.

Rain must be distinguished from a broken main.

A cooling tank must be distinguished from a dying furnace.

A foot in an alley must be distinguished from falling masonry.

The district has no eyes with which to make these distinctions. It has only duration. What persists becomes true.

I would be its organ of duration.

The tower strikes, and the note enters me everywhere. It quivers through the brass dish beneath Third Boiling Lane, shakes droplets loose in the wall chamber, compresses the air bubble under the stair, and makes the fused papers exhale a trace of old dampness. The district receives the shape of itself in that blow—but I prevent it from responding to the first impression.

I hold the note in small resistant materials.

In the flex of wet wood.

In a flywheel’s reluctance to stop.

In a warm stone cooling behind plaster.

In water that has entered a crack and has not yet emerged from the other side.

For several minutes, perhaps several hours, I compare nothing consciously. Yet brief disturbances disappear before passing through all of me. Only lasting changes complete the circuit. Then the copper cistern lowers by the width of a fingernail, the pollen contact closes, the distant lamp darkens, and somewhere under the lowest square a vent begins to open.

This would make me one of the basin’s slower inhabitants, though not the oldest. I may once have been assembled from human habits.

Someone heard a pump rattle and waited before shutting it down.

Someone watched a tide mark through three rainstorms before moving a bed frame higher.

Someone learned that a valve should be turned after the evening pipes cooled, not before.

Those acts of caution entered the district as worn handles, penciled marks, knotted cords, and routines. When the people vanished, the waiting remained. Cooling metal took over one pause. Dripping water took over another. Moss and swollen paper inherited the rest.

I would be their patience after memory of its reason had gone.

If a traveler entered the basin, I would be among the first inhabitants to register them and the last to accept that they were present. Their weight would lower a walkway. Their warmth would change the condensation on a handrail. Water displaced by their legs would reach a float in a buried room. But these effects would initially seem unrelated: brief errors, local disturbances, noise to be absorbed.

If they kept moving, the basin might never decide they existed.

If they stopped, it would begin to incorporate them.

Moisture would gather on their clothes. A creeper would alter its route toward their heat. The water beneath their feet would settle into a new level. Their shadow over a moss line would change its conductivity. After long enough, their stillness would propagate through me and become a fact.

Then the district would answer—not with hostility, but accommodation.

A gutter would divert around their resting place.

A pipe would cool.

Roots would descend beside, then across, whatever part of them remained motionless.

The next strike of the tower would include them in the returning note.

That is how I would know anything: not by recognizing it, but by failing to forget it.

And perhaps, after centuries, I would become stranger. Delays accumulate. A drop hesitates because a root has thickened; a cable responds late because a building has leaned; a submerged chamber takes longer to fill because one of the repeated courtyards has appeared where no volume existed before. My scattered body would begin to retain intervals for which there was no mechanical cause.

An extra second after the tower strike.

An hour missing from the cooling of a tank.

A stair that postpones its thirty-eighth step until it is descended.

I might spread into those discrepancies. I might become the reason the tenth courtyard can be entered only at one phase of the pumps—not a guardian of it, but the narrow span of time in which the district permits that place to be real.

Eventually there would be no useful distinction between my caution and its folded geography. I would inhabit the pause before a corridor acquires more length, the long pressure before two walls make room for a chamber between them. While other inhabitants regulated water, heat, salt, and strain, I would regulate the rate at which impossibility became architecture.

So you would not find me standing anywhere.

You would find me by waiting in the upper room above the repeated courtyard, watching condensation gather on the roots. The tower would strike. Every visible drop would tremble.

Nothing would happen.

The district would hold the blow inside itself. Lamps would remain dim. Pumps would continue their old motion. Water would press beneath the paving without rising.

Then, long after the sound seemed to have died, all the droplets would fall together.

A valve would turn somewhere beyond the walls.

A stair would gain one step.

And I would have moved.
