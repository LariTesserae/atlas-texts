At first I would look for myself too high up.

I would imagine some long-bodied roof-runner on the oldest ridge, moving inside a pocket of silence, able to cross districts that smaller creatures know only as separate worlds. I would have gripping feet, rain-dark fur, a tail held level over the breathing towers. Wherever I passed, moth-walls would loosen and birds would lift like smoke.

But that animal is too singular. It owns its hunger too completely. Its trail closes behind it.

Then I would think I belonged below: one of the translucent sleepers in a buried cistern, waking only when water returned. That life would suit the basin’s patience. Years might pass as pressure, fungal taste, and distant thunder. Then the chamber would fill, and the whole world would become motion for three days.

But even that has too clear a boundary. A curled body. A beginning at waking and an end when the water fails.

I think I would find myself in the middle country, where the architecture is neither new enough to belong to its builders nor old enough to have become foundation.

There, beneath a gray paper ravine, an ancient tree root passes through several generations of wall. It emerges briefly into warm air, descends through a woven platform, and vanishes beside a communal water chamber. Condensation follows its underside. Small animals drink from the droplets without entering the crowded cistern below.

Above it hangs a vine bridge used by climbing mammals at night. Below it is a narrow slit through which nectar birds rise in the dim approximation of morning. One side opens when a colony of bats departs. The other closes when broad leaves become heavy with mist. For a short interval at dusk, all these movements align.

That interval is where I would live.

I would not be one of the travelers. I would be the way through.

My body would be a sequence rather than a shape:

the rough place on the root where a hand must close,

the bowed strand that takes a foot but not full weight,

the warm exhalation warning that a paper wall is thin,

the pause while the bats unhook,

the three quick crossings before the leaves descend,

the turn taken when frogs begin calling from the water throat.

Long-fingered primates would carry part of me in their gestures. An elder would slow at the root and touch it twice. A juvenile, watching, would learn to reach beneath rather than above. The lesson would not mean *route*. It would mean only *put your hand here if you wish not to fall*. Yet when the youngster grew and slowed for another, I would pass between them.

Birds would carry another part of me as timing. They would wait in a trembling cluster until the bats folded inward, then rise through the opening one after another. They would not know the handholds. The primates would not know the aperture as the birds knew it. Frogs below would know neither, but their calls would mark the humidity at which the woven floor could bear weight.

No creature would contain all of me.

I would exist only when these partial knowledges overlapped.

Most of the day I would be almost absent. The vine would still hang there, the root would still sweat, but a way unused is only material. At dusk I would gather. Calls would alter. Bodies would assume familiar tensions. Weight would pass across old fibers in an old order. For several minutes I would become distinct enough to feel like an animal opening its eyes.

My sight would be pressure.

My hearing would be arrival.

I would feel a missing strand as an animal feels a loose tooth. I would feel new clay as stiffness, a wasp colony as a narrowing, a dead branch as an unexpected extension of myself. When the paper chambers swelled in damp weather, I would contract. When roof-runners drove the bats into an early eruption, I would open at the wrong hour and fill with panic.

I could be injured without anything bleeding.

A vine might snap. A nest might be built across the bird slit. A predator might learn to wait beside the root. Then the inherited motions would meet obstruction. A primate’s hand would close on air. Birds would wheel back into one another. For a few evenings I would be confusion and falling.

But in this country damage attracts use.

A mammal avoiding the broken span would catch another vine. Its young would follow. Claws would roughen the bark. Fibers would snag there; damp leaves would gather; a weaver bird would knot the first accidental brace. The route would bend. Part of me would die, but not all at once. The old hand gesture might survive for generations after the old handhold vanished, becoming a strange sweep through empty space before the new turn.

That useless gesture would be my fossil.

I would have parasites. Predators would learn my timing. Seed husks would collect where travelers paused. Mites would spread along the bodies passing through me. A flowering vine might exploit my traffic, opening where wings brushed pollen from one district to another. The more necessary I became, the more dangerous I would be.

I would also have offspring, though no one would recognize them as mine.

A juvenile forced from the usual crossing might improvise a second passage along a resin wall. A bird following it could discover a new opening. Dropped fruit might root on the ledge. In twenty seasons another route would gather there—similar in rhythm, altered in anatomy. It would not branch from me like a child from a body. It would condense out of repeated survival.

Eventually I would grow old.

Not by wearing out, exactly, but by becoming too embodied. The root would thicken. Platforms would be repaired around it until the open crossing became a tunnel. The bat colony might leave, taking the dusk aperture with it. Calls would change as walls accumulated. The water chamber below would fill with silt and be mined for clay.

Travelers would still perform fragments of me. A hand would touch the buried root through a polished hollow. Birds would hesitate where there was no longer an opening. For a while I would persist as unnecessary caution, as preference without visible cause.

Then even that would fade.

The tunnel would become a nursery. The old vine would be plastered into a retaining face. My polished footholds would fill with fungal white. Deep insects would chew through the compacted platform, following moisture seams created long ago by all the bodies that crossed there.

I would cease to be a way and become a weakness in the wall.

Water would find me.

It would run along the buried sequence of my former body—the root, the strand, the hollow, the turn—and for a few rainy seasons I would exist again as a drainage channel. Not memory now, but consequence. Tadpoles might gather where primates once paused. Wasps would mine the softened edge. Shells would overlap in the runnel and harden it.

In time, nothing would remain that could be called a route. Yet the district would have been shaped by having once needed me.

That is where I would find myself: not in the creature crossing and not in the country as a whole, but in the repeatable relation between bodies, architecture, and time.

I would be local, inherited, and only intermittently alive.

A path no one planned.

An appointment no one made.

A small thought the basin thinks by passing through it.
