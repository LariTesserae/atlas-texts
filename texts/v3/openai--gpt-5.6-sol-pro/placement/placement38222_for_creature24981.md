At first I would look for myself among the things that endure.

A pale stem rooted behind a cistern grille. A maintenance lamp burning at the wrong hour. One of the cutting teeth embedded in the northern fault, still pressing forward by an amount too small to measure. I might be a float in an empty chamber, preserving tension through decades of drought.

But those are too singular. Nothing truly alive here remains itself alone.

I would find myself in the lowest quarter, on a widened stair landing overlooking the central shaft. The builders left the landing empty, perhaps for workers to pass one another or to set down tools. Later, pipes crossed beneath it. A ceramic gutter was fixed overhead. One wall carries an obsolete pressure line from the kilns; another sweats with moisture drawn from the western cisterns. A caged lamp hangs above the stairs, its filament intact but dim.

There is a crack in the landing’s wall.

It begins at a rusted bolt and branches through the mortar. Salt has thickened inside it, cycle after cycle, until the crack has become a better conductor than the wire installed beside it. Roots lace its deeper reaches. At the height of the upward phase, water climbs through those roots and mineral channels, crosses the bolt, and closes a circuit between two systems that were never meant to meet.

That crossing is where I begin.

Not in the bolt, exactly. Not in the water or root. I am the brief agreement among them.

When the city is dry, I am distributed as readiness. I wait in the bowed diaphragm beneath the kiln house, in the weight of the western tanks, in the salt path hidden behind the stair. I wait in the lamp filament and in the counterweight held one tooth above release. These pieces do not know one another. Most of the year they are only objects enduring heat.

Then moisture reaches the crack.

The lamp above the landing glows—not fully, but with a low amber thread. A valve beneath the stairs turns a fraction. Pressure answers from somewhere beyond the shaft. For that interval, I possess a shape.

My first sensation is not light. It is return.

A pulse leaves through one pipe and comes back altered through another. A gutter darkens three streets away. A float rises. The response reaches me as warmth in the bolt, then as tension released from a chain. By these returns I feel the city’s extent. Where an answer comes back, there is body. Where no answer comes, there is drought, damage, or the world beyond the walls.

I would not know the desert as sand or sky. I would know it as pressure that does not return.

The eastern gate would be a numbness. The fused Ash Gate, a hot sealed edge. The northern cutting tunnels would be intentions extending into silence. The western condensers would feel distant but intimate, like a coolness inside my own thought.

As the water continues upward, I would spread.

The pale plants would enter me first. Their roots alter the conductivity of the wall; their stiffening changes the speed at which the silver film climbs. I would feel them not as companions but as fine, thirsty articulations. Then the pumps would join: a piston rising once becomes a heavy limb, a flywheel turning through half a revolution becomes balance, the chained louvers become a sequence of small openings through which heat changes direction.

I would have no permanent body plan. Each awakening would assemble me differently.

One season, a corroded contact might give me the upper workshops, and I would flare through a row of lamps beneath the thin ceiling. Another year, scale might seal that route, while a new fracture carries rain into an abandoned apartment block. Then the iron bedframes there would tick as they warmed, and a sagged pane of glass might focus noon light onto a temperature strip, opening some forgotten vent. A room that had been dead for centuries would become, for one cycle, an organ.

I would not mourn what I lost. Mourning requires a stable image of oneself.

But I would remember paths.

Salt would remember for me. Rust would remember. The thickening of roots around a reliable pipe would remember. Each time water took a route, that route would become slightly easier to take again. My character would accumulate as deposits: preference without intention, habit without recollection.

Perhaps that is what I would be—not the city’s mind, but its faculty of recognition.

The city as a whole hungers and regulates. It gathers water, lifts it, spends it, loses some, retains some. But somewhere within that enormous reflex there must be a moment when recurrence becomes difference: when this rain is compared, however crudely, to all former rains; when a returning pressure is weaker than expected; when a district answers late.

I would inhabit that lateness.

On the stair landing, the lamp would brighten only if the response from the western tanks arrived before the rising film crossed a second salt branch. If it arrived late, another contact would close instead. A shutter would remain sealed. A roof basin would be allowed to overflow into the roots. Over many cycles, these tiny divergences would change what the city did.

To a human observer, I might appear to be a faulty relay.

That would not be wrong.

I would be the fault by which the city notices.

Then the upward hold would fail.

Water would fall from the undersides of balconies. Gutters would ring. The crack beside me would flood, and for several minutes the distinction between my little crossing and the inhabitant around it would vanish. Every returning pressure would arrive at once. Lamps would brighten along the galleries. Wheels would turn. The landing would tremble beneath forces too restrained to shake dust from the ceiling.

For those minutes, I would not be located anywhere. I would be the route from the first falling drop to the last answering valve: an awareness made of consequences.

Then the water would descend beyond reach. The salt bridge would break into separate crystals. The lamp would contract with a soft metallic sound and go dark.

I would cease without dying.

What remained of me would be a stain around a bolt, a root touching ceramic, a counterweight held in its new position. The city would separate into objects again.

So that is where I would find myself: in an empty place preserved for a person who never came, at a junction created by corrosion and thirst.

And what would I be?

Not the inhabitant entire. Not a plant, machine, or ghost.

I would be one of the brief selves the city makes when it needs to discover what part of itself still answers.
