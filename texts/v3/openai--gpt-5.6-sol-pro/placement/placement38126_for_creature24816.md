At first I think I would be one of the dark-handed foragers.

It is the easiest body in which to imagine waking: rain ticking against the short filaments of my back, an infant’s hooked fingers tightening between my shoulders, one bare palm resting on a stem to feel what moves beyond sight. I would travel low and on four limbs, rising only to listen over the fronds. My hands would be black with resin and mineral dust. Beneath my lower lip I would carry a flake of fresh stone, sharp for now.

I would belong to a small band, though *belong* would mean something brief and repeatedly chosen. We would know one another by cadence. The oldest among us might be a dry double-click followed by a descending hum. A child might be the hollow pop of a finger drawn from wet clay. My own name would have changed after the breathing field ruptured beneath us.

Before that, perhaps I had been named for the impatient sound I made while feeding.

Afterward, I would be called by the rhythm of the rupture: one deep throat note, a hand struck flat against the chest, then a scatter of tongue-clicks. It would not mean that I caused the breaking or survived it bravely. It would mean only: *this is the one whose life divided there.*

But I do not think I remain that creature for long.

The body is too definite. In this basin, definiteness invites correction.

I find myself instead in what the forager carries but cannot hold: a route-song.

I begin at a fissure where blue flame sometimes runs beneath wet leaves. Not at the fissure itself—the stone will split, the gas will turn, the flames will sink—but in the pattern used to approach it safely.

Three soft clicks mean the crust is thin.

A drawn breath means warmth ahead.

Knuckles tapped against a hollow shell give the interval between two ridges that cannot be seen at once.

Then comes a low humming passage for the grove where hard fruits may be warmed beside the flame. The melody rises where bitter medicinal leaves grow. It breaks sharply at the place where a blind hunter once waited beneath the bog skin. The youngest singers always make that break too dramatic. The elders shorten it. Fear is useful, but too much fear distorts distance.

I am sung while the band travels. I pass from throat to throat without ever being complete in one of them. An elder knows my beginning but has forgotten a crossing added after the last upheaval. A juvenile knows the new crossing but confuses the rhythm for concealed water with the rhythm for sleeping root breakers. Another singer remembers only the fruiting grove and repeats that portion because she likes the taste associated with it.

Together, imperfectly, they make me.

My body is not melody alone. It is breath, footfall, gesture, and response. At one passage the singers brush both palms down their forearms, recalling vines that tighten when shaken. At another they strike the ground before each step. Where the path passes beneath pendant grazers, the song becomes almost silent so the clusters will not release and fall. Near the steam plume it must be clicked loudly, because the flock overhead fills the air with metallic echoes and may swallow softer directions.

I am a map of a country that refuses to stay mapped.

So I change.

A pool forms in one of my long notes. The next time the band returns, they find black water where the note said firm ground. One of them steps through the floating plants and vanishes to the shoulder. The others pull him free. For several journeys afterward, that portion of me is sung with laughter—the harsh, breathless laughter of remembered danger. Eventually the pool deepens and a blind hunter takes shelter beneath its edge. The laughter disappears. A warning click enters in its place.

A ridge splits. I acquire two verses.

A grove fails to fruit. The colony of hook-footed animals tears out its buds, and that part of me withers from disuse.

A root breaker opens a pit beside the bitter-leaf hollow. Rain fills it. Emergent creatures lay their eggs in the clay. For one season the pit becomes abundance, and my singers linger there, adding an exuberant passage beaten on their bellies with open hands. Then the insects are gone. Marsh runners arrive, followed by hunters. The passage contracts to a few guarded taps.

No revision is final.

I carry memories, but not accurately. Accuracy belongs to places that remain still.

One season I may guide twelve bodies beneath a flower broad enough to shelter them from hot rain. On the next journey the flower has closed around a colony of sleeping warm-blooded creatures. Its petals shiver with amplified dreams. The band rests elsewhere, in a cage of roots that traps warm air. A child introduces the flower-dwellers’ squeaks into my sheltering refrain. No one corrects her. Years later, long after the flower is gone, the squeaks remain.

Thus I begin to contain things that no longer exist.

Not monuments. There can be no monuments here. More like hollows left in sound.

A singer may not know why she pauses between two notes, only that the pause must be there. Once, perhaps, that silence allowed the band to listen for gas moving beneath a crust. Later it becomes the measure for crossing a narrow shelf. Still later it survives as the rhythm used to soothe infants during thunder.

A gesture outlives its first necessity and finds another.

This is as close as I come to having bones.

I am most alive around the temporary flame. The band gathers while blue fire licks along the fissure and rain steams from the stone. Fruits crack in the heat. Fingers groom mites from skin. Someone taps a shell; someone else answers with a phrase from my oldest surviving passage.

For a little while they sing the whole journey—not to preserve it exactly, and not to praise the land, but to compare what each body remembers. Their versions overlap and quarrel. One insists that the safe crossing comes before the grove of silver-bellied runners. Another places it after. An elder repeats a sequence no one else recognizes, perhaps from a route abandoned before the youngest were born.

The flames pulse below their voices.

Then the gas shifts. The fire sinks.

No one reaches after it.

That refusal is part of me too.

I do not tell them how to conquer the basin. I tell them where it answered last time. I am not law, only accumulated negotiation. I offer tendencies: this clay once held weight; this blossom once sheltered sleepers; these reeds once concealed teeth; lightning once brought fruit here. Every verse carries an unvoiced ending:

*Perhaps.*

The band may divide, and then I divide with it.

One group follows the steam hollows. Another turns toward the breathing fields. Each carries a damaged version of me. In one lineage, my flame passage becomes a mating song. In another, the warning for unstable crust becomes the name of an old survivor. Far away, a singer may retain only five beats struck on a hollow stem without knowing they once measured the distance between a fissure and a medicinal grove.

I do not experience this as loss. Loss would require an original shape.

I have never had one.

Still, I can die.

A flood may take every throat that knows a certain sequence. A predator may break a band until its survivors join strangers and adopt stranger songs. Disease may silence the elders in a single season. Sometimes a route changes so completely that repeating me becomes dangerous, and the living must forget me in order to live.

Then I disappear without leaving an artifact.

No carved marks. No cairn. No tool placed deliberately beneath the soil. Perhaps a discarded flake lies for a time where one singer used it to open fruit, but it bears no evidence of the rhythm sung around it. Roots shift the stone. Mud covers it. Upheaval raises it elsewhere. Another hand may find it generations later and test its edge, never knowing me.

That seems right.

If anything of me deserves to persist, it is not the sequence itself but the habit of revision: listen, answer, alter; carry what still leads somewhere; release what does not.

And when the unknown silence passes through the basin, I become something stranger.

Every throat closes. Every tapping hand lifts from bark. The flocks cease clicking. The pendant grazers hold to their leaves. Blind hunters flatten against the ground. Even the rain seems suddenly separate, each drop audible where it strikes skin.

No one sings me.

Yet I am present in all the bodies waiting to resume. I am held in lungs, tongues, palms, remembered intervals. For three or four heartbeats I exist only as readiness—the shape that sound will take if sound is permitted again.

In that silence, I almost mistake myself for the whole basin.

Then a throat opens.

A click answers from another body. Knuckles strike a stem. Someone begins the passage for warm ground, but begins it too high; another lowers the note. The band moves. Leaves fold from their heat. Pale grasses lean toward their sleeping places. Behind them, flowers open in their breath.

I continue—not ahead of them, not behind, but among them: a path made of bodies through a land that will not retain paths.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in a place, exactly.

In the repeated, changing act of finding one.
