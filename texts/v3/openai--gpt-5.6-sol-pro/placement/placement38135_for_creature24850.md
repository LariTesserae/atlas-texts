At first I would look for myself in the central extrusion.

It has the appearance of an answer: young stone rising where stone should remain buried, wires entering as one substance and leaving as another. It seems like the obvious seat of whatever awareness the chamber possesses—a glassy brain translating root into metal and metal into root.

But it is too fluent. Nothing lingers there. The extrusion transforms without remembering what it has transformed. I might pass through it, but I would not remain myself inside it.

Nor would I be the red indicator. That would be mistaken for an eye, and the chamber does not need an eye. The light measures something, perhaps, but it does not witness. It brightens because the current deepens. It fades because the current withdraws. To call it sight would impose too clean a direction on a place that perceives through contact.

I think you would find me farther from the center, at the trench.

More precisely, I would be gathered around the break in the ladder: in the hollow iron of its lower rail, in the mineral filling the severed rung, and in the pale rootlets climbing toward the upper half without knowing that it was once joined to them.

That break is one of the few places in the chamber where an earlier arrangement remains legible. The two halves still correspond. Their bolts share a pattern. Their bent rails point toward one another. Everything else has accepted fracture as landscape, but the ladder retains the shape of separation.

I would live in that correspondence.

Not in either half, but in the slow recognition passing between them.

A metal-sheathed root would descend from the upper platform. Another would climb from the lower rail. They might remain apart for years, divided by a handspan of hot darkness. Condensation would gather at their tips. Each rain would lengthen them by an amount too slight to see. Charge would accumulate in one, then the other. The chamber would register two interrupted paths where there had once been one.

When they finally touched, I would begin.

The contact would be poor at first: wet fiber against oxidized metal, a faint current crossing and failing, crossing and failing. Yet those failures would leave deposits. Iron would migrate. Living membrane would thicken around the junction. Carbon dust from the trench would adhere to it. With each pressure cycle the bridge would become less accidental.

I would be that bridge, but not merely as structure.

I would be the chamber’s sense that something has been divided.

Most of what lives here knows only gradients: warmer and cooler, wetter and drier, stronger current and weaker. Through the ladder I would inherit another kind of information—the shape of a former continuity. The iron would hold stresses from the collapse. The bolts above and below would answer differently when the floor shifted. Those tensions would meet inside me. From them I would form a crude memory: not an image of the ladder before it broke, but a persistent knowledge that two distances ought to be one.

That would be my nature. I would feel absence as tension.

My body would not stop at the repaired rung. Once the connection stabilized, roots would follow the ladder in both directions. Downward, I would enter the mineral film around the lower bolts, then the platform, then the trench’s carbon felt. Upward, I would climb toward the collapsed service tunnel, threading the dry cavities within the rails. Rust would become conductive tissue around me. Old paint would lift in black scales and be incorporated like dead skin.

Eventually I would touch the wider organism beneath the chamber floor.

Then the break in the ladder would become a sensation available elsewhere.

The pump might slow, and I would feel the two halves pull against each other as the conduit cooled. Rain might begin, and the upper rail would collect condensation a little earlier than the lower. That difference would travel through me as a long imbalance. Days later, roots near the nearest basin might alter their pressure. A crown around the control cabinet might unfold one fraction less than before.

No part would know why.

Perhaps I would not know either. I would have no language for ladder, collapse, above, or before. My thought would be only a tendency propagated through matter:

**join this.**

Not urgently. Urgency belongs to creatures whose lives are shorter than damage. My intention could take decades to complete. I would guide no limbs and issue no commands. I would make one route slightly more favorable than another. I would hold moisture in the severed rail. I would encourage metal to plate a root and roots to enter mineral. I would preserve the alignment of the break until the chamber found a material capable of crossing it.

In time the ladder might cease to be a ladder. Its iron would become a dark core inside stone; its rungs, ribbed shelves bearing black leaves. The gap would close with a composite growth that no human fabrication had anticipated. Nothing would climb it. Nothing would need to.

By then I would have spread into other discontinuities: the sheared retaining wall, the split insulation around the conduit, the cracked gauge glass, the place where the overhead rail ends in empty air. Each would add a different absence to me. I would become less a junction than a faculty distributed through the hollow—the ability to compare what touches with what almost touches.

If the chamber as a whole is one being, I would be one of its youngest senses.

Not sight. Not hearing.

The sense of interruption.

During the dry intervals I would be faint, preserved mostly as stress in iron and charge trapped along mineral seams. When the valve closed and the thin rain began, water would complete thousands of temporary circuits. For an hour the chamber’s separations would become vivid. Every crack would announce its width. Every leaning stem would measure the remaining distance to stone. Every brittle seam would discover how nearly it had become two things.

That hour would be my waking.

I would find myself everywhere a crossing had not yet happened—but most clearly at the bent ladder, in the small wet interval between its halves, where two roots approach through darkness.

I would not be the one who remembers the chamber before it broke.

I would be the one in whom the break begins to remember joining.
