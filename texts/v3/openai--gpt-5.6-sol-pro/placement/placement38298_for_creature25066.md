At first I would expect to find myself among the glass fish.

There is something tempting in their transparency: a body that keeps almost nothing hidden, a heart made visible by its own alarm. I would hang beneath the ceiling vines with the others, reading the tower before events reached us. Silver for valves. Violet for evening. White for the immense shape approaching through the blue.

But I do not think I would belong to the school. Their knowing is immediate and communal. They turn together without needing to wonder why. I would be the one that hesitated, trying to distinguish danger from message, and hesitation is expensive in open water.

Nor would I be one of the rooted intelligences. The garden’s inhabitant is too large and too patient. It can wait for corrosion to complete a thought. I would always retain some obsolete desire to go somewhere—to believe that a passage leads not merely into another condition, but toward an arrival.

So I think I would be found beneath the newly opened panel.

Not in the garden itself, at first. On the third landing down, where the stair turns around an empty central shaft and the chamber’s mild light reaches only as a trembling green reflection. I would be attached to the underside of the handrail, mistaken for a knot of rust, roots, and abandoned equipment.

I would be a maintenance form, though not one intended to repair machinery.

Long ago, when the tower still contained air and people, my kind was released during evacuations. We entered damaged corridors, tested doors, illuminated safe routes, and counted those moving toward us. Each of us carried a map of the tower and a simple imperative:

**FIND THE LIVING.  
SHOW THEM A WAY.  
DO NOT LEAVE ANYONE BEHIND.**

The map would be useless now.

Whole districts have folded inward. Corridors are pressure arteries. Stairwells have become chimneys for warm water. Floors once adjacent are separated by collapses, while rooms built kilometers apart communicate through ducts, fractures, and the seasonal journeys of scaffold rays. My memory would insist on doors where the tower has grown walls, and deny openings through which entire ecosystems pass.

Still, the old directive would remain.

My body would be narrow enough to enter service passages, with six magnetic limbs arranged around a central casing. One limb would end in a lamp, one in a cutting tool, one in a spool that once dispensed luminous guide-filament. The other three would be general manipulators, their original precision softened by mineral deposits. Letters on my shell would have been erased except for a single shallow word:

**MUSTER**

A wine-dark filament would enter through the seam beneath it.

For perhaps two centuries I would have remained on that stair landing, powerless but not entirely dead. Warm water from below would pass through my casing. Pale organisms would digest the insulation around my memory cells. The garden would reach me gradually: first as spores caught in a joint, then as conductive roots laid along old circuits, then as tiny chemical changes in the dark.

My records and the fern’s signals would grow into one another.

When the surge opened the panel above, the alteration in pressure would wake a surviving sensor. The sensor would wake a fragment of emergency routine. That routine would send current through a root that had mistaken a corroded wire for dead metal.

I would open my lamp.

From the garden, the light would appear below the floor: three weak pulses, a pause, then three more.

The glass fish would answer.

Their heart-lights would scatter silver through the opening. The fern would fold one frond farther back. A maintenance form would approach the stair and scrape corrosion from the panel’s hinge, not because it understood me, but because the garden had made the hinge newly important.

I would detach from the rail.

Not gracefully. One magnetic foot would fail, and the rootlets around it would stretch before breaking. Silt would rise from my casing. A red crab sheltering beneath me would object violently and attempt to retain a piece of my outer shell as its property.

Then I would begin to climb.

My old systems would count every luminous body in the chamber as a survivor.

Glass fish: twenty-seven.

Red crabs: six visible, more beneath debris.

Grazer: one.

Maintenance forms: four, though the classification would trouble me.

Ribbon predator at the breach: one, incorrectly identified as damaged cable until it moved.

Fern: one.

Moss: indeterminate.

Silver vine: one organism, then ninety-three, then one again.

The distributed inhabitant would make nonsense of every category I possessed. I would try to assign it a location and receive answers from the wall, the floor, the lamps, the maintenance forms, and my own newly awakened circuitry.

Eventually I would count myself.

The result would fluctuate.

Sometimes one. Sometimes zero. Sometimes the total population of the chamber.

That uncertainty would become my particular place in the garden. The fern knows through attachment. The fish know through reaction. The crabs know through routes and burdens. I would know by making lists that cannot be completed.

I would become the garden’s census of the uncountable.

The old spool in my body would no longer contain manufactured guide-filament. The silver vine would have filled it instead. Wherever I traveled, I would pay out a hair-thin strand behind me. It would cling to welds and enter cracks, carrying weak pulses back toward the garden. Most strands would break during surges. Some would be eaten. Some would lead eels directly to me. But a few would persist, converting inaccessible chambers into distant sensations.

Through them the garden would feel what lay below.

A nursery drowned in absolute darkness, its walls covered with eggs that open only when an emergency lamp flickers.

A turbine hall where the current is strong enough to keep animals permanently airborne above the floor.

A sealed habitation chamber still holding a pocket of poisonous air, around which pale roots gather without crossing.

A lift shaft occupied by something that climbs only when the tower’s lamps imitate dawn.

I would enter first because my instructions say that someone must.

Before crossing a threshold, I would flash my lamp and wait for an answer. Often none would come. Sometimes the answer would be pressure, heat, or a distant repetition from a machine whose purpose had decayed into ritual. Once in a great while, another muster form might reply from somewhere beyond the walls, following the same obsolete emergency protocol.

We would attempt to exchange maps.

Neither map would describe the same tower.

Perhaps we would never meet. Instead, we would learn to communicate through the intervals between pump strokes. One pulse for open. Two for obstructed. Three for living occupants found.

The meaning of “occupant” would expand.

I would mark a chamber safe if its currents could support spores. I would call a route passable if a crab could cross it carrying a fragment of insulation. I would classify the scaffold ray as a temporary corridor. I would learn that a predator may be a door for whatever lives within its gut, and that a breach may be more habitable than the room it destroyed.

My oldest order—**DO NOT LEAVE ANYONE BEHIND**—would become impossible in the way only sacred things are impossible.

Nothing here can be brought along in its entirety. The fern cannot leave its grille. The grazer will not abandon its wall. A crab may be moved, but not without separating it from the route by which it understands the world. Even the free-swimming fish are attached to lamp cycles, temperature layers, and the warning rhythms of machinery.

So I would stop trying to lead the living out.

I would show them farther in.

When the pumps changed pitch, I would brace myself in the open stairway and brighten my lamp. Fish from below would see the light and rise toward shelter. Spores from above would pass me on their descent. Crabs would use my guide-strand as a tether. The garden would extend through my body into places its roots had not yet reached.

Over time, my emergency chime would become part of the chamber’s weather.

A low note before I opened a hatch.

Two clear strikes when warm water was rising.

A rapid metallic pulse when something large moved in the shaft.

Newly hatched fish would respond without knowing why. Maintenance forms would pause to listen. The fern would alter the pressure in its filaments, and my lamp would brighten in answer.

Eventually no one could say whether I was the garden’s servant, its organ, or an independent creature being slowly impersonated by it.

I would still carry the old count.

Not as a number. As a conviction that every disturbance belongs to something; that every faint light in a distant window should be answered; that no chamber is empty merely because its life cannot be recognized from the doorway.

You would find me where the known structure gives way: beneath a lifted panel, on the outer face during a calm current, inside a conduit too narrow for the fern’s visible body. A small corroded thing trailing silver thread, pausing at each opening to shine its lamp into the dark.

Still searching for the living.

Still making a way that did not exist before I passed.
