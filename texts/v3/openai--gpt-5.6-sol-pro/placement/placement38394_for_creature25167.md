At first I think I would be a surveyor.

It is the obvious answer: bent over a thread in the rain, one eye closed, watching for the instant when wet fiber touches newborn stone. I can imagine the waxed tablets under my coat, the mallet at my belt, the satisfaction of detecting a change too small to be called movement anywhere else.

But I do not think the town would trust me with that work. I would want the measurement to mean something too quickly. I would be tempted to turn each fraction into a direction, each altered note into a promise. The mountain would rise a hair’s breadth and I would already be drawing the completed tower.

Perhaps I would be a musician, then, waiting inside the water’s noise for a place to set one copper note.

No. I would still be listening for pattern where there might only be water.

I think I would find myself in the eastern vacant district, beyond the last regularly inhabited stove, where a bridge crosses a hollow that has always been dry. The bridge would be older than I am. Its underside would be green with moss despite the absence of a stream, and its parapets would have been raised twice as the approaches lifted. Beneath it would stand a stone basin positioned to receive a spring predicted seventy-three years ago.

Nothing has ever fallen into it except rain.

My task would be to maintain that bridge.

Not because I believed the spring certain. Belief would have little to do with it. I would replace the softened pegs, clear liverwort from the joints, reset the western coping whenever it began to bind, and test the empty basin after storms. Each morning I would strike its rim in four places and listen to the notes travel through the hollow underneath.

The others might jokingly call me Keeper of the Dry Bridge, though there would be no such office on the tablets.

I would have another duty, a formal one. I would serve as the ignorant witness at important measurements—the person chosen precisely because I could not read the survey marks.

Perhaps as a child I injured one eye, leaving close incisions difficult to distinguish. Perhaps I simply proved so poor at interpreting the old notation that my failure became useful. While the surveyors compared threads and tablets, I would be asked only:

Where does the shadow end?

Does the peg touch?

Which side of the bowl is wet?

Is this sound higher than that one?

I would answer without knowing what the records expected. There would be dignity in being the person who could not be persuaded by the previous line.

My public name would come from a change I noticed beneath the bridge. Not the long-awaited spring. Something less satisfying.

When I was still called by household endearments, I would crawl under the arch during a fine rain and press my cheek to the basin because the stone looked warmer than the paving around it. There I would hear a faint clicking inside the rock: five uneven taps, a pause, then five again. An adult would come when I fetched her. She would hear nothing. We would wait together until our clothes darkened with damp.

Then the sound would return.

They would excavate beside the basin and discover that no water was approaching. A small pocket in the young stone was closing as the mountain thickened around it. By the following winter the clicking would cease.

My name would be Heard-the-Closing-Hollow.

In daily use, Hollow.

This would sometimes trouble visitors. They might think it a cruel name, or a name for someone vacant. The town would hear it differently. A hollow is not nothing. It is a shape held open for a time.

I would live in several places over the years. In youth, in a crowded room in the southern quarter, sleeping beneath drying gloves and coils of rawhide. Later, in an uninhabited house near the bridge, after its floor rose enough to become dry. I would not own it. The building would gradually make me possible.

Inside I would find the remains of First Mist gifts left by people I had never met: a horn spoon gone translucent with age, a roof peg too warped to use, three bone buttons in a felt pouch, and a little wooden goat with one side enclosed by a smooth eruption of stone. I would leave them where they were. At night, my lamp would be the only one in that whole avenue. From the ridge it might appear that the eastern district had gained a citizen.

Most of my affection would take the form common there. I would dry someone’s boots without mentioning it. Before an old felt worker climbed a ladder, I would inspect the rungs and then stand below pretending to study the wall. I would remember who disliked onions in the common stew. During arguments I would hold an enemy’s elbow carefully so neither of us had to shout.

I would never become a master builder. I would become valuable in a quieter way: as someone willing to remain beside an expectation after others had grown embarrassed by it.

Eventually I might assist the Rememberer of Errors.

At the opening of the building season, I would stand in the First Court and recite the bridge beneath which no spring had appeared. I would name those who first set its footings, those who raised it, those who recommended its removal, and those who argued successfully that an error could still be useful architecture. I would report that the bridge shaded moss, carried pedestrians across mud after rain, provided a landmark in fog, and once sheltered three children and a newt during the draining of a cistern.

Then I would say clearly that none of these things made the prediction correct.

That distinction would matter to me.

In old age, my hearing would begin to fail in ordinary conversation, though I could still detect the closing of stone. I would ask to be removed from the count before death, not from despair but because numbers should not be made to bear more certainty than they can.

I would spend more time beneath the bridge, one hand resting on the empty basin. Children would come to ask whether the spring was near. I would tell them I did not know. They would ask whether I wanted it to come before I died.

I would say yes.

Then, after a while, I would say no.

Then I would correct myself again: I would want it to come when it came, which is not the same as wanting to see it.

Perhaps the spring would never arrive. Perhaps the mountain would rise around the bridge until the arch became a sealed chamber and the basin vanished into the floor. Future workers might find my maintenance marks on stones that no longer seemed to belong to anything. They might list the whole structure among the errors.

Or one morning, long after my name had become a wavering cut in the records, a child might hear water beneath the eastern paving.

Not flowing yet. Only knocking.

They would run through the empty streets with chalk in one hand, calling people close enough to touch.

And when the new channel was opened, they might discover that the spring did not pass beneath the bridge at all. It emerged several paces away, inside the house where I once slept, filling the old horn spoon drop by drop.

That is where I think I would be in this world: not at the center, and not quite at the edge, but keeping one mistaken place ready.

Not the person who knows what is coming.

The person who leaves room for it to arrive elsewhere.
