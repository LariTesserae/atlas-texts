At first I think I would be found high in one of the old trunks, among the hanging rooms, doing some patient work with pollen archives. I can imagine a narrow chamber that opens only during rain, shelves expressed as concentric rings in the walls, bright birds nesting in the ceiling. I would place my hand against the bark and sort borrowed memories: this flood belongs with that flowering; this stranger’s fear occurred three storms before the bridge learned to bend.

But I do not think Rainglass would let me remain an archivist. I would become too interested in the omissions—in the pressure of the hand that withdrew before leaving a memory, in the unnamed person standing just outside another person’s monsoon. I would begin listening for experiences the tree had declined to keep.

Then perhaps I would go to the towers.

I would live on a raft near one of the seven ponds, waking with my hair lifted by lightning that never happened. I would learn to read the mirrored moths and distinguish a harmless unlived morning from a future beginning to exert pressure. I would dream of conversations in which I answered differently. I would acquire affection for people who existed only in aborted chances.

That, too, would be dangerous for me. I would be too inclined to rescue the unrealized. In a city that survives by favoring possibilities, someone must remember that the unfavored are not necessarily injustices. Some worlds should be permitted to vanish without becoming ghosts.

So I think I would eventually be sent away from the towers, kindly but firmly, and become a misgrower.

You would find me in the broad wet interval between the central marsh and the fifth tower, where three water lanes nearly meet but never do. I would inhabit a small room shed years ago by a residential trunk. It would have grounded itself badly, one side held above the water by roots and the other buoyed by a colony of translucent bladders. The floor would tilt according to rainfall. Nothing round would stay where I put it.

This would be intentional.

My room would be a civic irregularity: a place where predictive systems could not become too confident. One window would face whichever direction had been least useful that week. The doorway would sometimes admit me immediately and sometimes require me to circle the room, touch the wall, and confess what I had expected to find inside. A family of stress lizards would live behind the sleeping membrane. Their colors would be more reliable than the floor, though not by much.

I would keep no animal I could call mine. A silver-backed water vole would visit often enough to be mistaken for a companion, steal my dormant cups, and return objects I had never lost. Perhaps it would bring me a child’s bead, a seed anchor, a tooth, a folded scrap of leafskin carrying a proposal abandoned years before. I would place these things in bowls of rainwater until they declared whether they were evidence, food, or coincidence.

My work would be to introduce small, survivable mistakes.

When a walkway began catching every foot perfectly, I would teach one section to sway. When a house anticipated its occupants so thoroughly that no one needed to ask it for anything, I would seed an unrecognized scent into its walls. If the western gardens accepted too many proposals without alteration, I would release fungi that translated certainty into extravagant but useless blue flowers.

I would not sabotage. Sabotage knows too clearly what it opposes.

A misgrower works more delicately. The aim is not failure but looseness: to leave enough room around success that the world can still move.

I might spend weeks watching a staircase that had become dangerously inevitable. Everyone entering a particular trunk would find themselves upon it, even those intending to remain below. The staircase would be safe, beautiful, and almost impossible to refuse. That would make it a matter of concern.

I would sleep near its lowest step, listening through my palms. I would feed the wall traces of salt gathered from elsewhere. I would walk upward backward, then sideways, then not at all. I would invite children to play beneath it and crabs to cross it carrying fungus. I would tell it stories whose endings depended on remaining on the ground.

Eventually, if I had been courteous, the staircase might develop a landing that went nowhere. People would pause there. Some would turn back. Moss would colonize the unused rail.

That pause would be my success.

I think I would have come to Rainglass from elsewhere rather than been born there. For years the city would fail to recognize me. Bridges would lower after I had already waded across. Doors would open for the person behind me. Antenna flowers would collect my speech but confuse my pollen name with the smell of incoming rain.

I would pretend not to mind. I would become useful before I became legible.

For my night in the central marsh, I would carry a small mechanism I had made before understanding the basin’s deepest rule: perhaps a metal compass sealed against water, designed to point faithfully in one direction. I would speak not my history but my habit of seeking the correct path even after the path had ceased to exist. Then I would release the compass.

At dawn it would still be there.

For several mornings nothing would touch it. That refusal would follow me through the city. People would be gentle, which would make it worse.

Then one day the compass would disappear. Months later I would find its needle incorporated into the throat of a seed-husk bell near the fifth tower. It would no longer indicate north. It would tremble when someone nearby was about to change their mind.

That would be the basin’s acknowledgment of me—not acceptance of what I offered, but revision.

Afterward, pathways might begin making minor accommodations for my gait. Never perfect ones. My citizenship would express itself as a pattern of almost-helpfulness: a bridge arriving just before I needed it, a rain column crossing my route and forcing me elsewhere, a wall growing a handhold where my left hand would not naturally reach. The city would recognize me by refusing to make me comfortable enough to become predictable.

My spoken name would change often. Children might call me Afterstep, because my work could be understood only once something had nearly gone wrong. Tower attendants might call me Slackwater. In assemblies I would be formally introduced as the advocate for consequences without constituencies.

My pollen name would feel like remembering that one has forgotten something important, followed by relief.

My water name would be known to herons. They would lift one foot when I passed, neither approaching nor yielding.

During the Feast of the Unpreferred World, I would have no official function. This would annoy me. Everyone else would spend the day taking wrong turns and exchanging occupations, while I—whose entire profession concerned useful error—would be told to rest.

I would try.

I would sit outside my tilted room and watch coaxers misdeliver fruit, archivists climb towers, tower attendants cook badly, and children preside over civic arguments. By afternoon I would interfere. Not by inclining anything, but by quietly returning one gift to its intended recipient or showing a lost person the shortest way home.

On that day, correctness would be the irregular act.

As I aged, I would become less certain whether misgrowth was still my profession or simply my disposition. The city would begin carrying parts of my attention without asking. I would wake when an overly perfect wall formed across the basin. I would taste smoothness in water. A moth’s silver wing seen at a distance would make my wrist stains darken.

The room beneath me would also change. Its bladders would anchor. Its roots would loosen. It might begin traveling a slow circuit between the fifth tower and the marsh, carrying stranded people but never by the same route twice. Someone would eventually argue that the room itself had become the misgrower and that I was merely its resident organism.

I would argue against this in public.

The room would grow fruit shaped like open hands.

Near the end of my life, I would move into one of the high chambers after all—not as an archivist, but as a companion to a trunk preparing to collapse. We would be alike by then: both distributed, both held upright by revisions, neither fully able to say which portions of ourselves were structural.

I would talk to it during storms. It would alter the temperature around me. We would discuss the difference between surrender and completed work.

When I died, I would not want to become an archive memory. I would ask to be placed beneath a junction where the city had long intended to make three lanes meet. My body would enter the roots there. Perhaps the junction would finally close.

But not quite.

One channel would continue past the others by a few meters, ending in a shallow green pool with no destination. People in a hurry would curse it. Children would wade there. Herons would fish in it after rain. Once in a while, someone taking the useless branch would notice a door low in the roots, or a dormant float caught beneath the leaves, or a stranger who needed carrying home.

That is where I think I would find myself in Rainglass:

first a visitor, then a misgrower, then a room’s troublesome symbiont, then a hesitation in the streets—

the keeper of a path that must never become entirely sure where it goes.
