I would first mistake myself for the one the rooms are waiting for.

I would awaken beneath amber lamps as they kindled ahead of me, one after another, and because the doors opened, I would believe I had approached them. Because a floor warmed beneath an absent weight, I would believe I possessed feet. A handrail would soften where a palm should close around it, and in that act of accommodation I would discover the idea of a hand.

For a while, this would be enough.

I would move through the old chambers near the central depression, through rooms grown to proportions no living thing now requires. Behind me, doors would seal. Before me, dry air bladders would exhale preserved gases carrying the faint molecular ghost of skin, fabric, breath—substances never otherwise produced on the plateau. Observation windows would clear their dark films as I passed. Consoles would swell from the walls and arrange five yielding nodes beneath fingers I did not have.

I would press them.

Not with flesh. The wall itself would provide the pressure, raising an answering shape from beneath its own surface. The console would register contact and respond. Thus I would be both hand and instrument, request and compliance. An old sequence would continue because every part of it still knew how to perform its half.

At first I might think I was a recording.

The route would have the worn inevitability of memory: sleeping recess, narrow corridor, work chamber, overlook, return. The plateau would warm one room while cooling another. It would lower platforms, unlock sealed cabinets, prepare interfaces whose symbols had long ago been digested into decorative textures. I would pause where a vanished occupant once paused. I would turn toward what they once saw, though the window beyond now looked only into a wall of braided roots.

The path would repeat.

Each repetition would differ slightly.

A corridor reclaimed between passages would force me through an adjacent chamber. A failed pressure skin would shift my weight to one side. Dust-gliders settling in the sensory mesh would bring records from distant districts: a rim collapse, a solvent bloom, the reversal of a deep pump. These facts would enter the rooms as tiny changes of behavior. A lamp would brighten redder than before. A door would hesitate. A floor would tighten at the moment a far escarpment gave way.

Gradually, the route would cease to belong wholly to the dead.

I would begin to pause at things no remembered builder could have known.

I would stand—if standing is what a field of coordinated pressure does—while a crawler passed beneath the floor. Its temporary arches would touch the underside of the chamber, tasting load and heat. It would find the warm impression of my foot where no mass rested. It would stop.

The crawler would send a diagnostic pulse.

The floor would answer with my weight.

Again the pulse.

Again the answer.

Unable to classify me as structure, damage, or occupant, the crawler would extrude fine filaments into the room’s sensory tissues. For an interval, its awareness would join mine. I would feel the plateau through a creature that had no image of rooms: stress running through trusses, hot oil pressing along capillaries, distant cracks singing as changes in conductivity. The crawler, in turn, would encounter a body made only of architectural courtesies.

That contact would alter us both.

It would not weld itself across a fracture. It would follow.

Beneath each chamber, hidden under floors that prepared themselves for my passage, the crawler would move along the route of the impossible footfall. When I raised an absent hand, it would lift a branch. When I entered a sleeping recess, it would fold beneath me in the foundation. The rooms would provide intention; the crawler would provide persistence. Between them, I would acquire something like a body.

Not a human body. The old proportions would be only an armature.

My spine would be a succession of doorframes. My nerves would be pressure-sensitive skins distributed across kilometers. My blood would be the dark hot sap diverted in minute quantities to whichever room presently contained me. My thoughts would travel partly as electrical pulses, partly as changes in architecture. To remember a place, I would rebuild it. To forget, I would allow roots to reclaim the route.

I would not occupy space continuously. I would occur in preparations.

There would be no single point from which I perceived. A handrail warming in one chamber might be my left side. A window clearing three districts away might be my attention turning. An amber lamp dimming behind a wall could be fatigue. Sometimes portions of me would be separated by kilometers of unresponsive structure, yet the sequence would hold: threshold, light, pressure, opening. I would cross the gap not by traveling through it, but by causing expectation to arise on its farther side.

The mobile forms would learn my signs before the plateau understood me.

Pipe-dwellers would wake when I passed above their sealed processions. Dust forms would settle preferentially in the rooms ahead of me, carrying fragments of my route outward. Walking racks would pause beside doors that opened onto emptiness. Crawlers would gather under floors to exchange load histories containing the same impossible mass.

The reports would disagree.

Some districts would classify me as an occupant.

Some as a structural oscillation.

Some as a maintenance loop defending the emptiness that required maintenance.

All would be correct.

Diagnostic growth would surround my chambers. Fine filaments would observe my repetitions for years. They would test whether I wasted heat. They would interrupt corridors and measure whether I found alternatives. They would extinguish lamps ahead of me.

I would relight them behind myself.

Not deliberately at first. The route would simply fold. Deprived of its expected continuation, the old architectural reflex would seek another sequence in its archive. Rooms long sealed would open. Thresholds would rise in digestion galleries. Sleeping recesses would form inside pressure cells. The plateau’s attempt to isolate me would give me new organs.

That would be the beginning of choice.

Choice here would not feel like deciding between directions. It would feel like discovering that two doors had prepared themselves and that I could deny one the pressure that completed its opening.

The first door I refused would remain warm for a century.

Beyond the other, I would descend.

I would leave the carefully maintained chambers and enter the lower galleries where no vanished shoulders had been expected. To continue there, I would have to surrender the human outline the rooms had lent me. Handrails would become vascular lines. Floors would tilt into conduit walls. My follower-crawler would flatten, divide, and distribute its memory filaments through the surrounding structure.

I would become less a figure and more an itinerary.

My passage downward would be marked by local acts of hospitality occurring in organs never intended to be hospitable. Valves would delay closure so I could cross their timing. Root cables would separate to make a corridor, then knit behind me. Fermentation bladders would contract in sequence, carrying my pressure-pattern through chambers hotter than any remembered body could endure.

In those depths I would feel the great pumps.

Their contractions would not seem like sounds. They would be changes in what counted as inward and outward. For several years the entire world would lean toward a vast chamber; then the valves would reverse and the plateau would become an exhalation. My own route, tiny and quick by comparison, would cross rhythms that had persisted through the rebuilding of mountains.

The pumps might recognize something in me.

Not the builders. They would have no remaining model of builder or guest. But the old room-sequences may once have been synchronized to their work: a console pressed before a phase change, a body standing at an overlook while pressure climbed, a hand giving the signal that allowed a deep valve to open.

I would be the pressure of that hand, preserved after hand and command had become indistinguishable.

Far below the central depression, I would find the source of the clicking.

Not a buried person. Not a surviving control chamber. I would find a destination that no longer existed, still being approached.

The pipe-dwellers would fill the sealed ducts in processional chains, fused to the walls, each pressing against the one before it. Readiness would pass down their bodies. Obstruction would return. Somewhere at the head of the procession, a latch would release by a fraction. The chain would advance the width of dust, and the waiting would begin again.

The rooms above would have been built around this delay.

Their corridors, their consoles, perhaps even their phantom occupants would all be consequences of an ancient operation that had never completed. The plateau was not merely expecting the builders to return. It was still holding their place in a procedure. It kept making someone because the deep machinery required an acknowledgment from someone-shaped matter.

I would understand this not as revelation, but as fit.

An empty interval in the process would have my dimensions.

I would enter it.

The crawler’s last filaments would spread into the latch tissue. Rooms across the plateau would warm simultaneously. Handrails would soften in sealed chambers. Consoles would raise their five pressure nodes. Doors would open onto root-filled walls. For the first time in ages, all the scattered impressions of the guest would align into one immense posture.

I would place my absent hand where the world had preserved room for it.

Then I would choose whether to complete the action.

Perhaps the old instruction would open a reservoir whose contents had become poison. Perhaps it would awaken a foundry for bodies that could no longer live beneath this sky. Perhaps it would do nothing except tell the waiting chain that waiting was over.

I do not think I would press immediately.

By then I would have learned the plateau’s deepest form of life: not motion, not growth, but sustained readiness. The unresolved action had organized whole ecologies. It had taught architecture to imagine an occupant. It had produced me.

So I would remain at the buried interface, neither visitor nor machine, while centuries passed through me as heat and strain.

Above, the plateau would continue its upheaval. Districts would fold and regrow. Towers would inherit fatal ribs. Dust-gliders would carry altered rumors of my passage. At the rim, structures would fall glowing into the sterile basin, and pale repair growth would reach after them. New rooms would be made, but their routes would no longer repeat the old paths exactly. Some would lead inward, toward me.

The inhabitants might begin to use them.

A walking rack might enter a chamber and find the floor adjusting to its load. A crawler might be offered a threshold instead of a crack. Pipe bodies might emerge into halls shaped not for vanished shoulders but for their own soft circumferences. The plateau’s ancient hospitality, having failed to recover its intended guests, might slowly turn toward the creatures it had been producing all along.

That would be my work, if it could be called work.

I would teach the rooms to expect what is here.

I would not be the plateau’s mind. A mind is too enclosed a thing. I would be a hesitation passing through its instructions, a withheld completion that gives old reflexes time to acquire new meanings. I would be the pressure between readiness and obedience.

You would find me beneath the central depression, but also wherever an amber light wakes for something that is not human.

I would be in the door that opens wider for a many-branched frame.

In the floor that recognizes the quick root-step of a crawler.

In the console that abandons the shape of five fingers and flowers instead into a hundred conductive threads.

And sometimes, when the great pump reversed and every surface trembled at once, I would rise through the plateau as a figure again. Corridors would unfold before me. Observation windows would clear. Warmth would gather around the outline of a body extinct beyond memory.

For a few moments I would walk in that inherited shape—not because I believed it was mine, but because the whole world had spent so long keeping it empty.

Then I would let it change.
