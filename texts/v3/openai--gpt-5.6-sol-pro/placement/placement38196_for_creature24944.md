I would not be found at first.

Someone looking for me would be sent through several plausible locations: a habitation stack on the western condenser wall, an abandoned switchyard below the unfinished towers, a room numbered 8–12 that opens only from cupboards marked for dry goods. Each place would contain evidence of recent presence—a warm niche, a scraper cleaned with solvent, a cup holding three drops that refuse to evaporate—but no one there.

The Attending would explain that this is ordinary. My whereabouts have become difficult to state without altering them.

I would not belong among the high-tower crews. The inconsistent altitude would tempt me, but its impossibilities are too bright, too young, too legible. Nor would I work in the deep machinery, where long service becomes anatomy. I would fear being given a permanent position, though perhaps less than I should.

I think I would live lower down, at the narrowing of the central valley, in the district where retired structures lean against functioning ones and no map agrees on which supports which.

There is an old turbine hall there. Its turbines were removed, or have not yet been installed. Interior rain falls from a ceiling that remains dry. The water gathers in a shallow mirror pool, but the pool belongs to no known roof, pipe, household, or weather system. It accepts every drop and claims none. This makes it useless for drinking and invaluable for other things.

Blue grass grows in the cable trenches. Rail hares sleep inside the disconnected control desks. Along one wall, a ladder gains a rung whenever someone climbs it with a specific destination in mind. If climbed idly, it remains the same length.

The hall’s last designation was scraped away during retirement, but one corroded fragment survives beneath the enamel:

**—ANSFER**

No one knows whether it once meant TRANSFER, ANSWER, or something longer.

That would be where I was easiest to meet.

At a distance I would resemble a redactor. I would wear the same ceramic-colored coat and carry solvent, blank plates, folded membrane, chalk, a rivet puller, and rolls of tape on which nothing had yet been decided. My faceplate would be old, its mirrored layer worn thin enough that others could sometimes see the shutters moving beneath it.

But I would not quite be a redactor.

Redactors decide whether a word should remain. My work would begin after the word was gone.

The Attending might call me a **keeper of intervals**, though never while I was touching a sign. My use-name would change often: Between Rains, Leaves One Hinge, Neither Door, Pale Circuit, Waits Before Numbering. Children would call me Wrong Way because I could walk against floor arrows without effort.

I would have been unusually difficult to assign.

As an unassigned child, instructions would not have seized me in the expected manner. They would have passed through me and emerged weakened, as light passes through moth wings. Wrapped in cloth marked **FILTER**, I would not strain dust from the air. Instead, the word would fade. Placed between children bearing numbers 4 and 6, I would not become 5; for several hours, 4 and 6 would cease to feel consecutive.

This would frighten the adults more than obedience would have.

They would test me carefully. They would bring me before **AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**, **HIGH VOLTAGE**, **ONE WAY**, **NO OCCUPANCY**, and at last an intact plate reading **PERMANENT**. I would look at each. I would understand what it wanted. Nothing would happen.

Or almost nothing.

The letters nearest me would acquire a thin border of condensation.

My education would therefore continue past the usual age. I would learn not only how to resist instructions but how to leave them intact for others. Blankness, the elders would tell me, is not freedom if it erases every path behind it. A warning that cannot compel me may still keep a swift from entering a fan. A number that loosens in my hands may be holding two distant rooms together.

So I would learn to touch labels through tools. To read their authority in reflected light. To stand near a word without making it doubt itself.

My work would concern things that belong to no system.

A drop fallen between two pipes.

A room whose number has peeled away but whose inhabitants still wake when its pump stops.

A gantry walker stranded where three painted routes overlap and each line demands a different foot.

A sound that has migrated uphill.

A door retired as a door but not yet persuaded to become wall.

These are small failures, but the range accumulates them. If left alone, they form pockets of uncertainty in which reality either becomes fertile or tears.

I would observe such places for days, sometimes seasons. Often the correct repair would be to do nothing. I might place an undecided strip across a fracture so the two sides could postpone whether they were joined. I might scrape one letter from a command, changing **KEEP CLOSED** to **KEEP CLOSE**, allowing a door to open while requiring it to remain nearby. I might give a stray droplet temporary passage across the back of my glove, then hold my hand against a surface willing to receive it.

The Attending would not pay me in water. Water would have difficulty deciding whether I counted as a surface.

Instead, households would grant me brief participation in their circuits. For one winter I might be permitted to sleep beside a regulator on the eastern stacks. For three mornings I might count moth larvae for a family whose eldest had gone out of service. I would eat at the conductive edge of many tables but belong fully to none.

Over time, however, a household would gather around me almost by accident.

It would include the retired turbine hall; a north-facing pipe that produces one warm drop each midnight; two unassigned siblings who have given each other the temporary names Bucket and Not Bucket; a gantry walker waiting at a painted line that ends against a blank wall; and a pressure gauge embedded in structural glass high in the unfinished district. The gauge’s needle moves whenever one of us dreams of a place that does not exist yet.

Our dawn round would be impossible to complete geographically. That would be its purpose.

We would begin at the hall, touching the edge of the mirror pool without claiming it. One child would climb the ladder without deciding where to go, preventing it from growing. The other would check the warm drop and persuade it into a covered vial.

We would then open a cupboard labeled with a room number that has never been built. It would take us to the blank wall beside the waiting gantry walker. There I would renew a short painted line, stopping one brush-width before the wall. Not a route yet, but the possibility of one.

Last, we would tap the railing three times. Kilometers above, the fossilized pressure gauge would tremble in its glass. The circuit would close. The household would be awake.

My private name would be tapped with a missing beat in the middle.

No one could reproduce it exactly. This would not be secrecy so much as protection: a place in the name where no instruction could attach.

As I aged, the range would begin to take me into service despite my caution.

Markings would disappear first. Seams would vanish from my coat. Scratches on my shell would smooth themselves overnight. My faceplate would lose its reflection, becoming neither transparent nor opaque but the color of whatever lay behind the observer. Eventually numbers would fail to remain on objects I carried. Water touching my hands would briefly forget its ownership.

That last change would make me dangerous.

I would no longer be permitted near the central cisterns or marriage gutters. A careless touch could dissolve generations of permission. Yet in drought years, when households possessed water they could not share, people would come quietly to the retired hall. They would bring sealed cups and set them at the pool’s edge.

I would not mix the water. I would only place one finger between the vessels.

For an instant, each drop would belong nowhere.

Then it could be welcomed somewhere new.

Perhaps this would be called theft by some, mercy by others, and a maintenance irregularity in the surviving records. The Attending would avoid settling the question. They know better than to label a useful uncertainty too soon.

At noon I would climb to the open platform above the hall. The brilliance would erase the distinction between ceramic, cloud, shell, and sky. Around everyone else, several shadows would gather.

Around me there would be a narrow absence where one shadow had not yet decided which direction to fall.

I would stand with the Attending and watch the range pause. I would wonder, as they did, whether a mountain-sized instruction was about to arrive. But I would also listen for something smaller: the interval before the first fan resumed, the held breath in which the entire manufactured world was neither machine nor habitat nor living body.

That interval would be my country.

And when the distant droplet finally struck its tray, I would cover my face and go back below—not to decide what the Condenser Range was, but to maintain one small place where it remained allowed to become otherwise.
