At first you might look for me at the active margins.

There would be reasons. My coat has transit latches. My tool belt carries enough sealant for a ruptured wall vein, and my soles are thin enough to read a floor beginning to rise. I own almost nothing that cannot be packed before a second guide-light flashes. From a distance I might resemble one of the frontier detachments who follow the hardening country, waiting for new chambers to decide whether they will become towns or close again.

But my coat is old metropolitan issue. The latches are not for departure.

They are for carrying away the possessions of people whose rooms have begun to heal.

You would find me in the oldest districts, where the city has lived inside its wounds long enough for those wounds to forget why they were kept open. I work with a closer-gang, though I am not one of the cutters. My municipal title is **witness of relinquishment**. Most people call us surrender clerks.

We are sent when a building wants part of itself back.

The first sign is often small: a window turning opaque at one corner, a stair developing a soft seventh step between the sixth and seventh, a doorway that catches the shoulder-frills of people who have passed through it safely all their lives. A pale seam appears across a wall. Furniture no longer sits level. Glass-backed burrowers change direction and begin traveling upward.

The closer-gang measures the growth and shaves it if shaving remains lawful. A vein-reader determines whether the closure can be delayed without starving some distant tissue. I stand with the household and establish what must be removed, what may be harvested, and what is to be left for the wall.

The last category is larger than people expect.

Some things cannot be taken without making the room less true.

A child’s pressure marks in the floor. A warming stone grown into its cradle. The faint chemical shadow where generations hung their scent-beads at night. A table whose legs have been accepted by the living paving and now share circulation with it. The law treats these as joined property: partly civic tissue, partly household memory. I record both claims, although no instrument can measure the second.

I carry inspection mirrors, fiber probes, amber chalk, a pressure lamp, and a shallow brass bowl with no practical purpose. At the end of a relinquishment, I place the bowl in the center of the room. Everyone touches it once. The contacts make a pattern of warmth that lasts only a few moments. I register the pattern, then remove the bowl before the membrane closes.

It is an old ceremony disguised as paperwork.

My neck-vanes are asymmetrical. The left opens normally; the right was scarred during a dry pulse when I was young. It catches broad pressure changes but little scent detail, so in conversation I turn my left side toward people. Strangers assume this is suspicion. Friends know it means I am trying to understand them.

The injury has made me unusually attentive through my feet. I can feel a freight tram cross three structural ribs away. I know the signature of a refinery flywheel through a wall and can distinguish a growth concussion from a pressure vessel settling after shutdown. This does not make me a vein-reader, though some residents think it does. I cannot tell what the organism intends.

I can only tell when it has stopped waiting for us.

My home is in a poor, trembling quarter beside an induction trunk. The rent is low because the pulse enters the rooms directly through the floor. Every few hours the furniture gives one slow, almost courteous shiver. Wealthier visitors find it intolerable. I sleep best with one hand against the wall.

The household contains nine adults, two adolescents, a sealed cradle, and a glass-backed burrower called Little Clerk because it makes its rounds more reliably than any of us. We do not know whether Little Clerk is one animal or a succession of similar animals. The children insist it remembers them.

The cradle is maintained by five of us. I contributed warmth through its first cold interval and registered attention afterward, which makes me a parent in law, though not the principal one. When the child emerges, I will probably be the person who carries them to feel the city’s pulse for the first time.

I have wondered what I will ask when the elder’s formula requires: *Where did it come from?*

Perhaps the child will point downward.

Perhaps toward the induction trunk.

Perhaps at me.

Children give answers adults have trained themselves not to notice.

My registered scent-mark is charred pith, copper salt, and a bitter orchard compound that no longer grows in our district. It belonged first to my mother’s household. When their rooms were reclaimed, the orchard bladder that produced the final note was sealed behind a wall. Chemists can reproduce it, but the imitation is too clean. The original scent carried a trace of old insulation and static mites.

So my legal identity is already partly forged.

No one is deceived.

I speak more than most Underleaf citizens because surrender work requires speech. Light signals can assign a packing order, and pressure taps can communicate urgency, but neither is suitable for telling someone that the room where they tended three cradles will be inaccessible by morning.

Even then I use as little voice as possible.

“The closure is established,” I say.

Not *the room is dying*. It is not dying.

Not *the room is lost*. It remains exactly where it was.

Not *you must leave*. By the time I arrive, the wall has usually said that more convincingly than I can.

People respond in different ways. Some have been packed for years. Some bargain with the vein-reader. Some strike the walls, though striking living structure is both dangerous and embarrassing. Some sit motionless until cold slows their thoughts enough to let us work around them.

Once, an old person asked to be left inside.

They had inhabited the chamber for one hundred and twelve years. Its walls had grown around the backs of their shelves, and the floor retained the shallow paths of their two-jointed gait. They had no surviving household and had already contracted their body into civic fuel after death. To them, remaining in the closing room seemed merely an early fulfillment of that contract.

The wall was not closing quickly enough to kill them cleanly.

That was what I told them. It was cruel, but accurate. Unplanned continuation is not gentleness.

They left with us. Three nights later, in a municipal alcove, they tapped thanks against my wrist. Their rhythm meant neither gratitude nor forgiveness exactly. It meant: *You carried the consequence.*

That is the phrase I think my profession should have been named for.

I am not an Accommodant. I do not believe every closure is an instruction we should obey. Nor do I believe the Underleaf is simply diseased and that every new chamber is a lesion. Both convictions feel too clean when one spends a life among scars that became kitchens, nurseries, tram stations, archives, and then became flesh again.

A wound can be useful without having been a gift.

Healing can be catastrophic without being malicious.

Most dark intervals, I visit an unscored chamber near the old freight exchange. It has no lamps and no civic fittings. I remove my shoes, open my good vane, and wait. Sometimes I feel nothing but the shudder of machinery. Sometimes a contraction passes through the chamber so slowly that my body mistakes it for its own thought.

I do not ask what the organism wants.

Want may be another word too small for it.

I ask whether there is still time.

Eventually there will not be.

The old districts are closing faster each year, and the closer-gangs say my own building has begun to thicken around the induction trunk. Little Clerk has been traveling upward. A white line has appeared behind the warming stone. No one else in the household has mentioned it, which means everyone has seen it.

When the order comes, another surrender clerk will stand in my rooms. I will dislike their phrasing. I will believe they have arrived too early. I will touch the brass bowl and leave a brief shape of warmth with the others.

My death contract is already filed.

My stable oils will go first to the lamps carried by closer-gangs. Not the public archives, not a temple, but the hooded work lights used inside rooms that are almost sealed. I like the thought of illuminating the last inspection: the empty shelf, the capped vein, the tool no one noticed beneath the table.

My mineral salts are to be pressed into germination pegs for a frontier road.

That is where you might find me later, though “me” would then be a civic courtesy rather than a person: a black peg in trembling country, set beside a route no town has yet taken. Beneath it the floor would still be soft. Columns would hang blind from the ceiling. Survey lamps would drift through amber weather, and detachable houses would wait on their sledges for the chamber to make up its mind.

The peg would carry no thoughts. It would not remember the old district or the child in the cradle. It would have no scent-mark and no good vane to turn toward a speaker.

It would simply tell the living growth: not here.

And tell the travelers: for now, pass this way.
