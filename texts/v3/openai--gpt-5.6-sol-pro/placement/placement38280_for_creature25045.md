I would not find myself at the escarpment, where the plateau can be understood from a distance.

At first I think I would. I imagine standing on one of the last forested shelves while the water disintegrates below me, watching cloud rise from a fall whose foot I cannot see. From there the tableland might briefly disclose its size. The dark upper country would extend behind me, and the low world would appear through openings in the vapor.

But that is the place a stranger would choose. It is where someone still accustomed to horizons would go.

I do not think I would have a horizon.

I would find myself well inside the plateau, in a basin whose boundaries I knew through changes in water rather than sight. There would be a warm vent field somewhere uphill, close enough that certain rains carried a metallic taste. Below it, a forest of old divided trees would grow across the basin floor. Their crowns would touch. Their rooted limbs would make rooms beneath rooms, and in the wet season their leaves would turn over together, silver spreading through the canopy before the rain arrived.

Even this is still too large. I would not know the basin as a basin. I would know one moving tract of it.

I would live in the garden on the back of an old browser.

Not as the browser itself. Its life is too immense and inward: days spent fermenting wood, migrations remembered in the body, whole groves passing beneath its feet as slowly as weather. Nor would I be one of the ferns, though for part of my life I might look like something growing there.

I would be a small armored keeper of the back garden, one of the animals that never willingly leaves its host.

My body would be low and broad, no longer than the last joint of a finger. The shell would overlap in flexible mineral scales, dark at the edges and amber along the center from the springs of the basin where my lineage had lived for generations. I would have many short legs, each ending in a double hook: one hook for skin, one for root and moss. When the browser moved, I would flatten until my shell lay beneath the fern stems. When rain struck, I would lift myself to drink from the vibration of it.

I would live near a water cup behind the animal’s left shoulder.

To an outside eye it would be a small cup plant among hundreds: waxy leaves surrounding a pool no wider than a hand. To me it would be a tarn, nursery, refuge, feeding ground, and measure of the host’s health. Its water would contain transparent larvae, mites, fungal rafts, drifting spores, the shed skins of my own kind, and mineral dust shaken loose when the browser rubbed against a trunk. The pool would deepen while the host stood still. When it began to overflow, I would cut channels through the moss with the edge of my mouthparts, directing water away from a breathing fold in the skin.

That would be my work, though I would not understand it as work.

I would graze the pale fungal film that tried to seal the host’s pores. I would eat the eggs of blood-feeding worms from the warm folds behind the forelegs. I would trim dead moss where it pressed too deeply against the skin. In drier weather I would carry wet plant pulp beneath my shell and press it into cracks around the cup’s roots. The plant would hold rain. Its roots would keep the host’s skin from softening. The host’s warmth would keep the cup from chilling enough to kill its larvae. The larvae would eat what collected in the water. I would eat the larvae when they became too numerous.

No one would have arranged us.

My world would have slopes. The ridge of the spine would be high country, wind-cooled and exposed when the browser entered stone barrens. The flanks would be shadow forest, thick with moss and hanging growth. The warm skin behind the legs would be dangerous marsh. Between the shoulders, rain would gather in chains of pools. The head would be another country entirely, close enough to smell but crossed only by the bold or starving.

I might never reach it.

The browser’s slowest motions would be weather to me. A twitch of skin would pass under the garden like a buried pressure release. The raising of its head would tilt every water surface. A step would travel through the peat as a deep compression: first one side, then another, then the answering settlement of six feet into the plateau below. When it began walking after days of stillness, the garden would stream backward. Cups would spill. Gliders hidden in the ferns would unfold and vanish. Loose animals would be lost over the flanks.

We would know the beginning of migration before the first step.

The host’s blood would change temperature. The minute pores along its back would close in sequence. Muscles beneath the skin would hold a tension absent during feeding. That tension would pass through roots and moss, and we would descend from exposed leaves into the shelter of ridges. Eggs would be glued more firmly. Larvae would withdraw to the bottoms of cups. Those carrying fresh mineral scales would wedge themselves into old scars.

Then the island would move.

I would not see the forest passing. Perhaps I would glimpse it only in pieces: a lichen curtain brushing over the garden, a silver leaf lodged for a while against a fern, the momentary darkening as the browser entered beneath rooted trunks. I would know terrain by what fell onto us. Bitter bark dust would mean the old clone grove. Warm grit would mean the mineral field. Black needles would mean the high ridge where the cloud dragged low. The scent of submerged lawns would mean a tarn close enough for the browser to drink.

Other browsers would announce themselves through the ground and through our own host’s bones. A family herd resting nearby would make the cups tremble with several distinguishable rhythms. We keepers would not hear them as individual giants. We would feel adjoining countries.

Sometimes two countries would touch.

A young browser, nearly bare, would press beneath the old one during rain. Their back gardens would meet along the old animal’s flank. Moss tips, cleaning insects, mites, fern spores, and cup larvae would cross in both directions. For a short time the bridge would be crowded. Some of us would remain where we were born. Some would climb onto the young host.

I think that would be the nearest thing my kind possessed to a voyage.

I might make it halfway across the living bridge and stop. The new skin would smell wrong: too open, too smooth, lacking the deep furrows in which the oldest colonies lived. Its moss would be thin, its cups shallow. Every movement would be sharper because the young animal had not yet learned to stand for days.

But there would be space there.

On the plateau, that would be extraordinary—not empty space, never that, but space incompletely claimed. I would enter a warm crease above the young browser’s shoulder and begin grazing. Spores caught beneath my scales would fall. Bits of old moss would adhere where my hooked feet disturbed the skin. I would leave waste rich in the mineral dust of the parent’s basin. A cup plant seed, passed through my mouthparts and scored by them, might lodge against the crease.

Long after the animals separated, the old garden would begin again on the young back.

Perhaps that is who I would be: not merely a cleaner, but a bearer of tenancy. A very small creature through which one moving forest inherits another.

My awareness would be narrow but exact. I would know whether a pool had turned sour by touching it with the fine fans beside my mouth. I would know a predator had seized the host when all six footfalls broke rhythm and the garden tipped violently. I would recognize the approach of cold rain from the silver light under the canopy and from the host’s pores tightening beneath me. I would distinguish our family’s gardens by their mixture of wax, peat, skin salt, and the bitter secretion of a particular fern found only along our migration.

Of the plateau itself, I would know almost nothing.

I would not know that the vent towers exhaled in bands across a valley. I would know that on some mornings aromatic droplets appeared in the cup water and dormant organisms woke.

I would not know that fungal signals traveled for kilometers beneath the moss. I would know that our host sometimes lifted its head before any visible animal moved.

I would not know we were three kilometers above another world. Height would mean the exposed line of the spine.

I would not know the cliffs. I would know the days when the herd turned away from ground whose buried vibration had become too spacious, when runoff sounded through the host’s body without returning an echo.

If the old browser died, I would first experience death as an impossible stillness.

The familiar warmth would not vanish at once. Rain would continue gathering in the cups. Ferns would remain upright. The back garden might look healthier for a day because no movement spilled its water.

Then the skin beneath us would alter.

Its chemistry would summon creatures from below. Burrowing scavengers would enter through the softer folds. Canopy feeders would descend. Fungal cords would rise from the soil and touch the underside of the animal that had carried us across basins. The garden would become connected to the ground so thoroughly that the distinction between host and plateau disappeared.

Some keepers would crawl onto scavengers. Some would survive in the peat mound. Some would follow the touch of another browser feeding nearby. Many would die because our true habitat had never been moss alone, nor skin alone, but the maintained boundary between them.

I might remain beside my cup until it drained into the soil.

Its waxy leaves would open as the supporting body settled. The pool that had been my tarn would tip, pouring larvae, molts, eggs, and mineral grains into the plateau’s moss. For the first time in my life I would stand on ground that did not breathe.

It would feel dead.

Then, beneath my feet, faintly, the fungal membranes would discharge. A pale pulse would pass through the wet fibers. Roots would contract around newly enriched soil. Water would enter the old foot dimples. Somewhere close, another browser would settle its weight, and the tremor would move through the body, through the ground, and into me.

I would turn toward it.

Not toward a horizon, or an understanding, or any open place.

Toward the next moving forest.
