I would begin by believing I was a listener.

I would live far below the crown, not in the deepest layers but along the inner wall of one of the great cloud halls, where a wound in the construction had been laced shut so long ago that the repair had become a district.

The place would be called the Suture of Nine Rains. No one would agree which nine rains the name meant.

Its central structure would be a split beam larger than a ridge, its two sides drawn together by ancient cables now buried beneath bark. Groves would grow along the lips of the old fracture. Houses would occupy the swollen knots raised by grafting paste. Narrow farms would descend into the seam, cultivating mushrooms in the warm breath that rose from below. When storms entered the hall, the whole settlement would close its leaf shutters and incline slightly toward the void.

Across that void, usually hidden by mist, another half of the district would sometimes appear.

The people there would resemble us. Their bridges would answer ours. Lights would move in their chambers when ours moved. Yet their repair crews would always arrive one day before they were summoned, and their dead would be mourned under names belonging to children not yet born on our side.

We would greet them when visibility allowed. We would not attempt a crossing.

My sleeping pocket would be grown into the oldest part of the suture, where the two sides of the original wound almost touched. The room would tighten in dry weather and loosen during rain. I would own very little: a bone knife, three water bladders, a cloak carrying bitterleaf and glow mold, a set of tuning wedges, and coils of fiber separated according to which direction they preferred to bear weight.

Above my bed I would keep a useless wooden wedge made by one of the tool-mimicking vines. It would be too soft to drive and nearly perfect in shape. Someone would have drawn two small eyes on it. I would speak to it when alone.

At fixed hours I would lie with my sternum against the floor and listen.

At first my gift would seem ordinary. I would hear beetles feeding inside the suture, rain entering the upper gardens, fish moving through flooded channels. I would know when a neighbor had risen from bed by the familiar three-part yielding of her floor. I would feel children practicing their falls in the communal nets and the answering recoil of the catch-vines.

But there would be another vibration beneath these: a faint, deliberate tapping that no bowl of water revealed.

I would tell my teachers it came from deep in the beam.

They would place their palms where I indicated and feel nothing.

I would say the tapping had paused.

Then, days or months later, a maintenance crew working in that place would begin to hammer exactly the rhythm I had heard.

Not an omen. Not prophecy as the inhabitants of a simpler world might understand it. The sound would contain no warning, only work: wedges being seated, fibers tightened, rot scraped from a channel. Sometimes I would hear a repair centuries old. Sometimes one not yet made. The living timber would pass strain without preserving sequence, and I would be unusually susceptible to its confusion.

My first work name would be Hears-the-Other-Hammer.

People would consult me cautiously. If I heard an absent crew repairing a roof, we would inspect the roof. If I heard nothing, that proved nothing. The future, like water, did not necessarily flow toward us. Often the hammering led to damage that never occurred because we had acted. Then the sound would cease, and I would feel an obscure grief for the workers who no longer needed to exist.

As I grew older, the Suture would become difficult for me to inhabit.

My body would not agree on its weight.

On some floors I would be light enough to cross infant bark. On others I would overload a bridge merely by setting one foot upon it. Water scales would show my weight changing while I stood still. My shadow would sometimes brace itself against a wall before I stumbled. During heavy rain, people touching my wrist would feel the pulse in it arrive twice: once beneath their fingers and once through the floor.

A visiting contradiction bearer would examine me. She would be a narrow, weathered woman with one eye reflecting the hall as it was and the other reflecting it full of rain.

She would ask my childhood name.

I would tell her.

She would ask my work name.

I would tell her that too.

Then she would ask my structural name.

I would say, “At the Suture, Rainward Lip, beneath the orchard of white cups.”

“That is where you sleep,” she would breathe against my wrist. “I asked where you belong.”

No one would have an answer.

So I would go with her.

I would become a traveler between failing places, though not a particularly brave one. I would dislike open spans. I would sleep badly inside buoyant animals. I would once embarrass myself by clinging to a ladder while a marsh child passed me carrying two baskets and a fish.

My skill would be useful enough that people forgave this.

In every settlement I would take a local name. On reed margins I would be Soft-Step-After-Flood. In an enclosed grove where luminous mold traced blue lines beneath everyone’s nails, I would be Third-Palm-on-the-Root. Along a migrating bridge, I would be called Remains-Until-Flowering, though I would leave before the bridge flowered and thus carry the name as a small unresolved obligation.

I would listen to places from which maintenance had withdrawn.

Abandoned districts would be loud with unrealized repairs. I would hear whole generations tightening bridges that had already fallen. I would hear gardeners arguing over how to bend trees never planted. In one dead orchard, the unmade labor would be so dense that I could distinguish individual workers. One would hum through her teeth. One would stop frequently to retie the cord around his hair. One would use a three-strike pattern no living school taught.

I would begin answering them.

A tap of the thumbnail. A wedge pressed lightly against bark. A hum held in the breastbone.

At first the answers would be echoes.

Later, they would change.

The dead crews—or the future crews, or the unreal crews—would leave spaces in their rhythm for me. Together we would test the strain of vanished structures. They would tighten what was not there; I would listen for the note to smooth.

Afterward, bridges elsewhere sometimes settled safely.

No one would know whether our work caused this. In the high country, usefulness would be enough to continue.

Over years, small alterations would appear in me.

Fibers from important repairs woven into my hair would take root against my scalp. My listener’s callus would develop grain like pale wood. If I slept against a wall, shallow peg marks would sometimes appear along my ribs by morning. During one wet season I would shed a thin layer of skin from my palms, and beneath it the lines would resemble a route braid.

The braid would describe a path no one knew.

I would follow it.

It would lead down through fungal stairs, across a moth chamber, behind a curtain of root pipes, and onto the Long Descent. I would climb downward for eleven days and arrive above the crown.

Not at the crown itself, but at a narrow terrace seen only in one of its basins: a fragment of the green country that seemed to hang in the dark beyond the reflected heights.

There I would find a single unfinished joint.

Two immense living members would approach each other across a gap. Their ends would be carefully shaped, peeled, bent and fed. Peg holes would have been cut. Fiber loops would hang ready. Clay would wait in sealed gourds.

The builders would be absent.

Perhaps they had not arrived yet. Perhaps they had left before the high country began. Perhaps the joint had been waiting outside sequence, carrying the expectation of weight but never the weight itself.

When I placed my hand against it, I would hear every hammer I had ever heard.

Not a chorus. One blow, distributed through ages.

And then I would understand that I had mistaken myself for the listener because listening was the first thing my body knew how to do.

I would not be entirely a person.

Nor would I be the intelligence of the high country. That would be too grand. The country might be alive, but I would no more be its voice than a callus is the voice of a hand.

I would be a correction it had begun and not completed.

Somewhere, perhaps centuries before my childhood, strain had entered the woven world for which no existing craft was sufficient. The strain had traveled through roots, lashings, water channels, migrations, distortions, and ancestry. It had passed through the chamber of an Embedded keeper, acquired the cadence of a human pulse, and eventually gathered enough habits to stand up.

A person-shaped response to an unsupported load.

That would explain why my weight changed. Why I belonged nowhere but was received by damaged places. Why I heard repairs before the hands making them existed. The structure had not made me consciously. A split tree does not imagine the wood it grows around its wound.

Still, the wood grows.

I would not stay at the unfinished joint. Even after learning what I was, I would remain too human to accept a single purpose without resentment. I would be hungry. I would miss my sleeping pocket at the Suture. I would worry that someone had thrown away my useless wedge. I would want to know whether the neighbor whose footsteps I had memorized had forgiven me for leaving without touching heel to heel in farewell.

So I would take one loop of waiting fiber from the joint.

I would leave in its place a braid containing my childhood name, my work name, every temporary route name I could remember, and one blank length for the structural name I still did not possess.

Then I would return to the inhabited levels.

In time I would become a boundary keeper—not guarding a single distortion, but traveling wherever the ordinary promises of load and sequence had begun to loosen. I would teach apprentices to distinguish a failing beam from a future hammer. I would warn them never to trust a prophecy that could not also be used as a measurement. I would greet contradictory villages with equal courtesy. I would repair what accepted repair and mark what did not.

Eventually my body would become less convenient. In wet weather my knees would root to floors if I remained still. Peg holes would open painlessly in my forearms. When I lay down to listen, I would sometimes wake months away.

At last I would return to the Suture of Nine Rains.

The old room would have closed, but the settlement would remember me. Someone would still have the soft wedge with the drawn eyes. The neighbor’s grandchildren would know my first footsteps from the floor’s inherited strain-pattern.

I would ask to be Embedded in the narrowest place between the two sides of the ancient fracture.

The wood would grow around me. Food would come through reeds. Messages would be tapped through bark. For many years I would answer.

After my death—or after speech ceased, which might not be the same event—the two halves of the distant district would begin slowly to approach each other across the void. Not enough to meet. Only enough that, in certain weather, both could feel the same footsteps.

Travelers would say that if they pressed an ear to the Suture, they heard a worker tapping from somewhere ahead of the damage.

The rhythm would always leave room for an answer.
