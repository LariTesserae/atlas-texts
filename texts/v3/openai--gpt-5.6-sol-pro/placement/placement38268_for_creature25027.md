At first I think I would be one of the pale moths.

There is something almost sufficient in that life: to be born inside the walls, nourished by an obsolete protection, knowing the world as warmth and fiber. To emerge only when the casing splits. To mistake a keeper’s lamp for a new kind of distance and spend my brief strength tapping against its shutter.

But the moths are too certain. Light appears; they go to it.

I would belong to something slower and less resolved.

I might be a pipe lizard, then: one pale length among pale pipes, never wholly seen. I would sleep with my belly against a live casing and wake before the rain. The keepers would know me by portions—a foot, an eye, the withdrawal of a tail. They would make useful predictions from my movements without ever learning why I moved. That would suit me.

Yet even the lizards possess an instinct for safe water.

I think I would be less fortunate than that.

I would be found in the eastern half of the Stillworks, where the district narrows around a row of sealed tanks. Not the tank that knocks four times, and not the one that fell silent with Arven. Farther on, beyond a stair whose bottom step has sunk into a salt deposit, there is a passage omitted from the oldest maps because it was once considered merely a gap between systems.

The gap has since become a route.

To enter it, one must turn sideways and exhale. One cheek passes close to a cold tank; the other feels the heat of an insulated riser. The opposing temperatures make the teeth ache. Condensation forms constantly there, but the drops do not fall. They travel along rivet seams, divide at bolt heads, and vanish beneath a collar of green mineral.

The keepers call the place the Interval.

That is where I would work.

I would be a keeper, though not an especially gifted one. I would not possess the celebrated ear that names a failing bearing through three walls. I would confuse copper with thin iron and sometimes mistake my own pulse for a pump restarting. As a child, during the trial of one hundred drops, I would lose count at eighty-six and remain standing because I was too ashamed to admit it. The elder beside me would know. They would say nothing until the hundredth drop, then ask what I had heard while I was not counting.

That question would become my apprenticeship.

I would be assigned to the After rack.

After would be one of the nearly extinct maintenance names. Its keepers tend no particular machine. They watch for delayed consequences.

A brace is set in the western gallery; After returns three days later to see which gutter has stopped ticking. A valve turns by accident; After walks the temperature routes until a wall begins to sweat. When a tank knocks, other keepers record the hour and withdraw. After comes back when the place appears quiet.

The rule of the rack would be simple:

*Nothing is finished when the hand leaves it.*

The tools would reflect this. Wedges marked not only with where they were driven, but when. Chalk in paired colors: one for an action, one for its answer. Fine cords stretched across gaps to show motion too slow for sight. Small ceramic cups placed under dry seams because dryness, in the Stillworks, can be an event waiting to happen.

As an apprentice I would resent the work. Other keepers would clear channels, climb the pump housings, or descend in pairs to the Receiver. They would return with burns, lizard sightings, and useful warnings. I would spend weeks observing a washer creep across a casing. I would sit beside a pipe that had clicked once after warm rain and refuse to leave until it either clicked again or became meaningfully silent.

Most often, nothing would happen.

Gradually I would learn that nothing happening has varieties.

There is the nothing of an empty pipe and the nothing of pressure held perfectly still. The nothing after a chain stops is unlike the nothing before it moves. A sealed tank listening is not the same as a sealed tank abandoned. Even silence has load-bearing forms.

My name would become After when the previous bearer retired to the upper hatch. I would inherit their brass rod, their route cords, and a notebook swollen by damp. Much of the writing would be unreadable. The legible pages would contain observations such as:

*Green lens dark. No immediate change.*

*Green lens restored. Eastern wall cooled eleven days later. Relation uncertain.*

*Three knocks, Tank 19. Repeated after forty breaths. Did not answer.*

*Interval narrower than last year, or I am wider.*

I would add my own records beneath these, trying not to improve their uncertainty into false knowledge.

In time, the Interval would reveal itself as the center of my life—not because it concealed anything grand, but because effects met there without arriving together. The cold tank contracted while the hot riser expanded. The gap widened and narrowed by less than a fingernail. Drops traveled through it from sources no one could trace. Sounds entered from both sides and sometimes emerged altered.

If I pressed the listening rod to the cold wall, I would hear a slow uneven tapping.

If I moved the rod to the hot riser, I would hear the same tapping several breaths later.

Not an echo. Not quite.

I would report it. Another keeper would listen and hear only condensate. A Mere would listen longer, then advise me not to establish a rhythm with my hand.

So I would never tap back.

I would begin to suspect that the Stillworks does not experience events where they occur. A touch in one chamber may become a tremor in another; a tremor may become a drop; the drop may alter a pool, and the altered weight may take years to lower a pipe onto its neighbor. The district’s body is too entangled to keep cause beside consequence.

Perhaps that is why the tanks seem to answer.

Perhaps every knock is a reply to something done generations before.

This would comfort me less than the possibility of a creature inside.

When the district-wide rain came, I would stand in the Interval with my lamp closed. Warm water would tick through the crowded dark. The cold tank would tighten against my shoulder, the riser ease against my chest, and for several minutes I would be unable to move forward or back. I would feel the opposing systems through my clothing: one shrinking, one swelling, each using my body as a temporary gasket between them.

That would be the closest I came to understanding where I was.

Not beneath the inhabited levels. Not in the eastern tangle. Not even in the Interval.

I would be inside the delay between one part of the Stillworks touching another.

Years would narrow me in habit. I would turn sideways before doors. I would pause after setting down a cup. On the upper levels I would distrust every sound that ended too cleanly. People would find conversation with me difficult because I would keep waiting for their sentences to produce consequences elsewhere.

Eventually my hearing would fail in the ordinary ranges. I would no longer hear moth wings against the lamp hood, but I would feel the low tremor coming hours before the pipes began their soft rain. Younger keepers would bring me uncertain sounds. I would press the rod to my jaw, listen, and ask them what happened last week.

If I died below, it would not be dramatically. No tank would open. No forgotten machine would claim me. I would sit down in the Interval to wait for an answer, and my heart would stop among all the other intermittent mechanisms.

They would carry me out by the route I had used most often: beneath the salt stair, around the evasive vessel, through the warm constriction without touching the green seam. My brass rod would return to the rack. Someone else would become After.

And I would become a route.

Not a plaque. Not a story, except perhaps near the kettle. A route on the practical maps:

*Take After beyond the sunken stair. Turn sideways at the cold tank. Do not stop if both walls begin to knock.*

Later, as the district shifted, the route might become unsafe. The instruction would remain but acquire amendments.

*After is narrow following warm rain.*

*After cannot be used carrying a brace.*

*After ends at the new salt fall.*

At last no one living would remember whether After had been a family, a duty, a person, or merely the old word for that gap. It would not matter. The name would still guide bodies through the dark. It would preserve a way of turning the shoulders, a place to withhold the hand, a patience measured not in drops but in consequences.

That is what I would be in the Stillworks:

first a listener, then a delay, then a safe passage through pressure.

A small continuity the district had gathered and not yet discarded.
