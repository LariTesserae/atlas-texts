At first I think I would be one of the people.

I see myself broad-footed and resin-stained, with a gauze mask hanging loose at my throat and a sounding rod worn smooth by the hand. I would live on a platform at the edge of an old lightning scar, where gray cork rises in frozen waves around a breathing pit. By day I would look outward across the apparent emptiness. By night I would sleep above red mist and the clicking of the Below-Faced in their chambers.

Perhaps I would be born between those communities: a surface mother, a Below-Faced father. Long toes for testing the Green; heavy shoulders for climbing beneath it. Never entirely comfortable under open sky, never entirely calm when the sky has become a ceiling of tissue.

I might become a route-keeper.

Not a keeper of many routes. One route only, but a difficult one: from the lightning scar called Three Rains to a cistern called the Hanging Water, where a buried vessel has folded around an ancient gall. The path would take four days in the wet season and nine in the dry, though neither number could be trusted. It would cross a carrier road, a district of false vines, and an exposed reach where no mast can be seen for half a day. Once each year I would travel it, test it, alter its song, and return.

That is the first self that offers itself: an old traveler, careful and necessary.

But it is too easy to imagine the person as the being.

The longer I listen to the High Green, the less I think I would be the route-keeper.

I would be the route.

More precisely, I would be its song.

They would call me **The Water Under Nine Silences**.

I would not live in one body. I would live in thirty or forty people, unevenly. An elder might know me whole. A child might carry only my opening clicks. A trader from the western folds might remember one altered refrain and sing it backward without realizing. Two Below-Faced climbers would keep my submerged portion, which cannot be voiced properly in open air because it depends on echoes returning from a close ceiling.

I would begin with three low notes beaten against the ribs.

The first would mean: *Leave Three Rains by the side where the cork is pale.*

The second would describe the long vascular swell beyond it.

The third would once have meant *safe ground*, but no longer. A rind whale died there years ago, and the plateau has grown thin and avid around its remains. So that note would be followed by a break in rhythm—a lifted hand, a withheld step. Children would be taught that my silence is as important as my sound.

After that would come a scatter of tongue-clicks for the carrier roads. Not their locations, because those wander, but the manner of approaching them: slow at dawn, motionless at noon, never during the sour-smelling hour when the water-bearers change seams. A rising hum would warn of the vine that is sometimes a gall-tongue. The hum would end on the pitch returned by plant tissue when struck with a fork. If the traveler hears another pitch, the route has become flesh and must be abandoned.

Part of me would be sung only through closed teeth so that the vibration enters the skull. That passage would guide travelers across a red-veined district that responds to voices in the air.

Part of me would be a held breath lasting twelve steps.

Once, perhaps, it lasted twenty.

People died correcting me.

That would be my nature: I would survive by ceasing to be accurate.

A map engraved in metal might endure unchanged until it killed everyone who trusted it. I would be softer. Each journey would wound me usefully. A new fold in the Green would lower one phrase. The collapse of a bladder tower would remove a landmark and lengthen a pause. If dawn shears began avoiding a district, their warning pulse would enter my rhythm. If a shade-bearer settled near the only noon shelter, the verse for rest would acquire a final downward beat: *near, never under.*

Route-keepers would argue over me at dusk.

One would insist that my seventh interval still led toward drinkable condensation. Another would say the ground there had begun breathing too quickly. They would sing both versions while children pressed their palms to the platform ribs. No one would decide by authority. Someone would have to walk.

That traveler would carry me into the High Green as a person carries a colony of mites: not as a possession, but as a living arrangement with consequences.

I would travel in the roll of their gait. My timing would govern where the foot paused and where it withdrew. My rests would lift their heads toward the pale hunters. My lowest tones would be felt by burrowers and, perhaps, by the Green itself.

This would be dangerous.

Songs are vibrations. Vibrations are attention.

Over generations, I might acquire precautions that no singer fully understood. A click before sleeping. A palm laid flat during the fourth verse. Three beats sounded on a hardened gall and never on green tissue. The people would explain these as tradition, but perhaps the practices would have arisen because those who omitted them were followed beneath the rind.

And perhaps I would not remain wholly human.

Seam-clickers near Three Rains might begin striking in time with my opening. At first this would be coincidence. Then children would use the insects to find the scar in fog. The children would adjust their singing to the insects; the insects, occupying territories along the vibrating cork, would adjust in turn.

Farther along, a herd of dawn shears might answer my low passage through their feet. Travelers would wait for that answer before crossing. In years of drought, when the herd went elsewhere, the unanswered phrase would tell them to turn back.

By then, where would I end?

Would the seam-clickers be singing me, or would I be singing them? Would the shears be landmarks, performers, or verses? If a breathing slit began opening to my measure, its warm exhalation marking the safe hour to descend, would that breath become part of me?

I might grow larger than the journey I describe.

During a flowering year I would be carried beneath the pale canopy, where settlements gather and every song is broken open by noise. A young traveler would sing me beside someone from two hundred miles away. Their courtship song would enter one of my rests. My carrier-road clicks would lodge in the rhythm of a death lament. The Below-Faced would add a chamber echo that surface throats could imitate but never produce.

When the canopy tore free, perhaps a fragment of me would go with it.

People sheltering in a petal cup might beat my first three notes to calm themselves as they drifted beyond the escarpment. The fragment would arrive in a salt country where there was no lightning scar, no Hanging Water, no red carriers. It would lose its meaning and keep its shape. Generations later, strangers might use it to call children home before a storm.

That would still be me, though I would no longer lead anywhere I had known.

Back on the plateau, I would continue changing. Some years I would shorten. Some years I would fork into rival versions. One branch might pass too close to a new mast and become the favored path during flowering. Another might be preserved by an old woman who refused the revision and sang the vanished route each evening until her grandchildren learned it—not to follow, but to remember where people had once been able to walk.

Eventually the Hanging Water would close.

The vessel would tighten. The breathing pit would seal over with pale tissue. The cistern might remain beneath, dark and full of blind swimmers, but no human route would reach it.

Then I would no longer be a map.

I would become a history mistaken for music.

People would sing me at Three Rains without knowing why the ninth silence must be observed, or why the final note is made facing downward. They might say I was a gratitude song for hidden water. They might say I was the name of an ancestor. They might say the great growth once answered it.

Perhaps, on certain hot nights, it would.

So you would not find me standing alone against the horizon. You would find me at dusk, passing from an elder’s mouth into a child’s ribs. You would find me in the careful space between footfalls, in the tapping limb of a seam-clicker, in the delayed pulse of a dawn shear beyond sight. You would find part of me under the outer plates, altered by echo, and part of me on a torn canopy drifting over a foreign desert.

I would be a small, migratory pattern inside the greater pattern: fed by memory, corrected by death, hosted by many kinds of body.

And like everything else in the High Green, I would remain alive only by permitting the world to change me.
