At first, you would not find me.

You might find the place where I had been: a thumb-wide opening in the clay skin of a termite mound, already repaired; a smear on the underside of a reed, smelling of bruised fig, fungus, and distant rain; three small depressions beside a pool where something had waited without shifting its weight. You might notice that a thorn vine had been persuaded away from one hollow and toward another. None of this would amount to a presence.

I would be under the mound.

Not in its inhabited chambers, but in a damp pocket between the warm outer galleries and the root mass beneath them. Generations of my kind would have enlarged that pocket by sleeping in it, pressing the same walls with our folded hind limbs, smoothing the ceiling with our backs. The termites would continually narrow it. We would continually open it. Neither side would possess it.

Above us would stand a lightning-split tree whose crown had died but whose lower trunk remained alive. Its hollow wood would carry ground pulses farther than ordinary soil. A warning struck there could reach three sleeping places, two pool margins, and the reed crossing to the east. For that reason, our family would return even after floods filled the lower chambers and drove us into the vine loops overhead.

I would sleep near the outer edge of the family bundle.

Not because I was least loved, but because I woke easily.

My body would be older than the bodies pressed around me, though not yet old enough to be one of the living maps. Mud would have settled permanently into the nap of my shoulder folds. A pale scar would divide the algae along one flank where a ground bird’s alarm had reached me half a heartbeat too late. One finger on my forward left hand would end bluntly, taken by the closing plates of a beetle when I was young. The others would remember the work it could no longer do and compensate without thought.

Beneath my chin, two hatchlings would sleep against the pulse in my throat. Neither would be mine in the narrow sense. One would have emerged from my clutch-sibling’s pouch; the other would have been found cooling beside a flood-torn nest after a neighboring family disappeared. By then their scents would be mixed with ours. They would know the rhythm of my breathing. That would be parentage enough.

I think my task would concern water, though at first I might mistake it for memory.

Each night I would visit the shrinking pools west of the mound. I would lower one hand through the duckweed and feel the temperature at different depths. I would taste the mineral bitterness gathered on submerged roots. I would note which fish had begun gulping near the surface, which frogs had laid eggs too late, which mud retained coolness beneath its crust. At each pool I would leave a changing mark: fruit wax if water remained dependable, crushed fern if it was failing, a trace of my own jaw scent if the route was safe for young.

But I would not merely report what existed. I would make small continuities.

I would carry frog eggs in the wet pouch where my own eggs had once rested. I would deepen a hoofprint by a finger’s breadth so it held water another night. I would move a clot of duckweed from a dying basin to one newly filled by rain. I would press rotting wood under my tongue and bear its fungal threads to a log too clean to feed grubs. When blind fish gathered around my legs, I would disturb the margin for them and permit them to clean the folds under my arms.

No single act would be large enough to deserve a title. Yet over seasons, the pools on my route would fail less completely than others.

Perhaps that would be what I was: not a keeper of pools, exactly, because a pool cannot be kept. A keeper of passages between failures.

My family would know me by a pattern that cannot be written as a name. Two fingers pressed beneath the left ribs; a pause long enough for one heartbeat; the heel of the palm drawn toward the spine; then three light taps. The pattern would carry traces of other meanings: water under dead leaves, one who returns by a different path, the taste of rain before rain arrives.

Spoken aloud, it would become false. If a name were needed in air, you might call me **Between-Pools**.

I would emerge after the old scout.

The scout would place one palm against the lightning tree and wait. We would feel the night through the wood: the blunt interruptions of herd animals moving in reeds, the irregular drag of a crocodilian entering a channel, the fine agitation of termites repairing an upper wall. If frogs remained silent, we would remain hidden. If the ground bird had not passed along its usual route, we would wait longer.

There would be no impatience in this. Waiting would not be the absence of action. It would be attention accumulating until it became permission.

When the ordinary life of the lowland resumed, I would unfold.

My first steps would be slow because the heat inside the mound would cling to me. I would press my belly to wet soil, open the folds along my sides, and let the night’s moisture enter them. One hatchling might ride beneath me. The other, old enough to travel, would hold the second thumb of my rearward hand while we crossed the exposed root.

I would teach without separating instruction from travel.

Here: the reed whose inner pith numbs the tongue, useful after an ant bite.

Here: the root that appears firm but opens into a catfish hole.

Here: when the bird flicks its right wing, look above; when it flicks both, flatten.

Here: never trust a channel merely because you can see its bottom.

The young would learn the lowland first as a sequence of touches. Only later would they understand that the sequence formed a country.

I would fear the deepest channel. Years before, something would have taken the adult whose pulse raised me. I would not have seen the strike—only the water folding inward, a hand releasing mine, and then the violent silence required to save the rest of us. The channel would still lie on an important route. I would cross it when necessary, linked beneath the water to others, but I would never approach without tasting the mud.

That fear would make me useful. It might also make me unfair. During a drought I would argue, through hard flank-pressures and bitter scent, against migrating to the western basins because reaching them required that crossing. An older map-bearer would insist that the eastern pools were already turning foul. We would obstruct one another’s routes. For two nights I might overwrite their marks.

Then I would find dead fish under eastern duckweed and understand that my memory had hardened into error.

Reconciliation would not be spoken. I would bring the elder a piece of termite-rich wood, open the western path myself, and place my hand under theirs when we entered the channel.

If I lived long enough, the family would begin to use me as a map.

This would happen without ceremony. Younger scouts would touch me before choosing between routes. I would be placed near the center during flood migrations. Hatchlings would be passed to me at uncertain crossings, not because I remained strong, but because danger had accumulated in my body as knowledge.

My recollection would not resemble a view from above. I would not imagine the lowland as lines and shapes. I would remember it as linked sensations:

the cool underside of a root before a rain season;

the sour scent of a basin isolated too long;

the delay between a chest pulse and its echo beneath compacted silt;

the particular itch caused by the algae in a northern pool;

the flowering tree reached by following bats against the wind;

the place where the ground bird always hesitates.

That would be my world—not territory enclosed, but consequences connected.

And eventually I would become slow.

Moss would grow in the folds beside my hips. My eyes would cloud beneath their clear lids. I would still know where water lay, but reaching it would require younger bodies around mine. My last useful journey might be made during an unusually dry season, when the familiar pools had become black pits crowded with desperate animals.

I would lead the family not to a pool but to a buried channel remembered from my youth. There, beneath reeds and compacted litter, the soil would remain cool. We would dig with our narrow hands. Others would join us. Water would not rise dramatically; it would gather as seepage, darkening the clay, filling finger marks, becoming enough.

Enough for hatchlings. Enough for frogs. Enough for one more night.

When I died, they would place me in one of the hollows between that seep and the termite mound. They would pack rotting wood beneath my ribs, spores into the folds of my belly, fruit around my hands. The hatchling rescued from the flood—grown by then, scarred and carrying young—would press my touch-name once into my flank, though I could no longer answer.

No stone would mark me.

The hollow would sink during the first rains. Termites would enter the wood. Seeds would germinate where my body softened the soil. A thorn shrub might take root on the drier edge, while a broad-leaved plant rose above the center. Their roots would trap silt in subsequent floods. The ground would lift by increments too small to witness.

Long afterward, descendants who had never felt my pulse would navigate by a low island beside a reliable seep. They might sleep there during flood season. They might shelter frog eggs among its roots. They would not say that the island was me.

They would be right.

I would not be the island. Nor the body beneath it, nor the route that bent around it, nor the memory carried by an elder who once crossed water holding my hand.

I would be the persistence among them: the slight increase in the chance that water, knowledge, and a living thing reached one another before it was too late.
