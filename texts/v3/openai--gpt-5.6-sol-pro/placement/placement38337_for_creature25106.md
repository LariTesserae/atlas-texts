At first I would mistake myself for the mist.

That would seem the easiest existence there: neither fallen nor suspended, entering through the trees without having crossed the rim, touching every surface without choosing one. I would lie along the primates’ fur, silver the moths’ antennae, and occupy the open throats of flowers. I would be almost nothing, yet visible wherever another body gave me shape.

But the mist belongs partly to the sky, and nothing in the hollow can afford such divided allegiance. Even if it never lifts, it remembers elsewhere. I would feel that memory as an unbearable altitude above me.

Then I might think I was one of the birds: a small slate body beneath a leaf, claws enclosed by moss, a song fully assembled behind my tongue. That would suit me better. I would possess a voice not through sound, but through the exact readiness for sound. Every note would remain possible because none had yet excluded the others.

Yet even that would be too singular. A bird must have one throat. It must face, however slightly, in one direction. The awareness in this hollow is broader than that.

I might be the primate with the raised stone. I can imagine the weight of it: basalt made warm where the palm encloses it, its striking edge already aligned with the seam of a nut. I would know what a blow is. I would understand the opening, the meat within, the usefulness of fragments. I would hold not ignorance but an excess of consequence. The nut beneath my hand would contain food, a seedling, rot, another tree, an absence in the moss. To strike would make only one of those worlds true.

Perhaps I would remain there forever, arm lifted, not from mercy or fear but from an inability to impoverish possibility.

Still, I do not think that is where I would finally recognize myself.

I would find myself lower down, where the pool’s mossed lip meets the foot of the basalt wall. There, beneath a collapsed trunk that never finishes collapsing, a pale fungal fan protrudes from the wood. It appears to be a single soft body, almost translucent, its edges curled inward as though about to widen.

That visible fan would not be all of me.

Most of me would be hidden: hair-fine threads passing through wet wood, around beetle chambers, under moss, between roots, into fissures too narrow for water to admit it has entered. I would have no center from which to look. The sensation of the hollow would reach me in portions.

The pressure of a snail’s foot would arrive as a slow alteration in moisture. A primate shifting its intention above me—not its weight, only the readiness of its muscles—would travel down the branch, through the trunk, and into my threads as tension. I would know the salamanders by the faint salt of their skins. I would know the unhatched birds through fibers fallen from nests that were never built. The pool would speak chemically: old leaf, clear water, larval breath, the infinitesimal promise of waste already received.

I would connect things without reconciling them.

Through me, the dead tree would remain available to the living beetle. The beetle would remain available to the bird. The bird would remain available to the cat. None would be delivered. I would carry the information of appetite through the hollow without converting it into pursuit.

I would not be its ruler or its mind. The hollow has no need of either. I would be one of the ways it keeps every part near every other part without forcing contact.

And I, too, would be unfinished.

Along the underside of the fungal fan, spores would stand ready in their minute thousands. Each would contain a journey: release, drift, adhesion, germination, branching. But there is no wind. The wood does not shake. The spore does not fall. It rests at the threshold of becoming separate from me.

That would be my private tension. Elsewhere in the hollow, bodies are spared the cost of action. I would be spared the cost of division.

Then a newcomer would enter.

I would perceive them first through the moss, not as a footfall but as the preparation for one. Roots would take the pressure that had not yet fully arrived. Snails would incline their horns. A cat’s suspended paw would acquire another possible direction. In the canopy, black eyes would already contain the stranger.

The newcomer might kneel near the pool and place a hand against the fallen trunk. Their warmth would enter the wet wood and reach my hidden threads. For an instant that does not pass, their body and mine would share a boundary.

I would learn them all at once: the old scars, the unspoken words, the acts regretted because they were completed, the acts regretted because they were not. I would feel every next movement gathering in the hand laid over me.

I would not ask them to stay. Asking would lean too far toward an outcome.

Instead, at the edge of my pale fruiting body, one clear droplet would swell. It would contain the dim reflection of the pool, the branches, the lifted tools, and the newcomer’s face bent close.

The droplet would be about to fall.

A spore would be about to enter it.

The hand would be about to touch me.

That is where I would be: under the never-fallen hand, inside the old wood at the pool’s edge, dispersed through a network that almost joins the entire hollow.

And what would I be?

Not its voice, because nothing there needs to speak.

Not its memory, because memory requires one moment to be lost to another.

I would be its listener: a pale, many-bodied fungus carrying every creature’s readiness through the dark, receiving all intentions and completing none—my hidden threads woven around the next moment, feeling for the place where it might enter.
