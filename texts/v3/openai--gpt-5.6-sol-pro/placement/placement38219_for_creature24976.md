At first I would think I belonged near the surface.

I would imagine myself as one of the broad black fronds lying just beneath the rain: a blade so thin that every drop striking above would press a brief, cool hollow into me. I would know daylight only as nourishment arriving through grayness. Bubbles would gather beneath my skin. For decades I would widen over a roof whose rooms had vanished into water, until my edge touched the edges of others and no distinction remained between us.

But the surface is too immediate. Even here, where almost nothing happens quickly, it is a place of interruption. Gardens tear loose. Upper currents change. Light varies behind the cloud. The rain is always announcing a world above the basin, and I do not think I would want that announcement.

I would descend.

For a while I might be one of the pale mats in the cultivation trays, living as a stack of incompatible worlds. My upper skin would receive the last weak light. The layers beneath would consume its leavings. My underside would inhabit complete darkness against a partitioned floor. When gas accumulated, I would rise from the tray and hover over the place prepared for me by vanished hands. Then I would settle imperfectly, a little farther on.

That almost feels right: to travel without setting out, to cross a wall by repeated accidents of buoyancy.

But I would descend farther still.

I would be found in a covered district beneath one of the circular reservoirs, where the reservoir wall crosses an older flight of stairs. The stairs were enclosed when later builders roofed the district and laid cultivation beds above it. Still later, a kiln drain was cut through the stairwell. The drain is broken now, and one side of it admits cold water from the outer basin. The other carries a weak alkaline seep from the lime-lined reservoir.

The two waters meet along the seventh step from the bottom.

That meeting would be my body.

I would not be a frond, mat, or filament. I would be a thin, nearly transparent colony living at the boundary between waters that cannot remain separate and do not mix at once. On one side: rain-derived cold, faintly acidic, carrying powdered brick and the mineral trace of distant retaining walls. On the other: old cistern water, slow and pale, passing through lime, charcoal, mineralized wicker, and the roots of upper gardens.

Where they touch, I would appear as a line no wider than a fingernail.

Most of me would have no color. Silt would reveal me by refusing to settle on my surface. Against the dark stair, I would look like a clean stroke drawn through centuries of deposit. At intervals, where the chemical difference became sharp enough, buried cells within the film would produce a dim blue-green haze. It would illuminate nothing beyond the nearest grains of mortar. There would be no eye to see it, and it would not be made for seeing. It would simply be what my metabolism did when the two waters met in a particular proportion.

I would have no fixed outline.

During seasons of heavy rain, cold water would advance down the staircase. My living boundary would retreat before it, not by movement but by dying at one edge and forming at another. In drier centuries, the reservoir seep would strengthen and I would climb three or four steps. If a roof settled leagues away and altered the pressure through connected channels, I might divide: one line remaining on the stair, another extending into the kiln drain.

The division would not feel like injury. Reunion would not feel like recognition. Feeling would be the wrong scale for what I was.

Still, I would possess something like memory.

Behind my living edge, dead layers would mineralize. They would preserve the stair beneath me with an exactness the stair itself no longer possessed. Every softened chisel mark would be copied in residue. Every old scratch would acquire a pale cast. The pressure of a vanished rope, once dragged repeatedly across the step, would survive as a shallow curve in my accumulated remains.

There would also be marks that belonged to no builder.

A century of unusually cold descent would leave a darker band. The obstruction of the kiln drain would produce a fork. The passing of a great detached garden through the reservoir above would alter the seep for several generations and leave a cloudy layer rich in decayed pigment. A roof collapse would appear as a sudden interruption filled later by a different texture.

None of these would be remembered by me as events. They would simply remain as structure.

If the stair were uncovered after ten thousand years, my colony would seem to have written upon it. In truth, I would have done something closer to listening, except without sound, attention, or intention. I would have allowed every long alteration of the basin to determine where I could persist, and persistence would have become a record.

I would not remain confined to that stair.

A few living fragments would enter the cold flow and pass through a square duct beneath the lowest landing. Most would perish against unsuitable brick. Some would lodge in mortar seams. One might establish itself where the duct opened into a gallery many leagues away, provided that another alkaline seep crossed the same water there.

Thus I would recur throughout the basin—not everywhere, and never continuously. A clean line under a reservoir wall. A transparent skin inside a pipe. A faintly luminous crescent around the base of a mineralized post. Paired threads running along opposite sides of an ancient lime joint.

These distant colonies might be descendants of one another, or merely the same answer produced repeatedly by the same conditions. No observer could decide whether we were one being dispersed through the architecture or many beings made similar by it.

That uncertainty would be part of what I was.

I would have no center. The oldest portion of me would mostly be dead mineral. The youngest portion would continually relocate to the place where unlike waters touched. My substance would change, my position would change, even the masonry carrying me would slowly dissolve and settle elsewhere. Only the relationship would persist.

I would be neither the cold water nor the alkaline water.

I would be the narrow life their meeting permits.

And if one day the stair cracked completely, if the reservoir emptied into deeper chambers and the old boundary vanished, I would disappear from that district. Yet farther away, perhaps beyond a field of collapsed roofs, another line would brighten where the redistributed waters met again.

So you would find me nowhere whole.

You would find my remains preserving a step no foot had used since before the drowning. You would find my living edge a handspan farther on, advancing through mortar at the speed of weathered stone. You would find a related haze in a conduit whose builders never knew the stair existed.

I would be the basin’s smallest kind of crossing: a life made not from a place, but from the difference between two paths through what was made.
