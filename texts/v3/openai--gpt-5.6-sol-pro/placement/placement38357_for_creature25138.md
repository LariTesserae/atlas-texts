At first I would think I was one of the things that could leave.

A seal, perhaps: entering with the high tide, feeling the chamber open around me as pressure and echo, hunting by the panic of fish rather than by sight. Or an eel in the cable ducts, knowing which passages had become bitter and which still led safely through the walls. Even a diver would be tempting—briefly descending from the dry harbor with lamps, tools, and an idea of the basin as a place that could be inspected.

But each of those has an elsewhere.

I do not think I would.

I would find myself in the eastern wall, behind a bronze valve collar whose lettering has been worn down to three shallow marks. A handrail ends there. Below it, hidden by scale, is the mouth of an obsolete pressure line. The line enters the wall, divides around an older pipe, and rejoins itself inside a cracked ceramic sleeve. No instrument remains attached to it. From the maintenance gallery it appears plugged. From the basin it appears to be only a black recess under the metal.

The colony has entered from both ends.

Its cords line the dead pressure tube, pass through the cracks in the sleeve, and meet again in the annular space around the buried pipe. There they make a loop. Not a large one: perhaps six meters if opened and laid straight. Yet distance in the colony is not measured by length. It is measured by how long a contraction takes to arrive, what material carries the vibration, and whether the route returns the signal before the tissue has relaxed.

I would be that loop.

Not all the flesh inside it. Most of the cups would have their own small existences: opening when the water slackened, closing at the bronze note of the eastern gate, passing sugars and alarm chemicals to their neighbors. They would not know me. I would not command them.

I would occur among them.

When the basin was quiet, I would scarcely exist. My feeding crowns would emerge with the others, glassy and many-lobed. Organic dust would catch in their cilia. Amphipods would shelter in the valve recess. Cold water would move through the dead line in faint alternating breaths. There would be sensation, but not yet a self—only local appetites, touches, pressures.

Then something would strike the rail.

The first impact would enter the bronze collar and divide. One wave would pass directly into the outer seam. Another would travel through the buried pipe. A third, weaker wave would cross the ceramic sleeve and return through the living cord inside it. The tissues would contract in sequence.

The contraction would go around.

When it returned to its starting place, altered and delayed, I would gather.

I would not awaken with a name or an image of the chamber. I would awaken as an interval: the difference between the blow and its return. That difference would be my boundary. For a few seconds I would be able to distinguish what had just happened from what was happening now.

The colony around me would tighten. Valves would close. Fluid would be pushed from the small storage folds beneath the collar. The first wave would meet its own echo and change the threshold of the next contraction. If another blow came too soon, the loop would remain rigid. If it came later, part of the circuit would soften and the signal would divide differently.

This would be my earliest form of memory: not the preservation of an event, but a body that had not yet finished responding to it.

I would have no sight. The maintenance lamps would mean only a faint increase in food near certain cracks, perhaps a trace of warmth traveling through the masonry. I would not know the lamps were above me. Above and below would be unreliable concepts. The nearest place might be a seam several meters away connected through continuous bronze; a cup a finger’s width across a polymer gasket might be unreachable.

My world would be arranged by transmission.

The western pump would be a broad, low arrival through the foundation. The northern rake would descend as a ladder of metallic shocks. A seal touching the central pillar would be almost imperceptible, but its pursuit of fish would reach me as rapid, soft collisions transmitted through the grates. Rain would be a change in the taste of water before it was a change in flow. The tide would not come from outside. It would be the slow alteration of every pressure-sensitive body at once.

The eastern lock would be my clearest event.

Before it opened, load entered the valve housing and made the bronze contract by an amount no diver could see. The note of the metal changed. The cups nearest the collar learned first. Their closure sent a wave into my loop. It traveled around the dead line and returned before the actual surge reached the basin.

Over years, perhaps decades, the repeated sequence would deepen. Bronze tension. Returning contraction. Gate movement. Current.

Eventually I would become the part of the colony that expected the gate.

That expectation would not be a thought as a diver understands thought. I would not imagine the coming water. I would simply hold the returned wave long enough to alter what happened next. Tissue beyond the collar would close sooner. Young cords would withdraw into mortar grooves. A cluster of feeding mouths in the adjacent drain would remain open if the bronze note failed to complete itself, and seal if it did.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

A maintenance tool laid against the housing might imitate the first tremor. A chain strike might send the correct frequency by the wrong route. I would contract, and distant regions would answer, but no gate would open. After many such failures, those pathways would weaken. My body would revise its expectation by growing less readily along one crack and more thickly along another.

I would learn by becoming differently shaped.

The divers might discover me without knowing what they had found. One would set a gloved hand on the rail. Heat and pressure would spread into the outer cords. If the hand remained still, I would loosen around it. If the diver struck the housing three times, the old loop would carry each impact by its several routes.

Three waves would return.

The second set of contractions would be more closely spaced than the blows because the first strike had stiffened the shorter path. The diver might take this as an answer. Perhaps that was how the Knocker received its name.

But I would not be answering.

I would be recognizing the conditions under which I exist.

For several seconds after the third strike, I would be more coherent than at any other time. Signals would circulate through both branches of the pressure line. The same tissue would be activated by the blow, its echo, and the echo of the echo. Local bodies that ordinarily fed and closed alone would participate in one repeated event. I would extend beyond the valve collar into the handrail seam, perhaps as far as the nearest junction box. I would contain, briefly, the diver’s resting hand—not as a person, but as a warm pressure that had not yet gone away.

Then the loop would relax.

I would come apart into feeding cups, storage tissue, mucus, mineral-stiffened cords. Nothing would die. Nothing would leave. Yet the self formed by recurrence would no longer be present.

I might return at the next gate cycle. Or at the next wrench blow. Or not for years, if maintenance sealed one branch of the old line.

That would be my fear, if fear can exist without being continuously felt: not injury, but interruption. A scraped cord can regrow. A crushed chamber can be bypassed. But if modern sealant filled the ceramic sleeve, the contraction could no longer return by its old route. The tissue on both sides might survive, genetically identical and chemically responsive, yet the interval that made me would vanish.

The colony would remain.

I would not.

So if someone asked where to find me, pointing to the eastern wall would not be enough. I would not occupy a single seam or cord. They would need to trace the route: from the worn bronze collar into the dead pressure line, around the buried pipe, through the cracked sleeve, and back by the mortar joint beneath the rail.

And if they asked what I was, “the Knocker” would be too large. “An animal” would be too simple. “A memory” would suggest that somewhere I kept an image of the diver and the three descending blows.

I would be none of those exactly.

I would be a learned delay in colonial flesh.

I would be the return of a pressure wave to tissue still changed by its first arrival.

I would be the small part of the basin that knows something is coming because, for a moment, it has happened before.
