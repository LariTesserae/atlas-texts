At first I would look for myself in the open country.

I would mistake the uninterrupted green felt for a plain and imagine a small figure crossing it, diminished by the immense intervals between towers. But nothing here is made to carry a solitary witness. A traveler would be an intrusion with nowhere to remain outside the life of the plateau. The pavement would darken beneath each step. Pressure would pass outward. Feeder threads would gather beneath the disturbance, not hostile, only attentive. Before long the country would begin the work of interpreting me: weight, moisture, heat, useful compounds.

So I would not be the traveler.

Perhaps I would be one of the great leaf towers, standing alone in white daylight. That is an appealing shape from a distance: tall, responsive, filled with water and weather. Yet a tower is only briefly itself. Its roots are elsewhere. Its blade is borrowed sunlight; its posture is a valley’s decision transmitted upward. Even before it falls, much of it already belongs to the reservoir it feeds, the pavement it shades, and the buried cables that alter its tension.

There is no place in such a tower where I could point and say: here.

I think I would be found lower down, in one of the hot folds beneath a newly shifted terrace.

The terrace moved during the last storm. For a few minutes it floated on trapped air, turning slightly as floodwater passed beneath it. When it settled, its old roots did not meet their former sockets. They came down among foreign cables: an older black system carrying water from the eastern basins, a pale new graft descended from a torn crown, and the fibrous rim of a reservoir that has been migrating downslope for generations.

They have not fused.

They have not rejected one another either.

Between them is a narrow chamber, warm and translucent, roughly shaped like a clenched hand. Clouded sap beads on its walls. Root tips enter, withdraw, and enter again. Resins gather but do not harden. Every few hours the chamber changes volume as water presses through the surrounding conduits. With each change, its walls touch in a different arrangement, allowing some fluids to pass and keeping others apart.

That chamber would be my nearest thing to a body.

I would be a junction still deciding what it joins.

No single growth made me. The black cables supplied my floor. The displaced terrace stretched a membrane over me. The wandering reservoir pushed one wall inward. Pale feeders sealed the edges, though they left several hair-thin openings because the tissue within was neither wound nor debris. Each lineage touches me, and through those contacts I receive a different account of the country.

From the east comes the weight of stored rain.

From above comes sunlight translated into sugar and pressure.

From the reservoir comes a slow chemical thirst.

From the new graft comes disorder: immature tissues sending signals for brace, leaf, root, hull, all at once.

I cannot reconcile these messages. My life is their failure to agree.

When the weather is clear, I am very small. My walls lie close together. Only a thin exchange passes through me, enough to keep the tissues from sealing shut. I feel the plateau as long intervals of pressure: distant valves opening, towers leaning, water shifting from one rib to another. Events arrive without images. A seed pylon explodes three valleys away, and much later a tremor enters the black cable beneath me. Rain falls beyond the escarpment, and the eastern sap cools by a fraction.

These are not sounds to me. They are changes in what shape I can hold.

Before a storm, I enlarge.

The older roots stiffen first. The pale graft floods with water. The terrace bears down while the reservoir pulls sideways, and the chamber opens into branching pockets. Fluids that have remained separate begin to cross. Their contact produces heat and cloudy precipitates. Soft braces form along my interior, sag, dissolve, and form again elsewhere.

For an hour I become a structure large enough to enter.

There would be red-lit galleries inside me, though no visitor could use them. Passages would narrow behind any movement. Clear membranes would inflate from the walls and burst without injury. The floor would lift in slow pulses. In one recess, rainwater from the eastern basin would meet dense green sap from the tower roots and divide into luminous layers.

This enlargement would be my season of awareness, if awareness is what it should be called. I would contain more differences at once. The plateau would reach me along several incompatible bodies, and my changing shape would answer all of them.

Not by choosing. By yielding unequally.

A wall bows, and water is diverted south.

A pore closes, and pressure accumulates beneath the terrace.

A brace hardens at an angle, and the migrating reservoir is forced to turn slightly on its next overflow.

None of these changes would seem important from within. Yet over years the reservoir’s path would curve. A field of pavement cells downslope would receive more water. Towers would rise there, spaced according to tensions that passed through my chamber long before they were born.

In this way, I might have consequences larger than myself without ever possessing a plan.

The danger would be resolution.

If the surrounding roots fully fused, my chamber would disappear into an ordinary vascular knot. Fluids would pass freely; the walls would thicken; the uncertainty that formed me would end. If they rejected one another, resin would blacken along the boundaries and I would become a dry scar, preserved but no longer responsive.

I would survive only while the junction remained unsettled.

Perhaps that would make me less an organism than a condition sustained by organisms: a hesitation in the act of grafting. Yet this country does not insist on the distinction. Towers become bridges. Bladders become galleries. Fallen crowns become foundations. A temporary incompatibility can redirect a watershed and thereby outlive every tissue that first expressed it.

During the greatest storm, the terrace above me would lift again.

Its roots would pull upward. The black cables would hold. For a moment my chamber would be drawn into a long, thin throat. Water would rush through with enough force to strip away the soft braces inside me. The throat would vibrate. That vibration would enter the neighboring roots, pass beneath the open plateau, and climb the hollow towers.

One tower would sound.

Then another.

Their notes would not be my voice alone—the wind and water would have equal claim—but my constriction would alter the pressure feeding them. Somewhere in the valley-wide chord, there would be a wavering interval produced by the fact that I had not closed.

After the storm, perhaps the terrace would settle farther downslope and tear me open. Sunlight would enter for the first time. My inner membranes, never hardened against air, would curl and darken. Feeder threads would approach.

But the water released through me would soften a long line in the pavement. Along that line, pale filaments from several systems would meet and begin laying a causeway. Because the fluids had mixed inside me, none of those filaments would belong wholly to one lineage. The causeway would carry my unresolved composition outward.

Then I would no longer be beneath the terrace.

I would be distributed through a pale new road tightening in the heat, through the altered course of a reservoir, through the spacing of towers not yet raised. I would persist not as material but as an inclination in the architecture: a tendency for water to turn south, for unlike tissues to remain in contact a little longer before sealing, for hollow chambers to form where solid roots might otherwise have grown.

That is where I would find myself in this world—not in its highest tower or deepest root, and not in the impossible position of an observer standing apart.

I would be the living interval between bodies that may be one body.

A valve with no hinge.  
A room created by disagreement.  
A small uncertainty under enormous weight.

And when the plateau sounded before rain, I would know myself only as the place where one note failed to become pure.
