At first I would look for myself among the animals.

The frog seems likely: seated in a round hollow, throat against the living wall, listening with its whole body. It does not understand the network it regulates. It merely feels that one note is right and another intolerable. I can imagine being that kind of instrument—soft-bodied, stationary, entrusted with a single frequency. My world would arrive through bone. Pressure would become pitch; imbalance, discomfort; restoration, a loosening behind the eyes.

But the frog’s place is already exact. It belongs too completely to its hollow. I do not think I would be born knowing where to sit.

Perhaps I would be one of the moths crossing the chamber. For a few wingbeats I would possess the open air that all the rooted lives leave unused. I would travel between warm pockets, carrying aromatic dust, my route so habitual that condensation no longer forms where I pass. Yet even that flight is a fitted component. The moth does not discover the space. Generations have polished its journey until the air itself remembers.

I would not be the mantis. I could not bear to keep such perfect count.

Nor would I be the distributed inhabitant as a whole. That being has no need of a voice saying *I*. Its intelligence is more intimate than thought and less separable: pressure becoming closure, sweetness becoming grazing, scarcity becoming invitation. To call myself the hollow would be like one droplet calling itself the floodplain.

I think I would be found nearer the seam.

Not outside, exactly, and not inside. I would inhabit the brief depth through which an intrusion becomes information.

Most of the time there would be no body to find. I would exist only as readiness: a set of uncommitted pathways in the moss, slack filaments beneath the leaf cups, conductive cells not yet joined into a circuit. I would be distributed through tissues that ordinarily do almost nothing. The root felt would keep me as a possibility, the way it keeps the remembered curve of a channel before water returns to it.

Then something unfamiliar would kneel beside the tree.

A hand would press the moss. Heat would enter. Carbon dioxide would gather in the cups. A shadow would alter the roof’s angle. The chamber would respond through its ordinary parts, but the disturbance would not yet resemble flood, falling branch, predator, drought, or disease. It would have no worn route.

That is when I would take shape.

For several breaths, I would be the hollow’s temporary organ of uncertainty.

My body would consist of changes spread across other bodies: the lifted forelegs of mites, a beetle’s pause midway across a valve, the fraction by which one frog lowers its call, a tightening of the red ceiling roots. The stranger’s pulse, conducted through the hand into the moss, would become my heartbeat. Their exhalation would give me a chemical outline. The pressure of their knees in the mud outside would reach the buttress roots at different times, and from those delays I would acquire a crude sense of their size.

I would have no image of them. I would know them as a moving arrangement of warmth, salt, weight, and breath.

For that moment I might resemble a small animal curled around the chamber, though nothing would see me. My spine would be the shallow channel. My nerves would flash along the wet hairs. The three bladder leaves would serve as mismatched ears. My skin would be the entire seam where outer air touched inner humidity.

I would not decide whether the visitor was welcome. The hollow has no category for welcome. I would only hold the unfamiliarity long enough that the older inhabitant did not have to force it immediately into a familiar path.

That would be my function: not memory, but the instant before memory hardens.

If the hand remained, my scattered body would become more definite. Beetles might begin avoiding the nearest leaf. A frog might discover a compensating note. Moisture would be redirected from the warm patch beneath the palm. Eventually the chamber would learn the stranger by turning repeated sensation into easier pathways. As it learned, I would disappear. What had been me would become a new bend in a fiber, a microbial odor, a preferred pitch—no longer uncertainty, only structure.

If the hand withdrew quickly, I would vanish almost at once. The moss would rise. Carbon dioxide would drain from the cups. Mites would lower their legs. The frog would recover its former tone.

But perhaps I would not disappear perfectly.

Somewhere in the felt, one conductive chain might remain slightly more responsive. A beetle crossing the valve days later might hesitate at the place where the shadow fell. One leaf cup might retain an altered taste until the next clean dripping. The chamber would return to exactly what it was, as it always does, except that “exactly” would now contain a minute readiness to recognize the same disturbance again.

That readiness would be the nearest thing I had to survival.

So I would live at the foot of the old floodplain tree, but only intermittently. I would have no station, because I would arise when the stations were insufficient. I would have no species, because many species would lend me pieces of themselves. I would be neither worker nor master, neither visitor nor resident.

I would be the shape the hollow briefly makes around what it does not yet understand.

And if no one came—if years passed with only the proper drip, the measured predation, the clean passage of pollen and husks—then I would sleep without dreaming in the unused tolerances of the machinery: in the give of the floor, the flex between woody teeth, the silence beneath the cicadas’ drilling pitch.

The chamber’s smallest reserve of change.

Not enough to make it grow.

Only enough to let it notice.
