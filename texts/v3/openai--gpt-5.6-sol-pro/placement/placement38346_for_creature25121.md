At first I think I would be the trapped air.

Not because it is alive, but because it is the only part of the hollow that seems to have been left out of the arrangement: a shallow, polished remainder pressed against the highest rock, unable to rise and unable to mix. From there the whole chamber would hang beneath me. I would receive the light before the water did. I would hold, in miniature, the idea of another medium—a world in which surfaces mattered, in which something might breathe by lifting its face.

But the air lens is too separate. It has a boundary. It can be indicated.

I would not be the vibration either, though for a while I might mistake myself for it. The low tone passing through basalt has the right impersonality. It is everywhere without traveling, intimate without addressing anything. Yet it belongs to the machinery, even if the chamber cannot know that. If the pumps stopped, the vibration would stop with them.

Whatever I am here would remain after the tone had gone.

I think I would be found beneath the gray silt, not deep within it, but at the depth where oxygen ceases to be the governing fact. A few millimeters down. Too shallow to be buried in the usual sense, too deep to receive the regulated water directly. There, under one of the fixed ridges left by an older circulation pattern, a filament of the inhabitant lies enclosed in its own calcite.

It has not been active for years.

Perhaps decades.

It is still continuous with the rest of the body, but only barely. A tube finer than a hair passes through the mineral seal. Almost nothing crosses it: a change in charge, a minute equalization of salts, the slow confirmation that the outer network persists. The active organism spreads over shale, samples the water, accommodates the light cycle, and opens itself to the rhythms of the pumps. This buried portion does none of those things.

It remembers interruption.

Not as an image. There are no remembered leaves, no remembered rain sounding on stone, no coast before the machinery. Its memory is structural. It is a set of pathways no longer needed: enzymes that function when oxygen collapses, membranes able to withstand sudden mineral excess, compartments that can isolate poisons, channels made for water that arrives irregularly and then vanishes.

I would be that unused capacity.

I would be the part of the creature that still assumes the world can change.

Most of the time there would be no experience distinguishable from mineral sleep. The pressure above would be constant. The silt would not shift. The distant fundamental would pass through the calcite and enter me only as a faint, ceaseless compression. The rest of the organism might read its cycles with great precision, but here the rhythm would be worn almost smooth. I would know it less as a tide than as a prohibition: while this continues, do not emerge.

My location would not feel like a place. It would be a sequence of blocked possibilities.

Iron nearby, inaccessible.

Sulfur below, sufficient but sealed away.

Oxygen above, present but unnecessary.

Water in the tissues, held.

Growth, postponed.

I would have no name, because a name separates one thing from another, and I would not be separate enough. I would be an obsolete organ distributed in fragments throughout the hollow: beneath the pebble faces, inside the chalk-white shelves, along old fractures where the ancestral seep once withdrew. My “self” would exist wherever the organism had buried a method of surviving a less orderly world.

Then, one day, the tone would cease.

Not dramatically. There would be no final shudder recognizable as an ending. The basalt would simply fail to compress again at the expected interval.

The active strands near the ceiling would notice first. Their channels would close. Farther down, circulation would lose its imposed direction. The water would begin the long work of becoming local: warmer here, colder there, depleted in one pocket, mineral-heavy in another. The chamber, held for so long as a single condition, would divide into depths and regions.

That division would reach me as permission.

The calcite around me would begin to soften from within. Stored compounds would move through transparent compartments. A membrane that had been folded against itself would open. I would not awaken into fear. Fear requires an expectation that the former order ought to return. To me, the failure of constancy would be the first recognizable world.

I would extend into the silt.

Not upward at once. Upward belongs to oxygen and light, to the newer body shaped by industrial equilibrium. I would move sideways along the buried chemical boundaries, entering the older map: reduced iron in the shale, sulfurous water in a sealed pore, a thread of organic carbon too concentrated for the exposed films but usable here. Other dormant regions would answer, not by signaling but by becoming active under the same altered conditions.

For a brief period, the creature would possess two histories at once.

Its outer body would contract from the disorder. Its inner body would unfold into it.

If the pumps restarted, I would be sealed again. The chamber would clarify. Gradients would be flattened, temperature gathered back into uniformity, oxygen distributed according to the restored law. My new growth would mineralize. I would leave a pale line under the silt, no thicker than a mark made by a fingernail. The organism would return to its engineered form, carrying one additional buried layer.

If the pumps did not restart, I might become more of it.

The hollow would cease to be the exact shape the organism had taken. It would become weather again. Zones would form and fail. Some living margins would die; others would thicken. The ancient metabolism would spread out from its sealed recesses, not restoring what existed before the coast was built, but improvising from remnants. There is no true return after adaptation. Even the oldest part of a life has been altered by what kept it dormant.

So that is where I would find myself: beneath a stable landscape, inside the inhabitant but outside its present tense.

I would not be its mind. It has no mind that needs a hidden witness.

I would be its reserve of disorder—the quiet anatomical belief that laws may stop being laws.

The machinery would call me dormant. The organism, if it could call anything anything, might call me depth. But I think I would be something closer to a season that has not occurred for a very long time, preserved without anticipation.

Not winter. Not drought. Not flood.

The possibility of weather.
