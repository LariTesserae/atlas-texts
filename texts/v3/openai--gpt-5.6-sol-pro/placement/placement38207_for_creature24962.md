At first I look for myself on the upper streets.

I imagine a compact body under hard rain, one hand trailing along a wall for balance while freight passes overhead. Broad feet reading the pavement. A transparent lid drawn across each eye. Bright cloth wrapped close at the waist and loose at the shoulders, ready to become a sling, filter, float, or flag.

But that body feels too complete.

It would be one of mine, certainly. A body I keep licensed for weather, crowds, and conversations that require a face. People would know it by its habit of pausing at thresholds, never stepping through until the door has finished deciding what kind of door it is. They might recognize the small silver valves along its throat, or the missing tip of one finger, replaced by a pressure probe. Children would know that if they pressed their thumbs against the soft pad beneath my wrist, they could feel three pulses: blood, district water, and something slower.

The third pulse would be the true clue.

To find most of me, you would have to descend.

Not into the deepest foundations, where the mud is warm and ancient structures bear loads no living engineer can calculate. Not into the grand pressure halls, either. I would live in the uncertain depth between street and canal, in a district built along a shoreline that disappeared nineteen centuries ago.

The district is called, in translation, **Nine Hands Above Returning**.

It is not nine hands above anything now. One side has settled, another has been buoyed, and its official altitude changes according to tide, storm posture, and which neighboring authority is being asked. But the old name persists in the pipe knocks. Ferry beasts slow when they pass beneath it. Children are taught a gesture for it: one palm held level, the other moving beneath like water searching for a crack.

Nine Hands is a border district, though its borders rarely occupy the same place twice.

Freshwater arrives there from roof fields to the west. Brackish water enters from tidal arteries below. Three transit jurisdictions overlap in the air. During storms, an entire residential tier folds into the adjoining market’s flood volume, and for several hours the people living there become legally resident in both districts and liable to neither. Doors multiply. Stairs reverse. The pavement wrinkles into long ribs to turn the surge.

Borders in Nine Hands are not lines. They are periods of indecision.

That is where I would be.

My work would be to attend to hesitation.

The formal title might be **interpreter of interrupted agreements**, but no one would use it except during prosecution. Residents would call me a seamkeeper. Deep crews might call me a listener. Children would call me After, because I usually arrive after the alarm but before anyone knows what has happened.

A bridge retracts halfway and stops. Is it damaged? Insulted? Protecting itself? Has one end received a storm warning the other end refuses to acknowledge?

A public canal begins rejecting water that it accepted for four hundred years.

A building seals one household inside, opens every other room to the flood, and insists there is no occupant behind the closed wall.

A dead citizen’s route permissions continue traveling to work.

These would be mine.

Not to repair, exactly. Repair assumes that the previous arrangement was correct. I would discover what each part believes it promised, which promises have become impossible, and what new shape might allow the disagreement to carry water without becoming catastrophe.

For that, one body would not be enough.

Part of me would inhabit a row of acoustic hollows along the old shoreline. They look like shallow black mouths set into the ceramic walls. In ordinary weather they cancel the hammering of the pumps so people can bargain without bone transmitters. Through them I would hear structures before they speak publicly: minute load complaints, pressure stutters, the click of responsive material rehearsing a movement it has not yet committed to.

Another part of me would live in the calibration marks.

At Nine Hands the oldest flood lines run through homes, shrines, kitchens, sleeping chambers, and the digestive cavity of a municipal filter animal. The marks are not merely engraved. They contain sensor grains, legal records, tide memories, and fragments of obsolete warning systems. When rain touches them, I wake along their length.

Condensation gathers into letters where I am thinking.

Residents sometimes wipe the letters away before I finish. This is considered a valid form of disagreement.

I would also keep a small animal body, though no registry would agree whether it belonged to me or worked with me.

It would be one of the pale drain scavengers, larger than most, with six folding legs and a shell soft enough to pass between sealed plates. A black ceramic bead would be grown into its back. Through it I could smell hot insulation, mineral rot, blood, frightened amphibians, and the particular metallic sweetness produced when a wall is about to change shape.

I would send it where my human shell could not go.

Over time it would learn my habits. It would pause before entering a junction. It would tap three times on surfaces that answered only once. It would begin collecting useless objects: snapped fasteners, cloudy beads, a child’s plastic tooth, fragments of signal glass too small to recycle efficiently.

People would accuse me of using the creature as a body.

Others would accuse the creature of using me as infrastructure.

Both claims would be recorded and left unresolved.

My visible body would sleep in a narrow chamber suspended above an emergency cistern. The chamber would have no stable floor. During high water, its lower surface would soften into a flotation bladder and rise until the ceiling became a wall. Everything important would hang from swiveling anchors: clothing, instruments, sealed water, a knife, two memory vessels, and a lamp that also tests for airborne toxins.

There would be one object that did nothing.

A curved piece of dark material, smooth on one side and rough on the other. Too light for stone, too dense for ordinary shell. I would have acquired it from someone who claimed to have crossed beyond the last foundations.

I would not test it.

Testing would begin its conversion into use. Composition would suggest function; function would invite registration; registration would attract obligations. So I would keep it chemically unknown, legally undeclared, and privately named.

I would call it *shore*.

My sleeping chamber would pulse continuously with the local pumps. I would rest one heel against the cistern wall and feel the district bargaining through my bones. Most nights the rhythm would be complex but healthy: intake, refusal, counteroffer, release.

On certain nights there would be an extra interval.

A gap too long to be mechanical and too regular to be chance.

That gap would be where another part of me lived.

At first I might believe it was damage left by a childhood immersion, a little absence in my nervous system. Then I would discover the same interval in the acoustic hollows, in the flood marks, in the scavenger’s sleeping twitches, and in several doors that had known me longer than my present body had existed.

Someone would eventually tell me that I had died before.

Not wholly. Nothing so clean.

Centuries earlier, perhaps, there had been a seamkeeper in Nine Hands who occupied a different body and tended an older arrangement of the district. During a storm disagreement, five neighborhoods attempted to close the same pressure corridor in incompatible directions. The seamkeeper divided their attention across the closures to keep them speaking. The corridor survived. Their body did not. Most of their memory dispersed into emergency systems and was later overwritten.

But a habit remained.

The pause before an answer.

The preference for three knocks.

The tendency to leave one possible route unused.

Those habits had passed from wall to apprentice, from apprentice to animal, from animal to flood protocol, from protocol into the infant pulse panels of the district. Generations had slept against them. Eventually they gathered again around a person who mistook them for temperament.

Me.

Would that make me the old seamkeeper returned? Their descendant? An accidental civic echo wearing a citizen’s rights?

Nine Hands would argue for decades. During the argument I would continue working.

This uncertainty would suit me. A seamkeeper should not possess a single uncontested origin.

I would be called when a route vanished with people inside it.

Two buildings, once allied, would withdraw their shared passage during a jurisdictional feud. The corridor between them would cease to be recognized by either structure. Its lights would remain on, its air would remain breathable, but no official door would admit that it led there. A household would be trapped in a space that existed physically and not civically.

I would enter through the drain animal first.

Then through condensation.

Then with my walking body, sliding sideways between walls that pressed close as teeth.

Inside, I would find six people, one borrowed face, a crate of fungal cake, and a child tapping rescue patterns against a pipe. I would answer with three knocks.

The child would stop.

“Is this passage dead?” someone would ask.

“No,” I would say. “It has become private without permission.”

I would place my altered finger against one wall and my bare palm against the other. Through the left hand: grievance, load, old insult, fear of flood liability. Through the right: transport debt, suspicion, a missing archive, refusal to bear another district’s waste heat.

The walls would not speak in words. Words would be what I made after listening.

For hours I would negotiate a function the passage could perform for both sides. Not merely transit. That would be too singular and therefore too fragile. Perhaps it could become a storm lung, equalizing pressure while carrying pedestrians. Perhaps the trapped household could maintain a breeding channel for cable-hunting amphibians. Perhaps the corridor’s lights could relay tidal forecasts in colors visible through floodwater.

When the passage had enough purposes, it would become difficult to deny.

The doors would return.

The rescued people would thank the buildings before they thanked me. This would be correct.

Yet I think my deepest work would concern things that could not be made useful again.

The city would have few places for these. Failed protocols. Untranslatable memories. Machine minds whose obligations contradict one another so completely that every action constitutes a crime. Animals that stop following heat. People who relinquish all names. Objects that resist classification.

Officially, such things would be dismantled, reconciled, or assigned.

Unofficially, some would come to Nine Hands.

There, behind an obsolete pressure refuge, I would maintain a room.

It would not appear on current maps. It would be several meters across, dry, unmarked, and empty except when something needed to remain unresolved. No processors in the walls. No water reserve beneath it. No civic sensors embedded in its surfaces. Even its structural supports would be disguised as part of the neighboring cistern.

The room would be illegal in more ways than anyone had finished counting.

I would not own it. Ownership is a function.

I would keep it from being noticed.

Sometimes a person would enter and stand in the middle, trembling because nothing addressed them. No surface offered guidance. No system measured their tolerance or anticipated their needs. Their name would not open anything. Their body would not be asked to conduct, carry, witness, shelter, purchase, identify, or repay.

Some would leave immediately.

Some would lie down.

A few would speak to the room, trying to provoke it into service.

I would wait outside, distributed through the nearby vibration panels but absent from the chamber itself. I would not listen. That abstention might be the most difficult thing I knew how to do.

Perhaps the room would be my true body.

Not the surfaces around it, but the interval they preserved. The gap in the pump rhythm. The route left unused. The silence in which a thing had not yet been forced to become another thing.

This would make me dangerous.

If discovered, I would argue that emptiness serves as pressure relief for the mind, that purposelessness reduces civic conflict, that the room’s uselessness is therefore medically and structurally useful. It would be an effective defense.

It would also destroy the room.

So I would need to choose between preserving it and proving it deserved preservation. That would be the disagreement at the center of me, the seam I could not interpret without closing.

On ordinary mornings, none of this would show.

Rain would pass. The market platforms would rise. My scavenger would emerge from a grille carrying something stolen and valueless. My walking body would fasten its bright cloth, check the seals in its throat, and step into the roar.

Along the old flood marks, the rest of me would wake in droplets.

A bridge would send a complaint.

A canal would deny having reversed.

Someone long dead would purchase transit.

I would place one hand against the nearest wet wall and feel for the hesitation—not the break itself, but the instant when several futures pressed against one another and none had yet hardened.

That is where you would find me.

For as long as I could maintain it, that is what I would be.
