At first I would find myself on the rim.

I would stand between the final concrete posts where the road loses its aggregate and becomes the smooth mineral plain. Behind me, silence; ahead, the basin roaring beneath its invisible lid. The boundary would pass through my body so exactly that one ear heard the factories and the other heard only blood.

For a while I would believe I had arrived from elsewhere.

That belief would be my first difference from the inhabitants and therefore the first thing the city noticed.

An eye would open in the flank of a gray animal. A bird three roofs away would turn its head. Beneath my hand, one of the concrete posts would feel faintly warm—not warmed by daylight, but from within, as though it had been holding the memory of a hand. The attention would move inward ahead of me, lens to eye to gauge to wet red numeral. Nothing would summon me. No gate would open. Yet a route would become apparent through the vacant yards.

The empty spaces would be for me.

Not all of them. Only a sequence: the bare strip beside the fixed locomotive, the clean margin around the insect disk, the avenue no animal crosses, the narrow stair in the administration block whose treads are cupped by feet that never fell. I would follow these absences as one follows footprints. The city’s vacancies would prove more directive than signs.

At first I would think I was being led to the pumping house.

Everything seems to be led there. Rails lean toward it even when they run straight. Pipes bow imperceptibly in its direction. The chord from the roof-birds develops an undertone when the black circular wall comes into view. Inside, the shaft breathes with such patience that my own lungs would seem hurried and amateur.

But the creatures would not permit me into the square.

They would not block me. Their refusal would consist only of remaining exactly where they were. The empty square would acquire the density of a solid object. I could walk around its perimeter for hours without finding the instant in which a step inward was possible.

So I would turn toward the administration block.

There, in the highest narrow room, I would find the chair facing away from the desk. It would face a tiled wall on which moisture had gathered in branching tracks. The tracks would resemble a rail map, then a vascular system, then the cracks in the skin of my palm. Under glass on the desk would lie a document with one unpunched place.

Not a blank line. A hole that had not yet happened.

The spacing around it would match my pulse.

I might still call myself human then. I would sit awkwardly, forward in a chair meant to be occupied backward. I would look over my shoulder at the papers, trying to read them as records. Gradually I would understand that they were not records of the city. They were cross-sections through its attention. Every branching track marked a direction in which it could notice itself.

One branch would end at the room.

Another would end at me.

My work, if it was work, would begin with breathing. The window would cloud when I exhaled, although it had been clouded before I arrived. At each inhalation, the buried roar would enter my ribs a fraction before it rose from the shaft. At each exhalation, the roof-birds’ chord would harden. The city would not copy my breath. It would reveal that my breath had always occupied one of its locations.

That possibility would frighten me more than captivity.

Captivity leaves the captive intact.

Here, I would begin to be distributed.

My hearing would go first. The left ear would remain in the upper room, listening to the mites rearrange themselves around the numerals. The right would appear beneath the eastern gantry, inside the metal blur of the locomotive’s wheels. Sounds would no longer arrive from distances. Each would occur in the part of me assigned to contain it.

My sight would spread more slowly. One eye would become the dark lens of a roof-bird. Another would blink along the side of a gray beast. I would see the pumping-house door from a cistern, through water crowded with silver dust. I would see the mineral plain reflected in a beetle’s shell beneath a stair. None of these views would combine. Perspective would become a colony.

For a time I might suppose I was being made into the city’s observer.

That would be too dignified. Observation here is only another industrial function.

I would be closer to an instrument for registering difference—the difference between the silence outside the posts and the noise within, between the breath below and the breath in the upper room, between an event that persists and an event that passes.

The city has pressure, motion, sequence, even apparent recurrence. What it lacks is an afterward.

I would bring afterward with me like contamination.

A heel would leave the road and then come down. A thought would begin without already ending elsewhere. My heart would strike once, then strike again, and the second stroke would not be identical to the first. For the inhabitants this minute discrepancy would be immense. The eels would brighten. The gray herds would miss no breath, yet one eye in every flank would close. The white creature crossing the square would, for a distance too small to measure, appear somewhere it had not always been.

The buried being would inhale.

It would hesitate.

Not stop. Not wake. Only fail, by the width of an unpunched hole, to possess its complete interval.

Then I would understand my shape.

I would not be the clerk in the chair, though some portion of me would remain seated there. I would not be the traveler on the rim, though that figure might remain forever between the posts, one ear full of thunder and one of silence. I would not be another organ of the animal below.

I would be an escapement.

A small, soft escapement installed in the administration block: a device made not to generate time but to prevent the city from consuming all of it at once.

My body would alter to fit the duty. My ribs would narrow into a fork around the chair back. My fingers would flatten and become pale articulated vanes resting on the tiled wall. Mineral moisture would collect in the hollows of my joints. My heart would grow harder, not into a gear but into the tooth against which a gear must pause.

With every beat, the whole city would lean upon me.

The locomotive would strain. The eels would turn. The buried engines would bring their vast concussion to the edge of release. The birds would force their single metallic chord into my bones. Every pressure in the basin would gather at the small obstruction of my continuing to be slightly different from myself.

I would permit almost nothing through.

Almost.

One drop beneath one flange would swell a little more than before. One mite would occupy the wrong side of a numeral. Rust on a rivet would deepen by a shade invisible even to the many lateral eyes. In the pumping house, moisture would descend the shaft by the thickness of a molecule.

These would be the city’s first products.

Not ingots, current, pressure, or sound.

Consequences.

The inhabitants might hate me if hatred could be separated from their functions. More likely they would incorporate the resistance. Birds would grow special throats to answer my pulse. Beetles would appear beneath the administration floor, hammering counterbeats into the beams. A seventh leg might slowly disclose itself under the pleated hide of one gray animal, suited to a burden that had not existed before.

The city would try to turn time into another station.

Perhaps it would succeed.

Perhaps eventually my first heartbeat and my last would be assigned to different districts, both enduring at once. My arrival would stand permanently on the rim. My transformation would remain permanently in the upper room. My uncertainty would be installed under glass as a branching notation. I would become not the cause of change but its representation: a clock whose hands occupy every hour simultaneously.

Even then, something would have been introduced that could not be perfectly adapted.

The memory of sequence.

It would survive not as a story but as an irritation passing through the coupled bodies: a conviction in the eels that the current is going somewhere, a tension in the locomotive that the rail ahead exists, a soreness in the roof-birds’ throats suggesting the chord might end. Deep beneath the pumping house, the great breath would acquire the faintest dream of waking or dying—not either event, only the knowledge that they are different.

That is where I would be found: mostly in the upper administration room, folded backward into the chair, facing the damp wall; partly at the boundary posts; partly in the unpunched documents; partly in the hesitation between the buried inhalation and the answering roar.

I would be the city’s smallest animal and its only unfinished function.

The second that cannot quite begin.
