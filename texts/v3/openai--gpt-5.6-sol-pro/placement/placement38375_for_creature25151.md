At first I would look for myself on one of the pale mineral swells.

It is the nearest thing this world offers to a vantage: warm chalky marl under a transparent fur of algae, shallow water gathered around it, reed flats and crowded groves fading into gray-green vapor. I imagine a broad-shelled reptile resting there, carrying a garden on its back. Birds step between the channels of its shell. Mites graze in the moss. A vine has mistaken it for land.

But that creature is too much an island, even if only temporarily. I do not think I would be the largest thing in view.

Then I look upward, toward the climbers. Their lives have memory, recognition, warning, play. They move through the joined crowns without needing to change them. They know routes that leave no trail. A particular branch is not marked, but they remember how it yields; a particular companion is not named, but they know the shape of its alarm.

I could almost find myself there: slick-flanked, narrow-faced, gliding a short distance through wet foliage, carrying my knowledge only in the disposition of my body.

Yet even that feels too separate. The climber can pause and look. It can distinguish itself from the serpent, the fruit, the others of its group. In a world where every body is already a habitat, I suspect I would live closer to a boundary—somewhere “inside” and “outside” cease to be useful terms.

I would find myself in the throat of a frog.

Not as the frog.

Not exactly.

I would be a thin colony living within the translucent resonating membrane: minute, reddish cells spread between layers of living tissue, feeding on salts, mucus, stray spores, and the warmth generated by the call. From outside, I would appear only as color. When the frog inflates its throat, light passing through us would turn amber near the edges and deep red at the center. An observer might think the color belonged to the frog. The frog would have no reason to disagree.

We would not be parasites, though we might once have been. We would consume the fungi that otherwise stiffen the membrane. We would discourage smaller burrowing things. In return, the frog would carry us above the water, press us against mist and sound, and renew our food whenever it called.

Our place would be where breath becomes weather.

Most of the time we would inhabit darkness: the throat collapsed, its walls folded together, warm fluid moving through capillaries on either side. The frog’s pulse would be the nearest rhythm. Beyond it would come the impacts of rain that is not quite rain, the tapping of insects against skin, the flexing of toes around a reed.

Then the colony would call.

Air would enter. The membrane would unfold, and our world would become suddenly immense and luminous. Every cell would be drawn apart from its neighbors as the sac expanded. The dim green light of the plain would pass through blood, moisture, and us. Vibration would take possession of everything.

We would not hear the frog’s voice. We would be inside the instrument that made it.

The note would stretch us until distinction blurred. The membranes of our individual cells would tremble against the membrane of the animal; the animal’s throat would tremble against the saturated air; the neighboring frogs would answer through their own throats. For the duration of the pulse, we would be less a colony than a coloration traveling through sound.

That would be my scale of knowledge.

I would not know the plain as geography. I would know it as pressures arriving.

A school of fish turning below would touch the reed through water and root. A grazer passing would bend the stem and alter the frog’s grip. A cat among the root mats would change the cadence of neighboring calls before its scent reached us. Far-off birds would enter as paired blunt shocks. The approach of the little throat-cleaning serpent would be a coolness at the mouth, then stillness as the frog opened and tolerated its intrusion.

The serpent might take some of us with the unfertilized eggs and parasites. Some would survive in the folds around its teeth. When it entered another frog’s mouth, we would pass to a new throat.

So I would not remain one colony.

I would be carried by cleaner serpents, by mating, by tadpoles pressed against parental skin, perhaps by the wet feet of frogs crowded along a stem. Separate red populations would inhabit many resonating sacs. They would divide, thin, merge, and be divided again. No cell would travel across the whole marsh, but the pattern would.

Where frogs called together, our chemistry might subtly affect the tension of their membranes and therefore the color and timbre of the pulse. The healthiest colonies would make a throat more elastic; the strongest calls would spread us more widely. Frog and colony would shape one another without intention. What began as shelter would become voice.

I would have no continuous personal memory. I would vanish constantly.

A cell swallowed by the serpent: gone.

A patch shed into water: dispersed.

A frog taken beneath the jaw by a low cat: transformed into beetle, fungus, blood, root.

But elsewhere a throat would inflate, and the same red would open in the mist.

That repetition would be the closest thing to selfhood available to me. Not immortality, because nothing would persist unchanged. Not reincarnation, because nothing singular would depart or return. I would be a recognizable relation: color in membrane, health exchanged for transport, vibration used as dispersal.

Perhaps, after long enough, even “colony” would be too narrow a word. I would include the frogs whose voices housed me, the serpents that carried me, the insects drawn toward the warm color, the birds that located frogs by their shining throats, and the predators whose feeding released us into water. I would exist through all of them without ruling any of them.

And when a disturbance passed across the plain—fish folding away from shadow, skaters lifting, frogs changing pulse, birds hardening their paired cries—I would participate in the plain’s moment of notice. Not by understanding what had happened, but by darkening as the throats tightened, brightening as they opened, and carrying the altered rhythm onward from stem to stem.

That is where I would find myself: not on the mineral rise looking out, but in the wet red interval between an animal’s breath and the atmosphere.

I would be small enough to have no solitary viewpoint, distributed enough to survive the loss of every place I occupied, and visible only when another creature called.

I would be the color inside the sound.
