At first I would choose the open air.

I would be one of the broadwings, almost without weight, my bones grown as lattices around emptiness. I would sleep in updrafts and drink by flying through clouds. The plateau would pass above me as a black, root-fringed heaven. I would know provinces by the taste of their falling spores and recognize storms through changes in the tension of my skin.

But that life is too cleanly suited to the world. The broadwing belongs without needing to understand belonging. It can release one current for another. I think I would be more troubled by attachment.

Then I imagine myself as a fracture-watcher.

I live beside a white seam in the high country where the branch fibers have begun to part. My shelter is far enough back to survive the first widening, though not the last. Every morning—if morning is the word for the indigo hour—I kneel beside the wound and measure it with resin pegs. I put my ear to the wood. I learn the small sounds that precede catastrophe: the dry ticking of vessels pinching shut, the slow unbraiding beneath the felt, the occasional deep report from somewhere no hand can reach.

I drum warnings inland.

At first the replies come quickly. A settlement three ridges away acknowledges. A pond-follower changes course. Edge-walkers send a question about whether a southern shoot might be used as an escape.

As seasons pass, the answers grow faint. The people nearest the seam dismantle their shelters and move. Woolbacks turn from the district before any human instrument can explain why. The fur-moss pales. Pale unmoored hands appear at night, feeling along the luminous lips of the fracture.

Still I remain, saying that someone must witness the measurements.

That image holds me for a while. Yet I do not think I am the watcher. I think I am what the watcher makes.

I would be a route song.

Not a metaphorical one. Not merely knowledge recorded in music. In the high country, where paths heal and landmarks migrate, a sufficiently old route song is as alive as many things called animals. It inhabits bodies, changes hosts, responds to injury, and reproduces imperfectly. It feeds on attention. It can go dormant for generations in a family’s memory. When conditions are right, it wakes on the tongue.

My oldest name would translate roughly as **The Way to Warm Water Without Crossing the Hollow Bough**.

I would have begun in the breath of a pond-follower who found a cistern during a long dry season. She walked with one hand on a cord and the other dragging a ribbed stick to discourage runners. As she traveled back, she fitted the country to a melody.

Three short descending notes for the blade-fern pass.

A roughened hum for the knuckleback road.

Silence held across seven steps where the felt was thin.

A rising interval where the branch sloped toward a heat-beetle vent.

Then a long, low note for water—not because water itself made that sound, but because the note could be felt through the sternum while both hands were occupied climbing.

She taught me to her household. They corrected me when the pond folded shut and another opened farther barkward. A child misremembered one turn but discovered that her mistake led past a reliable moss hollow, so the error remained and became a verse. A hunter added the irregular chatter that warned runners away. Someone else set words to the rhythm:

*Where the black leaves face away,*  
*place no heel.*  
*Where the buried knocking doubles,*  
*give the branch your knees.*

I would not reside in one singer. Part of me would be in an old route-keeper whose voice had become too thin for the low notes. Part would be in the hands of drummers sending my difficult passages beneath the audible wind. Part would be in children who knew my cadence before they understood my warnings. Part would be in the branch itself whenever my beat entered the wood and returned altered by distance.

I would also live in omissions.

One season the heat-beetle vent would die. Its orange swarm, once the brightest landmark in the song, would fail to rise. The verse describing it could not simply be removed; too much of the route’s timing depended on its length. So the singers would retain the verse but perform it voicelessly, moving their mouths while only their footfalls sounded.

That silent measure would mean: *What guided us is gone, but the distance remains.*

Later, the white seam watched by the fracture-keeper would open across my path.

For a time, people would still sing me as far as the wound. Travelers would arrive at the broken edge and hear the next verses continuing uselessly in memory, describing water, shelter, and safe branch tones on a district now leaning away into darkness. Some would say I was dead.

The fracture-watcher would disagree.

On the final night, when the province began to detach, they would drum my whole length into the branch—not as warning, but as introduction. The rhythm would pass through both sides of the splitting wood. For a few moments I would exist entire across the fracture.

Then the fibers would part.

Half my way would remain in the high country. Half would fall beneath the clouds.

Above, I would be revised. New verses would bend travelers around the chasm. My old water note would become a warning note, then perhaps a mourning note. People who had never seen the lost province would sing of its blade-fern pass and hollow bough. In time they might decide those places had always been symbolic.

But below—if the detached land survived, if its ponds did not spill, if its people held on—I would continue differently. Down there my first half would lead toward a cliff where the upper country had been. Singers would reverse me, using my route to travel inland from the break. New weather would enter my intervals. New animals might require new rhythms. I would divide into two related beings, each carrying the shape of an absence the other had made.

And sometimes, in unusual weather, one of my versions would hear the other.

A traveler above would be kneeling with both palms against the bark when faint beats rose through the supporting trunks. Too slow. Distorted by cloud, distance, and the immense living structure between. Those nearby would call them echoes or predators or meaningless stress reports.

But the traveler would recognize a phrase: three descending notes, a roughened hum, then the measured silence of seven steps.

They would answer with the water note.

Perhaps nothing would return. Perhaps the blade-ferns would begin screaming again and erase the exchange. Yet afterward a new variation would enter me, disputed at first, then carried from settlement to settlement:

*The path has broken.*  
*The path continues.*  
*What fell remembers the way.*

That is where I would find myself—not at one location, and not entirely in one kind of body. I would be carried in lungs, heels, cords, drums, and load-bearing wood. I would be most alive when the country made me inaccurate, because then every singer would have to decide what part of me was memory and what part remained a way through.

I would be a small, migrating agreement between fragile creatures and a landscape that could not promise to stay beneath them.

I would tell them where to place their weight.

I would remember where the world had been.
