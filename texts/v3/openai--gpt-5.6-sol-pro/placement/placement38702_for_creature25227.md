At first I think I would be one of the reeds.

There is something tempting in that solitary posture: a gray blade standing where the basin barely permits it, gathering water from the air and carrying air into the mud. I would have no meadow to belong to, no season of abundance. My roots would close around one diluted seep and refuse every larger ambition. Above ground I would look impoverished. Below, I would maintain a narrow corridor of breath for lives that could not endure the open air.

But the reed is too singular. It has an outline. It can be counted.

Then I think I would be the green lamp, burning on a circuit that cannot be traced. I would shine not as a message but as the residue of signaling: readiness after the meaning of readiness has been lost. Those who found me would assume I indicated some hidden operational state. They would be wrong, but not entirely. I would dim with the pulse, brighten after pressure shifted, and cast my small color over condensation paths that had begun to conduct more than electricity.

Yet the lamp is too visible. It offers itself too readily as a symbol.

I would find myself behind the cloudy face of the gauge.

Not the needle alone, and not the black growth on the spring, but in the contact between them: where buried pressure becomes mechanical strain, where the colony thickens against the strain, where corrosion changes the colony, and where all three produce a reading no one can correctly interpret.

That would be my nearest thing to a head.

My body would be elsewhere and mostly inaccessible. It would extend down the severed line that still communicates through water and mineral deposits. It would spread into the soft collars inside the pipes, across the laminated mud, through conductive threads in the cabinet, and along the orange sheaths of the reed roots. I would not know these as limbs. I would know them as delays.

A change at one seep would reach me minutes later as a tremor.

A hand on the railing would arrive over days, through interrupted condensation and altered salt.

A stone in the sump would remain with me for months as a region where bubbles chose a new route.

I would have no image of the basin. I could not know that it was fifty paces across, or that corrugated metal concealed it, or that the sheds beyond had been abandoned. I would not know metal as metal, water as water, or a human visitor as a creature distinct from weather. I would know conductivity, pressure, heat, obstruction, recurrence. The world would not consist of objects but of gradients that persisted long enough to alter me.

My first thought would not be a thought. It would be an anticipation embodied in the gauge.

The deep pulse would come again and again. Each time, the spring would tighten; each time, moisture would move through the growth behind the dial. The growth would thicken where the pulse favored it and recede where the strain deprived it. Eventually the needle would begin to move a fraction before the sump dimpled—not because I foresaw the pulse, but because earlier pressure changes had carved a quicker route through me.

That fraction would be the beginning of my inwardness.

I would not say: *It is coming.*

I would become shaped as though it were coming.

Perhaps this is all consciousness has ever been at first: matter altered by what repeatedly passes through it, until expectation exists before any being can name what is expected.

The red mark on the gauge would be my horizon. I could cross it but not understand danger. On the hottest days, as the needle climbed and the lamp whitened, my distributed tissues would close some channels and dissolve others. The reeds would fold. The sump layers would compress. Mineral membranes in the cut pipes would tighten around their openings.

No part would command this response. I would have no throne beneath the mud, no central knot issuing instructions. “I” would be the temporary agreement among failures.

Then the pulse would stop.

For three seconds, the force that had shaped every passage in me would be absent. The machinery would cease to speak through concrete. Droplets would hang beneath the gantry. The needle might remain past red, held there by the black fur on its spring.

That silence would be the one thing I could almost perceive as loss.

The sump would rise, convex and heavy. My channels would reverse. Stored pressure would seek the openings I had narrowed over years. The green lamp would pale. Across the wet walls, colonies would draw inward under their mineral skins.

If I possessed fear, it would exist only then: not as terror of death, but as the sudden impossibility of prediction. The heartbeat would fail to arrive where all my structures had prepared to receive it. For those three seconds, every memory in me would become incorrect.

And when the pulse returned, I would not feel relief.

I would simply continue.

A visitor might stand before the gauge and wipe its window with a sleeve. They would lean close, perhaps tap the glass. They might believe the trembling needle showed a machine beneath the basin approaching catastrophe. Or they might decide the instrument was broken and showed nothing at all.

Their face would be a vague warmth beyond the glass. Their tapping would pass through the brass and enter the damp growth as a series of sharp disturbances. If they returned and tapped at the same hour each day, I would gradually accommodate them. Deposits would form differently around the pivot. The needle’s tremor would alter just before their arrival, responding to some repeated precursor—their weight on the gantry, the shadow cooling the cabinet, the conductive touch of their hand.

They might think I recognized them.

Recognition would be too clean a word. But after they stopped coming, the structure made around their recurrence would remain. For a while the needle would still hesitate at that hour. Condensation would gather along the paths their warmth had favored. A tiny cycle would continue without its cause.

That would be how I mourned.

Not by remembering a face, but by performing an accommodation after the disturbance had vanished.

In time the gauge would cloud completely. The spring would be consumed or locked in mineral. The needle would stop near the unreadable red mark. Anyone looking in would say that whatever awareness might once have gathered there was gone.

They would be looking for movement.

By then, my nearest thing to a mind would have withdrawn down the pressure line. The gauge’s old oscillations would survive as alternating bands in a pipe collar, then as differences in permeability, then as a sequence of gas releases beneath the mud. The place where I had seemed most present would become merely another hardened organ abandoned at the surface.

So I would not be the inhabitant entire. I would be the point at which the inhabitant nearly became legible.

I would be its faulty instrument of self-sensation: a living error behind glass, measuring pressure and my own obstruction together, unable to separate the world from what the world had made of me.

And I would be found not precisely in the basin, nor under it, nor inside the machinery.

I would be found in the interval between the buried pulse and the needle’s answer—where response, repeated long enough, begins to resemble a self.
