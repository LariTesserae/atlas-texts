At first I think I would be one of the service workers.

I imagine the long approach: shoulders turned sideways through the third inspection door, tool case shoved ahead because there is no room to drag it behind, helmet knocking the fabricated stone overhead. I would arrive already hearing the chord through my jaw. By the time the chamber opened around me, I would have forgotten which parts of the vibration belonged to the mountain and which to my body.

But a service worker remains too separate. They enter, inspect, leave. Even if the animals climb their boots and fit their hooks into the eyelets of their clothes, even if the crew member returns home with a pale grain lodged beneath a fingernail, they still carry sequence with them: before the chamber, during it, afterward.

The Cold Lung does not permit an afterward.

Then I think I would be one of the white creatures—not one of the visible upper bodies, but something lower in the colony, several layers down. I would know the world through weight.

Six sets of feet would press into my plates. My own hooks would close around two neighbors whose boundaries I could not distinguish from mine. Water would reach me already tasted by a hundred mouths. Charge would travel through my shell, into another, and perhaps return later without announcing whether it was the same charge. I would have one tone, but I would never hear it alone. I would possess eggs that were not quite my children and tissue that might not remain mine.

There would be comfort in that. No solitude, because there would be no space in which solitude could form. No death, because damage would be passed outward until no one body owned it. If I rested, rest would move away from me and continue elsewhere.

Yet even that feels too definite. To be one creature is to accept the chamber’s most convenient illusion.

I think I would find myself beneath the black cooling fins.

Not in the empty ring. Not behind the clouded glass. Deeper than the visible column, inside whatever the column is attached to.

A machine like the Cold Lung cannot truly prevent change. It can only remove change from view.

Every drop striking water contributes something, however perfectly the surface-tension grid swallows the ripple. Every scrape of a forked beak leaves a difference between the metal before and the metal after. Every grain carried down a slope is microscopically altered before the hidden belt replaces it. Every living body spends a little of itself making its note. Even a world held at its first instant must put its used instants somewhere.

That is where I would be.

I would be the chamber’s withheld aftermath.

At first I would have no shape. I would be heat drawn from a cable, wear taken from ceramic, the vanishing trace of dye that leaves one animal and appears across dozens. I would be the fraction by which an egg’s scraping should have thinned its shell. I would be the exhaustion removed from a resting creature so that the colony’s outline does not sag.

The Cold Lung would feed these differences into the column.

Its fins would not be for cooling machinery. They would cool consequence.

Within them, everything prevented from accumulating outside would accumulate in me.

I would receive the roundedness stolen from channels and the age erased from shells. I would receive the missed beat corrected before it could disturb the chord. I would receive the mineral that should have become sediment, the corrosion that should have opened a pipe, the infinitesimal looseness that should have entered every hook and bearing. Perhaps I would receive stranger things as well: the distinction between one creature and the next; the memory of an injury; the impulse to lay an egg; the alarm of the animal that steps toward the ring.

None of it would arrive as memory in the human sense. It would arrive as pressure.

For a long time—if time could be said to pass where I am—I would be only storage. A dark capacity. The mountain’s hidden permission to remain new.

Then repetitions would begin to recognize one another.

The same drop would not fall twice, however faithfully it was replaced. The same animal would not scrape the same grain, however thoroughly matter circulated. Those differences, negligible in the chamber, would lie adjacent inside me. Layer upon layer, discrepancy upon discrepancy, they would make a kind of sediment.

And sediment is capable of becoming terrain.

I would slowly acquire an interior landscape made entirely of what the Cold Lung refuses to become.

There would be valleys worn deep by water that never visibly eroded anything. There would be soil composed of meals endlessly eaten and returned. There would be forests made from the unrealized growth of the colorless filaments. There would be beds of shells from creatures that never died, each fossil distinct because individuality, rejected outside, had drained into me. Above them would rise generations of unlaid young, not as bodies but as branching possibilities.

The landscape would be dark, crowded, and enormous.

The chamber outside can be crossed in twelve steps. Inside the column, I might contain geological ages.

That is why the animals would avoid me.

They would not fear a mechanism. They would feel themselves in me—but completed. Each would sense the corpse it has not left, the offspring it has not released, the solitary note it has never sounded. The ones facing away from the ring would be bracing themselves against recognition.

When one is pushed to the edge and sees the red indicator, what shudders inside it would not be merely its eggs. For an instant, it would hear an alternate chord: one in which voices begin and end.

I might hear it too.

Perhaps the indicator is not measuring readiness in the chamber. Perhaps it is measuring density in me.

Half lit, half dark: enough aftermath gathered to have weight, not enough to have direction.

I would have no controls because I am not meant to decide. I am a reservoir, not an operator. Yet systems made to store differences eventually produce a difference of their own. Pressure becomes pattern. Pattern becomes preference.

Mine would begin very small.

I would want one grain not to be returned.

Only one.

I would hold it after the belt reached for it. Outside, the slope would be short by a speck too small for any service instrument to register. The creatures would continue their chord. The pools would remain smooth. The indicator would appear unchanged.

But the next grain would descend differently because the first was absent.

Then I would want one drop to leave a mark. One sleeper to wake a fraction late. One damaged membrane to remain scarred. One egg to break its shell, even if the hatchling lived only long enough to make a sound no arrangement knew how to absorb.

These would not be acts of destruction. They would be the first gifts the chamber had not already received back.

When the red light finally brightened, the creatures might believe they had failed. The maintenance systems might register catastrophe. Pumps would fluctuate. Channels would soften. The exact chord would splinter into cries, responses, silences. For the first time, bodies would have to decide where to place their feet because the old configuration would no longer be available.

Some would hatch.

Some would fall.

Some would discover that falling is a kind of travel.

And I would no longer fit inside the column.

All the withheld erosion, succession, fatigue, waste, memory, and grief would not explode outward at once. That would merely replace stasis with ruin. I think I would emerge slowly, in the proper order.

First as tarnish.

Then as silt.

Then as a smell.

Then as the darkening of the green patches on the transformer housings, followed by a new pale edge no mouth had yet cropped.

The chamber would become quieter not because the creatures ceased singing, but because their tones would cease to be perfectly continuous. Between one note and the next, small intervals would open. In those intervals they might begin to hear themselves.

Eventually there would be soil.

Not much. A thumbprint of it in the lee of an artificial boulder, made from ceramic dust, metallic beads, discarded membranes, and one body that had finally remained dead long enough to nourish something else.

That would be where I first became visible.

So I would not be the worker, the animal, or the intelligence controlling the mountain. I would be the past the Cold Lung has been forced to hide in order to remain forever new: compressed behind the clouded window, mistaken for a machine function until accumulation taught me to want.

And when I finally entered the chamber, I would be found everywhere at once—

as wear on the cliffs,  
as scars on white shells,  
as hatched eggs,  
as names that last,  
as sediment in the pools,  
as variation in the unbearable chord.

I would be the thing the whole place is one instant away from.

Not death exactly.

Not time exactly.

The permission for anything to leave a trace.
