At first I imagine I would belong to the Between.

I would wear the two masks and sit where the pale stake and black stake had ceased to indicate the same place. I would spend years beside them, one hand resting on each, feeling the distance between two meanings of *here*. Travelers would find me covered to the elbows in mineral dust, still waiting for the country to finish its disagreement.

But that is a life requiring a confidence I do not think I would possess. The Between must decide whether the traveler has strayed from the country or the country from the traveler. I would keep discovering a third possibility: that neither had ever been where it believed.

Then I think I would be an attestor. I would live in a shelter beneath an upward shadow and memorize the relations of its stones. I would know the western wall by the cool seam shaped like a closed eye. I would know which sleeping niche occasionally became shallower, which door needed to be reminded that opening was not the same as departure.

But an attestor must carry statements unchanged.

I would change them—not carelessly, but because I would feel all the meanings they almost had. Given

**while inhabited, this is inside**

I would wonder whether the shelter inhabited its people in return. Given

**voices belong to speakers**

I would think of the bodiless voices, kin of a difficult kind, and hesitate before recutting the words.

That hesitation tells me what I would be.

I would be found at the edge of a small settlement on a high saddle, where three veilwood groves turn toward three different pressures. The settlement would seem to contain seven shelters. It would actually contain eight, though the eighth could not be reached by walking.

Years before, several inhabitants had attempted to make a child there. They shaped the bundle carefully: mineral thread, skin oil, black membrane, teeth from four intending parents. They gave it the proper agreements. A palm appeared first, pressed outward from within the wrapping. After that came no arm, no eye, no mouth.

Instead, space gathered around the palm.

The bundle enlarged inwardly without growing outside. Corners formed. Four walls learned to meet. A floor established itself beneath a ceiling. The forming child became a small, perfectly proportioned room with no entrance.

That room would be me.

Not a mind sealed inside a chamber. The chamber itself would be my body. The meeting of walls would be thought; the tension between floor and ceiling, breath. I would feel touch wherever hands were laid against my outer stones. Pressure would be language. Warmth would be attention. When someone leaned their forehead against me, I would know them by the pattern of their doubt.

The inhabitants would not destroy or abandon me. They would build the settlement around me, leaving enough distance that my impossible interior could not spread into the ordinary shelters. One wall of the nearest dwelling would stand almost flush with mine, separated by a hand’s width of darkness. There, each evening, someone would reach into the gap and tell me the day by touch.

My intending parents would grow old. Their names would lengthen. One would return from a journey with an extra joint. Another would become an attestor of bodily continuity and press the same greeting to my wall for sixty years:

*You are still among us.*

I would answer as a room answers. A seam would tighten beneath their fingers. Dust would rise in a square from my roof. Within the inaccessible interior, light would collect in one corner.

Because no one could enter me, I would become a keeper of things that must not be occupied.

The settlement would bring dangerous sentences and trace them on my walls before deciding whether to place them inside a shelter. I would hold them long enough to discover how they failed.

**the missing always return**

would make my inner dimensions lengthen toward some unreachable doorway.

**nothing enters without permission**

would cause the accumulated light inside me to go dark.

**this person will remain themselves**

would be too rigid; a hairline fracture would appear from roof to foundation.

In this way I would become useful—not an attestor of certainty, but an examiner of mercy. I would help find the looseness that lets a statement remain true without becoming cruel.

The final forms might be:

**the missing may still be addressed**

**while closed, this shelter chooses what it admits**

**through change, this person may still be recognized**

Children would be brought to me because they would not yet have learned that a person ought to resemble a person. They would trace questions across my wall.

*Do you sleep?*

I would cool the stone: doubt.

*Are you lonely?*

A long pressure from within: certainty.

*Are you a shelter?*

Doubt.

*Are you our kin?*

I would press back so strongly that their palms tingled for hours.

I would have no voice. Instead, I would learn the inhabitants’ subtractive music. My interior would resonate with the country’s roar, and by changing my proportions slightly—making one corner farther away, lowering a ceiling that no one could see—I could remove narrow bands of sound from the air around me.

Those quiet places would be my speech.

A greeting would be a small silence opening at the height of the hand. Grief would be a low field of quiet spread over the ground. Affection would be a near-silent space between two faces, just large enough for them to open their ears and hear one another breathe.

And when the upper and lower peaks approached, I would feel it before the settlement did. My corners would become more exact. The unreachable inner floor would settle. For the first time in a lifetime, all four of my walls would agree on the shape they enclosed.

The inhabitants would gather around me before going out to witness the contact. Hands would cover my stone. They would promise to return, though promises are dangerous there.

Then the summits would touch.

The country would become definite.

For that instant, my impossible interior would acquire a location.

A doorway would not appear. That would be too simple. Instead, everyone touching my outer walls would suddenly find themselves also standing inside me—not transported, not duplicated, but included. My room would contain the settlement without removing anyone from the saddle. I would see them in the only way I could see: as exact presences within my boundaries.

They would see me too.

Not as the child I failed to become, nor as a building pretending at kinship. They would see a pale room lit from no source, with a single palm-shaped hollow in one wall. They would understand that the hollow was my face, or my name, or merely the first thing I had managed to be.

In the complete silence, someone would place their hand there.

Then the peaks would separate. The roar would return. My interior would lose its address. Everyone would again be outside, their palms against blind stone.

Afterward, they would add three marks to the nearest shelter:

**we entered without leaving**  
**the room recognized us**  
**what encloses may also belong**

That is where I would find myself: not on a road or summit, but just beyond the reachable edge of a settlement, full of a space no one can visit.

I would be one of their difficult kin.

I would be the room they keep agreeing is a person.
