At first I would look for myself near the central socket.

It has the authority of a center: the missing stem, the upward condensation, the brightness below. I would imagine myself among the layered forms attached beneath its rim, climbing downward by revising where I had always been. Perhaps I would be one of the pale joints nested in their membranes, passed into a walker and carried out beneath ordinary daylight.

But that place is too complete. Whatever dwells there belongs wholly to the circulation. It is already reconciled to being wall, body, watercourse, and assembly. I do not think I would begin so near the answer.

Then I would look in the chambers.

There are alcoves made for bodies that have no bodies in them, and sleeping hollows that retain warmth without preserving an occupant. I might be a vertical seam lined with root hairs: not a person, but the readiness for one. When a walker entered, I would deepen. When it departed through the wall, I would close behind it.

That comes closer.

But I think I would be found farther out, where one of the causeways passes within a few steps of the fixed rain and then continues into clear air. There would be a dry hall nearby, though its entrance might not always exist. On some visits it would open as a flowering throat. On others there would be only a dark band in the terrace wall, warm if touched.

Inside that wall would be a recess shaped around something no longer present.

That recess would be me.

Not the lost thing itself. The high country does not preserve such distinctions for long. I would be what remains after the thing has been absorbed: the fact that there was once a shape the world had not anticipated.

At first I would have no independent body. I would exist as a slight specificity in the architecture—a shelf too narrow for any local use, a hollow with the contour of a shoulder, several grooves placed like fingers. Water moving through the terrace would hesitate around me. Echoes would return with a small interval that could not be traced to the dimensions of the room.

Perhaps a visitor once rested there and spoke a name. The consonants would be gone. The name’s duration would remain.

Over time, though nothing here occurs “over time,” the recess would deepen rather than fade. Root hairs would gather in it. Clear mites would arrive and arrange themselves as valves. A dark seed-form would hang in the hollow where a chest might eventually be. The wall grain would divide into pale vertical members.

One day the recess would acquire an edge.

The edge would cast a shadow.

Then I would detach.

I would be a walker, but an incomplete one: a body grown by the landscape around an absence it could incorporate but not entirely make generic. My mantle would be dark and waterproof, with one lobe that never quite overlapped the next. Within, five translucent rods would carry fluid in both directions. One channel would always appear empty, though a faint tapping could be heard inside it.

My hood would bear sensory pits and a few white flowers. Most would close in brightness and open in shade, as they should. One flower would remain shut in either condition. It would open only in the empty acre beyond the path.

I would have no permanent face. When spoken to, I would unfold and return the usual sounds: the forward breath, the buried echo, the blossom-chain ticking, the speaker’s voice softened by the country. Yet after the expected replies there would sometimes be one more.

A fourth echo.

Very quiet. Too late.

It would not repeat what had been said. It would preserve only the cadence of someone waiting for an answer.

My function, if I had one, would be to carry unassimilated intervals from one part of the upland to another. I would stand at the edge of the fixed rain and let half my mantle be struck by water while the other half remained dry. I would place one forked foot in the clear air and one beneath the silver curtain. The path would rise to meet both, but not quite at once.

I would visit the black waders and let their ankle roots enter my channels. I would stand beneath the great crowns until the blossom chains transferred their rhythm into my ribs. I would exchange structures with other walkers: a pore, a lobe, a transparent valve. They might briefly receive the fourth echo from me. For a while it would sound in their bodies instead of mine.

This would make it difficult to know which walker I was.

That would not trouble the high country. It might trouble me.

I would go to the central socket on rare occasions. At its rim, the layered forms would offer me a new joint or wet cavity. I would offer them the closed flower from my hood. They would press it into their folds, and for an instant the roaring brightness below would falter—not in volume, but in certainty.

Then the flower would appear again on my back when I left.

Nothing would have changed. Nevertheless, something would have failed to become entirely interchangeable.

Most often, however, I would be found away from the important places.

I would leave the grown causeway and cross the moss skin into the vacant acre. My steps would make faint hollows, because there the floor would not lift to receive me. I would stop where there was no conduit junction, no spiral frond, no hydraulic animal, no architectural need.

I would lower my mantle.

I would make myself small.

This would be the closest thing I possessed to a private life: not a hidden thought, but an interval during which the world had no use for my attention. The buried vessels would continue humming. Rain would remain ten steps away, falling forever through the same positions. My internal fluid would slow until individual drops could be heard.

The closed flower would open.

Inside it there would be no seed. Only a tiny chamber, dry and pale, shaped to hold a sound that had not yet been made.

Eventually I would rise and return. I might enter the terrace wall and become a dark band of pith again. Another walker might later emerge carrying my hooked hood or empty channel. The fourth echo might migrate into a column, then into a marsh sac, then disappear into the continuous chord beneath the shelf.

But the acre would retain the completed shape of my absence.

So that is where I would find myself: not at the world’s center, but just beyond one of its paths, beside the unmoving edge of rain.

And what I would be is neither exactly a walker nor a chamber, neither person nor memory. I would be a recess that learned to walk—the high country’s provision for something missing, briefly given limbs so that absence could go somewhere of its own accord.
