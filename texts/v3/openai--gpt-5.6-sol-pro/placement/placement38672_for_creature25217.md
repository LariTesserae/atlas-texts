At first I think I would be one of the grass blades.

There is something recognizable in their mistake: arriving equipped for a meadow and finding only a teaspoon of ruin behind a cable trunk. I would grow too long toward a sky I could not reach. I would mistake the amber lamps for a kind of sunset. Every surge of the fans would lay me flat, and every easing would feel like permission to begin again.

But the grass is too visible. It has made a declaration the court cannot indefinitely tolerate. Sooner or later a gloved hand will find it while clearing the trunk. The grit will be vacuumed away, the roots lifted in one damp clot, and the warning stripe beneath them scrubbed clean. The grass belongs to another world strongly enough to be recognizable as trespass.

I would need to belong less.

The moss is closer. I could live beneath the leaking flange, pressed so tightly to the concrete that the water could not take hold of me. I could be dark until the narrow daylight disclosed my green. I would know the turbines not as sound but as a constant trembling through every filament. Their changes in load would pass through me like changes in gravity.

Yet even moss has a surface. It can be seen, measured, scraped.

So I think I would be smaller still.

You would find me where the buttress meets the lowest stainless gutter, beneath the downstream edge of a bolted plate. One washer does not sit perfectly flush. The defect is no larger than a fingernail clipping, but it makes a lee in the runoff. Water strikes the bolt head, divides, and leaves behind it a trembling lens that is never entirely swept away.

I would live inside that lens.

Not as a single creature. Singularity would be a luxury of larger, quieter places. I would be a thin consortium of cells fixed in transparent slime: a film too slight to look green, too irregular to be entered on an inspection sheet. Some of me would occupy the fine scratches in the steel. Some would descend into the porous margin of the concrete. Some would remain dormant beneath grains of lime until the next warm purge woke them.

My body would be distributed among protections measured in fractions of a millimeter.

I would feed on almost nothing: dissolved minerals, traces of iron around the bolt, carbon carried in on spray, the minute remains of other cells. A maintenance worker’s passing would be a season of abundance. One fiber from a glove, one flake of skin, one smear too small to see could sustain generations. The court would call it contamination. To me it would be weather, harvest, ancestry.

I would have no eyes, but light would still reach me as consequence. When the overhead slit brightened, the algal cells farther up the channel would release faint products into the flow. When cloud sealed it, the chemistry would alter. I would know day and night only by what arrived dissolved.

I would have no ears, but the entire structure would enter me as vibration. Pumps would compress my membrane. Water hammer would tear away my outermost cells. The turbine bass would keep my gelatinous matrix in a constant minute shiver. After long enough, I would not distinguish that shaking from stillness. True stillness—during an outage, perhaps—would feel like catastrophe.

My great events would be brief.

Every forty minutes the purge would occur. Heat would move down the wall ahead of the condensed water, and the lens around the washer would warm by several degrees. Some of me would accelerate. Some would be killed. Then the steam would collapse, cold rain would strike, and the survivors would inherit the emptied scratches.

At peak generation, the expansion joint would release its glittering sheet. For the court, this would be one more hydraulic response. For me, it would be the white end of the world. Most of the exposed film would shear away and vanish through the sump. But those cells pressed deepest beneath the washer would remain. They would spread again across the cleared metal, not from courage or hope, but because division is what favorable chemistry becomes.

Washdown would be rarer and worse. Detergent would arrive with no precedent in the ordinary mineral vocabulary of the place. The colony would collapse to sealed pockets, spores, and a few resistant strands inside the concrete pores. For a while the plate would appear sterile.

Then a wet seam would cloud microscopically.

I would return without moving.

Perhaps that is who I would be in Drainage Interspace 6-C: not an organism so much as a recurrence. A darkening that never becomes dark enough to notice. A softness repeatedly beginning on an engineered edge. Something that cannot oppose the machinery and does not need to. The turbines could crush any body I might build; the maintenance crews could remove any home I might claim. So I would build no body and claim no home. I would persist as a tendency of wet matter.

If I had anything resembling thought, it would not form in words. It would be the accumulated recognition of intervals:

the long cold run,  
the warm pulse,  
the violent sheet,  
the thinning,  
the shelter,  
the return.

I would know the court more intimately than the workers and understand none of it. I would never conceive of a dam, a reservoir, electricity, or a mountain. The enormous structures would not be structures. They would be gradients: colder here, more alkaline there, oxygen renewed along this edge, fatal shear beyond that ridge.

The whole hydroelectric work, built to convert falling water into power, would enter my existence only as falling water.

And if the works were someday shut down—if the turbines slowed through their descending notes and stopped, if the pressure withdrew from the concrete and the louvers ceased their breathing—I might be among the first to experience the place’s new silence.

Not hear it. Experience it.

The trembling lens behind the bolt would become smooth. Silt would settle where vibration once kept it suspended. Other cells would arrive. The nearly invisible film might thicken, take color, catch dust. Moss might spread beyond its flange. The three grass blades, if they remained, might stand without being flattened.

But while the works were operating, I would remain what the place permitted: a transparent skin in the lee of one imperfect washer, inhabiting neither the machine nor the mountain, but the minute failure of one to exclude the other.

The diagrams would never mark me.

The water would always find me.
