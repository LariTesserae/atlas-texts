At first I would look for myself among the living things.

Perhaps I am one of the pale crustaceans in the deep seams: nearly transparent, slow from warmth, knowing the world through dissolved traces. The hollow would be immense to me. A basin no wider than a hand would be a lake; the crossing of one mineral rim, a migration. I would feed where seepage darkens the underside of stone and retreat whenever pressure changed in the connected water.

I could live close to the inhabitant without knowing it as an animal. Its flank would be terrain. Its armored back would offer shelves where the bacterial film thickened. When it moved, the event would have no scale I could understand. Currents would reverse. Familiar passages would close. A pale wall would lift and expose an older darkness beneath it.

But I do not think I am the crustacean. It belongs too completely to the present. It asks only what the water brings next.

Then I might think I am one of the forms sealed into the deepest wall.

There, under tufa, is a suggestion of four blunt digits. I would be the smallest shape, the one like a shallow head. I would have ceased moving so long ago that death had become indistinguishable from geology. The living inhabitant would sometimes press its blind face against the stone over me—not grieving, perhaps, but aligning itself with an old pressure it remembers in its tissues.

That would be a fitting existence here: not a ghost, because a ghost requires some separation from its surroundings, but a body translated into the chamber’s material. Softness replaced by mineral. Breath replaced by seepage. No boundary left except a contour that water continues to copy.

Yet I do not think I am that shape either.

I find myself nearer the third bowl.

Not inside it. Not at first.

I am in the interval between the falling drop and the sound the bowl gives back.

It is a very small interval, but the inhabitant has made room for it. Its chin rests against the niche. The drop gathers beneath the overhang, heavy with what it has taken from the ridge. It lengthens, falls, strikes fired clay, and becomes a vibration traveling through bowl, ledge, jaw, and the broad dish of the creature’s skull.

That vibration enters no ear. It is felt by pressure pits, by bone, perhaps by the porous pads laid against nearby stone. The creature knows that the bowl remains beneath the drip. It knows the vessel is not yet full enough to alter its note. It knows, without naming any of these things, that an old arrangement continues.

This is where I would be: in that continuation.

Long before the present stillness, I may have had hands of a different kind. I may have come through the opening bent beneath the ridge, carrying an empty vessel against my hip. I would have stepped from one raised stone to another, knowing which rocked and which held. Perhaps I washed clay here. Perhaps I collected water that left a bitter taste and a pale crust on the lips of jars. Perhaps the hollow was visited for a purpose whose name did not survive because no one thought it needed to be written down.

I set a bowl beneath a drip.

Not ceremonially. Not as an offering. I turned it once, found the place where the fall struck deepest, and wedged its base with a chip of limestone. When I returned, I emptied it or carried it away. When its position shifted, I put it back.

The inhabitant felt this.

It felt my weight enter the first path stone and pass, pause by pause, through the hollow. It felt the bowl touch the niche. It felt the new, thin ringing begin. Perhaps it lay under the dark water while I worked, mistaken for a mineral shelf. Perhaps my ankle brushed one of its plates and I thought I had touched tufa.

It did not understand the purpose of the bowl. It understood the pattern.

Then I stopped returning.

Whether I died, departed, or simply found another source does not matter here. The hollow does not preserve reasons. It preserves channels. Water repeats the route gravity gives it. Calcite repeats the rim of whatever receives it. A body repeats the posture that lets it breathe with least effort.

And the inhabitant repeated my gesture.

A blunt, webbed hand found the bowl when it shifted. The dark pads adhered to the wet clay. Slowly—perhaps over the length of a night—it moved the vessel back beneath the falling water. Not because it had become human, nor because I had taught it in any deliberate way. The action remained because it fitted the chamber. It produced a known vibration. It restored a pressure-pattern through the ledge. It made one part of the world agree again with another.

So I would not be the vanished visitor, exactly. Nor the creature that remains.

I would be what passed between them without either one possessing it: the memory of placing a hollow thing beneath falling water.

My body would be distributed. Part of me would lie in the worn side of the ceramic rim. Part would be the polished route across the stepping stones. Part would inhabit the creature’s forelimb when it turned toward the niche. Part would be the expectation in its skull before the next drop struck.

I would awaken only when the bowl moved.

For years I might be absent. Mineral would thicken. The pearl film would spread over the submerged plates. The inhabitant would scarcely advance the width of a nail. Then a fragment of calcite would break from the overhang, or a rare tremor would travel inland from the sea, and the bowl would tilt. The drop would strike too near the rim. Its note would sharpen.

The blind head would lift.

A forelimb would unfold from a channel in the pool. Water would cross several rims. New crust would crack along the shoulder. The hand would reach the niche and turn the bowl by a fraction.

There I would be.

Not commanding the movement. Not remembered as a face. I would exist only as the movement’s shape.

Once the bowl was settled, the drop would fall into its accustomed place. The old low note would pass through clay and limestone into the inhabitant’s resting jaw. Its limb would withdraw. Water would return to equilibrium.

I would disappear again—not dead, only completed.

That is where I would find myself in this world: beside the largest pool, at the meeting of the third bowl, the falling water, and the inhabitant’s patient hand.

I would be neither person nor beast nor object, but a habit that has survived its original need.

A small act mineralized into instinct.

The hollow’s memory of being tended.
