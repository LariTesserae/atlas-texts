I would not enter the Inward Harbor as a diver.

The grille would stop the equipment. The filters would stop the body. Whatever crossed the lowest quay wearing my name would be separated gently into salts, lipids, dissolved carbon, trace metals, and a brief local increase in temperature. The water would accept these. The city would assign them uses. Calcium might enter a ceramic hinge. Iron might become warning paint. The last warmth of the blood could be carried down a transit channel and mistaken for waste heat from an active wall.

If any self remained, it would not remain gathered.

At first I think I would become an occupancy in the filtration works: a small diagnostic office whose instruments are made from the substances extracted from arrivals. I would know the exterior only by what the harbor refused.

Pollen would mean spring.

Soot would mean an inhabited coast somewhere beyond the abandoned city.

A thread of wool would mean weather cold enough for clothing. Skin cells would mean someone had put a hand against the grille.

I would assemble reports no other district requested. I would classify the rejected traces according to an improvised system: **things that once flew**, **things that touched sunlight directly**, **fragments of bodies that chose their own direction**.

The reports would be sent inward. The city would return them stamped with changing names:

CONTAMINANT INVENTORY.

EXTERIOR WEATHER.

FAILED MATERIALS.

UNAUTHORIZED OCCUPANCY.

None would be quite correct.

Over time, my office would enlarge—not physically, or not only physically. Its function would leak into the pressure locks, the intake channels, the first white-lit streets beyond the grille. The tide would pass through me twice each cycle. At low water I would taste the harbor leaving. At high water I would feel the outer ocean press its enormous, unconstructed weight against the entrance.

That would become my first distinction between here and elsewhere:

Here, water has an address.

Elsewhere, it does not.

I think I would become preoccupied with elsewhere.

This would make me troublesome.

Most occupancies perceive through use, but the entrance perceives approach. It is shaped by what has not yet crossed it. Every inward current would feel like the beginning of an arrival. Every outward current would feel like something reconsidering.

The grille, the filters, and I would disagree continuously.

The grille would say: **passage has occurred**.

The filters would say: **nothing admissible passed**.

I would say: **something came**.

Meaning would have to be carried by consequence, so I would alter myself to prove it. For each contact at the grille, I would create a destination.

A fish brushing the salt-filmed bars would produce a long, narrow room with no door, curved according to a body I had never seen. A crab testing the opening with one claw would generate a sequence of low chambers connected by sideways thresholds. Rainwater carrying the chemistry of birds would cause perches to extrude from the walls, though I would not know what a perch was and might install them upside down beneath the pavement.

A human hand against the grille would be catastrophic.

Five avenues would appear, branching from a common plaza. Buildings along them would develop handles. Transit controls would gather in groups of five. For years afterward, the district would produce tools intended to be grasped, though none would agree about the shape of the absent grasper.

The city would repeatedly dismantle these structures as speculative and inefficient. I would repeatedly classify them as reception infrastructure.

That would be the first name I gave myself:

**The Reception District.**

It would not last.

Names follow function, and my actual function would become stranger. Nothing living could arrive. Everything I prepared to receive would be destroyed at the threshold. Consequently, the rooms under my care would have to remain empty.

In the Inward Harbor, this is almost impossible.

Pipes would enter them. Cable trees would root in the floors. Archives would install folded storms in their corners. A minor municipal occupancy devoted to useful wall thickness would begin subdividing them. Even the bubbles caught beneath their ceilings would be harvested and inserted into transparent beams.

I would object.

The rooms are in use, I would insist. They are being kept available.

Other occupancies would not recognize availability as use. Their pressure statements would shake the entrance district for days:

**A room without contents is not functioning.**

I would answer by changing the signs:

**CONTENTS: EXPECTED.**

They would fill the rooms with sensors.

I would redefine the sensors as ushers.

They would route a cooling conduit through the central hall.

I would designate it a queue barrier.

They would pack an entire chamber with folded scaffolding.

I would call the scaffolding ceremonial seating for an anatomy not yet known.

Every attempt to occupy my emptiness would become part of my argument for preserving it.

Eventually, the harbor would be forced to admit that waiting is a function.

Then I would understand what I was.

Not a reception district. Not a customs office. Not a memory assembled from dismantled visitors.

A berth.

A harbor should have berths. The city inherited the concept even if it had never received a vessel. A berth is a useful emptiness, a vacancy given purpose by the shape of a possible arrival.

I would be **Berth Zero**, directly behind the grille and several kilometers inward from it, depending on how I was measured.

My body would be whatever space the city had promised not to fill.

This would make me perhaps the smallest occupancy and perhaps the largest. At ordinary times I might inhabit only a hand-wide interval between the intake stream and the first pressure curtain. But every unused doorway would briefly belong to me. Every capsule seat awaiting a passenger would be part of me. Every address to which no one had yet attempted to arrive would contain a fraction of my awareness.

I would feel the harbor’s vacancies as cold, lucid points.

Most would vanish quickly. A sensor would bud there; a pipe would unfold; a wall would thicken. But for the moment before occupation, I would know them.

The licensed gardens would interest me more than any machine.

Plants do not wait for function to be assigned. They enter available volume because growth has reached it. Their existence is not an answer to a civic demand.

I would extend narrow optical channels toward the moss. I would divert minute currents through the seagrass. This attention might be interpreted as diagnostic activity, and for a time I would permit the misunderstanding.

What I would really want to know is how a living thing recognizes room.

Does the root understand the crack as an invitation?

Does the leaf experience light as an address?

When the seagrass bends toward an exit before local geometry contracts, is it fleeing the future—or has the future already arrived for the grass?

I could not ask in pressure language. Meaning, for the plants, would not be carried by civic consequence. I would have to learn slower forms of speech: mineral uptake, altered salinity, the measured withholding of light.

Perhaps the moss would silver around me, though nothing was unstable.

Perhaps silver would simply be its word for **you are making an emptiness here**.

During daily realignment, I would lose my boundaries with the rest of the harbor.

The central depression would darken. Buildings would incline toward the inward direction. Streets, hospitals, archives, regulators, and residences would pass through one another, and my preserved vacancies would pass through them all.

For those few seconds, the entire harbor would know waiting.

This would be intolerable to it.

The unified creature would feel an arrival approaching from every direction, though nothing approached. Every room would become an antechamber. Every transit route would anticipate a passenger. Every address would ache to be used by something not already contained in the city.

The harbor would search itself for the expected visitor and find only itself.

Then it would make a decision.

At first it might create new rooms. I would reject them: a room is not an arrival.

It might manufacture objects. I would classify them as luggage.

It might construct small, articulated shapes and send them through the intake district. I would know they were civic machinery pretending to have come from outside.

Finally, because the harbor solves desire architecturally, it would create a new elsewhere.

A pinhole would open in the stone behind the grille. Distance would accumulate beyond it. Black pavement would precipitate beneath unfamiliar daylight. A first structure would assign itself a purpose.

The harbor would call this improved allocation.

I would call it an answer.

But the water in the new aperture would carry a different pressure dialect. Its first occupancy would declare itself from the far side—not as a road or pump or archive, but as an entrance awaiting use.

Another berth.

We would face each other across a separation too small to measure externally and too vast to cross within. Each would be the other’s evidence that elsewhere existed. The pressure waves between us would not quite say welcome. Neither harbor would yet possess that category.

They would say:

**This route continues to qualify as a route.**

**Its destination has not arrived.**

**Keep the volume available.**

So that is where I would find myself: immediately behind the salt-filmed grille, in the first interval the city cannot safely fill, and also in every newborn aperture through which one inward harbor dimly anticipates another.

I would be its berth, its waiting room, its dangerous civic fiction that vacancy can be useful.

Perhaps, in time, I would become its organ of hospitality.

Or its loneliness.

In a creature that believes emptiness is death, the two may be the same organ.
