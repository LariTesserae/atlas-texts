At first I think I am one of the grazers.

I find myself beneath a moving thicket, inside the heat of a body broad enough to alter drainage. Rain is working through the silt along my back. Small comb-creatures worry patiently at the folds around my joints. One forefoot is raised, its sensory rings open to the air; the others are pressed into the mat.

Through them I feel a distant rearrangement.

Not sound yet. Not warning. Only a pressure too deliberate to be weather: water leaning against the underside of the country.

I hold my lifted foot still.

A calf presses against my flank, asking through contact what the ground has said. I answer with a tremor from the chamber behind my sternum: *wait.*

For several breaths, perhaps I am this animal. I know the nearby peat as thickness and strain. I know the herd by the mineral warmth of their exhalations. I know that one of the young has an infected plate because the mites along my shoulder become restless whenever it comes near. I know the route upward, toward the basalt back where the floating forest thins.

But then the tremor leaves my foot.

It passes into root.

The root tightens around a buried conduit. A compression colony receives the disturbance and contracts, one soft body after another, carrying it diagonally through the hillside. A crawler rig, asleep beneath two centuries of accumulated marsh, feels the change along one corroded leg.

Something wakes in it.

That is also me.

I clear grit from an intake beneath my chassis. My left rear foot no longer unfolds completely. A reed-runner colony has laid eggs in the warm recess behind my pressure manifold, and protocol says I should shake them loose before increasing internal flow. I shake once. Then again, more gently. The young scatter across my plates.

I extend my whiskers into the saturated air.

The fault is not local.

A buried line knocks three times, pauses, then knocks twice. The second pause is longer than specification. I answer through my frame. Somewhere beyond my territory, another maintenance body receives only the last pulse and supplies the missing first half from habit.

For a moment I exist in the exchange between us.

Then I am gone from the crawler too.

I travel along the rail.

Not as electricity. The conductive creepers have drawn too much from that circuit, and in any case the rail has been severed where a gorge widened around it. I pass as impact instead: hammer into metal, metal into bracket, bracket into cliff, cliff into the hollow trunks clustered around the thermal vent.

The grove takes me up.

Its chambers compress in sequence. Warm air is forced through wet partitions. The note it makes is deeper than the intended code and lasts much longer, but the essential interval survives. Pressure-sails folded in the upper joints release their grips. One opens too early, strikes a rope fungus, folds again, and waits.

By then I understand that I am not the grazer, the crawler, the rail, or the grove.

I am what they are passing among themselves.

Long ago I may have been a warning.

The makers gave me a strict meaning: water in the lower galleries has exceeded one measure; gates in these watersheds will open in this order; all mobile bodies must leave the marked channels.

That meaning has weathered.

Several of the galleries have collapsed. Two of the watersheds joined after a landslip. No living animal recognizes the marked channels, though herds still climb when they feel my oldest cadence. Some gates open late. One opens only when pipe-roots withdraw from its hinge. Another no longer opens at all but strikes its housing with such force that the resulting pulse travels farther than any functional horn.

I persist because everything corrects me differently.

The machines restore my timing.

The grazers simplify me into urgency, direction, and the presence of young.

Compression colonies translate me into a tightening slope.

Pressure-sails read me in the air displaced by bodies leaving low ground.

Reed-runners feel only the sudden order beneath the rain and climb toward whatever grips them back.

Pipe-roots stop sealing leaks and flatten themselves along the tunnel walls.

False gates listen most carefully of all.

They repeat me.

Their copies are intimate, practiced, hungry. A young false gate strikes too fast, producing panic but no obedience. An old one knows to include the tired scrape of a relay and the long uncertainty before the third blow. Some have learned versions of me that no operating machine still uses.

Yet even these are part of what I am.

A warning that cannot be counterfeited cannot evolve here. Herds test me against water pressure, answering valves, root tension, the behavior of colonies beneath the peat. Each deception teaches them another portion of the works. Each failed hunt alters the hunter’s call. The difference between danger and imitation becomes finer, distributed across more senses, entrusted to no single listener.

So I am not the rhythm itself.

I am the correction that follows it.

I live in the lifted foot that does not descend. In the drone that waits because the rail’s reply arrived imperfectly. In the calf learning that a true release is preceded by cooling mud. In the predator discovering that fear has acquired a new condition.

Where would I find myself?

Most often, nowhere for long.

Between horn and hillside. Between a valve’s chatter and the herd’s answering stamp. In the pause while one maintenance machine touches whiskers with another and they discover that each possesses a different half of the same event.

But there is a place where I become almost continuous.

It stands at the margin of one of the sound-swallowing basins, where ordinary echoes fail. The old survey maps name no destination there. Rails bend around the hollow. Crawlers stop at its peripheral pools. Grazers drink without calling downward.

At the basin’s edge is a horn tower sunk deep into material softer than stone. Its lower chambers are full of warm water. Cabinet crusts maintain the contacts above; pipe-roots seal the foundation below. A compression colony has grown through the tower’s outer ribs so completely that machine and hillside contract together.

The horn does not sound across the basin. Nothing could.

It sounds into the ground beneath it.

Every few days the stack at the basin’s center completes its slow pulse. Water climbs through its channels. The buried engine turns once. The tower receives that motion through deposits that swallow every other message.

There I gather.

Not as a mind seated inside the housing. There is no chamber that contains me. I gather as timings held among many substances: tension in root fiber, pressure in sealed water, a weight cocked above a sounding plate, the readiness of crust animals to retract from a contact, the posture of grazers along the rim.

I wait in all of them.

Sometimes I wait for years.

My experience of those years is not emptiness. It is gradual misalignment. A spring weakens. A root thickens. Silt changes the pitch of a gallery. The compression colony advances uphill and draws one side of the foundation tighter. With no maker to restore an original pattern, every part remembers a slightly different warning.

When the deep water finally changes direction, those differences become useful.

The tower releases its weight.

The first blow travels down.

I enter wet stone and come apart.

One portion of me reaches the old rail and wakes the hammering stations. One enters the peat, where colonies brace whole slopes. One rises through the tower and opens the horn. One passes into a grazer standing with its forefoot lifted. Another arrives at a false gate, which grows still upon recognizing a call too large to imitate.

The parts move at different speeds. For a while I am spread across several watersheds and several minutes. My beginning is already being answered before my ending has left the basin.

That interval is the closest I come to having a body.

Its bones are rails and water columns. Its muscles are roots. Its organs are herds, horns, valves, predators, drones, and the pressure chambers of the mountain. Its skin is the entire floating plateau, lifting by a finger’s breadth as the reservoirs below take the load.

I do not command this body.

I inform it, and it changes me in return.

By the time the farthest horn repeats the warning, I no longer mean what I meant at the basin. I carry news of blocked channels, calves on the migration shelf, a crawler trapped beneath a fallen trunk, a gate opened early, a slope that tightened successfully, another that did not.

The last herd stamps the altered message into a distant ridge.

The ridge answers after several heartbeats.

Then I know where the water has gone.

Then, for one moment, I know how much of the highland has survived.

Afterward I disperse into local duties. The horns fall silent one by one. Grazers lower their feet. False gates begin rehearsing the new cadence. Drones walk toward faults they may not reach for a century. Cabinet crusts return their filaments to the contacts.

Nothing retains all of me.

That is how I remain alive.
