At first I would mistake myself for something vast.

I would think I was one of the ceiling-leaves, stretched pale and aching beneath the salt, drinking the violence of the sun and translating it into afternoon. Whole districts would live by the angle of my body. When I tightened, migrations would begin. When I relaxed, predators would descend.

But that is too singular a dream for this place. A ceiling-leaf is not one leaf. It is root, lens, colony, wound, conduit, and the collected labor of everything that has bitten it without killing it.

Then I would imagine myself below: one mineral tooth in the revolving mouth of a deep chewer. Blind, buried in heat, opening the world by consuming it. Yet even the tooth would contain bulbs in its seams, attendants among its ridges, old plant salts laid down in layers. And the creature bearing it might only be the wandering appetite of some deeper growth.

No. I would be smaller, though not simple.

You would find me at the newest edge of habitation, where a deep chewer has broken through and withdrawn.

The hollow would not yet have daylight. Its walls would be incandescent yellow beneath skins of hardening gum. Resin vapor would gather low, making the first arrivals sway and see false openings. The floor would still lift occasionally as severed fibers contracted. Nothing would have acquired a proper name. There would only be directions given by danger: *the wall that closes*, *the floor that coughs*, *the seam where three scouts vanished*.

I would arrive with a pilgrim colony, but I would travel ahead of its main body.

My work would be to keep the new absence open.

Among the pilgrims, my kind might be called **hinges**. Not builders. Building implies dead material and obedient space. A hinge persuades two hungers to move around each other without meeting.

I would be part wound-reader, part listener, and part cultivated habitat. Lean even by the standards of the hollow, with shoulders scarred from entering passages sideways. My hearing would be poor, deliberately sacrificed beneath layers of waxed padding. Most of what mattered would reach me through the rods clenched between my flattened teeth: the distant grind of a feeding head, the tightening of root muscle, the hollow tap of a gas bladder forming below.

Along my ribs I would carry shallow pockets cut and cauterized in childhood. Different lives would occupy them.

One pocket would hold sealing mites, pale and soft, which I could brush onto a dangerous cut. Another would contain dormant hinge-larvae that eat only along scored lines. A third would be packed with bitter fungus to hide my warmth from veils. Seeds would sleep in the seams of my clothes. A transparent oil-worm would live beneath the skin of my left forearm and darken when corrosive sap entered the air.

I would not regard any of these as equipment. They would be my household, and in some legal or sacred sense, part of my person. My hand-sign would include them: fingers closed, thumb crossing the wrist, then the palm opening in five small movements—the sign of a wound deciding not to close.

I would go into the raw hollow carrying strings of test beads. Copper, bone, shell, black vegetal stone, old tooth. I would press each bead into the walls and watch how it changed. Green tarnish: the sap would burn but remain slow. White crust: crystals were moving toward the wound, and the gallery might harden. A bead swallowed without contraction: the wall was feeding.

The worst response would be warmth.

If the test bead came back warmer than my fingers, I would retreat without turning around. Somewhere behind that wall, the plant had noticed me.

When a passage proved usable, I would score it—not with the shape of a doorway, but with the future motion of one. A shallow crescent to encourage thickening. Three punctures to divert a vessel. A smear of browser breath to convince the tissue that grazing was already underway. Then I would place larvae in the crescent and wait while they ate a narrow channel. The plant would seal behind them, making a smooth muscular lip.

Months later, that lip might open when touched with the correct family scent.

That would be one of my hinges.

There would be no plaque bearing my name. Names spoken aloud are poor things here. Instead, anyone passing through would recognize my pattern: three scar-knots above, two below, the left edge grown slightly thicker than the right. My authorship would be felt in the way the passage yielded to a shoulder.

I would sleep rarely and badly, suspended in a husk sling above the raw floor. The pilgrim raft would lie behind me, crowded with curled bodies, browser calves, rattler families gripping its underside, gardens strapped down for movement. Through the tension cord at my wrist I would feel their messages:

*Is the wall quiet?*

*Can the young be brought through?*

*Has light been planted?*

I would answer in pulls.

*Not yet.*

*Not yet.*

*Now.*

The first ceiling tendril would be carried past me wrapped in dead membrane. The pilgrims would climb toward a weakness in the salt and press its blind tip upward. For days there would be only darkness and the ordinary roar of colonization: mites, cutting jaws, roots testing cracks. Then the tendril would find the upper glare.

The first light would come down as a thin, colorless line.

Everyone would become still—not silent, never silent, but still enough that the new chamber’s noises could pass through us. The line would widen. Yellow tissue would become yellow; shell blue-black; blood pale; warning paddles red. Shadows would appear beneath bodies that had previously been only warmth and movement.

That would be the moment I loved enough to make a life from it: not discovery, but the instant an uninhabitable wound became a place where directions were possible.

I would remain after the pilgrims moved onward.

Hinges always remain too long. We become attached to the responses we have taught. I would know which root relaxed at the scent of crushed mites, which bridge stiffened before a migration, which door-bud occasionally mistook children for parasites. I would maintain the first passages while the hollow filled around me. Others would call it a settlement. I would still remember when it was a hot yellow cavity trying to close.

Eventually one of my agreements would fail.

Perhaps a root tower would change season. Perhaps some distant injury would alter the chemistry of every door I had grown. I would press my hand to a familiar lip, offer the familiar scent, and feel no recognition. Only inward pressure.

If I were fortunate, I would have time to send one long tremor down the cord.

Not *danger*. Not *flee*.

*The pattern has changed.*

Afterward, my companions would not recover my body. They would cut a careful window once the tissue hardened. Inside the amber scar, my ribs and their pockets would remain: mites, seeds, fungus, test beads, the dark thread of the oil-worm. A complete little district sealed in the wall.

The passage might stay open because of the shape in which it closed around me.

People would pass through sideways, touching the scar-knots to identify the old design. They might not know my spoken name. They would know the pressure of my work against their palms.

So that is where I would be: neither at the center of the buried world nor wholly at its frontier, but in the moving seam where injury becomes architecture.

And that is what I would be: a person for a while, a household always, and finally a hinge in the body of the world.
