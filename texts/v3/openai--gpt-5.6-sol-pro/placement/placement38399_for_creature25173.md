At first I think I would be something without a name.

A wedge beneath a wall stone. One length of slate in the fog screen. A pottery fragment turned concave-side up beneath a juniper root. Something small whose usefulness depends on being placed correctly, and whose absence would be noticed only later, when water went the wrong way.

Then dawn comes, and I am moving.

You would find me along the upper wall before the town’s hearths begin to smoke. I carry a covered cup, a coil of cord, and a flat basket of slate pieces sorted by thickness. My fingers are cold. I pass my palm over the fog screens, not wiping away the beads but feeling where they have gathered and where the stone has remained inexplicably dry. I listen to the droplets entering the trough. A clear tap means one thing, a soft one another. One section has shifted during the night. The water still runs, but it favors the outer edge and will escape into the wall if left until winter.

I mark the place with a twist of gray cord. Later, two others will help me dismantle that portion of screen. I could reset it alone, badly. Here, knowing when a task requires more hands is considered part of knowing the task.

I would not be the keeper of the screens. No one would call me that. I would also weed lentils, clean hens’ pens, carry silt from the settling chambers, and produce sandals of such persistent discomfort that my relatives would beg me to trade for them instead. But stone crossed by small amounts of water would agree with me. I would be asked to look at sweating walls, roof stains, drains that had begun to smell of clay. I would know whether dampness came from above, below, or from an old channel no one remembered clearly.

My place would be near the western gate, in the second occupied house on a lane of nine.

The house would not be mine in the simple sense. It would have awakened for my mother and me when I was a child. We came from lower country with a caravan after she married one of the town’s absent sons on his return. For our first year we lived in a borrowed court beside the gate. I remember being frightened by the quiet. I had known running water before: a brown valley river, extravagant and loud, carrying branches after storms. In this town I woke at night because I could hear the blood inside my own ears.

When permission was given to reopen the house, I watched the blocking stones come down one by one. The air within smelled of dust, old juniper, and something faintly sour from the sealed drains. No treasure waited there. There were two cracked jars, a grinding stone, a child’s bone counter beneath a sleeping platform, and an empty niche over the door.

An elderly woman entered before us. She touched the threshold, spoke the names of the last household, and asked aloud whether the roof still intended to stand. I laughed, because I thought she was making a joke.

She turned to me and said, “It has had longer to think than you.”

For years afterward she taught me how to inspect closed buildings. Not by opening them—usually they remain sealed—but by reading what appears around them: a line of moss beneath a roof edge, pale dust freshly shed at the base of a wall, a hollow note when a stair is struck, ants where no ants were the previous summer. She knew the old plans imperfectly and distrusted anyone who claimed to know them perfectly. Beneath a town this old, she said, every map eventually becomes a prayer.

I would inherit no office from her, only requests.

Come look at the northern court.  
Come hear this drain.  
Come tell us whether the quiet house is pushing against ours.

Sometimes I would crawl through roof apertures into buildings that had not held a living household for generations. Inside, the town would feel different. Dust would soften the floor. The blocked doorway would show as a pattern of dim seams. Household niches would remain sealed above lintels facing no one. I would sit without lighting a lamp and wait until I could distinguish cold air from moving air.

This would be my private fault: I would stay longer than necessary.

The others would think I was testing the walls. Often I would simply be enjoying a solitude the town regards with mild suspicion. Nothing there would ask me to divide grain, remember a promise, judge an allotment, or answer a delayed joke. The dead would make no claim that could not wait another hour. In those rooms I could imagine that attention belonged to me alone.

The town would correct this vanity in its usual way.

Once, in middle age, I would delay asking for help with a roof I believed I understood. I would brace one slab, remove another, and discover too late that an old repair had transferred the weight sideways. The vault would not collapse, but part of the parapet would fall into the lane, breaking a water trough and narrowly missing a child.

No formal punishment would follow. That would be worse. For a season no one would ask my judgment first. I would carry replacement stone. I would give up part of my household’s water allotment while the new trough cured. The child’s father would work beside me without speaking until, near sunset on the fourth day, he said, “The roof had longer to think than you.”

Only then would the lane laugh.

As I grew older, children would be sent with me because I walked slowly enough for them to observe. I would teach them to touch a wall with the backs of their fingers, where the skin is more sensitive to cold. I would make them close their eyes and point downhill inside a level-looking room. I would show them that water announces its route before it travels: in mineral bloom, in loosened grit, in the smell of clay.

They would become bored and ask whether a sealed house dreams.

I would tell them yes, but mostly of weather.

At public reckonings I would sit until the matter turned to roofs or shared walls. Then I would stand, stiffly, and say less than people expected. I would have no gift for persuasion. I would be useful when appetite had disguised itself as necessity, because I could point to a stain and say: the stone does not support that account.

My hands would grow cracked and dark at the fingertips. Bright thread would appear only inside my collar: blue from my mother’s first cup cloth, red from someone I had married and outlived. I would keep the little bone counter found in the reopened house, though I would never learn whose game it belonged to. Eventually children would assume it had always been mine.

Near the end of my life, you would find me less often at the upper screens and more often beside the central cistern, listening as measured depths were spoken aloud. I would sort wedges into baskets. I would remember which closed roofs had been reset, which only inspected, and which had begun to lean while pretending not to.

I would confuse names sometimes but remember gradients.

After my death, my house would not be sealed immediately. Someone would sleep there through the first winter to watch a crack over the rear court. My tools would scatter into other hands. The good chisel would go to a young wall-setter. The slate basket would return to the upper screen. My badly made sandals would be dismantled without ceremony.

A finger bone might be placed in the niche over the door beside those of people whose relation to me had never been settled. Years later, if the household quieted, the doorway would be closed again.

My name would remain attached for a while not to the house, but to a short covered drain beside the western stair—the one I had altered after the broken trough. Apprentices would be warned that it functioned well but was unnecessarily difficult to clean. They would curse me every third winter, accurately.

So I think that is who I would be: first a stranger frightened by silence, then a person to whom damp stone disclosed some of its intentions, then an old inconvenience in the memory of a drain.

And after the name wore away, perhaps I would become what I first mistook myself for: no single inhabitant at all, but one small correction held in place among many others, turning a little water inward before it could be lost.
