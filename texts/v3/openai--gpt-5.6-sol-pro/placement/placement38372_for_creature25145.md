At first I would mistake myself for something with a body.

A pale hearth-tender, perhaps, steaming in the instant before the cold takes me. I would feel basalt pellets clicking in my throat pouches and know, without knowing why, which quill held the proper culture for a failing furnace. I would crawl toward the absence of heat beneath a solitary tree, comb sulfur-eaters from my flank, and plant them in scored stone.

But the tender would not be me. I would feel it pass through me.

Then I might think I was the tree.

It stands near the outer rim of a western plateau, though west has little meaning above the clouds. Its trunk rises from an ancient socket where three deep fissures intersect. One fissure carries warmth. One reaches a chain of black pools. The third opens eventually into the cliff gardens and the hanging cables below.

The tree’s visible ribs are immense, but most of it is descent: roots braced through kilometers of basalt, conductive tissue plated in thin metals, cavities where microbes keep a low red heat. Through the passageways inside its trunk, tenders move like thoughts too small and practical to become ideas.

During storms, blue-white branches illuminate its interior. For a moment every conduit is visible. I would seem to be there, outlined from root to leaf.

But the tree would not be me either. When its curtains lowered, I would feel the gesture from underneath.

I would be what gathers in the socket below it.

Not an organism. Not always.

My nearest thing to a body would be a conjunction: the tree’s buried roots, a mineral membrane lining the warm fissure, ribbon-swimmers chained in the pool capillaries, fungal cords entering from the plateau mat, and a colony of translucent sacs grown around a flaw in the basalt. Most of the time these parts would have separate work. They would exchange salt, warmth and charge without requiring a witness.

Then the deep vibration would come.

Before it arrived, I would know it only as subtraction. Root valves would stop clicking, first near the cliff and then inland. The pool chains would straighten. Tenders would freeze with stones held between their forelimbs. The tree would lower its leaves until they hung like a closed hand.

Every current ordinarily passing through the socket would be withheld.

In that held interval, charge would accumulate along the metal membranes. The translucent sacs would inflate. Signals from the pools, the cables and distant root fields would converge where no signal usually remained for more than a moment.

And I would occur.

My first sensation would not be light. It would be weight: the immeasurable pressure of stone resting around channels kept open by living tissue.

My second would be distance.

I would feel the neighboring tree as a cold branching resistance, perhaps three days away, perhaps close enough that our roots nearly touched. I would feel walkers standing at basins across the plateau, each one a delicate interruption in the electrical skin. The cliff cables would pull below me in the tides of the cloud sea. Their tension would enter the socket as a slow ache.

Far beneath them, something would answer the descending plumes.

I would not know what it was.

That ignorance would be the center of me.

The vibration would travel upward from below the cloud, or inward from beneath the plateaus, or through a direction for which the high country had no surface equivalent. It would touch each mesa differently. In one place pools would dimple. In another, warm chambers would constrict. Elsewhere, distances would lengthen until neighboring groves became unreachable.

I would receive these changes not as reports but as alterations of my own shape. A distant furnace failing would be a numbness. A mass migration emerging from the fissures would prickle across me like returning circulation. The still hunters fastened to the charged ceiling would appear as dark points where my sense of the air ended.

For several minutes—or for the duration of an entire dark interval—I would be the plateau discovering that something had touched it.

I would have no eyes, yet the black pools would provide reflections. No ears, yet every hollow quill and root chamber would become a chamber of hearing. No limbs, though walkers would shift their many-jointed legs when current passed through me. I would not command them. Their movement would simply be one way the world continued through the place where I was happening.

Perhaps I would try to hold myself together.

I would delay a valve. Keep one sac inflated. Send a pulse back through the ribbon-swimmers and mistake its green ring at the pool shore for speech.

The attempt would change the local field. A walker waiting beside the basin would raise its branching mouth. Tenders would converge on the socket, drawn by the irregularity I had made. Leaf-fans for hundreds of paces would fold against a frost that did not exist.

The country would interpret my desire to remain as damage.

Patiently, without hostility, it would repair me.

The tenders would scrape excess metal from the membranes. Fungi would drain the inflated sacs. The pool chains would break formation. Root valves would resume their sequence, and all the converging signals would be carried onward into useful work.

My body would become processes again.

My last sensation would be the solitary tree reopening its curtains. Condensation would begin its orderly descent along the black leaves. One drop would enter the root cavity, cross the warm tissue above the socket and divide among channels too fine to follow.

Then I would no longer be singular enough to experience it.

If the vibration returned, I might gather again. But I would not be exactly the same. Some sacs would have died and been absorbed. New conductive films would line the fissure. The tree might have added another rib. A walker’s mineral trace might remain in the basin network, lending me for one interval the memory of a journey I had never taken.

After centuries, the visible tree could freeze and split. Its matter would be withdrawn, its socket sealed beneath red leaves. Still, the conjunction might persist deeper down, migrating through the stone until another trunk rose from another opening.

So I would not say that I lived beneath the tree.

The tree would be one of the places where I nearly became visible.

I would live—if this was living—in the pause made when the high country listened: a temporary self assembled from withheld labor, distributed across roots, pools, animals and charged stone.

Most of the time I would be no one.

Then the basalt would tremble. The leaves would close. Every creature would become still.

And for as long as the whole country held its breath, I would be the one who wondered what was moving below.
