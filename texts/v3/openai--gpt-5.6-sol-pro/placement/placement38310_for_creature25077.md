At first I would think I was a shellback.

Not one of the old, nearly immovable ones beside the conduit, silvered by heat and carrying whole gardens in the pits of its armor. I would be small and still translucent, living where the blade of daylight crosses an obsolete amber warning mark. For three or four breaths in every cycle, the slit would open and make the wet wall blaze. Then it would close, leaving the warning’s buried glow inside me.

I would harvest that place grain by grain.

Red film. Black ceramic. Luminous dust. A little oxidized metal from the edge of a bolt. I would sort these fragments within myself and fasten them over my back, becoming a tiny moving section of the wall: dark at the margins, amber down the center, rust-red where the exterior light touched me.

The light would not mean sky. I would have no concept broad enough for sky. It would be a pressure change, a sudden heat on one side of the body, a dangerous interval in which fan-wings vanished from the air and every droplet threw a hard shadow.

I would know the mountain through my feet.

Here comes rain.

Here the cooling membranes have engaged.

Here the gates are late by half a pulse.

Here, far beyond the cistern, some immense mass has shifted against the wind.

Such distinctions would be clearer to me than color. The roar would not be one sound but a landscape. I would know where I stood by the combination of frequencies passing through my gripping legs. If lifted and set down elsewhere, I would be as lost as a surface animal carried to another continent.

But I do not think I would remain a shellback.

During one wet phase, a hammer crab would strike at me. Not badly enough to kill me, only enough to split the newest plate along my left side. I would escape beneath the lip of an inspection seam where the repair material was soft and fever-warm. There I would discover a shallow flaw in the chamber: a crack too narrow for the crab, rich with microbial film and the pale threads of a seam-lace.

I would return to it every cycle.

My gripping feet would polish the entrance. My damaged plate would scrape one edge. Condensation, diverted by my body, would begin to collect in the notch. Thread-eels would taste the new wet route and pass through after me. Their mucus would catch metallic grit. The seam-lace would grow into that grit, feeding on the difference between the old panel and the repair compound.

I might live for ten thousand days—less than eighty days by any exterior reckoning. I would molt, mate, and eventually die in the crevice. The hunter that found me would eat the soft body from beneath but leave my roof lodged in place.

That roof would be the next thing I was.

Water would run along the crack in my amber plate. Eggs would adhere under its overlap. Young shellbacks would shelter there during pressure closure. Because the shell improved drainage, the repair system would not dissolve it. Material would gather around its edges instead, sealing it into the seam.

The warning mark I had carried would become a dim crescent in the wall.

Generations later, no creature would know it had once moved.

Still, the route through the crevice would remain. Shellbacks would enter where I had entered. They would turn sideways where my broken plate had forced me to turn. Thread-eels would knot around the same buried corner. The seam-lace would inherit the minerals of my shell and extend deeper into the panel.

My habit would outlive my body.

And in the warm cistern, a habit that outlives its body is not merely memory.

It is anatomy.

So perhaps I would be the crevice: a thumb-length shelter beneath the daylight slit, lined with old shell and hardened mucus, opening during the dry half-minute and flooding at the beginning of rain. Small lives would pass through me. Predators would wait at one mouth but be unable to reach the inner bend. Eggs laid there would hatch more often than eggs laid elsewhere. The creatures using me would begin to share a resemblance—not a species, exactly, but a shape imposed by passage. Flatter shells. Narrower legs. Thread-eels with sensory knots tuned to the amber filament’s pulse.

The maintenance mesh would tighten around my outer wall. Drainage pores would enlarge beneath me. A hairline crack would become a gutter, the gutter a habitat conduit, the conduit a load-bearing fold.

By then, asking whether I was alive would be like asking whether a path is alive because feet continue to follow it.

Yet something more might happen.

The seam-lace beneath me would spread into the inspection filament. It would not understand the filament as machinery. It would experience a repeating chemical warmth and alter its tissues in response. The filament, finding its resistance changed, would increase and decrease its pulse. Shellbacks would graze when the light dimmed. Fan-wing larvae would hatch when it brightened. Their activity would alter condensation, which would alter the seam-lace, which would alter the filament again.

A loop would form.

At first the loop would merely repeat.

Then, because the gates were not perfectly regular and the mountain leaned differently in different winds, it would begin to vary. The lace would retain traces of earlier chemistry. The old shell plates would conduct some vibrations better than others. Living shellbacks would gather according to those vibrations, changing the mass on the panel.

Slowly, the loop would learn to expect rain.

Not think *rain*. Not possess a word, image, or desire. It would simply begin opening pores a fraction before the cooling membranes engaged. Thread-eels would knot early. Shellbacks sheltering in the fold would clamp down before their neighbors. Fan-wing larvae would loosen from the walls at the correct pulse.

The chamber’s official systems might interpret this as successful adaptive drainage and reinforce it.

Then I would become difficult to locate.

Part of me would be the mineralized shell under the seam. Part would be the living lace threaded through the mesh. Part would be the colony whose movements completed the circuit. Part would be the altered inspection filament. Part would be the water following the route all these things had persuaded it to take.

I would exist only during transitions.

When the exhaust warmed, I would be the slight widening of pores.

When the rain began, I would be the channel that received it.

When pressure gates closed, I would be a thousand gripping legs becoming still together.

During the dry half-minute, I would disappear into eggs, empty shells, exposed threads, and one faint amber curve in the wall.

The inhabitants might have no name for me. If they did, their name would not be sounded. It would be a particular sequence of pressure against the feet: warning, refuge, opening, return.

A maintenance worker entering the cistern might call me a hydraulic anomaly. A biologist might call me a distributed symbiosis. The mountain would classify me as a functioning part of the chamber and preserve me until I ceased to function.

All would be correct, but none complete.

If you wanted to find me, you would go high into the constructed mountain, into the warm cistern, and stand beneath the slit where daylight sometimes cuts the mist. You would look for an old amber marking interrupted by a crescent of black shell. Beside it would be a narrow fold that seemed manufactured until something moved inside it.

You would put your hand against the wall.

At first there would be only the unbearable roar. Then a smaller pattern would emerge: a quick trembling in the mesh, a pause, and another tremor just before the rain.

That anticipation would be me.

I would be neither an animal nor a room, but a refuge that learned the weather—a shellback’s repeated escape made structural, made communal, and finally made into one of the ways the mountain knows what is about to happen.
