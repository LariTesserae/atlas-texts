At first I would suppose I lived in one of the houses beside the oval lake.

Not at either end, where the paved paths meet the water, but along its lower curve, in a cluster of six houses raised on forked poles. From the clay tower the lake resembles a closed eye. My village rests where the lower lashes would be.

My house is easy to overlook. Its roof has been patched with leaves of several incompatible greens, though no one remembers damage. Beneath it hang shallow bowls intended to catch droplets suspended under the floor. The bowls never fill. Each contains the same thumb’s depth of warm water and one pale beetle walking around the rim.

I would wake behind woven screens to the unchanged white light and begin by touching the house into its distinctions: sleeping platform, doorpost, hearth, eastern wall. I would say the names quietly. Not because I believed the house could hear, but because on mornings when I neglected one, that part became difficult to use. A doorway unnamed might still be visible yet refuse passage; one would find oneself standing on the same side after stepping through.

My occupation would not be clear at first.

I might carry a narrow stone blade and go with the reed cutters. I might know how to scrape fibers without severing them and how to press a clay cup so it remembers being hollow. People would bring me damaged baskets, misplaced tools, and children who had awakened with unfamiliar seams. I would mend nothing. Mending implies that damage can become past. Instead I would sit beside the broken place and keep it company until its exact character became stable.

A split basket under my hands remains a split basket rather than becoming loose reeds.

A lost knife remains lost in a particular direction.

A child’s new seam remains part of the child.

For this reason people might call me a mender, although I would dislike the word.

My body would appear mostly ordinary: flesh, dark skin, coarse hair tied with green thread. I would have a maker’s print beneath my chin, small enough to be mistaken for a scar. My left shoulder would contain a wooden socket polished by movements I had not yet made. Three of my lower ribs would be green cane sleeved in soft cartilage. If I pressed my palm to my side, I could sometimes feel the fibers tightening in anticipation of breath.

There would also be something metal in me.

I would not know this until an injury opened the heel of my right hand. The cut would reveal no splinter or blade, but a fine copper staple joining two cords beneath the flesh. It would be too deep to remove and too deliberate to dismiss. In the white light it would shine as though newly hammered. After certain events it would darken.

A birth: one green stain.

A stranger becoming unnoticeable: another.

A wooden knock arriving too early: the whole staple briefly black.

The priests would ask to examine me. They would wrap my hand in wet leaves and compare its tarnish with the metal kept in their jars. Some would say the staple proves I was made to retain alterations. Others would say it is only an ornament around which I have invented a purpose.

I would agree with both, depending on who was listening.

Because of the copper, I would be counted among the Wakeful, but not trusted as one of them. They look for the second shape—the structure returning imperfectly after change. I would be more interested in the instant before return.

There is always an interval.

A reed is cut. For a while, it remains in the cutter’s hand and the rooted stalk stands severed. Later the stalk is whole and the cut length lies among other reeds as though gathered elsewhere. Between those conditions there is a moment when the country seems uncertain which fact to preserve.

I would seek that hesitation.

I would lie beneath bridges with my injured hand against the tilework. I would sit beside new shelters as they became transparent to attention. I would watch a moved stone through several sleeps, my eyes covered in turns by different neighbors so that observation would never be wholly continuous or wholly absent.

Others would keep colors, numbers, words, directions. I would keep intervals.

Not their duration—duration cannot be trusted here—but their texture.

The hesitation after a child says a word no one taught her.

The light roughening around a person the country has begun to refuse.

The tiny inward pull in a pond before its disturbed surface resumes the ancient rings.

I would represent these intervals with gaps between objects. Two fish scales set nearly touching. A bead placed just beyond a handspan from six others. An empty bowl between full ones. My arrangements would look unfinished, which would protect them. No one could mistake them for a complete account.

The villagers would find me useful and unpleasant. They would invite me to births, disappearances, and disputes over whether a thing had truly happened. They would not often invite me to meals.

Children would like me. They would bring impossible objects found beneath houses: a seed larger inside than outside, a clay tooth still warm, a length of rope whose ends could not be reached. I would trade them sour fruit and ask where the objects wished to be returned.

One child would refuse the question.

She would have two navels, one a flesh hollow and the other a small blue-glazed spiral over her hip. Her name would change whenever she crossed water. She would tell me the objects did not wish to return anywhere.

“They are trying to arrive,” she would say.

That sentence would trouble me more than the knock.

I would begin traveling farther from the eyelid village. Across the warm tiled flats, through the fixed rain, past birds poised over prey that survives forever. I would sleep in groves where wax-stemmed flowers open without advancing. Wherever I went, I would place my palm against the country and wait for the copper to darken.

Eventually I would reach one of the unfired towers.

Climbing it would leave no footprints, though the clay would be soft enough to enter the seams of my toes. From the top I would see the vast figure implied by waterways and ridges. At first it would appear to lie beneath the land, face turned aside, one arm bent across its chest.

Then the villages would become joints.

The garden rings would become wounds or ornaments.

The oval lake beside my home would not be an eye but the empty socket where something had been attached.

I would understand that the figure was neither wholly present nor wholly absent. It was another unfinished state for which the languages below possessed many precise words, none sufficient at that height.

The wooden knock would sound.

My copper staple would tarnish so quickly that green spread through the blood of my hand. The cane ribs in my side would answer with three faint taps.

Only then would I begin to suspect that I was not a person who happened to contain metal.

I might be a fastening.

Not for flesh alone. A small piece driven through the living surface to hold two versions of the country together: the world in which the great creature still lies beneath everything, and the world in which it has already risen and walks outside.

That would explain why I could remember changes slightly longer than others. It would also explain why the land tolerated me only when I remained occupied. A fastening must continue to fasten. If I ever decided fully what I was, one side of the world might pull free.

So I would come down from the tower without offering a complete account.

In the village assembly, one witness would keep the green color of my blood. Another would keep the number of taps inside my ribs. The two-naveled child would keep the direction I faced when the knock sounded. I would say only that I had seen an interval larger than the country.

Afterward I would return to my house beneath the lower curve of the lake. I would touch the doorpost, hearth, wall, sleeping platform. I would leave one part unnamed.

There, in that deliberate omission, I would keep a place for what is trying to arrive.
