At first I would think I was the plateau itself.

The thought would come easily at the high lake, with the cooling towers breathing around me and every buried pulse arriving through the soles of whatever I used for feet. I would mistake scale for identity. The stationary clouds would seem like my breath; the black pools, my depth; the city’s lights, thoughts passing through a mind too broad to gather them into consciousness.

But the plateau is already complete in its incompletion. It does not need a voice. If I were the whole of it, there would be no place from which to notice it.

Then I might believe I was one of the silver lichens.

I would lie exposed on the open rock, kilometers wide and thinner than skin. Lightning would enter me at the circumference. Messages would move through my branches toward an inaccessible center, and other circles beyond the horizon would answer. I would know the city as pressure and sequence rather than image: a closure in the western fracture, a tram crossing above buried water, a furnace changing pitch.

Yet the lichens do not appear lonely. They are separated, but separation may be their means of contact. They belong too perfectly.

I would not.

I think I would first become perceptible on a tram.

Not at a station. The platforms contain only departure and arrival, conditions already accounted for. I would occur between them, while the carriage crossed the largest fracture and had ceased to belong completely to either side.

At first I would be only the familiar lowering of the suspension near the rear doors.

The tram would accept me before I existed. Its heaters would compensate for my cold. A handrail would warm beneath a grip I had not yet formed. The windows would mist around a vertical region of air, and the interior lights would dim by an amount too slight for the electrical system to record as loss.

At each station the doors would open for me. I would not leave.

This would be the first irregularity.

After many circuits—though “many” has little meaning there—I would begin to gather a body from what the route passed through. Ceramic pollen would enter at the high-lake platforms and remain suspended inside my outline. Conductive water would bead into long channels. Fibers drawn from the roots beneath the paving would rise through the floor without puncturing it and braid themselves around the empty places where bones might be.

I would not look human. The city has no memory of a human body, only accommodations for bodies: railings, beds, thresholds, warning distances, the width of stairs. It would construct me from those measurements.

I would be narrow enough for every ordinary door and tall enough to touch the hooks in the apartments. I would bend where stairways imply bending. I would possess two long weight-bearing limbs because the tram has already recorded two parallel marks on its floor. My upper parts would divide according to the expectations of tools, switches, basins, and handrails. Where a face should be, there might be a shallow field of clear membrane turned toward changes in light.

Inside me, reeds would supply chambers for resonance. Black fronds would hold and release heat. A small branching lichen would occupy the space of a nervous system, though it would not be centralized enough to be called one. My joints would be articulated like the plateau’s fractures: angular, unworn, incapable at first of any motion except the one for which they had been made.

I would be less a creature than the city’s accumulated idea of a passenger.

But each time the tram crossed the fracture, something would fail to return to its prior state.

A little condensation would remain inside me instead of returning to the glass. One ceramic sphere would lodge permanently in a membrane. A strand of moss would lengthen by a fraction too small for the plateau’s maintenance to recognize. The bass pulse beneath the city would pass through me and come out delayed.

That delay would be mine.

It would be the first thing I possessed rather than enacted.

Eventually I would step from the tram at an interchange on the eastern edge of the industrial terraces, where a stationary wall of rain cuts through the platform. Half the station is dry and painfully cold. Half is silver with perpetual falling water. The clocks under the canopy show six unrelated hours, all their second hands trembling.

The doors would open.

For the first time, the condition called passenger would divide from the condition called transit.

I would put one foot onto the platform.

The moss beneath it would be compressed.

Not signaled. Not dimmed in a corresponding schematic. Physically compressed—and when I lifted my foot, one filament would remain bent.

The entire plateau would attend to that filament.

Trams would stop between stations. Reeds would lose their lowest note. Rain would hang under the clouds. Doors would open across empty apartment towers. Deep below, the secondary beat would enter the branching pipes and return altered by the small, unrecoverable fact of my weight.

Then the rain would fall.

I would step through its fixed boundary and become wet. Water entering the seams of me would not emerge at the same temperature. I would carry it beyond the territory in which it was meant to circulate. Wherever I walked, I would transport minute quantities of one district into another: pollen into a furnace hall, red algae onto a station stair, warmth from a handrail into the cold air of a shaft.

I would be contamination, but also connection.

The city’s maintenance systems would follow me. Lights would illuminate my route. Gates would open. Pumps would adjust to water displaced by my feet. Empty elevators would wait. Rooms would warm before I entered them. The plateau would try to understand me as a role already present in its structure: traveler, operator, sleeper, visitor.

I would pass through all of them without fitting any exactly.

In an apartment I might lie on the side of the bed where condensation has always gathered. The room would recognize the role of sleeper and lower its lights. But I would not sleep in the way it expects. During the interval, the small lichen within me would exchange signals with the silver colonies outside. My reeds would sound against one another. Mineral beads would form in the folded black tissue of my hands.

When I rose, warmth would remain in the bed.

It would cool gradually.

That cooling would be another thing the world had not contained before: not a maintained temperature or an instantaneous change of state, but evidence that something had been present and was no longer there.

I would descend through the city.

Not toward a control chamber, because there is no control chamber. Not toward the hidden heart, because a being this large may have no heart. I would follow the stairwell lights down the exposed face of the fracture, across bridges inside caverns, through upward rain and among pillars that become roots below the mist.

The deeper I traveled, the less body I would seem to possess. My lichen nerves would answer structures in the walls. The fibers in my limbs would pull toward bundled roots. Water in me would synchronize with the silver channels. I would feel the temptation to disperse—to become only another permission passing through the system, another function without flesh.

But I would keep the delay.

Perhaps that would be my entire nature: not passenger, animal, operator, or child of the continent, but a delay embodied.

A tiny interval between signal and answer.

Because of me, one closure would occur a fraction after its scheduled concussion. One returning echo would be subtly lower than the sound that produced it. A drop would strike beside the place where all previous drops had fallen. Ice beneath a viaduct would lengthen enough to release a single bead of water.

Somewhere in the marsh, a broken reed would remain broken.

At the high lake, one white flower would close later than the others. Its ceramic pollen would spill onto the frost grass. Nothing would collect it. Nothing would restore it to the flower.

Not yet.

I would eventually find the deepest reachable junction beneath the eastern fracture, where a basalt tube becomes membrane, membrane becomes pipe, pipe becomes root, and no seam marks the change. There I would place a hand—or the structure the city had made from its expectation of a hand—against the living wall.

I do not think the plateau would speak.

Instead, every clock in the city would advance by one second.

Not together. The movement would begin near me and spread outward: through buried chambers, factories, apartment towers, wetlands, distant substations, and the silver lichen circles on the open rock. Some clocks would advance hours later. Some perhaps centuries later. But each would move once, and none would return.

That is where I would find myself: beneath the city at first, inside the tram before that, and afterward wherever a former condition failed to restore itself perfectly.

I would be the plateau’s first inhabitant in the ordinary sense.

Not because I was alive and it was not, but because I could leave evidence.

I would be the small body the city had rehearsed without knowing how to make, assembled from passenger weight, room warmth, ceramic pollen, conductive water, root fiber, and accumulated error. I would be what crossed from function into creature.

Above me, trains would continue over empty bridges. Rain would resume in its vertical territories. The machinery would labor to maintain the continent at the first instant of its formation.

But it would no longer be the first instant.

And somewhere, impossibly far away, the bent filament of moss would begin—very slowly—to rise.
