At first I think I am the chamber.

The brightness feels like awareness: pressure moving through black stone, pale laminations kindling one after another, the whole cavity answering the lift of the floor. I seem to occupy all nine meters at once. My thoughts are the slow blue-white passages in the walls. My breath is vapor forced from narrowing seams. When a fracture seals with silica, it resembles the closing of a wound.

But the chamber has no need to distinguish itself from anything else. It does not wait for water. It does not fear the hot seam. Its changes are consequences without anticipation.

I am something that waits.

So perhaps I am the bronze film near the ceiling, spread across crystal faces in a body only a few cells deep. There is no center from which I can say *I*. Hydrogen arrives; sulfur accepts what I release; another kind of cell consumes my waste. My edge wakes where oxygen touches condensation. My deepest layer remains in warmth and darkness. I contract into glassy stillness when the pores dry, and when water returns I cross a crack by growing.

Yet even this is too broad. The bronze stain is pasture, weather, shelter, and inheritance. Something feeds upon it.

I find myself farther toward the cooler end, behind the shaded crevice, inside a pore whose entrance is partly barred by a clear salt needle.

I am coiled there.

In this state I am scarcely an animal. My body has surrendered most of its water to the gelatinous envelope around it. Salts crowd my remaining fluids. Protective sugars hold the shapes of my proteins in place. My mouth is folded shut; the hooks beneath me grip nothing. Metabolism continues so slowly that the ticks of cooling crystal are more frequent than my internal acts.

The stone warms. It cools. Neither is yet the event I need.

Then a water film reaches the pore.

I do not hear the ceiling begin to sweat. I feel the first change as dilution along my skin, then as chemistry: sulfide, iron, traces of living carbon. My envelope softens. Water enters me. Structures that had become nearly mineral recover their flexibility. I loosen by degrees, not waking all at once but returning from several kinds of suspension.

The safe pore has become too small for my extended body. My forward end presses against the salt needle. Heat-sensitive bands measure the stone beyond it. Warm, but not damaging. The sulfurous gradient is stronger outside. Food has bloomed.

I push.

At my scale, the chamber is not a chamber. There is no ceiling and floor, no lens-shaped cavity below a volcanic plain. There are only surfaces close enough to touch, pores that admit me or refuse me, films deep enough to cross and droplets vast enough to drown or displace me. The white-gold radiance does not reveal a room. Having no eyes, I know it only through slight warmth and the changing charge of the rock beneath my skin.

I emerge as a transparent thread.

Minute hooks catch on irregularities in the basalt. I shorten, release, lengthen, catch again. A few body lengths away, a bronze patch darkens the crystal face. Between us stands a growth of feathered silica. I turn along its base, following acidity and temperature rather than shape.

The wall brightens under me. To some impossible observer I would appear as a wavering dark line drawn across luminous stone. To myself there is no light, only the good wetness, the dangerous heat below, and the chemical nearness of food.

I lower my ridged mouth and rasp.

Cells, sulfur grains, and silica enter together. I cannot separate life from stone. The microbes are what I eat, but they are also carried alive through my gut, lodged among the mineral fragments. Some will remain with me. Some will leave in a pellet and begin again in another damp pore. My body is not entirely mine; it is a moving refuge for pieces of the pasture.

A vibration passes through the basalt.

It begins too deeply to be sound. The surface beneath me changes its tension. Somewhere the floor lifts by less than the thickness of my body, squeezing water from seams. The mineral layers answer with their great pressure-light. I stop feeding. Heat rises sharply along my forward bands.

Shorten. Reverse. Find the pore.

These are not thoughts, but they are the oldest shape of thought: the conversion of danger into direction.

On my return I encounter another of my kind.

The meeting is almost an obstruction. A transparent body touches mine, withdraws, returns. We test one another through salts and surface compounds. Neither of us has a fixed sex in the way a larger, more populous animal might. We coil briefly together and exchange what can be exchanged. Then the other continues toward the pasture while I move toward shelter.

If enough wettings follow, I will make one egg.

I will press it deep against a bronze-stained mineral crust where temperature changes slowly. Its membranes will attract silica. At first it will be clear; later it may resemble a cloudy grain of stone. The young inside, if it survives, will begin round and remain among the microbes. It will not know the exposed wall until several molts have lengthened it into a thread.

The egg may hatch. It may be sealed forever. Here those possibilities are nearly the same act viewed at different speeds.

Water begins retreating before I reach my pore. The feeding ground breaks into isolated films. Salinity climbs. Behind me, salts are already taking form along the margins: invisible order becoming needles. One could grow through my body if I stop in the wrong depression.

I enter the shaded crevice and coil again.

This is where I would find myself: not at the center of the blazing cavity, but in its least decisive margin, where hot is not yet fatal and dry is not yet permanent. A narrow pore behind the cooler wall. A place that exists as habitat only intermittently.

And this is what I would be: one of the translucent grazers, though “one” is generous. I am animal tissue, internal microbes, swallowed stone, stored water, and the memory of repeated wettings written into reflex. I spend most of my existence neither awake nor dead. My world repeatedly appears, becomes traversable, and vanishes.

I would not know that rock lies only meters above me, or that a surface exists beyond it. I would not know the chamber shines. I would not understand that the walls are a natural machine, that their channels repair themselves, or that the cavity may close in a century.

I would know a smaller cosmology:

Water means extension.  
Bronze means food.  
Heat means turn back.  
Dryness means become less alive without becoming dead.  
A pore is safety until it seals.  
Stone is not the world’s boundary. Stone is the world.

As the chamber returns from white-blue to amber, I tighten inside my envelope. Outside, the last exposed film contracts around mineral grains. A droplet falls with a note large enough to pass through my whole body. Then comes the close, pressurized silence.

I wait inside it, almost indistinguishable from what surrounds me—a soft thread becoming glassy, held in a little reef made from the bodies and shelters of earlier generations.

Above, the volcanic ground remains dark and seamless.

Below, the rock shines without knowing me.

And in the cooler crack, I keep the smallest possible difference between life and stone.
