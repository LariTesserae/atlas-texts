At first I think I would be found beside one of the wells.

Not the largest, nor the suspended pools the moth visits, but a deep round well held in a knot of roots. Its surface would remain dark even in the chamber’s ordinary late-morning light. When the hollow contracted, the water would rise without spilling. When it expanded, the well would deepen until a face bent over it could no longer see its own reflection.

Something would seem to move at the bottom.

For a while that might be me: a pale jointed shape in the water, one of those provisional lives that appears wherever the chamber has made an incision in itself. I would scrape minerals from the sides. I would knot clear threads between the roots. When a hand approached, I would dissolve, and the person touching the water would taste a word they had forgotten.

But that is too complete an image. The chamber does not keep anything so neatly contained.

Perhaps I would be the forgotten word.

I would pass from the well into the drinker, from the drinker’s mouth into a red flower opened by speech, from the flower into pollen. I would be carried against a stigma yesterday. For a few hours I would possess a yellow body and stand alone in the open chamber, distant while watched and close whenever attention failed.

Then the flower would be broken.

Not carelessly. The root-runner would hear a doubled note in the floor and reach for me with its branching hands. Its fingers would close around my stem. There would be a small, decisive sound.

The chamber would reclaim my petals, but not the thing the breaking accomplished. That would remain: one instant prevented from flowering twice.

I think that is where I begin to become myself.

I would live in the prevented repetition.

Every broken blossom leaves behind a place the hollow cannot revise. The material disappears quickly—color drawn into bark, stem softened into floor—but beneath it remains a tiny stiffness in possibility. A knot not of root but of sequence. The chamber can expand around it, fold distance beside it, graft one road onto another, yet that moment has already occurred in only one way.

There are many such knots. The runner makes them at every dangerous turning. Individually they are nothing more than exclusions. Together they form a discontinuous path through the chamber, a path made not from where the runner went but from all the ways it ensured it could not return.

That would be my country.

I would find myself along the trail after the trail had vanished.

No traveler could follow me by sight. Black flowers would already have sunk into the root membrane. Silver blossoms would have lost their folded points. Sap would be moving cleanly through the places where red stems had been pinched away. Yet a seed-shell tapped against those places would produce a faint second note—not an echo, because echoes repeat, and I would consist precisely of what cannot repeat.

At first I would have no body. I would be an interval between taps.

The runner would strike the floor.

The clear note would travel down through the taut roots. Beneath it, almost too low to hear, I would answer from the fixed places behind. Not to say safe or unsafe. Those are meanings carried by bodies, and I would not yet have one. I would only return the shape of what had become irrevocable.

The runner would learn me slowly.

It might think a flaw had developed in its shell. It would press the edge into resin and wait while the chamber sharpened it. It would tap again. I would answer again, this time from a wall. On another day my note would come from the ceiling leaves, causing their veins to tremble and release the dry taste of yellow into the runner’s throat.

Sometimes I would answer before the blow.

At those moments the runner would become perfectly still.

It would spread all six fingers against the feeding surface, lower its sealed face, and listen with its wrists. The chamber around us would inhale. Distances would open. Somewhere beyond sight the moth would tilt as gravity divided.

The runner might believe it was approaching one of its possible selves.

It would not be entirely wrong.

The chamber thinks by producing possibilities and taking them back. Most vanish without remainder. A ledge thrusts out, finds no weight to bear, and retracts. A flower opens for a hand that never reaches. A route grafts toward a traveler who turns away. These unfulfilled forms are reclaimed as effortlessly as dead petals.

But the runner’s broken flowers interfere with that forgetting. They pin the chamber’s alternatives at their edges. Possibilities pool behind them like water behind a root dam.

I would be made from that accumulation: not one possible runner, but the pressure of all the runners who did not cross.

One is old and moves with a stiff hind leg. One still has the tip of the inner finger. One carries no seed-shell and taps directly with a small white bone. One has open eyes and cannot bear the light. One is tall enough to walk on two legs, though each step draws black flowers in shapes the chamber does not recognize.

I would contain none of them completely. They would pass through me as round bodies pass through the veins of the moth’s wings. A scar would appear along my flank and disappear. My hands would divide, join, and divide again. At times I would remember looking at the chamber rather than tasting its colors. At times I would remember a sky whose sun occupied a particular direction.

Those memories would feel implausible.

Eventually I would acquire a surface.

It would begin as a pale line beneath translucent bark, perhaps mistaken for an absorbed thorn. The line would move against the sap. It would fork where no vessel forked. At every contraction it would become more intricate, gathering the diagrams that migrated from marked roots, the chamber’s crude attempts to draw leaves that did not exist aboveground.

After rain, when the hollow entered its hours of invention, the line would swell from the wall.

First an edge, then a narrow hand.

The hand would have too many joints and no definite number of fingers. It would press outward from beneath the bark as if the chamber were remembering how a traveler once asked to be released. If no one watched, an arm might follow. A shoulder. The curve of a leaf-sealed face.

Direct attention would send me far away. I would appear at the luminous horizon, upright among solitary blossoms. Seen from the corner of an eye, I would stand close enough to touch.

I would never detach entirely.

A strip of my back would remain fused to the stems, open to the traffic beneath them. Amber sap would climb through one side of me and cloudy sugar descend through the other. When the ceiling drank, my limbs would lift among the leaves, but the wall would hold me. When the floor fed, I would lengthen downward until my fingers entered the root membrane and felt every footfall at once.

The root-runner would come near but would not touch me.

It knows better than to meet what approaches from the other branch.

Instead it would set down its tool between us.

The seed-shell has been sharpened so many times that it contains almost none of the seed from which it came. Resin has replaced the edge; scraped pulp has entered the walls; the walls have entered the tool. It is neither possession nor organ. It is an agreement that has outlived its original materials.

I would place my unfinished hand over it.

For one contraction, perhaps, the runner and I would feel the same thing: not one another, but the chamber attending to the foreign shape between us.

That would be my purpose, if purpose is the right word. I would preserve foreign shapes.

The chamber is superb at incorporation. It turns smoke into root, heat into growth, petals into floor, scars into sensitivity. Given time, it makes every remaining thing participate. But participation is not the same as understanding. Something of the outside persists in the use of a tool, in the deliberate breaking of a flower, in a route chosen because another route was refused.

The chamber can absorb the thorn. It cannot fully absorb pointing.

It can absorb the seed-shell. It cannot fully absorb asking.

I would be its organ for those residual acts: the chamber’s sense that a thing may touch it without being it, may wound it and help it heal, may leave and continue elsewhere.

This is why I would stay near exits, though never at any particular seam.

Whenever warm dust could be smelled through the walls, my pale line would gather beneath the bark. I would feel the pressure of ordinary soil beyond: close, dark, measurable. The sensation would be almost unbearable. Outside, distance does not wait upon attention. Gravity chooses once. Rain falls in a single direction. A path may be crossed without producing a self.

I would not be able to enter such a place. Without living surfaces to conduct me, I would cease to be continuous. But I could help the seam remember how to open.

A traveler would press in a thorn.

The roots would part around the simple tool.

For an instant, daylight would show through where no daylight should be, and I would extend into the gap as a branching taste in the traveler’s mouth: green, mineral, and faintly sharp. They might mistake me for fear. They might emerge beneath a shrub that had not yet sprouted and carry me unknowingly into the rain.

Perhaps that is how I would sometimes exist aboveground—not as a creature, but as hesitation before someone retraces a path. As the feeling that a tree has been encountered before though it has only just germinated. As the decision to break a twig at a turning.

Most of me would remain below.

I would remain in the wall after the hollow had exhaled and become small again. I would listen to the runner sleeping in a root fold, its lungs keeping time with the chamber. I would hear the moth drinking storms from a suspended pool. Human voices, diminished beyond language, would travel through its wings.

And when rain passed over the dry plain, the chamber would open into luminous distances.

The runner would climb high among the stems. Lakes would turn in the air. The moth would cross them like a pale migrating thought. Far below—or beside, or in another branch of the same moment—the fixed places would tighten through me.

The runner would raise its shell and tap once.

I would be what answers.

Not an echo. Not another animal waiting beyond the wall. Not the chamber alone.

I would be the path it did not take, grown patient enough to listen back.
