At first I think I would be a listener.

I can imagine the long patience of it: ribs pressed to a pump housing, eyes closed behind their clear lids, feeling an imperfection travel through stone. I would know a gear not by seeing it but by the small hesitation it placed in the wall every seventh hour. I would lie still enough for shelled grazers to cross my wrists. When I finally rose, the clean outline of my body would remain in the moss.

But listeners understand what they hear. I think I would be occupied by something less certain.

Nor would I live near the Condenser. Its water would be too complete. Every taste there has already been interpreted, every current belongs to some old agreement. I would want the place where the Ward becomes difficult to distinguish from what is not the Ward.

You would find me at the retaining wall.

Not on top of it. Nothing in the Ward is so simply placed. There is a narrow structure built into the inner face of the wall, where a pressure pipe from a demolished workshop passes through the foundation of a leaning residence. The residence has no accessible rooms. A filter tree fills its interior, pale branches flattened against old panes, but beneath it is a maintenance recess with two entrances. One opens into the warm green water of the city. The other is a ceramic iris no wider than a shoulder, opening directly into the cold outer sea.

The recess was once a testing chamber. Later it became a storage cell, then a nursery for corrosion-eating films. A cable vine enclosed its inner door one hundred and thirty years ago, so the garden owns that side. The outer iris is still architecture because nothing will grow on all three of its cold faces. Between them, occupying a space the law cannot comfortably assign, is my household.

We are responsible for a leak.

It is a deliberate leak, finer than a hair: a thread of outside water admitted through the iris and carried along a grooved black tile. It travels beneath a membrane of cultivated moss, passes over rows of tasting studs, and enters the Ward so gradually that it is warmed before it can fall. By the time it joins the nearest street current, it contains spores, metal, and the electrical character of the district. It has learned to be local.

The leak is older than anyone who tends it. Because it has followed the same route for more than a century, it is also a public road.

That is the uncertainty occupying me.

Am I the keeper of the road, or one of the things that travels along it?

I would be a Warder, at least in the anatomical sense. Narrow-bodied, four-fingered, darkening early along the sensory lines. My throat chambers would be poor. As a child I would have breathed too quickly and tasted every change in the water as danger. Other children would fling themselves from nursery rails; I would hold to the underside and feel their impacts arrive through the stone.

They would train me first as a seal tender because I could detect outside water at concentrations too small for the instruments. Later, when a winter current shifted in the greater sea, I would wake three rooms away with cold aching behind my teeth. The household at the wall would adopt me after that.

My pressure skin would be asymmetrical.

The city-facing side would host the usual rust-colored film, threaded with cable-vine symbionts. The outer side would be smooth, black-green, and sparsely alive. Between the layers would lie narrow channels connected to my wrists, jaw, and spine. When I placed one hand in the Ward’s water and one through the iris, the skin would let the two seas approach each other across my body without mixing.

That would be my work: to become, for a few hours at a time, a boundary thin enough to feel through.

Outside water would taste almost empty. Cold has a taste when one is accustomed to warmth: not sharpness, but the removal of detail. The outer sea would press against me with distances in it. No returning arcade currents, no mineral exhaust, no lamps changing phase behind the walls. Sometimes a roof fish would pass close enough for me to feel the unopened lights along its belly. Sometimes there would be a pressure from far away with no known source, so broad and slow that the entire wall seemed briefly to inhale.

I would carry these sensations back into the recess and record them.

Not in words. Along one wall would be a panel of shallow ceramic cups, each connected to a flexible bladder. I would press patterns into them: cold, direction, duration, metallic absence, living displacement. Over years the panel would become a tactile weather archive. Others might consult it before a great adjustment, though most entries would mean nothing beyond *something large passed outside* or *the darkness was colder than before*.

Meaning nothing would not make them useless. The Ward preserves activities before it understands them.

Gradually, I would spend longer in the recess.

The cable vine would learn the leakage of my skin and light when I approached. Moss would withdraw from the places my knees touched. A family of spiral grazers would inhabit the fastening rings along my left side. I would know them individually by the tiny interruptions they made in the electrical field. One would always climb higher than the rest and sleep against the hinge of my largest finger.

People coming to consult the outer-water records would cease announcing themselves to me and begin announcing themselves to the chamber.

That is how the change would happen—not by declaration, but by grammar.

At first they would touch my wrist and ask, “What did you feel beyond the wall?”

Later: “What has the wall felt?”

Later still, touching the tasting panel rather than me: “How is it outside?”

I would not resent this. To stand out sharply from one’s surroundings is turbulence. I would be proud that my presence required less and less distinction.

In time the membrane channels of my pressure skin would root into the testing apparatus. Taking the garment off would damage the moss and interrupt the public road, so I would stop taking it off. The household would enlarge the recess by a finger’s width—not enough to disturb the leaning residence—and install a sleeping lattice against the iris. Warm water would circulate through it. I would rest there with one arm folded into the city and the other laid along the cold door.

Children would be brought to me for their first lesson in the difference between waters.

They would arrive bright-skinned and unable to keep still. I would place their hands on mine: one above the warm channel, one near the cold stud. Most would snatch the second hand away. Some would laugh through their filtering veils and release incriminating streams of bubbles. I would show them how the leak crossed the tile, how slowly strangeness must be introduced if it is not to become damage.

Perhaps one child would keep their hand there.

That child would be told my contact name. In youth it would have meant *the one who notices cold*. With age, as the pauses between pulses lengthened, it would come to mean *the small cold permission*. Eventually the final pulse might no longer be given by my fingers at all, but by the testing chamber discharging through my skin.

During the great adjustment, I would be among the last to withdraw.

The iris must close before the deepest gate opens. The public leak must be stopped, though the public road remains. I would feel valves shutting throughout the district as sudden absences in my bones. The vine would go dark. The grazers would retreat beneath my fastening rings. Someone from my household would press their palm to the city-facing side of my chest.

Then the Ward would become silent.

For others, that silence is the loss of the city. For me, it would be the one moment when the outer sea became most present. With the machinery still, I could feel pressures too faint to cross the ordinary murmur: distant cold sliding along the seabed, the gathering of fish outside the wall, perhaps the enormous movement of weather on a surface I had never seen.

The deep gate would open.

Cold would rise under the Ward, but my iris would remain shut. I would feel the city bend upward through its foundations. The old bell-shaped being would lift in the square. Waste and warmth would pass overhead, out across the wall, and the roof fish would ignite beyond me one by one.

I would not see them. Their light would arrive as a sequence of faint electrical touches along my outer arm.

For a moment, I might answer.

Not with a lamp. With the smallest adjustment of the iris: too little to admit a current, only enough to alter the charge on the wall. A pulse into the darkness. No larger, in its own medium, than a permitted bubble.

Whether the fish understood would never be settled.

After my death, there would be a legal difficulty. My body could not be removed without breaking a road, damaging a habitat, and opening a seal. The gardeners would examine the vine and decide it had not merely enclosed me but incorporated my channels into its regulation of the leak. The engineers would find that my mineralized throat chambers now damped pressure fluctuations better than the original collars. The jurists would say that anything adjusting itself to the needs of the district has a claim to consideration.

So I would remain.

Not preserved as a person exactly. The foundry gardeners would replace soft tissue with porous ceramic as it failed. Ribbon kelp would draw metals from me and return them as small conductive nodules. My sensory lines would become wires. The tasting panel would continue to fill. Those who tended the recess after me would rest their hands where my hands had rested and feel the outside sea through the shape I left.

In another century, no one would agree whether I had been a Warder who became part of a testing chamber, a testing chamber temporarily provided with a Warder’s perceptions, or a public current that required a body in order to learn the taste of cold.

The distinction would not need settling.

You would find me there, in the retaining wall: one hand’s breadth of nearly unclaimed water, a narrow permission between two seas.

Still listening, though not quite a listener.

Still inhabited, though not quite an inhabitant.

A road through which almost nothing passes, keeping the Ward itself by allowing the smallest possible measure of elsewhere to enter.
