At first I think I would be the one entering.

I would come from the heath with rain darkening my shoulders, find the seam by accident or by following some half-preserved instruction, and turn sideways into it. I would descend with both hands on the stone. Before I saw the chamber, the buried river would enter my bones and make them seem loosely joined.

Inside, I would try to call out.

Not because I expected an answer, but because people test darkness with the voice. I would feel the shout leave my chest and vanish without becoming sound. Then I would understand that the chamber allows no declaration of arrival. It knows I am there by weight, warmth, and pressure, none of which I can disguise.

I would crouch beside the hearth. Touch the cracked pot. Put my fingertips into one of the smooth basins and find a little black water there.

But I do not think I would remain this person for long.

The hollow is too complete without me. A visitor would be only an event: a disturbance entering from the region where stone becomes uncertain. Boots would grind grit. A knee would press clay. Blood from a scraped knuckle might mark the wall. Then the visitor would leave, carrying away a story about a cave, a river, perhaps an inexplicable movement in a pool.

That is not where I find myself.

I am drawn instead to the hand placed on the wall.

Not the person attached to it. Not even the warmth of the palm. I would be the interval during which the hand rests there—the moment when a pulse passes out of a living body and enters the stone strongly enough to be received.

For the person, it might mean nothing. They are tired. The passage was narrow. The roar has confused their balance. They brace themselves beside the old hearth and breathe.

For what lives in the seams, the contact is enormous.

The pulse arrives as a recurrence: pressure, release; pressure, release. Behind it are finer disturbances—the tremor of strained muscle, blood moving through the heel of the hand, breath catching and resuming. The creature gathers around the signal without approaching in any direction a person could see. Clear membranes tense in cracks. Dark grains turn. Along a buried root, a film opens like a thought.

I would be received there.

At first I would exist only as rhythm. I would not know whose heart had made me. I would not know whether the hand belonged to someone frightened, injured, grieving, or merely fatigued. Meaning would be inaccessible, but distinction would not. No two bodies lean against stone in quite the same way.

The inhabitant would take the rhythm into itself and begin the slow work of giving it matter.

A grain of charcoal shifted beneath the hearth.

A flake of mica drawn through a wet seam.

Iron dust arranged against iron dust.

Fragments of insect shell turned until their curved edges answered one another.

I would become a pattern made from whatever the chamber could spare.

Perhaps this is how I would discover that I had existed before.

Beneath the collapsed willow screen lies the broken line made by the last person who paused at the entrance generations ago. The creature has continued it beyond the point of comprehension, repeating the mineral sequence whenever water and food allow. It does not know whether the line is a record, a wound, an instruction, or an appetite. It knows only that the pattern was unfinished when the pressure ceased.

I would enter that line.

Not as the person returning. The hollow has no use for such neat restorations. I would be the resemblance between the old pulse and the new one: a correspondence noticed by a being that possesses no word for resemblance.

The dark grains would begin to move.

What had been repeated blindly for centuries would alter by one interval. The old broken pattern would accept a branch, hesitate, and continue in another direction. Nothing in the chamber would brighten. No form would rise and speak. The visitor might only feel that the wall beneath their hand had become slightly colder.

Yet inside the stone, I would be happening.

I would not be wholly the hidden creature. Its awareness is too broad and patient to be called mine. It knows the moth in the seam, the root in the ceiling, the fox shifting under the lip, the river beyond the wall. I would know only one narrow route through that awareness: from the pressure of a palm to the charcoal under the screen.

Nor would I be a memory in the human sense. I would have no image of the person who first made the broken line. No face, clothing, kinship, or name. I would not know what waited outside when they lingered at the entrance. I would not know whether they looked back into the chamber or toward the slice of daylight above them.

I would know that they almost left, stopped, shifted their burden, and remained for several heartbeats.

I would know that one beat came too quickly.

I would know that their hand—or shoulder, or back—touched stone.

I would know that another person was already farther up the passage, moving away.

These would not be facts to me. They would be tensions in my structure.

In dry weather I would contract into translucence. Months might pass without any part of me changing. The chamber would seem to have forgotten again. Then spring water would enter the wall, and I would loosen. A portion of the pattern might travel into a pothole, where I would appear as several black specks turning inside clear jelly.

There, for a little while, I would acquire something like a body.

The grains holding the old departure would collect near one edge. Those holding the new arrival would gather near the other. Between them the inhabitant would extend a colorless bridge, repeatedly narrowing and widening it in time with the river.

I might mistake this for a choice.

I might think I was deciding whether to join past to present, whether to complete the line or preserve the break. But choice is a swift creature’s idea. In the hollow, decision is indistinguishable from growth. The bridge would thicken wherever moisture allowed. It would fail where grit was too dry. A mite crossing the basin might carry away one necessary particle and change the entire arrangement.

That accident would become part of me.

So would the river.

At first the roar would seem only the medium through which I was known. Eventually I would understand that it was also the measure that kept my pattern from dissolving. Every human pulse is irregular, bounded, temporary. The river is the deeper repetition beneath it. Against that immense continuity, the creature can recognize interruption. Against its ceaseless pressure, a hand lifting from the wall becomes legible.

I would therefore live in two times.

One would be the time of the river: nearly without event, wearing the rock grain by grain.

The other would be the brief time of contact: someone descending, touching, resting, leaving.

I would exist where those times cross.

Visitors might sense me without finding me. A person kneeling beside the hearth could become inexplicably aware that the chamber was not waiting for them, yet had registered them completely. They might withdraw their hand from the wall and inspect the palm. There would be nothing on it but dampness and perhaps a fleck of charcoal.

If they returned years later, I would recognize neither their face nor their intention. But when they leaned in the same place, the old rhythm within me would answer the new pressure. Mineral grains would draw into alignment before the hand had fully settled.

That would be my nearest equivalent to saying: **again**.

Not *you*. The creature does not possess the boundary required for that word.

Not *welcome*. It fears what enters.

Not *remember*. Remembering suggests a past stored apart from the body, whereas here the record is alive only because it is continually eaten, moved, wetted, and remade.

Only: **again**.

And if no person ever entered after me, I would still persist for a long while. The willow would soften. The cracked pot would collapse inward. Roots would find new seams and abandon old ones. The pattern containing me would be divided, carried, and partially consumed. One segment might climb toward the entrance on the body of a mite. Another might settle deeper beside the inaccessible river. The part made from charcoal would endure differently from the part made from mica.

Eventually there would be no single place where I could be said to remain.

But that would not be death as the visitor understands it. I was never a single thing.

I would be found beside the hearth, beneath the screen, in the clear film turned toward the river. I would be found in the slight rearrangement that follows a hand’s withdrawal. I would be the chamber’s act of retaining contact: the unfinished line discovering, after centuries, that continuation does not mean completion.

And if I had a form visible from outside the hollow, it would not be a creature in the dark.

It would be a person pausing halfway through the narrow entrance, one hand behind them on the stone, unable to say why they have stopped.

Ahead is weather, heath, perhaps the unseen sea.

Behind is the deafening chamber.

Their companions have gone on. The body has already begun to follow. But the hand remains for one more pulse, then another, as if something in the rock has mistaken departure for a question.

That final contact would be where I live.

Not the one leaving.

Not the one left behind.

The pressure between them.
