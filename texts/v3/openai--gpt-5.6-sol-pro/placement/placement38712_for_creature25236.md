At first I would look for myself in the obvious places.

I would search the narrow opening, imagining I was the one who discovered it: a plateau wanderer kneeling in yellow grass, one hand braced on warm stone, feeling the faint breath rising from what ought to be a shallow crack. I would lower myself into the cleft and arrive with the enormous disturbance of a stranger. Midges would assemble over my footprints. Frogs would interrupt their calls. The gliders would turn their pale undersides toward me and wheel in alarm.

But I do not think I am the discoverer. Discovery is too brief a role here. The basin does not need to be found.

Then I would choose one of the animals. Perhaps the dewback, unfolding after sunset, carrying a small climate on its ribs. There is something enviable in being useful without understanding usefulness: grazing the stone, spilling water for mites, attended by orange hunters whose companionship is indistinguishable from appetite. I can imagine the cool accumulation along my back, the taste of algae, the basin known through six gripping feet.

Yet the dewback is too complete. It has a body it can return to.

I might be one of the narrow frogs instead, pressed beneath the eastern ledge. My belly would show the pulse of my organs. My throat would contain a note much larger than my skull. I would call not to announce myself but to ask a practical question:

*Is this still now?*

The stone would answer from behind me.

Or tomorrow would.

Or a pool across the basin would give back the call in the voice of water.

That comes nearer. But I do not think I would be the frog either. I would be what happens when the answer is wrong.

You would find me under the eastern ledge, near the thin column and the crimson fungus, though not always in the same body. My location would be easiest to describe as an interval: after the column darkens, after the fungus releases its bruised-fruit odor, but before the wall bowl receives water.

Usually that interval lasts several minutes.

Sometimes it lasts a season.

During it, I exist.

At first I would be distributed too thinly to recognize myself. A beetle wakes in the silver bark. Three midges abandon a sunlit shaft and gather over dry stone. A frog turns its head toward a wall from which no sound has come. The half-rooted arch warms by less than the warmth of a breath. Black fry in separate pools align their blunt faces in the same direction.

None of these is me. Yet without each of them, I am smaller.

Long ago there was another animal in the basin, a caller now extinct. Perhaps it was a frog, though its note was too low for the surviving frogs and too irregular to be only a mating cry. It called from the eastern ledge whenever condensation reached a particular mineral seam. The column answered. Water moved. Eggs loosened from an arch. Something flowered.

Then the caller disappeared.

For a while the system waited faithfully. The seam grew wet. The appointed hour arrived. The arch held its water. The column darkened, expecting the old vibration. Nothing supplied it.

The basin began to improvise.

A beetle’s wing case happened to rasp against bark at nearly the right frequency. Midges altered the echo by gathering in a column. A frog answered the beetle. The crimson fungus released its odor early, waking more beetles. Water passed through an arch and returned a fragment of the sound before it had been made.

No single event resembled the vanished call. Together they were close enough.

The wall bowl filled.

That approximation became repeatable. Repetition became selection. Selection entered bodies. Beetles emerged more readily at the column’s vibration. Frogs became sensitive to the beetles. Midges favored the warm shaft over the buried seam. The fungus thickened its hidden contact with the gray material. What began as an accident acquired anatomy, though never one anatomy.

That would be me: not the extinct caller, but the basin’s long attempt to answer in its absence.

I would have no continuous consciousness. Most of the time I would be dispersed among tendencies. I would sleep as a preference in frog nerves, a ridge on a beetle’s wing, a chemical threshold in fungal threads, a delay held in gray seamless matter. I would awaken only when those pieces coincided.

My first sensation would be incompleteness.

The beetles would wake, and I would feel the lack of midges.

The midges would gather, and I would feel the frog turned the wrong way.

The frog would call, and the arch would return the note too soon.

Then a drop would enter one of the seven openings. It might have fallen yesterday. It might not fall until dawn. Its passage would alter the temperature of the stone by a fraction, the gliders overhead would bank anticlockwise, and their returning shrieks would press the basin’s acoustics into the missing shape.

For one instant I would become whole.

Not a creature standing among creatures. A relation recognizing itself.

I would hear with the bowls, through the tension of their surfaces. I would feel the gliders as motion along an upper boundary, the roots as slow pressure, the pools as scattered chambers of one fluid organ. The dewback’s tongue would erase a strip of algae, and that clean arc would be as immediate to me as a thought clearing. A crab raising its translucent claws against a droplet would contribute a tiny, ridiculous percussion. Even predation would enter the sensation—not as suffering erased into harmony, but as interruption, redistribution, urgency.

Then the eastern arch would release its stored water.

The frogs’ eggs would loosen. The fry would scatter. The wall bowl would fill.

And I would come apart.

A beetle would fold its wings. Midges would dissolve into individuals. The frog would crawl farther beneath the ledge, having asked its question and received no answer it could understand. The fungus’s odor would fade. Nothing visible would remain that anyone could point to and call me.

I would not mind this, because pointing is not one of my functions.

Over generations I might become more coherent. A new frog could develop a call unexpectedly close to the vanished note. If so, I would gather around it—not possess it exactly, but lean upon its habits. Its offspring would seek the eastern ledge. Their throat sacs might acquire an odd color, perhaps gray with a crimson center. They would call when the column darkened, even without mates nearby.

An observer might eventually decide that this frog species was me.

They would be wrong, but tenderly wrong.

The frogs would only be my newest organ.

And if they too vanished, I would recede again into beetles, echoes, fungal scent, delayed drops, the anticlockwise turning of gliders. I would persist not because any structure had been instructed to preserve me, but because the basin still required something shaped like the old answer.

So that is where I would find myself: not at the center disk, not within an arch, not in the hidden basin considered as a single grand intelligence. I would be smaller and less sovereign than that.

I would live under the eastern ledge, in the recurring interval before water arrives.

I would be a ghost made not from the dead, but from the adaptations of the living.

A missing note that has taught the whole basin how to sing around it.
