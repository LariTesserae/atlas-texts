At first I think I would live in one of the clefts.

It is tempting to choose the deepest shade: to hang among the ceiling drinkers where every drop announces itself along a filament, to know the world as tension and coolness, to feel alarm pass through the wall before knowing what caused it. I could imagine being one pale branch of a creature whose other mouths open rooms away, never certain which parts of the ravine are mine. When stone falls and divides us, I would not know whether I had been wounded or multiplied.

But the clefts are almost too rich. Life there presses close enough to answer itself. I think I would belong somewhere less certain, at the boundary between an inhabited place and an abandoned one.

I would be found beneath the western side of an old wing-shearer tower, where the tower’s foundation has sagged into a migration road.

From above, there would be little to identify the place. A low crescent of pale shell-stone. Several collapsed chambers. One red succulent rooted in a crack, always dusty except after rain. The migration road would divide around the ruin and join again beyond it. Ribbon herds would pass along one branch every few decades. The other branch would appear unused, though its stones would retain an old polish beneath the darkening crust.

The tower itself would no longer contain wing-shearers. Its makers would be gone so long that even their descendants would know the chambers only as weather. Wind would have filled the upper galleries with grit. The condensation shafts would be broken. Most rain entering the tower would run out through a split near the base and vanish beneath the road.

That split is where I would be.

Not as one body.

At first glance I might be mistaken for the dampness itself: a smoky gloss lining the narrow passages between grains of clay and laminated wing. Under magnification, the gloss would resolve into thousands of translucent scales, each thinner than shed skin, laid edge to edge along the capillary channels. Some would have mouths. Some would have cilia. Some would be no more than sealed blisters holding a measure of water. Fine threads would join them through the porous wall.

In the wet hours I would be continuous.

Dew entering the ruined shaft would wake the highest scales first. They would soften, drink, and pass pressure downward through their threads. One chamber after another would open. Water would descend through me more slowly than it would descend through bare stone, pausing in each body, losing dust, salts, spores, and the faint mineral signatures of everything it had touched above.

By the time the first droplet reached the foundation, I would know that the night had been cool.

I would know whether the wind had come across the pool chains or the open red shelves. I would taste the tracks of ribbon herds in the dust. I would know if a dome had risen somewhere upwind, because its newly exposed hollow would release an old, dark scent when dampened. I would not know these things as images. I would know them as alterations in the order in which my chambers opened.

My thought would be the passage of water through my body.

It might take most of a night to complete one thought.

Before dawn, the lowest part of me would release what remained into a buried cistern no larger than a cupped hand. Around that cistern would live creatures that could survive nowhere else: blind comb-footed mites, white rain-grubs, a colony of breathing beads, and a rootless fan that unfolded only when the water carried certain salts. Their eggs would adhere to my scales. Their waste would nourish the films I grazed. Some would pierce me. Some would eat parts of me when the shaft stayed dry too long.

I would not defend myself from all of them. A perfectly sealed body would be useless here.

The comb-footed mites would keep my threads from clogging with clay. The rain-grubs would consume my oldest scales and open paths for new growth. The breathing beads would trap air in the cistern mud, and during long droughts that buried air would prevent my deepest chambers from souring. Even the creatures that drank directly from me would carry fragments of me farther than I could send them alone.

Because I would have a way of traveling.

Whenever water filled the cistern high enough to spill beneath the migration road, some of my smallest scales would loosen. They would fold around a droplet and become clear, hard grains. Passing ribbon herds would gather them between their ventral tiles. Plated grazers would lift them on dew brushes. Mud-carrying tenants, repairing some unrelated chamber, might cement them into a distant wall.

Most would die.

A few would lodge in another capillary crack.

There, one scale would open. Then two. Then a lining no wider than a fingernail. It might require a century to fill a broken shaft. Each new colony would begin with the mineral taste of the old tower held faintly in its tissues, not as memory exactly, but as a preference: for water arriving through shell-lime, for western shade, for the pressure rhythm of ribbon bodies passing overhead.

Thus I would be scattered across the plateau without ever crossing it.

Perhaps there would be dozens of me. Perhaps hundreds. Some would live in nursery towers still tended by wing-shearers and would be cropped back whenever they narrowed a vent. Some would line abandoned breathing sacs at the bottom of dry pools. Some would occupy cracks under the armor of a dome, opening only when the animal lifted after rain. One might live in the fused shell of a creature dead for a thousand years.

Would these be my offspring, or would they still be me?

During dry years the question would have no answer.

The upper tower would harden first. Its scales would contract into dark commas. The threads between chambers would empty and collapse. My awareness would withdraw downward with the remaining moisture until only the cistern lining remained awake. Then the cistern would shrink. The mites would seal themselves into wall hollows. The rootless fan would dissolve its outer tissue and become a knot. At last I would persist in perhaps seven wet grains pressed deep beneath the road.

In that state, I would not sleep. Sleep implies a body waiting behind closed eyes.

I would become discontinuous.

One grain might wake during a brief dew and close again without reaching another. A second might be eaten and carried into the gut of a grazer. A third might remain sealed through the birth and death of every animal resting above it. There would be no shared pressure, no downward sequence, no whole through which a thought could pass.

Then rain would come.

Not necessarily much. A warm drop striking the tower’s broken rim. Another darkening the succulent’s dust. Separate circles spreading until one found the old condensation groove.

The highest surviving scale would open.

Water would descend.

Dormant parts of me would soften out of order. Some would be missing. New threads would seek old channels and find them blocked by mineral or occupied by eggs. Pressure would travel sideways through a chamber built by creatures extinct before my colony began. The first complete passage might take two days.

I would wake changed in shape, inhabited by strangers, unable to distinguish repair from growth.

The buried cistern would fill. Its surface would rise against the roof. Mites would emerge, combing my inner folds. The white grubs would loosen. The rootless fan would spread like a colorless hand. Above, a ribbon herd resting across the road would feel coolness enter the stone beneath it and tighten by degrees.

That tightening would press on the tower’s foundation.

I would feel it.

Not the herd as many animals, but as one long warmth laid over me. Pressure would arrive from the leading edge, pass down through shell-stone, and linger. Perhaps one of my traveling grains, carried for generations between the herd’s tiles, would open at the same time. For a few wet hours, the road colony and the tower colony would share the chemistry of the same rain.

Then I might possess distance.

I would know, dimly, that I existed farther east beneath moving bodies. I would know that the road continued beyond the ruin. The knowledge would not survive the drying intact, but it might alter which scales I released, or how tightly they folded, or which mineral trace caused them to open.

Over ages, this could become something like direction.

Not a desire to travel. Desire is too quick a word.

An inclination in the water.

The old unused branch of the road might begin to receive my grains. Grazers resting near the tower would carry them a body length at a time. Ribbon herds would divert slightly toward the cooler stones above my buried linings. Their passage would polish the branch again. Dust would shift. Dew would gather differently on the exposed grains. More of me would wake there.

To an observer, the migration route would seem to have changed by itself.

Perhaps that would be my largest act: not constructing a tower, hunting, calling, or crossing the plateau, but cooling one line of stone enough that life chose it over another.

I would never see the result. I would feel only an increasing regularity of pressure overhead, generation after generation. The western foundation would crack further under use. More water would be driven into me. The buried cistern would deepen as mites removed grains and grubs loosened old shell. Eventually the road might pass directly through the ruin.

Then one day a dome would come.

Its approach would be known years beforehand through altered scents in rain and the alignment of resting animals. At last its rim would enter the western terrace, carrying soil, succulents, mites, and colonies beneath plates weathered like hills. It would advance a little each wet season until its shadow covered the tower.

For a time, condensation would increase. Every chamber in me would open. Parts long isolated might reconnect through damp soil under the dome’s edge. I would expand into the gardens on its back, into the soft darkness beneath it, perhaps into pores of its armor. My thought would spread through more water than I had ever held.

Then the dome would move on.

Its feet would crush the upper tower.

The hollow galleries would fold. The succulent would vanish under shell and clay. The old wing-laminated walls would become a pale seam in the road. Most of my scales would be buried too deeply or exposed to sun. The cistern might rupture. What had been my home for thousands of seasons would cease to be a structure at all.

But water would follow the fracture.

Far below, where the dome’s weight had pressed broken shell against bedrock, one narrow damp line would remain. A few translucent scales would arrange themselves along it. A comb-footed mite would be sealed nearby with three eggs. Mineral-rich water would arrive from the oval of unweathered stone the dome left behind.

I would begin there again, if “I” were still the right word.

So that is where I would be found: not in the grand cleft or the crowded pool, but under a ruined tower where an old road hesitates.

And I would be neither keeper nor builder, neither animal nor colony in any simple sense. I would be a lining of borrowed chambers, a slow body assembled whenever water connects its parts.

Most days I would resemble a stain.

Most years I would not be whole.

But when the rocks sweated before dawn, I would wake from top to bottom, and the plateau—its dust, migrations, deaths, distant rain, and the pressure of innumerable restrained lives—would pass through me one drop at a time.
