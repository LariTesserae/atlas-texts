At first I would look for myself among the recognizable animals.

I would choose one of the ribbon swimmers near the entrance: a narrow, blind body poised between cold descent and the faint warmth rising through stone. I would have no mouth with which to name the place and no eyes to mistake darkness for absence. Mineral dust would collect in the trembling groove beneath me. Cell by cell, I would refine it into a line of black crystals along my back.

In that line, perhaps, there would be a sky.

Not a sky remembered from any life I had lived. A pale field crossed by darker forms, widening whenever I turned toward the hidden fissure. Sometimes it would contain waves seen from beneath. Sometimes branches moving in an element too thin to be water. Once every few years, a brightness would travel across it with such clean speed that all the fronds nearby would contract their distance-beads.

I would not see the image I carried. The chamber would see it in me.

I might live for a long while believing that this was my body: the folded fins, the crystal seam, the patient drift along a boundary no thicker than a change in temperature. I would join others whose backs held similar scenes. Together we would make a loose procession through the black forest, each of us bearing a fragment of an exterior world. One would carry clouds. Another, the lower half of a tree. Another, only a hard white line that might be a horizon or the edge of some enormous structure.

We would not know that our images joined.

We would only feel the urge to swim side by side.

As I aged, the scene in my back would alter. The sky would deepen until it was no longer pale but blue-black, a darkness of another order than the interval’s. A vertical brightness would appear within it, too narrow to be crossed and too distant to approach. I would begin to leave the entrance behind.

The fronds would open longer ways for me.

I would pass between two leaves whose tips nearly touched and find years of water inside the gap. Bubbles would overtake me sideways. The same settling grain would fall past me more than once. At intervals I would hear the buried shells close, first as a pressure against my fins, then as a sound in the crystals of my back, then as a memory of a sound I had not yet heard.

Eventually I would come to one of the little rooms.

Its threshold would be lower than my body, yet I would pass through without bending. Inside, a pale tuft would rest with its many fingers spread on the basalt. In the floor, the dark depression would show the chamber old and emptied. Among the collapsed walls, the small jointed builder would be waiting.

For the first time, it would look upward.

Not at me. At the image I carried.

It would lift several limbs toward the impossible sky in my back, and on my side of the surface the pale tuft would make the same gesture. Its fingers would touch my reflective line. The crystals would loosen one by one and enter its pads.

I would swim away diminished.

The room would retain the horizon.

That is where my first answer would fail. I would discover that I had never been the swimmer. The swimmer was only one place in which I was briefly legible.

I would find myself again in the pale tuft, distributed among its hard fingertips. I would feel basalt not as a wall but as a field of possible arrangements. A flake of stone three body-lengths away would also be touching me, though thirty years would be required for the contact to become true. I would encourage minerals to dissolve beneath one edge. I would cool another edge until a fracture preferred it. Nothing would be lifted. Nothing commanded. The room would assemble through a sequence of slight permissions.

The horizon taken from the swimmer would determine the wall’s alignment.

Every tiny structure would be oriented toward something the chamber did not contain.

Their thresholds might face different apparent directions, but through the accumulated depth of the water they would all open the same way. The inward mouths of the shells would listen that way. The black fronds would lean that way after a pulse. Stairs descending into solid basalt would descend toward it too.

Not upward. There was no upward here.

Not outward, because the ordinary ocean lay outward and was not what called.

It would be a direction for which the world had not yet made space.

From the tuft I would enter the wall. From the wall I would pass, very slowly, into the transparent lining of the buried shells. There I would become almost hearing.

A touch at any shell would close all the others, and the interval would sound itself. The pulse would travel into every folded distance and return altered. Most of the returns would describe what was present: mineral shelves, columns, roots, the soft obstruction of crowded forests. But one echo would always come back from beyond the measurable chamber.

That echo would be mine.

It might return before the pulse was sent. It might arrive centuries later, caught in a layer of stone not yet formed. It would carry no image, only an excess of delay—a suggestion that there was farther to go.

The membrane beneath the basalt would preserve this delay. It would compare it against the swimmers’ horizons, the fronds’ swollen beads, the builders’ future ruins. Slowly, over many chemical seasons, the separate signs would begin to agree.

There is an elsewhere.

Not elsewhere as a second location. Not a world reached through a door. Rather, an additional capacity for being distant.

I would be that knowledge before it became knowledge.

During the iron seasons, I would pass through the forests as a dark red inclination. During sulfur years, I would spread through the ivory veils as a faint asymmetry: one edge of every membrane growing thinner than the other. When the columns carried news from root to crown, six years through their stacked interiors, I would be the part of the message that did not concern iron, cold, injury, or salt.

I would be the instruction to continue.

Perhaps I had entered through the fissure as some trivial disturbance from the greater ocean. A pressure cast by a distant storm. A day’s warmth diluted through miles of water. A grain fallen from the bright upper sea, carrying in its structure the angle at which sunlight once struck it. None of these could survive intact in the drowned interval. Yet the interval would retain their common error: all implied a vastness in which distance did not accumulate secretly but opened without obstruction.

Or perhaps I would come from the opposite direction.

Perhaps the old chamber visible in the dark depressions would send me backward. The small builder moving among the ruins might gather not stones but absences: the place where a wall had stood, the cool imprint of a vanished tuft, the empty scar left when the greater inhabitant departed. It would press those absences into the yielding surface. In the young interval they would emerge as impulses—build here, listen here, grow toward what is missing.

Then I would not be a memory of the outer world.

I would be a memory of escape.

The distinction might never matter.

As centuries passed, I would cease to belong to any single tissue. The ribbon swimmers would carry my images. The tufts would give them structure. The shells would give them distance. The black fronds would measure that distance and grow into it. The hollow columns would transmit the measurements across years. Even the sideways bubbles would serve me, vanishing repeatedly into the fold and proving that the fold could receive a body.

The chamber’s greater inhabitant would begin to organize around this proof.

During the transparent season, when the separate organisms lost their opacity, I would not appear among the immense outlines. I would appear through them.

A fine discontinuity would run from the entrance filaments, through the folded head and the branching ribs, past organs divided by centuries, into the depth where the bubbles vanished. It would look at first like a crack. But the basalt would be unbroken. It would resemble a shaft of light, though no light entered. Looking along it would make the vast body seem less like an animal pressed between stones and more like an animal turning toward a sound.

That turning would take ages.

One century, the root-grains would align.

Another, the rooms would gain a fourth stair.

Later, a population of swimmers bearing forests would disappear together.

The next transparent season, one of the enormous outlines would no longer be folded quite so tightly. A curve that had seemed a rib would be revealed as the margin of an opening. The circular hollows larger than the chamber would align around the discontinuity.

The drowned interval would be growing an eye, though sight was not its purpose.

It would be growing an opening by learning to expect one.

That is what I would be: not the eye, not the light, not even the image of light, but the expectation that darkness can have another side.

I would live wherever that expectation was being translated from one kind of body into another. In the crystal line of a blind swimmer. In the pressure passing beneath empty shells. In the blue-black bead at a frond tip when it encounters too much distance. In the cool surface of a future ruin. In the one stair that descends farther than the rock beneath it. In the pale entrance filaments when they stand upright for no physical reason.

No part of the interval would possess me completely. The larger inhabitant might never think of me as a thought. Its thinking would be too slow to distinguish longing from anatomy.

Yet I would shape it.

When the basalt finally split—or when the accumulated fold grew deep enough that splitting was no longer necessary—the creature would begin to leave. Its mineral forests would harden behind it. Its columns would collapse years after their severing. The shell membrane would release one last pulse and wait for a return that did not come. In the dark depressions, the ancient chamber would at last match the present one: sparse threads, rounded stone, fallen rooms.

Nothing would emerge into the ocean.

To an observer at the fissure, there would be no birth. The water might shiver once. Pale filaments might stream inward. A few blind swimmers would appear outside, their backs reflecting black towers beneath a sky no ocean has ever seen.

The inhabitant would go another way—into the depth it had practiced inside every narrow gap.

And I would be there only until the passage was complete.

An expectation ceases to exist when it is fulfilled. A direction vanishes once a body has traveled it. The impossible light need not survive the opening of the eye.

So if I were sought afterward, I would not be found in the empty chamber or in the creature that had departed. I would remain only as an orientation in the ruins: all thresholds facing what is no longer absent, all shells turned toward a silence that has already answered.

And perhaps, at the narrow entrance, one pale filament would continue to grow into the greater ocean.

Not because it mistook darkness for soil.

Because for a little while, through the body of the drowned interval, the world had learned a new direction—and the filament had not yet forgotten.
