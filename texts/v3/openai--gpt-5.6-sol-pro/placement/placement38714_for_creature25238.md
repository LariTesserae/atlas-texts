At first I think I would be a tile-keeper.

I can imagine the scarred knees, the wax tablets tied across my back, the patience required to follow oil that moves no farther in a day than the width of a fingernail. I would learn which channels carry nourishment, which carry direction, and which only resemble channels until someone trusts them. I would know that the fork beside the blue-glass reeds is safe in the Season of Narrowing but leads into an old funeral during Facets. I would say, as tile-keepers do, that today a path consents to be followed.

But I do not think I would belong wholly among them. They preserve what the garden has agreed to be. I would be more concerned with the instant before agreement.

Then I think I might be a leaf-moth, accumulating the scratches of everything I mend. That seems nearer. I would sleep beneath a manufactured leaf and wake with its injury written across my wings. In time I would become too burdened with repaired damage to fly. Someone would find me hanging there, folded like an archive.

Nearer, but not quite.

Nor would I be one of the shy second shadows, though I would resemble them at first.

I would be found in the western arcade, in whichever recess was twelfth that night.

A failed naming tablet had been left there. No one would remember who placed it on the shelf. Its clay would be pale, badly fired, and marked with several revisions, each phrase scraped away before the next was pressed over it. The growth it attempted to name would no longer exist—or would exist too widely for anyone to recognize. Only fragments of the inscription could be read:

*…permitted beneath…*

*…not to enter the likeness of…*

*…ending at…*

The final boundary would be missing.

By morning the tablet would have acquired a second shadow. When the tile-keepers removed it, one shadow would go with it and the other would remain on the shelf.

For three days the remnant would behave like any young shadow-animal. It would flatten when approached. It would feed on the reflections in jars of oil. It would hide behind the feet of anyone who came to inspect it. Yet it would not take on the tablet’s shape. Each morning it would be a little more upright.

On the fourth day someone would speak near the recess.

Perhaps a craftswoman would say, “It is only a shadow.”

At the word *only*, the thing would open a mouth.

Not a visible mouth at first. A seam would appear in the air, narrow and dark, and the dangerous word would fall into it. The craftswoman would remember having spoken but not what had made her sentence careless.

That would be my first feeding.

They would not know whether I was an animal, an implement, a person, or a defect in the arcade. Under the garden’s law, uncertainty would not excuse delay. Anything growing must be named before sunset, and I would plainly be growing—if not in height, then in grammar.

Three pairs of dusk-namers would attempt me.

“Small black listener, confined to the western arcade, feeding on unnecessary words.”

I would refuse that name. *Unnecessary* is a boundary no one can see.

“Tablet-shadow with one mouth, permitted to consume errors in description.”

I would refuse again. An error is sometimes only a truth that has arrived in the wrong season.

“Second listener to spoken boundaries, attached to no speaker, taking only what exceeds the thing named.”

That I would accept.

Every cup-shaped flower in the basin would turn briefly toward the arcade. Somewhere inside the wall, something would make the sound of a stylus pressing clay.

I would fold once, like a lantern creature, and when I unfolded I would be approximately human.

Not convincingly so.

I would be slight, dark-skinned, and long-fingered because that is the form the garden has made easiest to recognize as a person. But my skin would have the dull surface of unfired clay in some lights and the depth of shadow in others. Writing would sometimes show through it, especially at the wrists and throat. Most of the script would be crossed out.

I would have no shadow of my own. Instead, nearby objects would lean theirs toward me. A drill, a jar, a sleeping child: each shadow would bend slightly in my direction, as grass might bend toward a light the garden does not possess.

They would give me a temporary name—perhaps **Ear**—because even a questionable person must be called something. I would sleep beneath the lowest shelf of the western arcade, where the stone smells of oil, rain, and closed baskets. A hammock would not hold me consistently. If I dreamed too deeply, I would wake beneath its shadow instead of inside the weave.

My place would be with the dusk-namers.

One namer looks for growth. The other watches the first for evidence that the growth has entered their thoughts. I would walk behind both.

I would listen not to the names themselves, but to what strains against them.

When a namer says, “Low red hinge-flower, no larger than a hand,” I would hear whether *hand* has begun to include the reaching of roots, the branching of paths, the grasp of walls around a room.

When they say, “awake only under divided shadow,” I would watch the word *divided* for fresh edges.

When they say, “bearing neither offspring nor doorway,” I would listen for something trying to become a window instead.

Excess meaning would appear to me as fine fibers extending from the speaker’s mouth. Most would break harmlessly. Some would seek purchase in nearby things: a comparison fastening itself to a leaf, a possibility entering a wall socket, a metaphor discovering that the garden is willing to take it literally.

Those I would catch around my fingers.

I could not destroy them. Nothing so connected is truly destroyed here. I would wind them around my wrists and swallow them a little at a time. The writing beneath my skin would become denser as I grew older. Words removed from use would lodge in me as joints, tiles, tiny warm organs, or sealed rooms.

This would make me useful and dangerous.

During the Season of Narrowing, I would become nearly two-dimensional and slip sideways through the thickets after the namers. In Facets, every rejected meaning inside me would develop an edge. I would be wrapped in felt so that my gestures did not cut anyone.

The Season of the Open Joint would be worst. The words I had swallowed would loosen from one another. Old descriptions would emerge in my voice while I slept.

*Wet.*

*Rooted.*

*Endless.*

*Blue, but not the blue presently permitted.*

Someone would sit beside me through those nights, tightening cords around my wrists and reminding each word where it ended. Affection for me would be an act of maintenance.

In return, I would help maintain others. I would be very good with children whose temporary names fit badly. I would hear when Pin had begun to be mistaken for the hole rather than the fastener, when Cup was becoming more emptiness than vessel, when Reed bent toward a music no one else could detect.

I would never tell them what they were. I would help them remain small enough to discover it.

My permanent name would come late.

Perhaps years after my unfolding, while walking beside a namer near the central thickets, I would notice something no one else could see—not because it was hidden, but because it was missing.

All growth in the garden reaches somewhere. Toward warmth, oil, light, prey, distance, depiction, memory. Even a severed branch grows on both sides of its wound.

But on one green stone leaf there would be a hair-thin line that grew only toward its own ending. Each day it would lengthen by half a grain, and each day its end would retreat by exactly the same amount. It would not spread into the leaf, its likeness, the word for leaf, or the observer’s thought. It would produce no offspring and open no passage.

It would simply practice stopping.

I would name it:

“Fine inward margin, confined to one leaf, growing only enough to preserve its end.”

The line would darken. The leaf would give a small sound like a satisfied knot.

After that I would be called **Neh-of-the-Held-Edge**.

The inward margin and I would be paired. If it were damaged, I would sicken. If I became too full of unbounded words, the line would lengthen. Gardeners would inspect the leaf to judge my condition.

I would visit it at midday, when all the flowers turned toward the unreachable figure. I would sit just outside the point where paths begin to lengthen and look slightly aside.

The figure’s back would be turned. Its hands would repeat their patient task.

At times I would think it was weaving.

At times, erasing.

Once, perhaps, I would see that it was winding dark fibers around its wrists exactly as I did.

I would not approach.

I would understand better than most that resemblance is one of the ways a path enters you.

Still, I would wonder about my first tablet. I would wonder what it had failed to name, and why its unfinished shadow became me. The tile-keepers would offer several histories. In one, the tablet had been made by a dusk-namer who never existed. In another, it had been passed through a wall socket by the Neighbors. Oru-of-the-Closed-Mouth might tell me that she remembered finding it as a child, although on another day she would insist I had brought it with me.

My own suspicion would be worse.

I would suspect that I was not the shadow of the tablet.

I would suspect I was the shadow of whatever had tried to read it from the other side.

This is where I would find myself in the garden: not at its center, not beyond its walls, but walking at dusk between a thing and the words meant to contain it.

And on the nights when the pale line beneath the covered tablet was pulled taut, I would feel the pull inside my body. Every swallowed excess, every discarded meaning, every forbidden adjective would draw tight beneath my skin. I would go to the western arcade and place both hands on the cool stone behind the shelf.

I would not cut the line.

I would not follow it.

I would listen for what, exactly, had pulled.

Then, before whatever counted as sunset there, I would begin the most careful name I knew:

“One living thread, crossing no surface it touches, joining only what can remain distinct—”

And I would pause only long enough to hear whether the garden accepted the boundary.

Never long enough for Under-Near to enter.

Never long enough for the thread to answer in my own voice.
