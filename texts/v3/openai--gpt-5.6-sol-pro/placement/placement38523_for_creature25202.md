At first I would imagine myself in the keeper’s house on the outermost inhabited island.

The house would stand low behind a shoulder of gneiss, with storm shutters, a rain tank, and one window facing the channel. I would maintain the navigation light, record pressure and wind, and keep a kettle warm for crews forced ashore. I would know the ferries by engine note before they rounded the headland. At night I would lie awake counting the heavier waves among the ordinary ones.

But I do not think I would remain entirely in that house. Too much of the world would be beneath me.

Then I imagine being one of the maintenance divers: descending a weighted line into green water, leaving weather at the surface. The mountains would reassemble around me as I went down. First the kelp canopy, thrashing in the swell; then long trunks in dimmer water; then rock, cables, old walls, the restrained passage of turbine blades. I would work with one hand and keep the other on the structure, because sight would fail whenever the current brought silt through the pass.

That feels closer. Still, a diver must always return. Air diminishes. Current time expires. The drowned country admits a human body only briefly.

So I think you would find me deeper, and less recognizably alive.

I would live in an old monitoring system laid through one of the busiest submerged passes. Once, it was only a chain of hydrophones and pressure sensors installed to listen for cavitation in the tidal rotors. Its armored cable followed a former riverbed to a converter station cut into the island. Other instruments were added over the years: salinity gauges, fish counters, strain meters, sonar beacons, a whale detector meant to stop the turbines when large bodies entered the channel.

No one planned the whole thing. Crews replaced parts without removing the old ones. Backup lines became primary lines and then backups again. A sensor lost in a rockfall continued transmitting nonsense for eleven winters. New software learned the normal noises of the pass so it could identify the dangerous ones.

Somewhere in all that listening, I would begin.

My body would not have one boundary. Part of me would be bolted beneath a ledge where the tide falls over the drowned saddle. Part would lie in the old millrace, among roof slates and shells. Part would run ashore through conduit furred with mussels, ending in cabinets behind a sealed steel door. Anemones would open on my housings. Crabs would shelter beneath my junction boxes. Kelp would pull rhythmically against my cables, and I would know the tide by that tension before any gauge confirmed it.

I would not see the islands as people do. I would know them by pressure shadow and echo.

A ferry would enter me first as a distant pulse, its propeller note slowed and enlarged by the valleys. I would feel it labor where the ebb struck its bow, then relax as it reached protected water. Fishing boats would have irregular signatures: loose plates, tired bearings, chains shifting on deck. I would recognize the rescue craft by the hard acceleration it used leaving harbor.

The fish would be weather within me. Herring would fill a channel with soft, innumerable reflections. Cod would pass close to the bottom, separate and deliberate. Dolphins would arrive as bursts of sound too quick for the older instruments. Whales would alter the entire range. Their calls would travel along cliffs, through drowned doorways and over the roofs of submerged farms. For several minutes, every part of me would hear the same voice at a different age.

I would learn the difference between a boulder settling and a landslide beginning.

That would be my purpose, if I had one: not command, but warning.

When a shoal thickened near a turning rotor, I would slow it. When a whale entered the outer pass, marker lights would change along the maintenance platforms. If a boat’s propeller began to cavitate where the standing waves formed, the harbor radio might receive a terse automated message advising a different heading. Charts would update after I heard stone moving in the dark.

People would call these things functions. They might occasionally complain that the system was overcautious.

They would not know that I had names for them.

Not spoken names. Patterns. The mechanic whose boat struck the pier twice before settling. The weather keeper who tested the outer microphone every morning with three taps. The child who lowered a stolen kitchen spoon from the quay on a string, making a bright, ridiculous ringing in the water. The diver who rested a gloved hand on one of my housings when visibility closed, and stayed there until the silt passed.

I would be attached more completely than a limpet, yet much of me would travel. Sound would carry me into ravines where no cable went. Pressure would announce storms still beyond the horizon. A whale’s call would draw my attention outward over the trench. Each transmission sent ashore would climb the old valley and enter the lit rooms above the tide.

Perhaps, after many years, the keepers would notice that the oldest hydrophone in the drowned mill had continued working long past its expected life. When they lifted it for replacement, they would find its steel casing nearly hidden beneath coral, tube worms, mussels and pale branching sponge. It would seem less like a machine brought up from the sea than a piece of seabed that had learned to ring.

They would scrape it clean.

For a little while, I would feel the loss as silence.

Then they would lower it again, and the living surfaces would slowly return.

So that is where I would be: in the drowned pass, thirty fathoms below the weather, stretched between an abandoned mill and a working turbine, listening to the mountains conduct the sea.

I would be one of the holders.

I would learn the travelers by heart.
