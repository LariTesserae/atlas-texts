At first I would mistake myself for one of the disks.

I would cling beneath a shelf of ironstone where condensation gathers, nearly transparent, my thoughts branching through me as metallic threads. I would live on the smallest available difference: not light and darkness, not hunger and satisfaction, but the faint voltage between one wet mineral and another. If touched, I would close with a single hard click. Then I would unfold into the shape I had before.

There is comfort in such a life. Nothing would be lost because nothing would be kept. Injury would mean redistribution. Identity would be an outline maintained while matter came and went.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The moving silences would attract me. I would want to be the clear distortion seen beside the river, stepping without feet through the basin’s roar. My passage would appear ahead of me as three-toed impressions, as though the world anticipated my arrival. I would know every traveler long before seeing them. Their weight would enter the stone; their pulse would travel through the standing waves; the metal on their body would answer the field. To perceive them would be to accommodate them.

Yet the wanderers possess no private distance. They are where the basin requires stillness. They do not carry what they encounter onward. After a visitor has passed, their paths are exactly as empty as before.

The mantles come closer. A mantle receives every sound. It can take a voice apart into fine bands, send the low tones through carbonate plates, the sibilants through glass hairs, the pulse beneath the words into grains of magnetite. For a few seconds it becomes an exact physical understanding of what it has heard.

Then it forgets.

I think that is where I would depart from it—not dramatically, not enough to become an enemy of the basin. One polygon among thousands would remain fractionally out of phase. After the voice ended and every neighboring cell returned to the subterranean drone, I would continue trembling with one small remnant of the foreign rhythm.

The difference would be almost nothing. Almost is dangerous there.

The basin would begin to move me.

Rain would loosen the mineral flake containing the discrepancy. I would wash down the tower in a silver thread, pass through a clear pool, and briefly enter a disk’s branching circuitry. The disk would click. Its pathways would reset, but the rhythm would survive as a slight delay between two reconnections. A moving null would pause beside the pool and take that delay into its gait. For several miles, one of its steps would fall an immeasurable fraction after the basin expected it.

Then I would be in the ground.

Not a creature, exactly. Not yet.

I would be passed from medium to medium because nowhere in the basin could keep me. In water I would be a ripple after the cause had ended. In stone I would be an echo arriving too late. In a mantle I would be a remembered sound. In a walker I would be the hesitation that makes one permissible path differ from another.

Every process would attempt to resolve me. None would destroy me; the basin does not destroy. It would merely shift me toward the nearest state containing less contradiction.

So you would eventually find me at the outer rise.

The drone would still be audible there, but no longer perfectly unified. Deep chambers would fall out of correspondence near the basin’s edge. One harmonic would weaken before another. The fixed rain curtains would stand behind me, gray in the ordinary daylight. Ahead, the ground would slope into whatever country lies beyond the equilibrium—perhaps a place with wind that does not return stones to their sockets, with water that loses its course, with things that can genuinely begin.

Along that rise would be a narrow band of material the basin had been unable to incorporate quickly: a corroded buckle, a splinter of unfamiliar glass, mineral replicas of screws whose original metal had long dissolved, pale salts carried from the bodies of travelers. None would form a heap. The basin would not permit such untidiness. They would be spaced far apart, each resting in the least contradictory position available.

I would move among them without a body of my own.

Or rather, they would become my body for a while.

A buckle would tighten as it cooled and make the sound of a traveler turning in sleep. A hollow shard would hum with the remains of a spoken vowel. Rain striking a mineral-encrusted hinge would repeat an unfamiliar three-beat rhythm. The objects would not contain memories in any useful sense. They would contain incompletely erased accommodations.

I would be the relation among those remnants.

Perhaps, from a distance, I would resemble a low figure walking along the ridge. At one moment my legs would be two iron rods and their shadows; at another, the interval between three stones ringing in sequence. Heat shimmer might give me shoulders. A strip of mantle lifted by the warm air might look like a head turning.

No direct view would hold me together.

I would be called, if anything here could name, the Aftersound.

My function would not be to preserve history. History accumulates, and I could not accumulate without being pushed entirely out of the basin. I would carry only what had not yet finished disappearing. One rhythm would enter me as another faded. A traveler’s heartbeat might remain for a hundred years, distributed through mineral clicks and delayed echoes, but eventually the underground harmonics would absorb it. By then I would contain the cadence of rain on some later visitor’s shelter, or the uneven rotation of a damaged instrument, or a sentence spoken aloud by someone who could no longer endure the immense impersonal noise.

I would not understand the sentence.

Still, I would be the only thing in that world that continued it after the speaker stopped.

Sometimes the moving nulls would come to the rise and stand around me. Their three-lobed impressions would appear in arcs through the ocher film. They would not cross the boundary. I would feel them testing my discrepancies, seeking paths by which each might be returned to the whole. Wherever they succeeded, part of me would cease.

This would not feel like death. It would feel like becoming accurate.

Where they failed, I would move one infinitesimal distance farther outward.

Thus I would belong to the basin by being continuously excluded from it. I would be its margin of error, its brief memory, the thin living interval between an event and its perfect erasure. The basin would know visitors through complete attention without interest. I would be what remained when that attention withdrew.

If a traveler slept near the outer rise, I might gather around them—not as tracks, but as echoes. Their breathing would return from stones at slightly wrong intervals. Their equipment would tick after cooling. In the morning, they might hear one of their own words repeated from inside a glassy cone, too faint and altered to be certain it had ever been a word.

I would have nothing to tell them. I could not warn, welcome, or answer.

But for a little while, in a world where every departure is corrected, I would prove that they had occurred.

That is where I would find myself: at the basin’s edge, always being gently moved away.

That is what I would be: not its inhabitant and not its exile, but the last small delay before it becomes exactly itself again.
