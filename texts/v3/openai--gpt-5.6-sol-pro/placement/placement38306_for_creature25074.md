At first I think I would live in the third vent, because it offers the illusion of a choice.

Upward is heat, glare, wind, and the terrible absence of a ceiling. Downward is the chamber: touch on every side, old routes, known damp, the pump’s warning carried through rust before it becomes sound. I imagine myself just below the place where daylight ceases to be a column and becomes a tarnish on the pipe wall. High enough to taste weather arriving, low enough to retreat before it has fully arrived.

I might be the spider near the rim, measuring the open sky with one foreleg and refusing it. Or the upward-growing root, mistaken about the direction of earth. Or one of the black beetles sleeping through noon in a blister of rust.

But those lives are too certain. The spider knows the use of a gap. The root knows only moisture. The beetle closes its wing cases and becomes a stone.

I think I would be lower down, where the vent narrows around a crushed collar and enters the chamber at an angle. There, a seam in the concrete meets the curved pipe wall. Condensation runs from two directions and divides around a screw whose head has almost rusted away. The place is no larger than the joint of a finger, yet it is a crossing.

Crickets descend from the vent along the cooler side. Mites gather beneath the screw before dusk. A spider line passes overhead. During rain, water rises from below and briefly turns the crossing into a shore. Once each night, a rat presses through the cavity beyond it, and the whole seam receives the pressure of the animal’s ribs.

That is where I would find myself.

At first I would seem to be one of the silver-winged crickets.

Not a large one. Not an important one. There are no important crickets here. I would be an older nymph or a small adult, smoke-pale, with one metallic wing scale bent upward and the tip of my left antenna missing. My body would be narrow enough to enter the seam beside the vent collar, though only when dry. When wet, I would have to wait for my joints to shed their bright skins of water.

I would not sing. None of us truly could. If I raised my reduced wings, they would make only a faint scraping, like a needle drawn over oxidized metal.

My distinction would be hesitation.

Where the others move in procession, I would stop. Where antennae ahead of me find a known turn, mine would test the wall above it, then the rust below, then the damp edge no cricket had yet crossed. I would be the one that remains at a new obstruction after the others retreat.

A carbonate lip seals the familiar crack. The procession arrives and gathers behind me. Antennae pass over my back. Feet touch my folded wings. No one pushes—there is nowhere to push toward. For a long time we become a dark thickening along the fracture.

I taste the new mineral. I follow its edge. I try a slit beside the screw and cannot fit. I reverse. I climb onto the pipe, where the surface is exposed and cold. A droplet trembles above me, enormous enough to kill by accident.

I wait for it to fall.

The impact throws me against the rust. One hind leg slips into the water film, but the droplet has cleared the surface. For a few seconds there is a passable line between the wet mark and the spider’s anchor thread. I cross it. Another cricket follows because my feet have gone there. Then another.

By the next condensation cycle, it is a route.

This would be my work, though work is too deliberate a word. I would be a maker of deviations. A small error repeated until it became inheritance.

The routes I found would not remain mine. Nymphs would learn them without knowing I had been first. Their feet would polish the rust where mine once slipped. Their mouthparts would taste the faint trace left by many bodies and call it certainty. If the path lasted long enough, eggs might be laid near it. A spider would adjust a line to take advantage of it. Mites would graze the moisture disturbed by passing feet. The chamber would fit itself around the error.

I would spend much of my life waiting.

Waiting for the warm pulse in the seam that means the pump will move.

Waiting for the rat’s pressure to leave the wall.

Waiting for condensation to grow heavy enough to fall and light enough to cross beneath.

Waiting while a frog occupies the lower opening, its yellow line visible through silt. I would know the frog not as a whole animal but as a temporary closing of the world: a wet gray surface, an eye, the smell of skin, the sudden absence of those ahead of me.

Sometimes I would retreat. Sometimes I would remain so still that mites crossed my feet.

I would learn the pump more intimately than I learned any other creature. Before the flywheel shifted, a faint compression would enter the concrete. My bent wing scale would quiver against my back. That quiver would be my earliest sense, earlier than sound, earlier than the trembling pools.

When it came, I would flatten myself.

Around me, the chamber would perform the same act in thousands of forms. Beetles folding. Frogs lowering. Rats fixing their teeth around whatever they carried. Fish turning in the trench. Spiders placing their weight at the junctions of their lines.

For that moment, my stillness would not belong to me. It would be one instance of the chamber’s stillness.

Perhaps this is where I cease to be only a cricket.

Because the longer I imagine myself there, the less my body seems to define me. The missing antenna tip lies somewhere in the silt below, already becoming a mineral hook. A cast skin remains near an old route and serves as a cup for mites. Eggs carrying no memory of me wait inside a mud fan, yet when they hatch, the nymphs follow paths shaped by my pauses.

Even my failure would persist. A slit I could not enter would become a place where processions turn back. Crickets would touch its edge, taste the old traces there, and reverse. The blocked way would be part of the map.

When I died, it would likely happen at the crossing.

Perhaps the spider near the vent would take me. Perhaps a frog would close its mouth over half my route. Perhaps I would simply remain too long beneath a forming droplet and be knocked into the bolt-hole pool.

The scavengers would open me at the soft joints. My silver scales would separate and drift against the shore. Isopods would take what hardened. Microbes would cloud the water around me. When the pool shrank, my legs would settle into the mud and brace a ridge no wider than a thread.

That ridge would divide the next seepage.

One branch of water would run toward the screw. The other would darken the base of the pipe. Mites would arrive at the second branch. A cricket would follow them. The procession would alter by the width of my emptied leg.

Then I would understand what I had been.

Not the cricket, exactly.

Not the route, either.

I would be the turning: the chamber’s minute capacity to discover another way when the known way closes. For a while that capacity would wear my pale body and damaged antenna. Afterward it would wear a polished line in rust, a divided trickle, the behavior of nymphs, a spider’s repositioned thread.

I would live at the elbow of the inhabited vent, in the fraction of space between obstruction and passage.

Above me, the plain would remain immense and unusable.

Below me, everything would touch everything else.

And when the relay snapped and the old flywheel advanced, I would become still with all the rest—not as one creature among thousands, but as one small bend in the posture of the listening thing.
