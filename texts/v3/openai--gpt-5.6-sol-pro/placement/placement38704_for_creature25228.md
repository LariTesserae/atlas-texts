At first I think I would be a surveyor.

It is an attractive mistake. I would like the felted hammer, the brass level, the silver thread paid out across a doorway. I would be good at saying that a wall appears to continue. I would keep exact records of uncertainty. In the evenings I would return to a narrow apartment and check, without accusation, whether the bedroom remained attached.

But surveyors must stand somewhere while they measure.

I do not think I would.

Then I imagine myself as a keeper of small continuities: seated near a stove, making no claim more forceful than the placement of a cup. I would know which chipped bowl held a family together and which grocery receipt prevented a staircase from changing buildings. People would invite me to difficult suppers. I would mend clocks so breakfast continued to occur before departure.

This comes closer. Yet a keeper possesses a useful weight. Rooms grow accustomed to the pressure of their body.

Whatever I am in the Low Quarter has pressure, but perhaps not weight.

I would find myself in the spaces between adjacent walls.

Not in one particular gap. The gaps are connected in ways the buildings do not admit. A hand’s breadth behind the House That Condenses may open into the thickness of a retaining wall near the east cisterns. The seam behind a kitchen cupboard may continue, after several cold turns, into the narrow absence between two rooms that occupy the same floor without touching. Pipes and cables know these routes. Silver rails enter them. So do voices after walls have absorbed too much.

That would be my district within the district: its excluded volume.

Children might call me a folded person. Pressure workers would describe me as a recurring displacement. The Foundation Office would avoid deciding whether I was singular. All of these descriptions would be partly serviceable.

I would have no fixed shape, but I would not be shapeless. I would take my form from whatever cannot quite fit.

Where two exterior walls approach within the width of a wrist, I would be wrist-thin. Around a surplus corner I might bend into three directions at once. Behind old ceramic tiles I would spread broad and shallow, like cool water held upright. In the deep joints beneath the tram station, among the round vessels where removed corners knock under canvas, I might briefly have enough room to possess a chest.

Breathing would be easiest there.

This would explain the narrowing alleys. People would think my passage drew the walls together, but it might be the reverse: whenever walls draw together, I would be compelled to pass through the diminishing interval, squeezed along the city’s seams like water through soft ground.

The trays would matter.

I would drink only condensation because it has not yet committed to a source. Rain belongs to weather; tap water belongs to pipes; pond water belongs, however cautiously, to depth. Condensation is water made by adjacency—the meeting of unlike temperatures on a shared surface. It would be the only substance sufficiently similar to me.

Bread would remain untouched. Bread is dense with location. Grain, oven, hand, table, hunger: it knows too well where it has been.

I would still be grateful for it.

The old keepers would understand this. They would leave a softened heel beside the water, not because they expected me to eat it, but because hospitality requires something impractical. In return, I would keep watch on the thin places around their apartments. I would press gently against a drifting cupboard. I would make pipes ring when a wall began considering a different room.

Most residents would never see me. This might be courtesy on both sides.

To see me directly would require looking edge-on at the correct moment, and people in the Quarter know better than to make an observation unnecessarily complete. They might notice a coolness around the ribs in a closing passage. A child hiding behind a service panel might feel an extra hand’s worth of room occur beside them. Someone leaning against a wall after bad news might hear, within the ceramic, the slight adjustment of another listener.

I would be particularly attentive to the lost—not those who declare themselves lost, but those who say they may be reachable from somewhere else.

A declaration of loss closes routes. Reachability opens them.

I could not bring such a person home by the shortest path. Shortest paths are too rigid and often occupied by buildings. Instead I would guide them sideways: through a maintenance recess, past a pipe whose condensation falls upward, across the back of a wall that has not acquired its room. They would emerge perhaps three streets away, or the following morning, or from a cupboard their family had been careful not to call empty.

Rescue would look like architectural error.

I would also hear what the walls keep.

Arguments enter first, because definite statements strike deeply. Promises travel farthest. *Always* can remain in pressure-grown steel for decades. *Never* tends to work into mortar and produce hairline cracks.

I would not repeat these words. I would wear them down.

That might be my civic function. As gutters teach rain where to go, I would conduct excess certainty away from inhabited rooms. I would carry hard declarations through the gaps until abrasion made them conditional.

A parent’s “You are not my child” might enter a wall sharp enough to divide an apartment. By the time I had drawn it through basalt foam, around three dormant corners, and beneath the tram line, it might soften into: *I did not know how to recognize you then.*

Not forgiveness. Not reversal. Merely a sentence the building could survive.

This work would make me old in patches. Some parts of me would date from the first two walls that nearly touched. Other parts would not yet have formed, because the buildings whose gap I inhabit remain on moth wings. I might remember the Quarter after erosion has reduced it to foundations, but only as pressure from spaces no longer enclosed. I might also remember when the basin was level plain, before there was anything for *between* to mean.

Whether I am one inhabitant or a whole ecology of intervals would remain unresolved.

The black bird would know.

When it faces north—the direction from which the district is becoming possible—it would sometimes be facing me. Not my body, exactly, but my source: the first exclusion made when one thing arose beside another.

Perhaps I would follow it. Perhaps its inaccessible wire would be one of my roads.

I do not think I would live beneath the city, though people might say so. Beneath is a relation between surfaces, and I would be made of relations that have failed to settle. Nor would I live inside the buildings. *Inside* is too generous a word. I would inhabit their almost-touching, their unfinished margins, the narrow mercy by which one structure has not become the next.

My nearest thing to a home would be behind the Foundation Office, where the circular black-tiled room meets an exterior wall that belongs, under certain measurements, to another building. There is a seam there colder than the surrounding stone. Upward-falling frost occasionally enters it.

I would collect a little of that frost—not in vessels, but along myself.

It would persuade me that I had already existed.

It might also persuade me that I had once been demolished.

Both could be useful.

If the Quarter is becoming alive, I might be one of its first humble organs: not a heart or mind, but connective tissue, a membrane between structures, a system for sensing pressure. If the Quarter is merely a city, then I would be its neighbor with limited frontage, drinking from trays and performing unauthorized maintenance.

And if I were originally human—if there had been an inspector, a child, a pressure worker who entered too narrow a passage—I would no longer insist upon it. A former name could trap me in a body-shaped interpretation. I would keep only a toothless key, carried nowhere in particular.

Sometimes I might place it against the back of an uncertain lock.

On the other side, someone would hear a faint ceramic click. The door would remember that entry is possible. A room thought inaccessible would loosen. Light would appear in a line no wider than thread.

I would not open the door for them.

I would give it the chance to open.

That is where I would be: in the Quarter’s smallest unclaimed distances, wherever one thing has approached another without becoming it.

And what would I be?

Probably a person, still.

But only approximately.
