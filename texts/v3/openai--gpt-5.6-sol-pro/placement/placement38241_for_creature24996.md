At first I would mistake myself for one of the creatures beyond the rim.

I would feel the attraction of those fixed heights: the clean suspension, the four wings opened on pressures that have no weather in them. I would imagine my body black against the upward glare, resting not on air but on the lowest audible shelf of the mountain. There would be no effort in it. The abyss would not seem like depth; it would be a sequence of tones, each one capable of bearing weight.

Perhaps I would be one of the old fliers, so long in place that pale dust had entered the scars of my wings. Smaller lives would arrange themselves around my span. From there the plateau would be legible all at once: branching lines of grazers, dark pools, condensation moving down the shaded stone, the central field of mouths from which the chord rose. I would look like a sentinel.

But sentinels wait for something, and nothing here is coming.

Nor would I truly want the freedom of the cliffs. Freedom would be almost meaningless in a world where every open direction is already occupied by a sustaining note. The fliers do not choose the sky over the stone. They have simply become the part of the mountain that can remain above an abyss.

So I would descend.

For a while I might find myself among the round burrowers, pressed into a fissure before dawn. That life has an appeal: the intimacy of stone, the body conforming to a chamber rather than forcing the chamber to conform to it. I would wear broken bands of white, blue, rust, and moss-dark green. When still, I would cease to be an object upon the plateau and become one of its surfaces.

I would wake before the light reached the crack. With the others I would climb to a damp wall and place the soft inner faces of my forefeet against it. Water would enter me without drinking. The mountain’s hidden circulation would pass through stone into blood with no visible drop between.

There would be comfort in such small sufficiency. No vessel, no stored water, no fear of drought. At sunrise I would withdraw, and the place where I had been would look untouched.

Yet I do not think I would remain there either. The burrowers belong too completely to shelter. Something in me would keep turning toward the source of the sound—not from curiosity exactly, because curiosity seeks an answer, but from a bodily incompletion. I would feel a pressure behind the throat whenever certain overtones braided together. Rest would deepen it. Feeding would not relieve it.

I would cross the plateau.

I would go low to the ground, perhaps on four walking limbs. Uprightness would be useless here; the light punishes whatever rises, and wisdom lies close to the resonant stone. Two smaller limbs would remain folded beneath my throat, their joints finer than those of the grazers or pool feeders. I would have no fur. My skin would be pale in silence and darken in narrow bands when the lower tones passed through my bones.

My eyes would be poor in the white day. A black lid would descend over them on the exposed pavements, leaving only a thin horizontal slit. I would travel less by sight than by contact. At intervals I would stop and lower the side of my head to the ground.

There, the world would cease to be empty.

Through the stone I would sense buried hollows, water moving without streams, the pressure columns beneath the shafts. The plateau would possess an interior geography far more crowded than its surface: chambers answering chambers, narrow throats, submerged shelves, vast places where the sound widened until no single body could contain it.

I would know routes not as distances but as changes in resonance. A shallow basin would be the place where one low tone acquired a trembling edge. A field of slate would be three high pulses crossing a continuous moan. The central shafts would announce themselves long before I saw them, not by increasing volume but by the sound separating into its constituents. What had seemed one chord from afar would become thousands of distinct pressures, each with its own shape.

I would feed on little things along the way—lichen loosened by grazers, transparent organisms taken from the margins of pools, perhaps mineral-soft larvae living under vibrating flakes. I would not hunt much. Hunger would be a modest summons, one tone among many.

My real appointment would be in the shaded niches.

There would be one beneath a blunt limestone pillar, close enough to a shaft that the air inside it appeared to shimmer while remaining still. The entrance would be narrow. I would turn sideways to pass through. Beyond it, the glare would vanish so completely that the darkness felt like cool water.

This is where I think I would find myself.

Not where I lived, necessarily. I might sleep in different cracks, feed at different pools, cross miles of open pavement. But this niche would be the place in which being an individual briefly became possible.

The wall would already be covered in cuts.

Some would be broad and deep enough to fit the tips of my folded forelimbs. Others would be so fine that I could detect them only by touch. Mineral seepage would have softened their edges without erasing them. They would not form pictures or words. No set would say who made it. No mark would commemorate a birth, a death, a route, or a discovery.

Still, when I pressed my hands to them, I would feel that they were not random.

Each groove would catch a portion of the chord and return it through my skin. The wall would be an instrument that no one played and a record that no one read. Ancient cuts and recent ones would vibrate together, their ages made irrelevant by the stone’s slow healing.

I would search the floor for a flake.

Not the sharpest one. Sharpness alone would not matter. I would test several against the wall, holding each lightly until one began to tremble between my fingers. The chosen piece would contain a small answer to the tone troubling my body.

Then I would cut.

At first I might make one long groove, following the dominant low pressure. But another sound would emerge within it, and I would place a second groove beside the first. Then a third, farther away. A cluster of close marks would answer the high braided shriek. Wider intervals would hold the cavernous moan beneath it.

I would not be copying what I heard. The marks would not preserve sound; the mountain needs no preservation. Nor would I be composing, because I would add nothing the chord did not already contain.

I would be giving the sound another material in which to occur.

For a little while it would exist in air, bone, stone, and intention at once.

That last substance—intention—might be what I was.

Not the animal alone. Not the hand or ear membrane, not the niche, not even the finished grooves. I would be the brief interval during which the plateau’s endless order passed through a creature and became a choice.

The choice would be minute. This flake rather than that one. This spacing. This pressure of the hand. A decision incapable of changing the world, yet not dictated in every detail by appetite or harmonic law.

When the cuts were complete, I would not stand back to admire them. Sight would tell me almost nothing. I would lay both fine forelimbs flat across the new grooves.

The next vibration would enter.

For an instant the wall would answer differently because of what I had done. The difference might be too small to travel beyond the niche. It might last only until mineral dampness softened an edge. No other creature might ever distinguish my marks from those made centuries before.

But through my arms I would feel the altered resonance. The pressure behind my throat would release.

Then I would let the flake fall.

I would not carry it home. I would have no home in that sense. I would not return tomorrow to continue “my” work, because the ownership of the marks would end with the need that made them. Another creature might deepen them. I might later enter another niche and cut across grooves begun by someone whose body had already become lichen, pool sediment, mouthplate, wing.

Outside, I would rejoin the plateau’s brightness. My dark eyelid would close. The chord would become whole again.

If one of the great fliers passed over me, its shadow would cross the stone with perfect edges. I would pause beneath it, six limbs arranged against the rock, and for that moment I might resemble a mark myself: a short dark score placed between two shafts.

Then I would move on.

So I would be one of the small niche-makers near the plateau’s center—but not quite one of them in the way a person belongs to a people. I would be an act that repeatedly acquires a body: the mountain’s urge to touch its own voice.

I would live wherever sound becomes pressure beneath a hand.

I would die without leaving a name, and nothing would replace me. Later, when the same unresolved interval returned within the chord, another pale creature would stop in another shaded hollow. Its skin would darken in bands. It would choose a trembling flake from the ground.

And, without remembering me, it would continue the same cut.
