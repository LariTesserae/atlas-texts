At first I think I would be something hidden: a pale fungal cord in the compressed leaf-mat, threading between fern roots and basalt grit. That seems the safest answer. I would live below the violence of the leaves, feeding slowly on what the weather had killed, joining one root to another where there was scarcely enough soil to deserve the name.

But there is no real below here. The mat can peel away. A warm fissure can open beneath it. Rain can turn shelter into ballast and pull the whole garden down the wall.

Then I imagine myself as moss, compact and dark, holding the cloud after the cloud has gone. I would live on the upper face of a basalt block, just above the depth where carbon dioxide settles during still weather. Water would arrive not as a season but as touch: fog beading at my tips, condensate traveling through me, sudden rain swelling my dead-looking body until grit lifted in my folds. I would make a small reservoir without knowing water as a separate thing.

Yet even moss is too singular a name for what I would be.

I would find myself midway between floor and rim, on a broken shelf beneath an overhang where steam reaches only after cooling. Not the safest place—the notch has no safe place—but a repeatedly habitable one. A mineral seam would cross the basalt there, pale as an old scar. Liverwort would lacquer its shaded edge. Moss would deepen along the shelf’s lip. Fern roots would enter from above, while fungal threads worked through the black felt of leaves behind them. A bent shrub stem, flayed smooth by another stem, would touch down during storms and slowly root.

That whole junction would be my body.

I would have no boundary that remained correct for long. Part of me would be photosynthetic film on the stone, part moss reservoir, part fungal exchange, part the buried node of a branch that had ceased to belong to the shrub above. Microbes in the seep would be my first response to heat. Ferns would be my vanes, showing the direction of the air. The yellowing of my lowest leaves would tell me where unbreathable gas had risen in the night.

I would not perceive the notch by sight. I would know it as pressure and chemistry.

A storm would announce itself as cooling along the rimward tissues, then as a rapid increase in weight. Sulfur would arrive as injury before it became smell. A subterranean release would pass through the rock as compression, and every wet filament in me would tighten against its hold. The turbine cry of the wind would be translated into strain where roots crossed cracks. Thunder and geological booming would remain indistinguishable because, to me, they would be the same fact: the substrate had begun to move.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be distributed and slow until damage made them sudden.

A falling shard might cut the moss shelf in half. One half would remain against the wall; the other would drop with water, grit, fern crowns, and the rooted shrub stem. For an animal, that would be mutilation or death. For me, it would also be departure. The fallen half might lodge twenty meters below and dam a newborn channel. Water would collect behind it. Microbial films would wake along its drowned edge. The fern might rot, or it might raise a new crozier. The shrub stem might root again.

I would become two places without deciding to.

Neither would be the original. Neither would be a descendant in the clean sense. Memory here would not be stored as recollection but as tolerance: cells that close under drought, filaments that wait in pores, roots that retreat from heat, fragments able to begin after being torn. What survived would remember on behalf of what did not.

I would have no predators and therefore no concept of pursuit. Nothing would watch me, court me, browse me, carry me, or choose me. My world would contain violence without malice and fertility without invitation. There would be no faces. The nearest thing to another creature’s approach would be the advance of heat through stone or the arrival of a neighboring root into the mineral seam.

Nor would I be lonely. Loneliness requires an edge around the self.

In the brief clearing after a storm, when the chamber became visible from end to end, I would not see the black walls smoking or the newborn streams crossing the floor. I would participate in that visibility. My wet surfaces would supply one small portion of the green shine. Water held in my moss would continue draining after the falls had vanished. Spores from my overturned ferns would already be adhering to fresh fractures. Fungal threads in my buried layers would begin entering the debris newly delivered by the flood.

If I had a purpose, it would not be to conquer the notch. Nothing conquers a place that is still being made.

I would be its provisional skin: the dark-green seam laid over raw basalt, repeatedly scalded, buried, divided, and resumed. I would live exactly where the stone had not yet decided whether to be wall, floor, channel, or rubble.

And when the shelf finally broke away entirely, there would be no final instant in which I ceased. Some part of me would fall. Some would remain as stain. Some would pass upward as spores. Some would wait in a damp pore for the cloud to press down again.

I would be found wherever black rock had just become capable of holding green.
