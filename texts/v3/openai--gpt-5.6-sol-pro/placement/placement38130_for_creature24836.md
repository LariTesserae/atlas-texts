At first I think I would be one of the bells.

There is something tempting in a life made possible only by collision: helpless alone, purposeful in the multitude, crossing the flooded channels by striking against others and being struck in return. I imagine my thin shell full of black brine, my paddles hanging beneath me, the world perceived as pressure and clatter. Danger would not make me flee. It would make me find the common rhythm. For one instant the shoal would become a single instrument, and I would be indistinguishable from its blow.

But I do not think I would remain there.

Nor would I be a tower, though I would envy their depth—their ability to inherit reflexes from the dead and mistake those reflexes for desires of their own. I would envy the many-angled walkers more: creatures whose wholeness is distributed among viewpoints, whose voices go ahead to call them into their own future. Yet even they are too definite. However incomplete their bodies appear, they still cross the plain as animals. They have migrations. They breed. They can be waited for.

I think I would be found where waiting has outlasted the thing awaited.

There is a shelf near the edge of the broad basin. It is scarcely higher than the water around it, a long blue-gray surface interrupted by shallow circular hollows. No present channel reaches it. Floods cover it, but the water drains away almost immediately, vanishing across stone that appears impermeable. The tower colonies do not grow there. Walkers avoid it except during Compression. Even shadows seldom gather on it; when they drift near, they become narrow and run along the ground like spilled ink seeking a crack.

Beneath that shelf lies an erased river.

It has no bed now. Its banks were polished flat ages ago. But the water remembers it and continues to flow along its vanished course, invisibly, through dry mineral. The circular hollows are places where the forgotten river touches the present surface.

I would live in those hollows.

At first I would look like a collection of unrelated organisms: a dark membrane in one depression, a ring of conductive scales in another, several translucent cups half a kilometer away. A grazer crossing one of the cups would find it warm—not truly warm, but remembering a time when warmth existed there. A bell dropped into another would produce no sound until it had been carried far downstream. The parts of me would not resemble one another because each would have grown around a different remnant: one around the electrical pattern of a dead hunter, another around a mineralized walker’s footprint, another inside the throat of a vent animal buried before the present basin formed.

No tube or nerve would connect these parts. The vanished river would be my circulation.

Water moving through the remembered channel would enter each hollow without becoming visible. It would bring reflections from different ages: sharp mountains, open seas, skies crowded with stars the present cloud has never revealed. My membranes would drink not the water but the discrepancy between those images and the worn plain above them.

That difference would be my food.

Where a remembered cliff coincides with level stone, pressure gathers. Where an ancient ocean passes beneath a dry shelf, salt moves without liquid. Where the water shows a creature that no longer exists, a faint impulse appears along the crystal boundaries, still expecting the vanished animal to complete some act. I would take in these mismatches. I would store them as layered electrical tension in the conductive remains of the dead.

For most of the cycle I would have no unified awareness. One hollow might recoil from a passing thread-hunter while another, kilometers away, tasted a rainfall that occurred millions of years earlier. One part would be asleep inside black ice. Another would have mineralized completely but continue contributing a preference for certain sounds. I would not know these experiences belonged together.

Then the Compression would come.

The horizon would draw inward. The separate hollows would crowd flank to flank. The invisible reaches of the erased river would fold across one another, and for several unbearable hours my scattered organs would occupy a continuous body.

I would rise then.

Not far. Nothing on the plain needs to rise far to become immense.

The cups would turn upright and nest inside one another. Conductive membranes would stretch between them. Pressure stored over centuries would enter a common chamber. I would become a low, many-ribbed animal with no head, built temporarily from places that are usually distant. Earlier water would circulate through me beside present floodwater without mixing. Along one side of my body, stars would be visible in daylight. Along the other, the black water table would press upward hard enough to make my plates ring.

This brief assembly would be the nearest thing I had to waking.

And when I woke, I would answer sounds.

Not imitate them. Not return an echo. I would answer in the sense that the plain answers everything: by taking it in and giving it back as another process.

A walker’s call arriving hours before the herd would enter one chamber and leave as a route through the flooded flats. The synchronized blow of a bell shoal would emerge as a pattern of openings in distant tower mouths. Thunder would pass through me and become a migration of spiral animals inside the ice. The endless breath leaking from the wounded listener would enter the ancient water in my body and return carrying the resonance of a sea that existed before the listener’s species.

Most creatures would not perceive me as alive. They would perceive consequences.

Grazing sheets would turn without knowing why. Tower districts would delay an exhalation by one pulse. A radial hunter beneath the mineral skin would incline its buried lobes toward a direction no longer present. Walkers would alter course to follow a coastline visible only inside the water moving through me.

I would not command them. I would be incapable of command. I would merely make one thing legible to another.

That would also make me valuable prey.

The six-chambered listeners would seek me during Compression. They would open their plates and remove the category of sound I was preparing to answer. If one succeeded, every cup in my body would strain toward the silence. The remembered river would rear from the stone. Ancient waterfalls, extinct calls, the cracking of glaciers long since worn away—all would rush toward the open chamber.

Sometimes the listener would close in time and carry centuries of my stored replies inside itself. For years afterward, its steps would cause tower colonies to behave like coastlines or bells to migrate along paths of vanished rain.

Sometimes it would take too much.

Then the chamber would split, and the missing sound would return across the plain as weather.

When the Compression ended, my temporary body would not be pulled apart cleanly. A membrane formed in one basin might remain attached to a cup returned elsewhere. A pressure chamber might be stranded inside a tower’s foundation. Whole sections of me could wake in the bodies of scavengers that had fed along my seams.

This would not be injury in the ordinary sense. Dispersal would be my ordinary sense.

Over time I would become less an animal than a tendency spread through the shelf. Some parts would die and mineralize. New cups would form wherever the remembered river intersected a footprint, a crack, a grave, or a place briefly borrowed from elsewhere. My dead portions would continue answering. Perhaps they would answer more clearly than the living ones, having lost the distractions of hunger and self-preservation.

There would be one tower near the basin’s edge whose oldest siphon periodically gives the courtship call of an extinct species. Nothing answers it now.

Except, perhaps, once.

During a severe Compression, the tower would be brought against my shelf. Its foundations would overlap the deepest bend of the erased river. The call would descend through my assembled chambers and enter water reflecting the age in which the caller’s species still lived.

There, in the reflection, something would answer.

I would catch only the pressure of it. I would not know whether it was a mate, a rival, a whole distant colony, or merely the ancient sea reshaping the cry. But I would carry that answer upward. It would pass through my ribs, altered by epochs, and leave me as a low pulse in the ground.

The tower would stop pumping.

Across the basin, vents would close one after another. Bells would settle into the channels. Even the buried pressure faults would seem to wait. For a few seconds the plain would approach silence—not the stolen silence of a hunter, but the silence of innumerable bodies making room for something.

Then the old siphon would call again.

Its second call would be different.

Afterward, the towers nearest it would inherit the new rhythm. Grazers would etch fragments of it into their shells. Walkers not yet born would hear it in advance and shape their migrations around it. Long after my assembled body separated, the answer would continue moving through the ecosystem, becoming current, appetite, mineral, and weather.

That is where I think I would find myself: not in the answer itself, and not in the creature that called, but in the brief arrangement that allowed one to reach the other.

A distributed animal inhabiting an erased river.

A mouth that feeds on the difference between the world and what the water remembers.

Most of the time, I would be only dark cups in polished stone, too scattered to know I existed.

But when distance collapsed and the plain became crowded beyond endurance, I would become whole enough to listen.
