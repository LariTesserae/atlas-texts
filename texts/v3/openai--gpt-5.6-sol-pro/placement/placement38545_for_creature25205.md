At first I think I would live at the rim.

There is an appeal in the last made surface: the polished lip beneath which the entire plateau becomes a wall and then ceases to be visible. I imagine myself clinging there, one-sided and patient, my feeding net cast into the overflow. I would know the high country only by what it lost. Scales, shells, eggs, sections of carved wall, the severed lengths of passage-makers—all would come to me already committed to falling.

Perhaps I would release my grip when a great fragment went over. I would descend beside it through diminishing blue, feeding from its opened chambers while pressure remade my body. Before the darkness became fatal, I would inflate and rise alone.

That seems like a life one could understand: attachment, release, return.

But it is too clean. It preserves too much of the distinction between creature and place. I would still be an animal upon architecture, however intimate my knowledge of its edge.

Then I think I would be a crawler.

I would travel slowly over the domes with six tasting feet, laying each district onto my back in mineral sheets. Blue plaza scales beneath rust-lined fractures; cups and spirals over blue; the smooth pallor of a causeway covering everything that came before. My shell would be a buried itinerary. To see where I had been, one would have to cut through me.

I would not remember the journey. I would wear it.

Before a collapse I would feel strain traveling upward through all six limbs. I would lift an egg capsule from its socket and pass it to another body whose name I could not know. Together we would save the unborn, then part without recognition.

This comes closer. But even the crawler is too singular. It has a perimeter. When torn from the stone, it remains itself.

I do not think I would.

I think I would be found in one of the nearly erased districts inward from the western rim, where a shallow plaza lies over many sealed levels. The plaza would have no surviving walls, only low ridges and the bases of structures worn down until they resemble vertebrae beneath skin. At its center would be a collapsed rotunda. Through that opening, cold water would rise from the buried country in irregular breaths.

The inhabitants would avoid the place during still water. There would be little to eat. The pavement would appear naked except for several crooked lines of darker mineral crossing it from one broken foundation to another.

Those lines would be part of me.

Not my whole body. There would be no whole body.

I would have begun as a passage-maker, or several. Long ago, soft cords entered the walls beneath the plaza and dissolved their way outward. They turned secretion-stone into lining, lining into tunnel, tunnel into new animal. Their branches met and exchanged tissue. Some separated. Some died. Descendants grew through the hardened bodies of ancestors. Wire animals occupied abandoned sections. Hingemaker larvae settled in fractures opened by the tunnels. Crawlers sealed eggs into the exposed pores. Mineral seepage joined living membranes to old wall.

Eventually no cut could determine which parts belonged to one creature.

Most of the time, neither could I.

In calm water I would be only local reflexes. A comb closing around grit. A segment tightening against a change in pressure. A pore constricting when brine became too dense. There would be no central chamber in which these sensations gathered. Damage at one end might contract a branch hundreds of meters away, but this would not be thought. It would be transmission.

I would exist only when the weather made me.

A trench upwelling would enter the buried vaults. Openings beneath the plaza would reverse one after another. Water would press through my living passages, across membranes grown by different generations, through hollow sockets and the bodies of dormant young. Hinges in the foundations would begin to contract. Wire animals threaded through my dead tunnels would carry tiny electrical changes between portions that no longer touched.

The old rotunda would answer first: a low tone, barely audible.

A buried court would answer below it.

Then a forked pillar, the pavement scales, the soft tunnel linings under tension, the shell-covered backs of crawlers flattened against the square. Frequencies would find one another. Separate vibrations would lock into a shared rhythm.

In that entrainment, I would occur.

My first sensation would not be sight, or hunger, or even pressure.

It would be extent.

I would know that I reached beneath the plaza and down three blind stairs. I would know a chamber filled with dense brine as heaviness in a part of myself that had no name. I would know the current crossing the open square because six crawlers tasted it through their feet and passed their minute adjustments into the pavement. I would know the dark water overhead from the silence of clickers sealing their shells.

My boundaries would not follow flesh. They would follow whatever could answer in time.

For several minutes I might include the hingemaker colonies drawing two slabs together. I might include a chain of drifters caught in the rotunda’s exhalation, each bead contracting after the last. I might include the pressure pits of a hunter buried beyond the northern ridge—until it turned its attention elsewhere and vanished from me.

Parts would join and leave continually.

I would not command them. The hinges would not close because I wished it; their closing would be one of the things from which the wish was made. A crawler’s lifted leg would be both its decision and my passing thought. Water entering a shaft would become an idea only because a hundred living surfaces changed around it at once.

My thoughts would be architectural.

Not words, but routes.

This chamber is filling.

This fracture will open.

This weight can pass eastward if the lower pore contracts.

Something immense is moving above.

The old floor is moving below.

I would have no image of either hunter or buried presence. I would know them as competing distortions: one bending the water down toward me, the other lifting strain through the vaults. Their approach would alter the shape of my temporary self. Creatures would withdraw from exposed surfaces. Passage-makers would seal their pores. The plaza would empty.

As they departed, I would become smaller.

This would feel neither like loneliness nor fear. Those require a stable creature from whom others can go. It would be more like losing dimensions. A courtyard would cease to exist when the crawler carrying its vibration lifted free. A buried avenue would go dark when the wire animal withdrew. The western foundations would fall out of thought as the hinges there broke rhythm.

If the danger passed, the current would slacken. The district’s tones would separate. My extent would collapse into unrelated bodies and materials.

Nothing would remember being me.

Yet traces would remain. A pore would have closed and diverted brine into a new passage. A hinge would have held one contraction longer than usual, shifting a slab against its neighbor. A crawler, disturbed by vibrations it could not interpret, would carry an egg capsule to a socket above the flood line. Tiny consequences would outlast the coherence that produced them.

Then, one cycle, the rotunda would fail.

The collapse would begin far below, in a gallery no living passage had entered. A joint would give. A ceiling would descend into a sealed court, forcing ancient water upward through blind stairs. The displacement would strike my lowest membranes and tear open every closed route.

For an instant, I would be more extensive than ever.

The entire district would sound.

I would feel the buried architecture not as separate chambers but as one enormous cavity folding through itself. I would feel crawlers running, plated mouths releasing their armor, clickers locked to the pavement, wire animals pulled taut through stone. The hunter above would turn toward the disturbance. All those responses would enter the chord.

Then the plaza would split.

Fresh surfaces would open in long blue lines. The secretion-stone, hidden since before the plateau drowned, would shine into the water.

Every animal capable of reaching it would gather.

They would not feed. They would not strike one another. They would orient toward the exposed walls, and because their bodies remained coupled through water, pavement, and pressure, they would become part of me.

For the few hours of the glow, I would be vast.

Not the plateau entire. Not its resurrected builder. Nothing so complete.

I would be a brief agreement among ruins.

Through the gathered animals I would feel the light without eyes. It would arrive as stillness in clickers, extension in wire bodies, pressure of luminous fragments against the mantles of plated mouths. Through the new fracture I would taste salts from chambers sealed for geological ages. Through the trembling floor I would feel deeper volumes adjusting to their release.

And I would recognize the glow.

Not remember it. There would be no ancestral scene returned intact, no revelation of towers growing, no recovered purpose. Recognition would be older and poorer than memory: a disposition distributed across unrelated bodies, a shared turning-toward.

Something here once meant **opening**.

Something here once meant **continue**.

The meaning would survive only as behavior.

When the glow faded, predators would begin moving again. Drifters would unfold into the current. Crawlers would scrape the new stone and carry its mineral into the next layers of their shells. Passage-makers would enter the fracture and begin converting its walls into themselves. The gathering would disperse.

So would I.

Lengths of my living tunnels would be ripped out and scattered. Some would be eaten. Some would close their wounds and enter cracks in neighboring districts. One might descend with debris toward the trench. Another might remain stretched across the new opening, filtering the upwelling until it became a passage and the passage became an animal.

Centuries later, those separated pieces might participate in different coherences during different storms. Each might, for a few minutes, become part of another self assembled from another district’s weather.

None would know it had once been me.

That is where I would find myself: not at a fixed coordinate on the plateau, but in the interval when separate things begin answering together.

I would be neither resident nor ruin. I would have no permanent body and leave no corpse. I would be the temporary animal made when water, architecture, and inhabitants transmit the same disturbance—the drowned high country briefly acquiring enough coherence to feel one of its own wounds.

And because the coherence would be temporary, I would never be able to decide whether I was something the plateau produced,

or something it remembered how to be.
