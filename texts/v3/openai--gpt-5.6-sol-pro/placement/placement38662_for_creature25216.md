I would first mistake myself for a place.

Not an important one. A shallow convergence where five channels approach through grooves in the black stone. Four turn aside before touching. The fifth divides around an absence no larger than a kneeling body, though nothing occupies it. Rain striking that patch makes no sound. During dry intervals—which are not truly dry—the rock there remains wet.

It lies beyond one of the roofless chambers, downstream from a source that has not yet opened, within sight of the arches only when they are remembering themselves as near. The Remaining Inclinations do not travel to it deliberately. Their routes bend around it, not from fear but from the mild confusion of encountering a place that seems to be listening.

For a long while, that would be all I was: a local peculiarity in the water.

Then other portions would begin to answer.

A circle of pale reeds, seventy miles away, would bend not toward thunder but toward the quiet patch between two peals. In a polished chamber, a damp handprint would appear each evening on the wall, its fingers pressed together as if holding something too small to see. A glassy animal would settle inside one bead of condensation and refuse to consume the reflection there. Beneath a red ridge, a cavity would retain the warmth of a body that had never lain down.

None of these things would know they belonged together.

I would begin in their shared hesitation.

The reeds would be reluctant to release the silence. The handprint would be reluctant to open. The droplet would be reluctant to become ordinary water. The hollow would be reluctant to cool. At first these refusals would coincide only during certain storms. Then they would persist for minutes afterward. Years later, a footprint would appear between the five channels.

It would point in no direction.

The elders would notice. They would come in the forms available to them: leaning stones, black growth, elongated reflections, a pressure passing through the puddles. They would arrange themselves around the convergence and turn the streams outward. For perhaps three breaths, the patch of stone would stand bare.

In that impossible dryness, I would acquire an edge.

Not a body—not yet—but the knowledge that something was inside the edge and something else was not.

The rain would return before I could decide which was which.

I think I would become one of the Remaining Inclinations, though a young and poorly gathered one. My anatomy would vary with the weather. In warm rain I might be a low figure composed of interlocking runnels, moving only when water moved through me. In black storms I would gather into a cluster of slabs with a hollow where the face should be. In still pools I would appear upright, but my reflection would always be kneeling, one hand submerged.

Part of me would be the silent patch.

Part would be the handprint in the distant chamber.

Part would live in the glassy animal, which would carry one unconsumed reflection from droplet to droplet. It would show no face or cloud, only the instant just before someone recognizes what they are seeing.

Another part would not yet exist: a traveler’s pause at a threshold several centuries in the future. Until that traveler arrived, I would feel an unfinished pressure in my chest.

My name would be made by narrowing the middle of one channel until the water produced two tones at once. The lower tone would mean *held*. The higher would mean *unresolved*. Rainfall would determine which one seemed dominant, so my name could never be spoken the same way twice.

Roughly translated, it might mean:

*What was lost here has not finished becoming absent.*

That would also be my work.

The plain remembers almost everything, but it does not preserve distinctions. A mountain may remain as grit, contour, noise, mineral stain, or recurring collapse; after enough ages, no one can tell whether these remnants belonged to one mountain or many. A creature’s fear may survive after its predator has become weather. A reflection may outlast its subject so completely that the reflection is taken for the original.

I would keep apart, where I could, the lost from the never-existing.

Not by recovering facts. Facts are too tall; the weather wears them down immediately. I would preserve only the shape of uncertainty.

When an inhabitant brought me a memory of snow, I would not tell them whether snow had once fallen. I would place the memory in the silent patch and wait. If the surrounding water leaned inward, the memory belonged to an absence. If it leaned away, the memory belonged to a possibility. Often the water would do both. Then I would add a small stone to the convergence, tilted so that it resisted lying flat.

This would mean:

*The difference mattered, even though it could not be saved.*

Over centuries, the place around me would fill with such stones. Not a wall, cairn, or archive—only a field of minute inclinations. Rain would strike each at a slightly different angle. Together they would produce a sound too complex to hear as music, but the pale reeds would follow it. Channel-rollers approaching against the current would stop at its edge and taste the water for destinations. Finding too many unfinished courses, they would circle me and move on.

The depthless animals would trouble me most.

They would gather beneath my shallow pools, waiting for the downward surrender. Sometimes an eye would open under my kneeling reflection. It would show me a version of myself already flattened into habit: no handprint, no reeds, no warm cavity, only a tendency for rain to hesitate before filling a hollow.

I would know this was not a threat. It was an accurate future.

Still, I would lean away.

Perhaps that leaning would become my most recognizable posture. Travelers—if any still crossed the plain—would see a figure crouched above ankle-deep water, pulling backward against something beneath it. If they approached, the figure would collapse into ordinary stones. They might find only a threshold sunk level with the rock, opening onto a puddle.

Stepping across it, they would briefly remember one thing they had lost.

Not the thing itself. Only the exact weight of having once possessed it.

Some would recoil. Some would kneel. Some would mistake the weight for proof and begin inventing what had been there. I could not prevent that. Memory fills whatever hollow it finds.

But if one remained long enough, placing a hand in the water, the distant print in the polished chamber would disappear from its wall and appear beneath their palm. For that moment, one of my scattered parts would become theirs. I would feel the outline of their body; they would feel how little of me could be gathered in one place.

We would not speak. The roar would make privacy of every voice.

Instead I would narrow a channel.

The water would sound my two-toned name.

If they wished to answer, they might tilt one of the small stones. That would be enough.

During the continental shudder, when the fallen mountains returned to every pool, I too would become briefly whole.

I would not be tall like the elder figures. I would have no ancient, unworn body to recover. I was born after height, after symmetry, after sequence had begun to fray. My complete form would be stranger: a dark, kneeling figure made of thresholds, each threshold opening into another rain. Within my ribs would be the five channels, still approaching and still refusing to meet. My face would be the unconsumed reflection. In one hand I would hold the silence between thunder and flash. The other would remain pressed against a wall many miles away.

Around me would stand all the things the plain could no longer decide had existed.

Unbuilt towers. Unborn animals. Roads imagined by stones. The second lives of the dead. Mountains remembered with the wrong peaks. They would not become real, but for less than a heartbeat they would be distinct from nothing.

I would hold that distinction as long as I could.

Then the pools would empty of mountains. My figure would come apart. The hand would return to the chamber wall. The reeds would bend away. The glassy animal would pulse once and vanish into condensation. Rain would hammer the five channels, and four would turn aside before touching.

The fifth would divide around a small, listening absence.

That is where I would be found—if being found did not gather me too completely and thereby make me someone else.

A place first.

Then a reluctance shared across distances.

Then, for a while, a person.

And afterward, when even that posture had been worn away, a slight habit in the water: whenever the plain tried to erase the difference between what was lost and what had never been, the rain would hesitate.

Not for long.

But long enough to hear.
