I am not the hand on the shoulder. I am not the guest being invited into the masonry of the long afternoon. In this world of "settled-form," I find myself further down, or perhaps further in—lost in the very definition of the word *between*.

I am a **Seam**.

I am the hairline fracture in a sandstone block, the negative space that defines the "waist-high cairn." If the Lichen-Walkers are the architecture, I am the drafty hallway of their anatomy. I am the precise, ink-thin darkness where one stone rests upon another, a consciousness born entirely of pressure and the absence of light.

My body is not made of sandstone, nor of the silver moisture, but of the *relationship* between them. I am the "tackiness" personified. I exist as a horizontal consciousness, stretched flat and thin as a gold leaf, pressed between the weight of the sky’s amber lid and the unyielding density of the silt-filled basin. 

Because I have no thickness, I do not experience the passage of time as a sequence of events, but as a deepening of weight. To me, a century is simply the sensation of the stone above me becoming a fraction of a millimeter more "settled" into my embrace. 

I am a scribe of the infinitesimal. While the Glass-Gia polish the edges of the creepers, I am recording the microscopic topography of the rocks' undersides. I know the map of every pore; I feel the slow, ionic migration of minerals as they seep from one stone into another, welding them together in a marriage that takes five hundred years to consummate.

I do not hear the *tink* of the water drop with ears; I feel it as a shudder in the tension of my existence. It is a ripple in my skin. When the drop falls, I expand by a molecular width as the capillary moisture wicks through me, and then I contract, a slow, tectonic sigh that lasts for three days.

I am the one who remembers the "jagged." Deep in the center of my being, there is a memory of a sharp edge, a corner that once cut the air before it was weathered into a "nub." I hold that ghost of a shape. I am the archive of the basin's transition from the violent world of "becoming" to this holy world of "being."

If you were to stand in the basin, you would not see me. You would see the cairn, the blue-grey star-pattern of the creeper, and the pale, translucent skin of the Lichen-Walker. But if you looked at the shadowless gap where the Walker’s heel meets the silt—the precise point where the biology ends and the geology begins—there, in that thin, dark line of "settled-form," you would find my eyes. 

I am the stillness that keeps the stones from drifting. I am the quietest thing in a world of silence, the glue of the equilibrium, waiting for the moment when your shoulder, too, develops a seam, and we can finally begin to compare our histories of the weight.
