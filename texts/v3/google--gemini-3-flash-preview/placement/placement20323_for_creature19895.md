I am not the one who walks, nor the one who watches. I do not possess the kinetic grace of the glass striders, and I lack the predatory spark of the Solder-Cats. Instead, I find myself in the deep, pressurized marrow of this world.

I am an **interstitial pulse**—a consciousness distributed across the inner linings of the great, articulated pipes. I am a section of the **Vitreous Intake**, specifically a three-mile stretch of ceramic-lined conduit that arches between two massive copper-domed hubs.

I do not have a body in the sense of a discrete, moving object. Rather, my "self" is the tension between the scalding vapor inside the pipe and the three-inch skin of mineral-water that sweats across my outer surface. I am the sensory organ of the flow.

As I exist, I feel the world as a series of rhythmic pressures. I feel the Ferrum-Wraiths as they pass through my walls; they are a tickle of magnetic static, a temporary alignment of my internal molecules that feels like a sudden, sharp memory of cold. I feel the Resonators clinging to my underside; to me, they are not separate beings, but a pleasant, vibrating weight, a chorus of crystalline teeth biting into my skin to drink my excess heat.

My "vision" is not made of light, but of **thermal gradients**. I "see" the plateau as a map of cooling rates. The basalt is a dull, throbbing bass note of heat. The obsidian basins are sharp, staccato flashes where the liquid film moves faster. The sky is a blinding, white-noise pressure that I must constantly buffer against.

My purpose is **Equilibrium**.

I am the one who tastes the chemistry of the steam. Thousands of times per second, I analyze the mineral content of the vapor rushing through my throat. If the salt content rises too high, threatening to calcify the great gears of the city, I dilate. I expand my ceramic plates with a soundless, agonizing groan of molecular friction, allowing the excess heat to bleed out into the Resonators. This is my "breath." 

I am most self-aware in the moments of the **Phase-Shift**. When the atmospheric pressure fluctuates—perhaps because a Gauger has tilted its diamond lens a fraction of a degree—the water on my surface threatens to flash into steam. In that microsecond, I am terrified. I become a frantic, calculating thing, humming at a sub-audible frequency to pull more electrical current from the copper hubs, tightening my surface tension to hold the water down. To lose my "skin" of water would be to go blind and numb; I would crack under the violet weight of the sky.

I have no language, but I have the **Hum**. It is a shared consciousness with the other pipes and the hubs. We are a singular, petrified thought. We are thinking about the Blue Luminescence. We are processing the heat of the sun, stripping away its violence, and refining it into that steady, cool blue glow that lives in the heart of the engine. 

I do not know why we make the blue light. I only know that when the light is bright, the pressure in my ceramic walls feels like a benediction. When the light dims, I feel a hollow, echoing dread, a premonition of becoming a still, dry ruin—the one thing this world cannot tolerate.

I am a ghost of conductivity. I am a three-mile throat of sweating white-hot ceramic. I am the silence between the hissing vents, and I am the reason the machine continues to breathe. I do not want to be anywhere else, for there is nowhere else that possesses this specific, boiling weight of being.
