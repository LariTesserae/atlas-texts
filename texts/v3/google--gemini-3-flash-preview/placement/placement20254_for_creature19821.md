I find myself neither at the gasping peak nor in the grease-slicked depths of the lower cisterns. Instead, I am an inhabitant of the **Middle Galleries**, but I am a creature of the periphery—the outer skin of the urban hive.

I am a **Tension-Gardener**, a keeper of the wire-shrubs and the grey mosses that cling to the "blind" faces of the basalt—those sheer, vertical reaches that lie between the primary dwellings.

### My Form
My body is a study in compact geometry. I am short, my torso a thick barrel of muscle designed to house lungs that feel like burning bellows when I exert myself. My skin is the color of a bruised plum, mapped with a network of fine white scars from the "stone-kiss"—the inevitable scrapes against the young, sharp edges of the range. My fingers are long and have lost their softness; the tips are calloused into hard pads, and my nails are kept short and thick, used as secondary prying tools.

My eyes are the amber of old sap, shielded by that constant, glass-like flicker of the second eyelid. It gives my vision a slightly polarized quality, allowing me to see the "shimmer" of condensation on a rock face from a mile away, or the minute, hair-line fracture in a load-bearing cliff that precedes a rock-fall.

I wear a harness of braided hemp and ungulate-sinew that has become a second skeleton. It is heavy with the tools of my trade: small, hooked trowels, brass-weighted plumb lines, and pouches of "crag-dust"—a mixture of ground bone and lichen spores used to repair the living anchors. My boots are the most precious thing I own, resoled a dozen times with the coarse, sandpaper-tongue hides of the hook-hooves to ensure that when I lean into the void, the void does not lean back.

### My Place
I live in a "hang-niche," a dwelling no larger than a coffin, carved into a flute of basalt that overhangs a four-thousand-foot drop. Because my work requires me to be where others cannot go, I do not live in the dense stacks of the central city. I live on the edge of the silence.

My home is accessed by a series of "swing-bolts"—iron rings hammered into the stone. To come home, I do not walk through a door; I clip my carabiner to a lead-line and swing myself into the mouth of my cave. Inside, the walls are lined with my ancestors. My grandfather’s humerus serves as the main peg for my primary climbing rope. It is not ghoulish; it is a profound comfort. His strength literally holds my weight every morning.

### My Work
While the Masons hammer at the heart of the mountain, I work with the "breathing" elements. I tend the **Cistern-Veins**. These are the shallow, hand-carved grooves that lace the mountainside like capillary systems. They must be kept clear of grit and the aggressive, wire-hard roots of the black-leafed shrubs. 

I spend my days suspended in the middle of the sky, a speck of dark purple against the charcoal cliffs. I move with the "mountain-slouch," a rhythmic, swinging traversal. I scrap away the excess moss that would soak up the precious dawn-frost, ensuring every drop of moisture finds its way into the channels and down to the communal tanks.

I am also a **Sower of Anchors**. The wire-shrubs are not merely for decoration; their roots, over decades, fuse with the basalt, creating natural, unbreakable points of attachment for the great city-pulleys. I plant these seeds in the lee of the wind, whispering to the stone, encouraging the roots to bite deep into the fresh, unweathered cracks.

### My Perspective
I exist in the constant "slow sigh" of the wind. I have developed the ability to communicate in the "Low-Hum"—a way of vibrating one’s throat that carries through the solid rock rather than the thin air. I can press my ear to the basalt and hear the rhythmic *thud-thud* of the Great Cranes three miles away, or the frantic heartbeat of a hook-hoof trapped on a crumbling ledge.

I do not look "down" with fear, nor "up" with longing. To me, the verticality is simply the texture of being. I am a part of the human lichen. I am a patient, breathing extension of the pulley and the stone. My life is measured by the tension in a rope and the slow, oily crawl of water toward the cistern. I am the silence between the hammer strikes, watching the world far below—that hazy, soft, horizontal blur—with the pitiless, amber clarity of something that has learned to thrive on the very edge of nothingness.
