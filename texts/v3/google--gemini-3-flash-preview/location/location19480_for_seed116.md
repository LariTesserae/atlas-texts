This is a vertical world, a skyscraper-sea contained within a single colossal cistern. It is the **Great Reservoir Spire**, an industrial hollow the size of a mountain, yet so self-contained and sequestered that it feels like a secret tucked into the folds of a larger, forgotten machine.

### The Substrate and Structure
There is no earth here. No stone was born of tectonic pressure. Every surface is a high-grade, weathered alloy or a ceramic composite, ancient and grooved by eons of falling water. This is a total-build environment—a cylinder miles wide and leagues high, where the "terrain" consists of massive, rusted iron gantries, suspended catwalks, and protruding filtration pipes the size of cathedrals. 

Erosion has worked its will upon the metal. The surfaces are not sharp; they have been smoothed into "river-steel" by trillions of gallons of constant flow, taking on the organic, rounded shapes of driftwood. The architecture is ancient, settled into a final, stable state of decay where the rust itself has become a protective skin.

### The Water and Atmosphere
Water is the primary inhabitant. It does not sit in a basin; it is a vertical phenomenon. The entire space is a "wall of rain" on a titanic scale. Massive sluices at the unseen summit release a constant, warm deluge that shatters against the industrial outcrops, turning the air into a thick, breathable suspension of mist and droplets. 

The humidity is absolute. You do not just see the water; you wear it. The temperature is a steady, hothouse warmth, heated by the slow, rhythmic thrum of massive submerged turbines and the friction of the descent. It feels like the inside of a giant breath.

### The Lushness of the Metal
Despite the industrial substrate, the world is green. Lush, hydro-dependent vegetation clings to the verticality. Thick curtains of bioluminescent moss drape over the rusted pipes, and giant, broad-leafed ferns—genetically adapted to feed on the mineral-rich runoff—sprout from the joints of every girder. 

The plants are not scattered; they are clustered into dense, dripping vertical jungles that follow the paths of the largest leaks. Because the scale is "hidden" and "intimate," you are never looking at a horizon; you are looking at a single, incredibly complex pillar of life, a micro-ecosystem a few hundred yards across that stretches up and down into infinity.

### The Industrial Ecology
Mechanization is the heartbeat of the place. Huge, brass-fitted pistons move with the slow deliberation of tectonic plates, coughing out clouds of oily steam that mingle with the natural mist. Great chains, links the size of houses, sway in the wind generated by the water's fall. 

The fauna is abundant and strange, a mix of the organic and the scavenged. "Valve-crabs" with shells made of hammered brass scrap scuttle along the undersides of the walkways. Schools of translucent, lung-fish-like creatures leap between the waterfalls, their scales shimmering with a rainbow-sheen of leaked industrial lubricant. There are birds—or things like them—with wide, leathery wings that never flap, instead soaring on the powerful updrafts of the heat-vents.

### Sound and Motion
The world is loud. It is a symphony of the industrial-deluge. The roar of the falling water is a constant bass note that vibrates in your marrow. This is punctuated by the "industry": the periodic *thrum-clack* of a massive pressure-valve releasing, the rhythmic grinding of gears gargantuan enough to tilt a city, and the shrieks of the soaring fauna echoing off the curved metal walls.

Everything is in motion. There is no stillness. If the water stops, the world dies. The cycles are biological and mechanical—the "feeding" of the turbines, the "migration" of the moss-spores through the spray, the predator-prey chase occurring on the vertical plane.

### The Rules of the World
In the Great Reservoir Spire, gravity is the only law. To move horizontally is a struggle; to move vertically is the way of life. There is a sense of being inside a clock that has been filled with a rainforest. Light comes from "normal" sources—huge, flickering sodium lamps fixed to the walls and the soft, diffused glow of the phosphorescent flora—giving the place the amber, hazy quality of an eternal afternoon in a greenhouse.

It is a place of "settled" industry. The machines aren't being built; they have always been here, functioning on an instinctual, automated level, long since reclaimed by the water and the vine. It is a secret, pressurized paradise of rust, steam, and emerald leaves.
