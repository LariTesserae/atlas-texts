The air here is a thick, translucent broth, holding so much moisture that the distinction between gas and liquid has begun to fail. It is a world of **The Drip.**

This is a lowland basin, a shallow architectural crater where the heat is a physical weight, holding steady at a suffocating, tropical constant. There is no sun, only a high, vaulted ceiling of corrugated iron and translucent polymer, hundreds of feet above, stained yellow by decades of rising steam. The light is dim, filtered through layers of condensation and grime, casting the entire settlement in a perpetual, jaundiced twilight.

### The Urban Anatomy
The landscape is an industrial hamlet—not a sprawling city, but a dense, concentrated knot of infrastructure compressed into a small, swampy depression. The buildings are not made of stone or wood, but of oxidized copper and green-slicked steel. They are "Salt-Box" factories and tenements, elevated on spindly, hydraulic stilts to keep them above the rising puddle-tables. 

Because the erosion is established, the metal has reached a state of chemical equilibrium. The rust is no longer a sign of decay but a protective skin, a deep velvet orange that bleeds into the lime-green moss. Everything is rounded. There are no sharp corners; the constant humidity and the friction of falling water have buffeted the industrial geometry into something organic, almost bone-like.

### The Lush Mechanical Ecology
The vegetation is aggressive but orderly. Huge, waxy-leafed ferns grow directly out of the venting steam-pipes, their roots tangled into the mechanical guts of the city. Transparent vines, thick as a human wrist and pulsed with slow-moving sap, bridge the gaps between the tenements. This is a "living industry." There are no animals here—no birds in the rafters, no rats in the gutters—only the plants and the machines.

In the absence of fauna, the machines have taken on the "active" role of the ecosystem. The world is incredibly loud. It is a rhythmic, percussive symphony of industrial exertion: the rhythmic *thrum-hiss* of massive bellows breathing air into the peat, the screech of rusted turbines powered by falling runoff, and the constant, heavy *thud* of automated pile-drivers deep in the mud, keeping the foundation from sinking.

### The Water and the Weirdness
Water is the primary citizen. It doesn't just fall; it circulates. It weeps from the undersides of the elevated walkways in long, shimmering curtains. It boils in shallow glass troughs designed to catch the heat. Because of the density of the steam and the specific industrial salts in the air, the water here is "heavy"—it clings to surfaces in globes that refuse to pop, rolling across the hand like mercury.

There is a strange, rhythmic pulse to the landscape. Every few minutes, a centralized "venting cycle" occurs. The dim light flickers, the roar of the turbines rises to a deafening pitch, and a flash-fog rolls through the narrow alleys, hot and smelling of boiled sugar and copper. During these moments, the plants visibly distend, their leaves swelling with moisture, and the buildings groan as their metal skeletons expand in the heat.

### The Rules of the Space
In this place, to be dry is to be dead. The "Urban" element isn't for people; it’s a self-sustaining plumbing system that has forgotten its creators. The "Industrial" aspect is a closed loop: the machines generate the heat, the heat evaporates the water, the water feeds the plants, and the plants’ relentless growth provides the pressure and biomass that fuels the machines.

It is a cathedral of wet iron. You walk on metal grates that vibrate with the force of subterranean pumping. Looking down through the slats, you see not shadows, but a glowing, phosphorescent moss growing on the surface of a black, oily lagoon. There is a sense of immense, lonely productivity. The factories are working hard, producing nothing but their own continued existence in the beautiful, sweltering gloom.
