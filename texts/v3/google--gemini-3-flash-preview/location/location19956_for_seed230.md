The air at this altitude is thin and perpetually cool, stinging the lungs with a clean, sharpened edge. We are in the **Cistern-Town of Oru**, a jagged settlement perched on a limestone shelf that juts from the side of a massive, wind-scoured massif. 

The scale of the place is intimate—an urban density compressed into the size of a single cathedral’s footprint. Because the horizontal space is so limited, the architecture has grown vertically and inward. It is a town of stacked, hand-chiseled stone cubes, connected by narrow ladders of seasoned timber and bridges made of tensioned rope. Everything is built from the same pale, grey-white rock as the mountain itself, making the town look like a natural geological formation that has begun to dream of geometry.

### The Physics of Stasis
The most unsettling quality of Oru is its absolute lack of flux. There is no wind here, despite the height. The flags made of coarse, hand-spun fiber do not flutter; they hang in stiff, permanent folds. Smoke from the low-temperature peat fires doesn't drift or swirl; it rises in perfectly straight, glass-like columns until it dissipates into the thin blue sky. Time doesn't seem to pass so much as it accumulates. Even the light feels anchored—a steady, clear morning-state that never softens into dusk or sharpens into noon. This is a "frozen equilibrium," where the energy of the world has settled into a final, comfortable notch.

### Water and Material
Water is the town’s primary concern and its only luxury. It is not found in rivers or rain, but in the breath of the mountain. Periodically, the temperature drops just enough for heavy condensation to slick the stone walls. The inhabitants have carved "tear-tracks"—tiny, precise grooves in the masonry—to guide these microscopic beads of moisture into central collection basins. These basins are the hearts of the town, guarded by lids of heavy slate to prevent evaporation. 

The vegetation is a testament to this scarcity. There are no trees. Instead, a species of silver-green lichen grows in the shadows of the stone bridges, and occasionally, a single, spindly stalk of "ghost-grass" will shiver out from a crack in the mortar, its blades translucent and stiff as parchment.

### The Urban Microcosm
Despite the primitive technology—there is no wheel here, no gear, no molten metal—the organization is total. This is a city of manual mastery. The stones are fitted with such precision that no mortar is needed; they are held together by friction and the weight of tradition. The "infrastructure" consists of a complex network of gravity-fed troughs and acoustic tubes carved into the rock, allowing a whisper at the highest tier to be heard by the basin-guards at the bottom.

The inhabitants are rare, and their movements are deliberate. Because the world is so quiet, every sound is magnified: the *clack* of a wooden sandal on stone, the *scrape* of a bone needle against leather, the rhythmic *thump* of a weaver’s loom. These sounds do not echo; they are swallowed instantly by the porous stone, leaving a vacuum of silence in their wake.

### Ecology and Fauna
Life is a guest here, not a tenant. Occasionally, a high-altitude scavenger—a bird with a wingspan like a tattered kite—will drift over the town, its shadow a sharp, moving blotch on the white stone. It never lands. There are small, sightless beetles that live in the moisture-basins, their shells polished like obsidian, moving with a slowness that mimics the mountain itself.

### The Rule of the World
The rule of Oru is **Conservation**. Nothing is discarded because nothing is new. The tools—chisels of flint, hammers of heavy basalt, needles of splintered bone—are ancient, passed down through generations until they have molded themselves to the shape of a human hand. There is no sense of progress, only maintenance. 

The erosion here has reached its end-state. The wind has finished its work; the water has found its paths; the stone has settled into its final weight. To stand in the center of the Cistern-Town is to feel as though you are inside a great, cold clock that has finally, mercifully, stopped ticking. It is a landscape of profound, static peace, where the only thing that moves is the slow, deliberate pulse of the people who call the stasis their home.
