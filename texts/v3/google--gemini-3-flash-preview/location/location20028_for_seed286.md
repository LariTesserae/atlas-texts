There is no ground here, only a vertical ocean of chlorophyll and pressurized mist. 

At this elevation, the atmosphere has become a liquid suspension—a world-sea held aloft by the monumental tension of the trees. Imagine a forest where the canopy is so thick it acts as a sea floor, and the "air" above it is a cold, translucent brine, miles deep, stretching toward a pale, normal sun. This is a drowned highland, a continental shelf of solid wood and tangled kelp-looms that exists in a state of terminal, industrial stasis.

The scale is planetary. The horizon does not curve; it dissolves into a periwinkle haze of infinite distance. Because the density of the living mass is sparse despite being "total," the world feels like a cathedral of emerald glass. There are thousands of yards of open, tea-colored water between the colossal stalks of the *Lignum Mechanica*—the dominant plant species that has replaced geology.

These stalks are not mere wood. They are biological machines of an industrial order. The trunks, hundreds of feet in diameter, are ribbed with copper-hard bark and pulse with a rhythmic, mechanical thrum. They function like massive, organic piston-pumps, moving millions of gallons of nutrient-rich silt from the unseen depths below to the flowering heights above. The sound is a constant, low-frequency roar—an unending industrial drone produced by the friction of vascular fluids moving through miles of fiber at high pressure. It is the sound of a factory that never closes, yet there is no one here to run it.

The temperature is a crisp, unchanging cool. It never fluctuates. There are no seasons, no nights of frost, no afternoons of heat. The thermal equilibrium is absolute, maintained by the constant circulation of the global sea-forest. 

In the wide spaces between these thrumming wooden towers, the fauna thrives in the "sparse" emptiness. Enormous, translucent rays with leafy, photosynthetic wings glide through the water-air, their movements slow and rhythmic, synchronized to the heartbeat of the trees. Tiny, silver-scaled schools of air-fish dart through the hanging moss-curtains. The life here is abundant, but it never crowds the view; every creature seems to have miles of personal territory, a vastness that makes the census of the world feel both lonely and teeming.

There is a profound sense of erosion here, but not of stone. The plants themselves are "weathered." The bark of the oldest towers is scarred and pitted by centuries of fluid friction, smoothed down like river stones. The ecosystem is "settled." It has reached a point of perfect, unchanging balance where nothing new grows and nothing old dies. It is a floral machine in a state of perpetual motion, yet it is utterly static. Evolution has stopped because the design has been perfected.

The "weirdness" of the place manifests in the light. Because the water is so clear and the elevation so high, the light does not come from "above" in the traditional sense. It is refracted through the giant, bulbous fruit-lamps that hang from the underside of the canopy—biological lanterns that capture the sun and broadcast it into the deeps. The light is steady, sterile, and unflinching.

Rules of the world:
1. **Buoyancy is Life:** Nothing here touches a solid floor. To exist is to float, to swim, or to cling to the great thrumming trunks.
2. **The Constant Hum:** Silence is a sign of death. If a tree stops its industrial vibration, the local ecosystem collapses as the water stagnates.
3. **No Entropy:** Leaves do not fall and rot; they are reabsorbed by the vines before they can detach. The water remains perpetually distilled and clean.
4. **The Industrial Pulse:** Everything—the heartbeat of the fauna, the flickering of the light-bulbs, the drift of the currents—is paced to the 60-cycle hum of the forest’s vascular pumps.

It is a place of cold, green majesty. It feels like the interior of a massive, flooded engine room designed by a god who loved moss and copper. There are no ruins here because nothing has ever been "built" by hands—the trees have simply outgrown the need for architecture, providing their own scaffolding, their own power, and their own eternal, humming stability.
