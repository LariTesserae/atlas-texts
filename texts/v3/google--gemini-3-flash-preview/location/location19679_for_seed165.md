The air here does not circulate; it vibrates. At this altitude, the atmosphere is thin enough to see the stars in the afternoon, yet it carries a weight like cold iron. We are at the ceiling of the world, a plateau of jagged, violet-tinged slate and frozen shale that shouldn't exist at such a height. The elevation is so extreme that the sky is no longer blue but a bruised, abyssal indigo, fading into a charcoal void at the zenith.

The cold is absolute. It is a dry, sucking cold that pulls the heat from the marrow of the bone, yet the ground is not entirely dry. There is a persistent, illogical dampness—not from weather, for it never rains here, but from the stones themselves. The rocks "perspire." Cold condensation beads on the underside of every overhang, forming thick, gelatinous droplets that never fall. They cling to the stone like clear parasites, trembling with the pervasive sound that dominates the region.

The sound is a monolithic roar. It is not the wind—the air is deathly still—but the sound of the planet’s rotation, or perhaps the friction of reality rubbing against the vacuum. It is a low-frequency tectonic hum that shakes the vision. It makes the sparse, wiry shrubs—the only vegetation that can survive—grow in corkscrew spirals, their woody limbs hardened into natural tuning forks that ring in sympathy with the earth’s groan.

The erosion here is "finished." The mountains have been ground down into sharp, geometric fins and flat-topped mesas that have not changed their profile in a million years. There is no dust. The wind has long since carried away anything smaller than a pebble. What remains are the "scattered marks"—monoliths of a nameless transition. These are not buildings, but they are not natural. Large, hollow cubes of translucent, obsidian-like glass sit embedded in the peaks, some miles apart from one another. They contain nothing. They have no doors. They are simply there, cold and indifferent, acting as anchors for the silence that tries to fight the roar.

Time has reached an equilibrium here. The shadows do not move. Although the sun passes overhead, the light remains a constant, dim twilight, as if the atmosphere is filtering out the passage of hours. This is a landscape of "frozen-equilibrium." A rock balanced on a needle-point will stay there for an eternity because the forces of gravity and entropy have reached a stalemate.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the *Glass-Striders*. These are spindly, translucent organisms, more like solidified vapor than flesh. They move with agonizing slowness across the slate, licking the damp condensation from the rocks with tongues that sound like sandpaper on silk. They have no eyes; in this dim, roaring world, they perceive through the vibrations in their spindly limbs.

The weirdness manifests in the horizon. Because the scale is so vast and the density of matter so low, the perspective begins to fail. Distant peaks do not appear smaller; they simply appear more transparent. You can see through the furthest mountains to the curvature of the earth behind them. Reality is thin here. If you were to shout, the sound would not echo; it would simply stop, swallowed by a space that has more "nothing" than "something" in it.

The dampness, the roar, the frozen time, and the impossible height create a sense of standing on the very edge of a finished creation. It is a place where the world has stopped dreaming and has settled into a final, cold, and echoing consciousness.
