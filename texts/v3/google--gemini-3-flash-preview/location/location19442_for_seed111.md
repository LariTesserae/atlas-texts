The air here is thin enough to ache, a cold, sharp tonic that tastes of mineral dust and wet fur. We are at the throat of a mountain, a high-altitude sanctuary pinned between two jagged, adolescent peaks. Because the erosion is young, the landscape has not yet learned to be soft; the rocks are all razor-edges and obsidian glass, black and unweathered, thrusting upward with the violent geometry of a fresh wound.

This is a "pocket" world, no larger than a cathedral's nave, walled in by vertical cliffs that block all but the faintest bruised purple of a sub-arctic twilight. It is a place of permanent gloom, where the only light is a bioluminescent seepage—a dull, rhythmic throbbing of pale blues and fungal greens that coat the undersides of the rock shelves.

The moisture is not a rain, but a condensation. The cold rock breathes, and the thin air weeps against it. Droplets gather on the sharp stone lips, swelling slowly over hours before falling into the darkness below.

But the silence one expects at this height is absent. It is replaced by a sound that is rhythmic, wet, and communal: the sound of a million heartbeats and a billion tiny lungs.

The space is impossibly crowded. Vegetation is a mere afterthought—clumps of translucent, rubbery succulents and gray lichens that hug the cracks—but the fauna has overtaken the geography. Every square inch of the vertical stone is carpeted in life. These are not insects, but something soft-bodied and heavy. Thousands of pale, eyeless "marrow-slugs" the size of human forearms cling to the cliffs, their translucent skin revealing slow-churning internal organs. 

They do not move quickly—the world here is agonizingly slow—but they are dense. They overlap like shingles on a roof, shifting with a collective, dragging friction that creates a constant, low-frequency hum. Layered over them are "glimmer-moths" with wings of heavy damp felt, too heavy to fly, which instead crawl in undulating blankets over the slugs, feeding on the mineral-rich slime they secrete.

There is a sense of a biological machine at work. The density is so high that you cannot see the black rock beneath the heaving mass of pale, cold flesh. 

Every few hours, a “shudder” passes through the chamber. It begins at the base of the eastern cliff and ripples upward—a slow-motion wave of contraction as the organisms adjust to a shift in temperature or a change in the moisture level. As they shift, they let out a collective, wheezing sigh—a sound like a bellows fueled by damp earth—that echoes off the high, sharp ceiling.

The weirdness lies in the symbiosis of the "primitive" elements. Propped against certain flat stones are long, tapered shams of bone—splinters from the mountain’s own internal pressure—that have been polished and arranged by something small and nimble. These are not buildings, but "anchors." Simple, hand-wedged stone levers have been jammed into fissures to divert the trickle of condensation toward specific colonies of glimmer-moths. It is a rudimentary engineering of the environment, a pre-technological intervention born of a need to manage the scarce water. 

There are no voices here, only the pressure of too many bodies in too small a space. It feels like the interior of a giant, breathing lung, high above the clouds where the oxygen is thin and the light never arrived. The world is changing, the mountains are still rising, but here, in this dark hollow, life has decided to huddle together and wait out the eons in a loud, wet, and crowded dark.
