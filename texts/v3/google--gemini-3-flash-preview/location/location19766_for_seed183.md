At these heights, the air is not thin, but thick with the ghost of water. We are at an elevation where the sky feels heavy and pressing, a shelf of the world pushed so high it scrapes the belly of the light. There is no green here. There is no brown of soil or grey of bark. The world is a vertical labyrinth of translucent, weeping minerals—looming pillars of salt-dense quartz and layered mica that reach upward into a blinding, shadowless glare.

The light is a constant, punishing brilliance, diffuse and omnidirectional. It does not seem to come from a sun, but from the atmosphere itself, which glows with a high-energy luminescence. It reflects off the wet surfaces of the towering spires, creating a visual roar of refraction that makes it difficult to judge distance.

Everything is damp. The rocks do not just sit in the mist; they hemorrhage water. It seeps from the pores of the stone in rhythmic pulses, governed by some internal pressure rather than local weather. There are no plants, but the minerals themselves seem to possess a slow, crystalline metabolism. In the absence of biology, the geology has become ornate. Elaborate, brittle lattices of silicate bridge the gaps between the high peaks, spun like glass webs. These are not built by hands, but grown by the constant evaporation and re-deposition of mineral-heavy runoff in the shrieking wind.

The sound is a physical presence. It is a dissonant, multi-tonal screaming—the result of high-pressure air forced through the natural flutes and apertures of the eroded spires. It sounds like a choir of bells being shattered in slow motion. Underneath this treble rasp is the heavy, percussive *thrum-thrum* of the water-pulses within the rock, giving the entire mountain range a mechanical, heartbeat-like rhythm.

The "built" elements are strange and infrequent. They are not cities or homes, but primitive, necessary interventions. Stuck into the sides of the weeping cliffs are heavy, hand-tooled bronze basins and hammered lead gutters, tethered by thick, brine-soaked ropes. They are meant to catch the mountain’s "blood," perhaps for some unseen purpose further down the slopes. There are also "sound-breakers"—clumsy, circular stone stacks built to disrupt the wind-screams, though they have been eroded by the constant moisture into smooth, melted shapes that look like half-dissolved teeth.

Reality here is fluid. Gravity does not always pull toward the center of the earth; sometimes the weeping water flows upward, spiraling around the spires in glittering ribbons before dispersing into the white light. The "established" nature of the erosion is evident in how the landscape has adapted to these anomalies. The stone has grown curved to accommodate the sideways rain. The "scattered" structures are built with an understanding of where the air might suddenly thin into a vacuum or where the light might solidify into a physical barrier.

There are no animals to hear the noise. There are no insects to crawl in the mineral webs. There is only the restless, dynamic motion of the salt-crusts forming and breaking, the constant shedding of water, and the slow, grinding shift of the peaks. It is a landscape that feels ancient and finished, a mineral clockwork operating at a frantic, high-altitude pace, indifferent to the concept of life. It is cool to the touch, sharp to the eye, and deafening to the soul.
