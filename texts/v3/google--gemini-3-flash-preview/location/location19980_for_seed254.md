Deep below the surface of the earth, ensconced in a natural limestone pocket that has been almost entirely overwritten by organic industry, lies the Root-Engine District.

The space is roughly the size of a cathedral’s nave, but the height is difficult to judge because the air is thick with a heavy, golden-green mist—a constant suspension of humidity and spores. Here, there is no sky, only the massive, ribbed undersides of colossal fungus-shelves and the tangled mesh of iron-hard vines that form a secondary ceiling. 

Everything is wet. The heat is thick and humid, a steady 85 degrees that feels heavier because the air is saturated. Water does not just fall; it bleeds. It weeps from the porous rock walls and perspires from the surface of every living thing. It gathers in shallow, obsidian-dark pools that reflect the low, amber glow of bioluminescent mosses and the dull orange sparks of grinding machinery.

This is a world where vegetation has become the literal substrate of civilization. There is no soil, only a compressed, multi-layered floor of peat, thick moss, and woven root-systems that have been planed flat to serve as streets. The "buildings" are not constructed on the land; they are grown from it and then modified. Massively thick, hollowed-out gourds and reinforced banyan-like trunks serve as multi-story tenements. These structures are hard as oak, their bark scarred by the installation of brass portholes, iron ladders, and heavy, weeping pipes.

The sound is immense and rhythmic—a thundering, subterranean industrial pulse. Huge, piston-like stalks, driven by the pressure of the rising sap and steam, move up and down in slow, agonizingly powerful cycles. They are connected to iron flywheels that grind against one another, slick with moss and lubricating oils. These machines aren't for fast production; they are part of a slow, massive metabolism. They churn the water, dredge the mineral-rich silt from the lower sumps, and pump nutrient-rich slurries through a network of copper veins that disappear into the vegetation-choked walls.

Electricity exists here, but it is dim and temperamental. Strings of glass bulbs are encrusted with salt and algae, glowing with a faint, flickering filament that barely cuts through the gloom. The light is predominantly a murky, underwater green.

There are no animals. No birds nest in the high cables; no rats scurry in the gutters. The only "life" is the flora and the humans who tend to it. The people move with a sluggish, deliberate patience, their skin perpetually damp, their clothes made of heavy, waterproofed fibers. They are as much a part of the ecosystem as the vines, acting as a maintenance crew for a forest that has been domesticating itself for centuries.

Erosion has softened every edge. The iron gears are pitted and smoothed by a hundred years of humidity; the stone foundations are rounded and slick. The city doesn't feel like it was built so much as it was allowed to happen. There is a profound sense of "settledness." The conflict between machine and nature ended long ago in a draw, and now they have fused into a single, humid, mechanical organism.

The scale is claustrophobic. Visibility is limited to thirty or forty feet before the fog and the density of the growth swallow the view. You are always surrounded by a presence—the vibration of a pump, the drip of a leaf, the smell of wet earth and hot grease. It is an urbanity of the interior, a packed, sweating, slowly-turning clockwork jungle where the air itself feels like a weight against the chest. Change here is measured in decades—the slow widening of a pipe-clamping root, the gradual deepening of a water-cut channel in the stone. Everything is working, everything is loud, and nothing is in a hurry.
