The horizon does not exist here as a line; it exists as a fading, a gradual surrender of dark slate rock to a sky the color of a bruised plum. This is a world of the **Great Moraines**, a planetary-scale expanse of high-altitude plateaus situated so far from their sun that light arrives only as a suggestion, filtered through layers of perpetual, freezing overcast.

The terrain is a testament to time. Erosion has finished its primary work. The mountains are no longer jagged; they are immense, slumbering hogsbacks of basalt and schist, smoothed by eons of glacial retreat into rounded domes that stretch for thousands of miles. The scale is so massive that the valleys between them harbor their own isolated micro-climates, though even these are muted. It is a dry cold, punctuated only by a delicate, rhythmic dampness—the "Breath-Rain." Every few hours, a fine mist descends, not from clouds, but from the sudden condensation of the air itself as it hits the freezing stone. It coats once-sharp ridges in a thin, glass-like rime before evaporating back into the gloom.

Vegetation is a rare, desperate occurrence. It takes the form of "Iron-Lace," a brittle, dark-red lichen that grows in fractal patterns inside the deeper fissures of the rock. It does not photosynthesize; it feeds on the mineral salts leached out by the intermittent moisture. It is the only thing that breaks the monochrome grey of the world, appearing like dried bloodstains on the knuckles of the earth.

Despite the desolation, the world is crowded. It is crowded by **The Migrants**.

The fauna here is overwhelming in its presence, though nearly silent in its passage. Imagine beasts the size of cathedral naves, six-legged and heavily armored in overlapping tectonic scales, moving with a slowness that borders on the geological. They are "Lithovores." They walk the high ridges in endless, single-file lines that stretch from one horizon to the other. There are millions of them, a slow-motion river of flesh and bone that has walked these same paths for a million years. They do not cry out. They do not fight. They simply move. The only sound is the rhythmic, sub-audible *thud* of a limb the size of a redwood trunk meeting the stone—a sound felt in the marrow rather than heard in the ear.

The "industrial" nature of this world is not found in cities or factories, for there are no buildings here. The technology is the biology of the world itself. The Lithovores are living machines. Within their massive, hollow carapaces, rhythmic hydraulic organs pump specialized fluids at high pressure, sounding like the distant, muffled chugging of a steam engine buried deep underground. Their breath is a hot, mechanical exhaust, venting from slits in their sides in pale plumes of steam that vanish into the thin air. They represent a "biological industry"—a scaled, systemic production of heat and movement that keeps the planet’s atmosphere from collapsing into a liquid state. They are the engines of the world, wandering parts of a global machine that no one built.

Space here is vast and empty, yet heavily occupied. You can walk for three days across a flat basin of crushed gravel and see nothing but the looming shadows of the Great Moraines, and then, suddenly, you are in the shadow of a beast so large it eclipses the dim sun for an hour as it passes. 

The air is incredibly still. The silence is heavy, a physical weight that presses against the eardrums. Because the atmosphere is so thin and the temperature so low, sound does not travel; it dies within a few meters of its source. Even the colossal grinding of the beasts’ joints is swallowed by the vacuum-like quiet. 

There is a profound sense of "settledness." Nothing is new. The erosion has found its equilibrium; the beasts have found their paths; the mist follows its schedule. It is a high-altitude wasteland of industrial-scale life, functioning with the cold, slow, and lonely precision of a clock left running in an empty house. The only rule of this world is the **Maintenance of the Path**: keep moving, keep venting, keep the silence. In the dim light, the world continues its slow, mechanized crawl toward an end that was decided at the beginning of time.
