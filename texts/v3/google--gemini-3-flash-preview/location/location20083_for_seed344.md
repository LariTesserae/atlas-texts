You are standing inside a humid, metallic throat. 

The scale here is cramped, almost intrusive. This is not a vast horizon but a "pocket" of geography—a sunken utility court or a deep, narrow fissure in a young crust of basalt and iron. The walls are high and remarkably sharp, lacking the soft curves of wind-worn stone. These ridges are fresh, jagged, and black, smelling of cooling slag and wet minerals. Because the elevation is so low, you are at the mercy of the basin’s heat. It is a thick, sweltering 2.7/3 warmth—a greenhouse effect trapped between high, obsidian-slick walls.

Water does not flow here so much as it perspires. The air is at a saturation point; the atmosphere is a constant, heavy condensation that turns every vertical surface into a weeping curtain. Warm, translucent beads of moisture swell on the undersides of overhanging rocks before dropping into shallow, stagnant pools that silver the floor. The ground is a patchwork of jagged stone and dark, industrial sludge, always wet, reflecting the white, flat light from above with a greasy sheet.

This is a place of industrial birth and biological hesitation.

The vegetation is a rare, desperate presence. Thin, translucent stalks of a pale, waxy flora grow in the lee of the machinery. They do not have leaves, only bladed ribs that catch the falling condensation. They are scattered far apart, lonely occupants of the steam. There are no insects to pollinate them, no birds to nest in the crevices. The only "life" is the slow, rhythmic pulse of the architecture.

Massive, rusted iron pistons—part of some buried, subterranean engine—emerge from the rock faces at irregular intervals. These are the "built" elements: scattered, heavy-duty mechanical limbs that seem to have been fused directly into the stone while it was still molten. They move with an agonizing, geologic slowness. A giant, grease-slicked rod might take an hour to retract three inches into a sleeve of copper. 

But for all their slow movement, the sound is overwhelming. 

The "loudness" is not a sudden crash but a constant, vibrating roar—the hum of high-voltage transformers hidden behind the rock, the hiss of pressurized steam escaping through hairline fractures, and the deep, bone-shaking thrum of the earth-engines. It is a 2.5/3 sonic environment: a wall of industrial noise that makes the standing water in the pools dance with concentric ripples. The sound is so thick it feels like a physical weight, pressing against your chest in the hot, wet air.

Nothing here is old enough to have crumbled. The iron is pitted by rust but hasn't lost its structural integrity. The stone hasn't been smoothed by the water yet. Everything is in a state of high-pressure "just-begun." 

The rules of this world are dictated by the machine-veins. The water is likely a coolant, heated by the friction of the deep pistons, rising as steam only to cool on the jagged ceiling and fall back down in a perpetual, closed-loop sweat. It is an intimate, claustrophobic cathedral of heat and hiss. You are an accidental witness to a slow, mechanical digestion taking place in a hidden fold of the crust, where the only thing that changes is the gradual accumulation of mineral scale on the humming, hot iron.
