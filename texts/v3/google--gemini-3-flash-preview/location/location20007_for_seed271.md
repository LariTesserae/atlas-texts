The air here is thick with a heavy, unmoving warmth, like the breath of a greenhouse that has forgotten its plants. This is a low-lying basin, a shallow bowl of land perhaps no larger than a city square, tucked away where the wind cannot reach it. 

The terrain is a study in "settled-form." The rocks are not jagged or violent; they are nubbed, smooth-backed, and deeply weathered into shapes that suggest they have occupied these exact positions since the dawn of time. They are made of a porous, pale sandstone, soft to the touch and darkened by a permanent, light dampness. There is no running water—no rushing streams or hidden springs—but rather a persistent, capillary moisture. It wicks up from the deep earth, manifesting as a thin, silver sheen on the undersides of overhanging ledges and a slight, cool tackiness on the stone.

The vegetation is a master of patience. There are no trees and no grasses. Instead, the landscape is populated by fleshy, succulent creepers that grow in radiating star-patterns, anchored in the silt-filled cracks of the basin floor. They are a dull, glaucous blue-grey, their leaves thick with stored condensation. They do not sway, for there is no breeze; they do not grow toward a sun they cannot see through the permanent, amber-tinted dimness overhead. They simply exist, spaced yards apart from one another, respecting the thinness of the soil.

Scattered through this quiet bowl are the only signs of a "built" world: small, waist-high cairns of the same pale sandstone. They are not ruins, for they do not seem broken; they are merely arrangements. It is unclear if they were stacked by hands or if the slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction of the damp earth over eons simply urged the stones into these precarious, vertical symmetries. They are solitary and occasional, standing like sentinels in the emptiness.

The light is a constant, shadowless tea-color. It feels as though the sky is a heavy lid of clouds or perhaps a high, vaulted stone ceiling far above, absorbing all the brilliance and leaving only a dim, golden residue to pool in the basin. 

Everything here is governed by a profound, "never-changing" equilibrium. There are no animals to disturb the silence—no insects to drone, no birds to cast fleeting shadows. The only sound is the occasional, resonant *tink* of a single drop of water falling from a stone overhang into a shallow pool below. But even this sound is rare, occurring in intervals of hours, a slow metronome for a world that does not use time.

There is no sense of decay here, only of wear. The erosion has reached its final state; the stones have found their resting angles, the plants have reached their maximum possible spread for the available water, and even the air feels saturated to a point where no more evaporation can occur. It is an "established" place, an eternal afternoon in a hidden room of the world, where the dust has long since settled and the clock has finally wound down to a stop.
