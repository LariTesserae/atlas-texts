The world is a single, immense cistern, a subterranean limestone lung known as the Glistening Sub-Ward. It is not deep in the sense of a bottomless abyss, but it is deeply tucked—just beneath the skin of the upper world, where the ceiling is a rhythmic sequence of weeping vaults and the floor is a perpetual, shallow glass of dark water.

**The Architecture of the Drown-Works**
The landscape is an industrial hamlet suspended in a state of elegant decay. Broad, cast-iron platforms, rusted to a deep velvet umber, sit on squat pylons that have long since been swallowed by the rising water table. These platforms are the foundation for the "urban" core: a series of compact, three-story tenements built from saturated terracotta brick and green-patina copper. The scale is intimate, a cluster of buildings no larger than a village square, huddled together under the low, arching ribs of the earth.

The industry here is hydraulic and patient. Massive, slow-turning turbines—some thirty feet in diameter—are submerged in the dark pools. They spin with such a glacial momentum that a single revolution might take an hour. They don't roar; they hum a frequency so low it is felt in the marrow rather than heard in the ear. Braided copper cables, slick with condensation, sag between the buildings like the webs of a giant, metallic spider.

**Atmosphere and Light**
The air is cool, heavy, and smells of wet stone and ozone. It is a world of the "drip." Every surface is an active conduit for water. The ceiling, peppered with stumpy stalactites that have been ground flat by centuries of industrial maintenance, constantly sheds a fine, misty rain. Light is a rare, precious secondary effect. There is no sun, only the dim, amber glow of sodium lamps encased in thick, fogged glass, and the bioluminescent pulses of the ecology that has colonised the machinery. The light does not travel far; it is swallowed by the moisture in the air, creating small pools of visibility separated by vast, velvet voids.

**The Living Machinery**
While the vegetation is sparse—limited to patches of pale, rubbery liverwort and iron-eating moss that clings to the rivets—the fauna is dense and unsettlingly synchronized with the tech. 

The most common inhabitants are the Pipe-Eels: translucent, ribbon-like creatures that weave through the gears of the turbines. They feed on the static charge generated by the slow-turning wheels. Swarms of "Clock-Flies"—small, metallic-shelled beetles with translucent wings—cling to the warm casings of the transformers. They don't buzz; they click rhythmically, their collective sound mimicking the ticking of a thousand submerged watches. 

Everywhere, there are the Soft-Shells—amphibious, crab-like scavengers with legs like delicate glass needles. They move across the rusted iron grates with a faint, metallic *tink-tink-tink*, picking at the mineral deposits that crust the industrial runoff. They are unafraid of the few humanoid inhabitants, moving around boots and pilings like water around a stone.

**Erosion and Flux**
This is not a place of sudden change. The erosion here is "established"—the water has already won its primary battles. The brickwork is smoothed, the sharp edges of the iron beams have been rounded into organic curves, and the lime-heavy water has begun to "heal" the buildings, depositing thin layers of calcite over the mortar. The town and the cave are becoming a single, mineralized organism.

The dynamics are excruciatingly slow. The water rises and falls by an inch over the course of a year. The rust creeps across a plate like a glacier. Even the residents move with a deliberate, heavy-limbed lethargy, their voices hushed to avoid shattering the fragile silence of the Glistening Sub-Ward.

**Rules of the World**
In this place, sound is a pollutant. The inhabitants wear soft, felt-soled shoes to avoid disturbing the Clock-Flies, whose sudden flight can short-circuit the exposed wiring. The primary law is the Law of the Leak: water must never be blocked, only redirected. To plug a leak is to invite the pressure of the upper world to shatter the limestone ribs. Instead, the people build small brass gutters, guiding the ceiling-drip into the turbines, turning the weight of the world's weeping into the slow, dim energy that keeps the amber lamps burning.

It is a cathedral of the damp—a quiet, industrial womb where the distinction between "built" and "grown" has been eroded by a thousand years of falling water.
