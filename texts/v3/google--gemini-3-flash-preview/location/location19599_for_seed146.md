It is a world of stagnant, sweltering weight, tucked deep beneath a crust that has never known a sun. Here, the concept of "up" is merely a transition from one density of stone to another. This is the **Lithic Womb**, a vast sub-surface cavity so immense that its ceiling is lost to a permanent, heat-shimmering haze, and its floor stretches for continental distances, flat and unyielding.

The atmosphere is thick, not with oxygen or nitrogen, but with a heavy, aromatic gaseous sulfur and suspended dust of ground silicate. It is warm—not the searing heat of a forge, but the wet, oppressive warmth of a fever. Because there is no water, the air does not carry the scent of rain or damp earth; instead, it smells of parched flint and baked clay.

### The Terrain: Sharp and Young
The ground is composed of obsidian-slick flats and fields of jagged basalt. There is no wind to soften the edges, no rain to round the stones. Every crack in the floor looks as though it was made moments ago; the landscape is "young" in the sense that it is architecturally perfect, devoid of the softening influence of erosion. The scales are gargantuan—great crystalline pillars, hundreds of miles apart, act as the stalactites of this world, pinning the ceiling to the floor. Between them lie the Dust-Seas: vast, level plains of fine-ground mica that crunch underfoot with a sound like breaking glass, yet remain perfectly still in the dead air.

### The Fauna: Abundant and Crystalline
Though there is not a single leaf, spore, or blade of grass, the world is teeming with life. This is a non-aqueous biology, a kingdom of "Dry Life." 

The animals are many-legged, segmented things with shells of translucent horn and silicate. They move with a rhythmic, clockwork clicking. In the absolute darkness, they do not have eyes; instead, they are covered in sensory hairs that detect the faint thrum of tectonic shift. Vast herds of *Lithovores*—creatures the size of cathedrals with mandibles like industrial crushers—grind the basalt floor into powder, processing the minerals for energy. They are slow, majestic, and terrifyingly numerous, their carapaces clashing against one another in the dark like muffled cymbals.

There are also smaller, buzzing things—geometric "flies" with wings of thin mica that vibrate to stay aloft in the soup-thick air. They gather in dense clouds around the heat-vents, looking like swarms of embers.

### The Built: Primitive Fragments
Amongst the jagged outcroppings and the herds of stone-eaters, there are signs of a slow, steady hand. Scattered throughout the Womb are low, circular walls made of stacked flat stones. These are not ruins, but active shelters. The "people" here—if they can be called that—are primitive, hunched forms that carve sigils into the obsidian using nothing but harder diamonds. 

Their technology is tactile and heavy. There is no fire, as there is nothing to burn and no oxygen to feed it. Instead, they use friction. They have mastered the art of "pressure-lighting," rubbing specific phosphorescent minerals together to create a dim, sour-green glow that barely illuminates their hands. They do not build upward; they build outward, tracing geometric patterns across the desert floor that can only be understood if viewed from miles above.

### The Rules and the Dynamic
The most striking feature of the Lithic Womb is its terrifying stability. In the world above, there are seasons, tides, and storms. Here, there is only the Equilibrium. The temperature has not shifted by a degree in ten thousand years. The air does not move. The shadows cast by the green-glow lamps are permanent, etched into the psychology of the inhabitants.

Time is not measured by the sun, but by the slow movement of the great Lithovores. A "day" is the time it takes for a herd to cross from one crystalline pillar to the next. 

### The Sound: The Great Muffle
Everything in this world is quiet, yet thick with presence. The density of the air swallows sound quickly. You can hear your own heartbeat, and perhaps the distant, rhythmic *chink-chink-chink* of a primitive mason a mile away, but the sound feels isolated, as if it is being pushed back into the earth. The only constant is a low-frequency hum—the sound of the planet’s core, vibrating through the solid stone floor, a sound that is felt in the marrow rather than heard in the ear.

It is a world of crowded solitude—millions of living things, thousands of stone structures, all suspended in a warm, dark, breathless eternity.
