It is a vertical city contained within a fissure no wider than a cathedral’s nave, a deep-pressure crevice in a world of dry, brittle basalt. Because the elevation is extreme and the scale is pocketed, the "landscape" is less a horizon and more a recurring upward claustrophobia. You are standing on a gantry looking into a throat of industry that stretches miles toward a pinpoint of violet sky, and miles down into a heat-haze of grinding gears.

The world is parched. There is no moisture here, only the dry slip of graphite lubricants and the rasp of fine mineral dust. Instead of water, the ecology relies on a non-aqueous, lipid-based chemistry—viscous, amber oils that bead on the sharp, un-eroded edges of the rock. Because the geological features are young, the stone has not been softened by wind or rain; every outcropping is a razor-edge of obsidian or fresh volcanic glass, biting into the structural steel of the city.

The air is cool, chilled by the massive intake fans of the industrial shafts, but the density is overwhelming. Buildings do not sit side-by-side; they are bolted onto one another, a frantic, layered urbanism of iron cages, pressurized boilers, and suspended catwalks. It is a city of "the gap," where every cubic inch of the crevice is utilized. Steam pipes—venting dry, superheated gas rather than water vapor—weave between the dwellings like the roots of a plant that never grew.

Life here is abundant, but it is not recognizable as "nature." It is a fauna of the machine. Small, multi-legged scavengers made of chitin and brass clockwork scuttle over the rivets, feeding on the friction-heat of the engines. They move in dense, jittering clouds, their shells clicking against the metal like hail. Larger, soft-bodied "drifters"—organisms like leathery bellows—pulsate in the updrafts, filtering the mineral dust through permeable membranes. They are the lungs of the crevice, prehistoric and silent.

The atmosphere is dim, lit only by the orange glow of furnace-slits and the occasional flicker of arc-welders from the lower tiers. The light does not travel far; the density of the infrastructure swallows it, creating a world of deep shadows and sudden, sharp glints of copper.

Though the crevice is mostly quiet—a low-frequency thrum of spinning turbines and the distant, rhythmic *clack-clack* of the scavenger swarms—the internal state of the place is violently dynamic. This is a landscape of "anthill energy." The city is in a constant state of self-cannibalization. To your left, a crane is dismantling a housing block to scavenge the steel for a new boiler turbine; to your right, a pressurized seal has blown, and the frantic inhabitants are soldering it shut with molten lead. Nothing stays fixed. The architecture is a turbulent process, an industrial storm that builds and destroys itself in a cycle of desperate utility.

There is no green. The only "vegetation" is a species of crystalline fungus that grows in the cooling vents, brittle white structures that shatter at a touch, recycling the metallic salts from the air. 

As you look up, the sheer verticality makes the head swim. The "ground" is a memory; the "sky" is a distant myth. There is only the Shelf, the Pipe, the Gear, and the Swarm. You live in the tension between the cooling rock and the heating engine, a tiny, frantic occupant of a mountain's secret, mechanical wound.
