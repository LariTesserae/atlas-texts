This is the Great Sump of the All-Manifold. It is a world-sized basement, a continental machine-floor of sweating brass and recursive iron, where the very concept of "nature" has been paved over by a million years of industrial intent.

The scale is sickening. Looking to the horizon does not reveal a sky, but rather a distant, hazy ceiling of ribbed lead and glass four miles overhead, through which a bruised, amber sun filters a constant, "normal" daylight. There is no weather here except for what the machinery creates; because the atmosphere is so vast, it has developed its own internal convection. Hot, humid air rises from the belly of the world, hits the cold lead of the high ceiling, and falls back down as a perpetual, tepid drizzle.

Everything is wet. The ground is not earth, but a vast mosaic of interlocking, non-slip steel plates, worn smooth but still sharp-edged. Between the plates, warm water wells up—not from springs, but from leaking gaskets the size of city blocks. The landscape is a flat, low-lying expanse of shallow, square pools and weeping metal bulkheads. It is a marsh of oil-slicked copper.

There is not a single leaf, spore, or blade of grass. The "ecology" is entirely synthetic and biological in a way that defies taxonomy. The world is dense with life, but it is a fauna of the machine. Great, soft-bodied, translucent "siphon-slugs" the size of freight trains pulsed rhythmically as they cling to the sides of vertical cooling towers, sucking condensation through porous membranes. Tiny, clockwork-legged mites, billions of them, carpet the wet surfaces in shimmering waves, scouring the rust from the joints. In the deeper pools, blind, pale things with skin like wet parchment weave through the submerged cables, feeding on the electrical leakages. 

The fauna is overwhelming—eyes blink from the gaps in the masonry; soft, leathery wings flap in the rafters; the sound of a million tiny carapaces clicking against wet brass creates a texture so thick it feels like a physical weight.

Yet, for all this life, the world is eerily quiet. The machines do not roar; they hum at a frequency just below the threshold of human hearing, a bone-deep vibration that makes the water in the puddles dance in geometric cymatic patterns. The animals do not scream or call; they communicate through subtle electrical pulses and the soft, wet sliding of flesh against metal.

The terrain is young in its details but ancient in its bones. Because the world is "built" at a level of 3.0, even the erosion follows mechanical rules. The sharp edges of the iron stairs and the crisp moldings of the brass intake valves suggest they were cast only yesterday, yet they drip with the ancient residue of a thousand cycles. There is no decay, only a constant, static maintenance.

The most striking feature of this place is its terrifying stability. It is dynamic at a 0.2 scale—which is to say, it is frozen in a state of high-functioning equilibrium. The gear-teeth do not grind down; they are replaced by the mites as they wear. The water does not evaporate away; it is reclaimed by the ceiling and shed back down. The temperature remains a constant, stifling 85 degrees Fahrenheit. Nothing grows, nothing dies, nothing evolves. The "industrial" nature of the place is not one of progress or production, but of a closed-loop existence. It is a factory that produces only its own continued presence.

You could walk for years in any direction and find only this: the gleaming, wet metal floor, the sweating pipes, the silent, overwhelming swarm of pale, soft-bodied creatures, and the amber light of a captive sun, all held in a vast, breathless stillness. It is a world that has reached its final form and found it to be a damp, iron cathedral.
