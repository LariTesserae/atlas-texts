The air here does not hold moisture; it holds heat and a dry, metallic static. This is a vertical world, a landscape defined by an upward violence that has not yet been smoothed by the passage of time or the friction of rain. There is no water to carve valleys, no wind old enough to have rounded the jagged edges. Everything is raw, ascending, and newborn.

The floor of this world is a deep, shadowed abyss, and the "ground" is merely a collection of soaring, needle-thin spires that rise kilometers into a charcoal sky. Because the light is so low—a dim, bruised twilight provided only by the faint, internal glow of the lithology itself—the landscape reveals itself through silhouettes and the heat it radiates against the skin.

The material of these spires is not common stone. It is a cooling, vitreous mineral, something like obsidian but infused with veins of phosphorescent sulfur. Because the erosion is near zero, the peaks are impossibly sharp. They are like obsidian shards the size of cathedrals, reaching toward the upper atmosphere in a desperate, crowded rush. 

The warmth here is visceral. It does not come from a sun, but from the thermal energy of creation. You can feel the vibration of the crust beneath your feet—a low-frequency hum that suggests the entire region is still being extruded from a pressurized core. The state of the world is embryonic. These mountains are not eroding; they are growing. 

The dynamics of the place are turbulent and unstable. Occasionally, with a sound like a shattering tectonic plate, a new spire will thrust upward from the depths, displacing the dark air with a roar of kinetic heat. Or, a section of a cliffside, still soft and viscous at its core, will buckle and fold over itself like cooling taffy, changing the topography of a hundred square kilometers in a single, agonizingly slow hour. It is a "Solaris" of stone—a mineral mind or a blind geological engine that is constantly iterating on its own form.

Life here is unrecognizable. There are no plants, no chlorophyll, no lungs. Instead, something simpler and more alien clings to the vertical faces: lithotrophic webs. They look like fine, silver threads or crystalline lace, stretching between the gaps of the spires. They are not biological in our sense; they are mineral encrustations that move with the heat, growing toward the warmest patches of the obsidian walls. They pulse with a faint, rhythmic amber light, harvesting the thermal energy of the shifting mountains. They are sparse—lonely constellations of silver silk in a vast, dark cathedral of glass.

Scattered throughout this vertical desert are the "Built." These are not cities, nor even ruins, but singular, monumental marks of intent. Every few hundred miles, a spire has been modified. You might find a perfectly circular hole, a hundred meters wide, bored through the heart of an obsidian peak. Or a series of colossal, flat platforms made of a denser, matte-black material, cantilevered out over the void without any visible support. They are silent, cold, and ancient—fixed points of stillness in a world that is otherwise folding and thrusting. There is no technology left behind, no wires or glowing screens; only the heavy, oppressive geometry of something that once stood here and watched the mountains grow.

The sound is a study in extremes. Most of the time, there is only the "mostly quiet"—the sound of hot air rising through a flute of glass, a faint, whistling hiss. But this is punctuated by the sudden, terrifying crack of a peak fracturing under its own heat, a crystalline "BOOM" that echoes indefinitely through the high, thin air. 

Standing on one of the high precipices, looking out over the flickering amber webs and the black needles of the range, you feel the scale of the place. It is open, vast, and terrifyingly empty. It is a world that is "becoming," a dry, dark furnace of geometry and glass, where the only rule is the upward pressure of the deep earth and the slow, silver hunger of the mineral webs. There is no place for a soft thing here; there is only the heat, the dark, and the infinite, rising edge.
