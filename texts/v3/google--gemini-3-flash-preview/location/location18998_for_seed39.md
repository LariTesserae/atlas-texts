At this altitude, the air is too thin to hold warmth, but it is thick enough to hold an ocean of mist. We are high above the world’s lungs, in a realm of jagged, prenatal geology where the mountains have not yet been softened by time. The peaks are needles of unweathered basalt and raw obsidian, black and glass-sharp, thrusting upward into a dim, permafrost sky. 

There is a profound sense of *incompleteness* here. The rock is not old; it is fresh, cooling from some deep, interior birth. The edges of the cliffs are so crisp they could cut the wind. Because erosion has not yet begun its slow work of sanding and smoothing, the landscape is a collection of impossible geometries—shelves of rock that jut out over miles of vertical emptiness, perfectly flat plateaus, and deep, narrow fissures that breathe steam.

Water is everywhere but it does not flow in rivers. Instead, it weeps. Because of the extreme elevation and the cold, the atmosphere is a constant engine of condensation. The black stone is permanently slick, glazed in a thin, shivering film of meltwater and fog-drip. In the basins between the sharp peaks, the water gathers in shallow, black mires—marshes of liquid slush and silt that never quite freeze but never grow warm.

The scale is vast and hollow. You can look across a valley and see a peak thirty miles away, separated by a void so clean and empty it feels like a physical ache. There is nothing to break the sightline; no trees, no forests, no hills. Only the black teeth of the earth and the gray veil of the sky.

Vegetation is a desperate, rare occurrence. It clings to the sheltered lee of the crags—tough, translucent mosses that look like frozen bile, and low, wiry shrubs with leaves like needles of ice. They do not sway; they huddle.

And yet, despite the terrestrial silence, the landscape hums with a heavy, industrial rhythmic throb.

This is not a natural mountain range; it is a planetary engine. Massive, automated iron extractors—the size of city blocks—crawl on multi-jointed hydraulic legs across the vertical faces of the mountains. They are dark, rusted hulks of high-tech industry, mindless and autonomous. They hiss with pressurized steam, their massive drills grinding into the "embryonic" rock to harvest the raw minerals within the mountain’s core. The sound is a distant, low-frequency vibration—the *chug-hiss, chug-hiss* of titanic pistons firing in the mist. 

These machines are the only "architecture," and they are not built for habitation. They are nomadic factories, dripping with oil and condensation, leaving trail-marks of crushed stone behind them. They move with a slow, biological pace, like iron beetles or grazing whales made of soot and steel.

The fauna has adapted to the giants. This is a crowded ecological niche. Clouds of high-altitude "glider-bats" with leathery, iridescent wings cling to the warm exhaust vents of the walking factories. Small, six-legged scavengers with coats of thick, white grease skitter over the machine’s hulls, feeding on the mineral dust and the thick lubricants that leak from the joints. In the marshes below, massive bipedal birds with heavy, downy plumage stalks through the slush, their eyes wide to catch the dim, filtered light. They hunt the oily grubs that fall from the bellies of the machines.

It is a world of active, cold motion. The weather cycles are frantic—sudden flurries of sleet give way to heavy, blinding fogs, which are then cleared by the scorching thermal plumes of the industrial harvesters. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and heavy diesel. 

There is no "civilization" here—no houses, no roads, no intentional design. There is only the raw, unformed earth, the mindless machines that harvest it, and the opportunistic animals that have learned to live in the shadow of the pistons. It is a place of birth and extraction, happening simultaneously in a cold, dim, and terrifyingly open sky.
