The air here is a thick, velvet weight, saturated with a humidity that feels more like a physical suspension than a gas. It is constant, a low-lying basin where the heat (an unwavering, sweltering 2.7) traps the moisture against a floor of dark, weathered stone. The scale is regional—a vast, shallow depression the size of a city-state, stretching toward a horizon obscured by a perpetual, dim orange haze.

This is a world of **sweating mineralogy**. There is no green here, no chlorophyll, no cellulose. Instead, the landscape is defined by the "built"—an ancient, industrial sprawl of black iron, basalt masonry, and tiered copper piping that has been eroded by centuries of salt-heavy condensation. The architecture is dense but hollowed out; towering skeletal frameworks and massive, rusted vats rise from a floor of black grit. These structures do not feel like ruins, however. They feel like a permanent, geologic feature of the terrain, weathered into "established" forms as natural as a canyon.

The ground is never dry. Water, dark and oily, pulses through the lowlands in shallow, sluggish sheets. It weeps from the joints of the iron towers and pools in the centers of vast, circular basins. Because the temperature is so high, the water is constantly transitioning—hissing softly as it contacts hot vents, thickening into a heavy, ground-clinging fog, and then raining back down in large, warm droplets that taste of copper and brine.

Despite the industrial density, the world feels "empty" in a volumetric sense. The massive structures provide a skeleton for the world, but the spaces between them are vast, vacant voids of gray air. There is no clutter, no debris—only the immense, rusting monuments and the water that flows around their feet.

The "life" of this place is paradoxically overwhelming yet quiet. In the absence of plants, the fauna has claimed every available niche. They are not strange or surreal creatures; they are deeply recognizable in their biology, yet they exist in a state of hyper-abundance. 

The air is thick with millions of pale, leathery-winged insects, their carapaces clacking softly like dry seeds as they settle on the sweating iron. In the shallow waters, schools of heavy, scavenger fish—armored with thick scales to withstand the heat—churn the silt in a rhythmic, restless motion. On the rusted catwalks, colonies of sleek, hairless mammals, similar to colossal otters or seals, sprawl in the heat, their breathing a synchronized, low-frequency hum that vibrates the metal underfoot.

It is a world of "normal" biology pushed to an industrial scale. The animals do not scream or roar; the soundscape is one of muffled, wet mechanical processes. There is the persistent *clink-drip-hiss* of the cooling iron, the heavy *slap* of water against stone, and the distant, rhythmic *thrum* of geothermal pumps that still cycle deep beneath the earth, unaware that their masters have long since vanished.

The light is a permanent twilight. The sun is a pale, bloated disk filtered through layers of steam and industrial particulate, casting long, soft shadows. There are no sharp edges here; the erosion has rounded the corners of the great factories, and the constant moisture has blurred the silhouettes of the teeming beasts.

Movement is the primary language of the landscape. The biological pace is frantic beneath the surface stillness: the constant cycle of heat-exchange, the ceaseless migration of the swarms from one iron tower to the next, and the slow, inevitable creep of oxidation eating away at the world’s skeleton. It is a productive, active, and crowded world that asks for nothing, fueled by the heat of the earth and the unending cycle of the rain. It is a city of beasts, built on a foundation of iron, drowning in a warm, permanent fog.
