The ground here does not know the concept of a river. It is a world of bone-dry mineral silt and expansive, unweathered plates of silicate that have not yet been broken by the patience of water. The elevation is a constant, low-slung plain, stretching out toward a horizon that feels impossibly distant, as if the curvature of the world is too gentle to notice. Because there has been so little erosion, the land is sharp-edged and raw; it looks like a sculpture only half-hewn from the block, a continental expanse of "new" geology that has never felt a raindrop or a freeze-thaw cycle.

Yet, despite this profound thirst, the world is crowded. It is a paradox of dry lushness.

Vegetation here does not rely on aqueous sap. Instead, the flora is a dense, high-biomass architecture of fibrous, resinous stalks and waxy, carbon-rich leaves that shimmer with a dull, metallic luster. The "forests" are not groves of trees but tightly packed, vertical monoliths of biological matter that grow so close together there is no room to pass between them. They resemble organic skyscrapers—cellular, organized, and structural. These are the "built" elements of the world: vast, urban-scale complexes of biological infrastructure. These living towers grow in hexagonal grids, creating "streets" of bare mineral dust that are perfectly straight, as if planned by a city’s architect, though they are birthed purely by the competitive pressure of root systems.

The scale is gargantuan. These biological megacities stretch for thousands of miles, honeycombing the flat earth. From a height, the landscape looks like a sprawling, infinite metropolis, a green and bronze circuitry covering the planet's face.

The air is warm and heavy with the scent of dry spices and heated dust. Because there is no water to cycle through the atmosphere, the weather is driven by the heat of the sun hitting the dark, packed leaves. This creates massive, slow-moving thermal currents—invisible dust-storms that carry seeds and spores in silent, swirling patterns. 

The silence is the most striking feature. Even though the world is teeming with life, there is no rustle of wind through wet leaves, no trickle of streams. Sound is muffled by the incredible density of the biological mass. The fauna move with a predatory, muffled grace. Many-legged creatures with chitinous plates glide through the narrow corridors of the living city, their feet padded with thick, felt-like hairs that swallow the sound of their movement. They are abundant—vast herds of dry-climate grazers that consume the resinous secretions of the towers, and slim, silent hunters that track them through the endless, sun-dappled alleys.

Everything is in a state of high dynamic motion, but it is a silent motion. The towers are constantly growing, shedding skins of bark that pile up in crisp, unrotting layers. New "buildings" of vegetation push through the mineral crust with tectonic force, their expansion slow but irresistible. It is an ecosystem that functions with the organized complexity of a high-tech civilization, yet there is not a single tool, no metal, no fire. The "tech" here is purely genetic, seen in the way the plants grow into specific, functional shapes—bridges, canopies, and storage vessels for volatile gases—to better survive the crowding.

The light is steady and bright, filtered through the upper reaches of the canopy into shafts that illuminate the dust-motes. It is a world that feels "full." There is no emptiness, no "wild" space that is not already occupied by the rigid, urban geometry of the flora. It is a planet-wide hive of quiet activity, a dry, warm, and crowded masterpiece of biological engineering, still raw from its own creation.
