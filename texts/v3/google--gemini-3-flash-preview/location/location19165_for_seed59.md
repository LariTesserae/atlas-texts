The air here is a heavy, chill silk, saturated with a constant suspended drizzle that never quite resolves into rain. It is a world of verticality and drip, held in a high-altitude basin where the clouds come to rest and stay.

This is not a forest that grows upon a mountain; it is a city of plants that *is* the mountain. There is no mineral earth visible. Every square inch of substrate is a complex, weaving carpet of liverworts and thick, freezing mosses that hold water like sponges. Beneath the surface layers, centuries of compressed peat and interlocking root-systems form the "stone" of this place. The terrain is a series of bulbous, oversaturated plateaus connected by sagging vines and living bridges, all suspended over an abyss of misty green.

The architecture is indistinguishable from the ecology. The inhabitants—a dense, bustling population of humanoid figures clad in rain-slicked furs and woven reed capes—live within the hollowed trunks of gargantuan, tree-sized ferns. They have not built so much as they have guided the growth. Ladders are not constructed of timber but are the lateral rungs of climbing epiphytes, reinforced with simple fiber lashings. Walkways are thick, matted cables of living willow-variant, beaten flat by the constant transit of thousands of feet. 

The heat of the place is low, a persistent "refrigerator-cool" that keeps the water from evaporating, yet the energy of the environment is frantic. It is a bazaar of the biological. The density is claustrophobic; everywhere you look, something is being harvested, woven, or eaten. 

The sound is an overwhelming, textured roar. It is the combination of a thousand trickling rivulets of runoff falling from height, the constant squelch of many feet on saturated moss, and the deafening, rhythmic chorus of the fauna. The animals here are as dense as the plants. Iridescent, heavy-bodied beetles the size of loaves of bread hum through the air in erratic swarms, their wings vibrating with a low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the chest. Tiny, pale amphibians with translucent skin cling to every weeping rock-face, chirping in a shrill, overlapping cacophony that signals the shifting of the mist.

This is a "high" place, not in the sense of a barren peak, but a lofty, oxygen-rich shelf where the pressure of life has reached a boiling point. The dynamic is one of "turbulent stability." It is an anthill at peak hour. People move with a frantic, purposeful agility, leaping across gaps in the vegetation, hauling nets full of succulent water-tubers, and maintaining the vast, primitive infrastructure of clay-lined troughs that direct the constant flow of water away from the living quarters. 

There is a primitive, heavy industry of the hand here. You see groups of people using stone-weighted presses to squeeze the moisture from giant lily pads, turning them into stiff, waterproof shingles. Others use simple bone scrapers to strip fibers from the ubiquitous vines, spinning them into cordage even as they walk. The "urban" feel comes from this density of labor; there is no wilderness here, only managed growth. Every branch has a purpose; every dripping overhang is a sheltered workshop.

The light is a steady, pearlescent glow, filtered through leagues of overhead canopy and heavy fog. It lacks a clear source, casting soft shadows and making the saturated greens and bruised purples of the flora glow with a dull, inner luminosity. 

Life here is a cold, wet, and incredibly fast-paced struggle. The world is in a state of constant, high-speed repair. Because everything is organic, everything is rotting and growing simultaneously. A bridge might collapse under the weight of a heavy fruiting, only to be woven back together by a dozen laborers before the sun moves a hand-span. It is a landscape that never rests, a high-altitude swamp-city that breathes, drips, and shouts in the cold, bright mist.
