The heat is the first thing that defines the space: a heavy, wet, sweltering pressure that carries the weight of a greenhouse without the presence of green. It is a low-lying basin, a regional depression where the world has settled into a state of permanent, steaming saturation.

The ground is not soil, but a complex, non-geological substrate—an urban sprawl of mineralized structures that were never built by hands. Imagine a city made of calcified bone and weeping salt, stretching out into the dark. These are "urban" formations in their density and organization—streets of ribbed obsidian, plazas of porous, honeycomb-like stone, and towering, windowless obelisks—but there is no technology here. There are no wires, no lights, no engines. The architecture is a biological or metaphysical byproduct, a coral-like growth that mimics the geometry of a metropolis.

It is profoundly dark. The sky is a ceiling of thick, churning vapor that traps the heat and smothers the stars. Light exists only as a rare, dying ember deep within the translucent walls of the structures, or the occasional faint shimmer of bioluminescent silt washing through the gutters.

Water is the primary actor here. It doesn't just fall; it oozes. The atmosphere is so thick with moisture that the air feels like a thin liquid. Every surface is slick. Low-lying areas are flooded with knee-deep pools of warm, brackish brine that taste of iron and old copper. The "erosion" is long-established; the water has carved deep, rhythmic fluting into the sides of the stone buildings, smoothing the edges of the city until it looks like a wax model melting in the sun.

There is a disturbing lack of life. No birds cry in the heights; no insects hum in the stagnant pools. The "vegetation" consists only of rare, fleshy stalks—taller than a man but few and far between—that rise from the muck like lonely, leafless fingers. They do not photosynthesize; they seem to breathe, expanding and contracting with a wet, leathery sound.

The reality of the place is fractured. The "physics" of the landscape are governed by a logic that predates matter. Occasionally, the water does not flow downward but crawls up the sides of the buildings in shimmering sheets, defying gravity to pool on the undersides of overhangs. The perspective of the streets is off—a walk toward a distant spire might take hours, or the spire might suddenly be behind you, its reflection shimmering in a puddle that wasn't there a moment ago. 

Sound is rare and muffled by the heavy air. You hear the constant, rhythmic *thrum-slap* of water hitting stone, and the occasional low, groaning vibration of the earth shifting. It is the sound of a massive, wet machine made of rock trying to turn over in its sleep.

The scale is vast. From a slight rise in the "terrain"—a mound of fused glass and silt—you can see the city extending for miles into the gloom. It is a hollow civilization. It is a place where the infrastructure for life exists—the shelters, the paths, the reservoirs—but the life itself never arrived, or was long ago dissolved by the heat and the salt. Everything is active, in motion, and churning, but it is the motion of decay and mineral growth, a slow, geological metabolism that needs no inhabitants to justify its existence.
