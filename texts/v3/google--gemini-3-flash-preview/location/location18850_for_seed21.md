The air here is a thick, shimmering suspension of heat and humidity, pressed against a high-altitude plateau that feels unnervingly close to the sun. At this elevation, the sky is a deep, bruised indigo, yet the atmosphere is so saturated with moisture that the light does not travel in straight lines; it diffuses through a constant, heavy silver mist that clings to the skin like a warm, wet veil.

This is a young landscape, an uplifted shelf of obsidian-slick basalt and jagged silicate that seems to have burst through the crust only recently. The edges of the rocks are razor-sharp, unrounded by the passage of eons, yet they are already weeping. Everywhere, water is being forced upward. It doesn't fall as rain so much as it perspires from the stones themselves. Every vertical face of dark rock is slick with a downward-crawling film of water, and the ground—a jigsaw of sharp plates—is interrupted by perfectly circular, bubbling pools that boil not from heat, but from a pressurized subterranean gas that smells faintly of ozone and crushed mint.

The scale of the place is intimate, a high-mountain courtyard hemmed in by looming, needle-like crags that act as natural walls. It is a "cathedral of the damp," perhaps no larger than a village square, yet it feels infinitely complex.

The vegetation is lush but strangely spaced. Instead of a tangled jungle, there is a deliberate, rhythmic distribution of life. Great, translucent ferns with fronds like spun glass rise from the wet crevices. Their stalks are hollow, pulsing with a visible, bioluminescent sap that moves in time with a slow, planetary heartbeat. Between these glass-ferns, the ground is carpeted in a moss so thick and saturated it feels like walking on a waterlogged sponge. These moss-beds are a vibrant, chemical green, startlingly bright against the black rock.

Within this small, steaming sanctuary, there are "marks." They are not ruins in the traditional sense, for they do not look decayed. Scattered across the plateau are several floating rings of a matte-white, frictionless material. They hover exactly three feet above the bubbling pools, perfectly stationary despite the high-altitude winds. These rings are fragments of a technology that bypasses the mechanical; they hum with a low-frequency vibration that keeps the surrounding mist from ever fully condensing into droplets. Beside one ring, a thin pillar of liquid light rises directly into the sky—a tether of pure information or energy that makes the hair on your arms stand up. It is "built" in the way a shadow is built: it occupies space without possessing weight.

The ecology is a quiet, lonely clockwork. Fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the *glimmer-skaters*—six-legged insects the size of dinner plates with iridescent carapaces. They do not walk; they use the high-tech resonance of the hovering rings to glide an inch above the moss, harvesting the ozone-rich bubbles from the pools. Their movements are the only sudden gestures in this world.

Sound is swallowed by the moisture. The world is a muffled sequence of wet pops from the gas pools, the rhythmic *thrum* of the white rings, and the constant, directional hiss of water sliding over stone. It is a lonely sound, the sound of a laboratory left running in the middle of a cloud.

Everything here is in a state of high-energy stasis. The heat is oppressive, nearly three units of solar intensity, yet the water never evaporates; it is held in a cycle of perpetual emergence and re-absorption by the strange white rings. The rules of physics here have been edited. Gravity feels slightly lighter, as if the plateau is being buoyed by the same force that holds the rings aloft. You are standing in a high-altitude greenhouse designed by an intelligence that has moved past the need for walls, using light and magnetism to garden the very clouds. It is a place of "active stillness"—a young, sharp piece of the world being groomed by ancient, invisible hands.
