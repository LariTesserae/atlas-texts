The air here is not air, but a thin, chilled suspension of silicate dust and dry, aromatic ethers. It carries no moisture; to breathe it is to feel the velvet rasp of microscopic needles against the throat. This is a vertical world, a kingdom of sheer obsidian needles and limestone fins that pierce upward through a perpetual violet dusk. We are at the roof of the world, where the gravity feels precarious and the stars are visible even during the dim, bruised-colored day.

The terrain is a nightmare of elevation. Every surface is a precipice. The ground does not exist as a flat plane; it is a series of razor-thin ridges and plummeting chasms that fall away into a bottomless, fog-filled throat of gray gas. The rocks are ancient, black, and glass-smooth, weathered by eons of dry wind into shapes that resemble frozen ribbons or the interior of a hollowed-out bone. There is no water—not a drop, not a crystal of ice. The chemistry here is based on the slow, dry friction of powders and the sublimation of solid minerals into gas.

Yet, this desolation is densely, impossibly inhabited.

Sprawled across the vertical faces of these cliffs are cities. They are not built of wood or metal, for there is no fire and no forge. Instead, they are grown—vast, calcified hives of interconnected cells and vaulted corridors that cling to the rock like coral. These "urban" sprawls stretch for miles along the cliffside, thousands of tiers of windowless, arching architecture. There is no technology here; no wires, no lights, no engines. The construction is a feat of biological or perhaps geological masonry, a communal accretion of mineral-rich bile and compressed dust, hardened into a substance stronger than granite.

The silence is the most prominent inhabitant. It is a heavy, pressurized quiet, broken only by the occasional *chink-chink* of a loose pebble falling miles into the abyss, or the low, mournful moan of gases moving through the hollowed-out towers.

There are no plants. The only life is fungal and alien. In the crannies of the stone, pale, translucent ruffles of "glass-moss" grow in the dark, drawing energy from the static electricity generated by the dry winds. They do not photosynthesize; they vibrate. When the wind picks up, the entire cliffside hums at a frequency just below the threshold of human hearing, a sound that vibrates in the marrow of the teeth.

But the world is breaking. Despite the stillness and the cold, there is a frantic, impossible dynamic at work. 

In this high, thin atmosphere, the laws of Euclidean geometry are beginning to fray through overuse. You will see a bridge of white stone connecting two spires, only to watch it fold into itself and vanish as you walk toward it, reappearing a hundred yards higher. The perspective here is deceptive; a tower that looks miles away might be reached in three steps, provided you do not look down. Shadow and light do not behave. A shadow might cast itself toward the dim sun, or detach from its owner to linger on a wall for several minutes after they have departed.

The rare fauna are the architects of this madness. There are things here—large, spindly-limbed beings made of the same gray-white mineral as the cities. They move with an agonizing, glacial slowness, their bodies unfolding like origami. They possess no eyes, only sensitive pits that track the warping of the air. They are not animals in the traditional sense; they are more like sentient sculptures, rearranging the stones of their cities to compensate for the shifting reality. As a section of the cliff begins to phase out of existence, these creatures enter a frantic, clicking dance, anchoring the architecture with tethers of spun silk made of liquid quartz.

The environment is in a constant state of "active repair." The world is trying to dissolve, to return to the void that presses in from the edges of the horizon, and the cities are the only thing holding it together. The "urban" density is a survival tactic—a collective weight of consciousness and construction intended to pin down a landscape that wants to float away.

The light is a constant, sallow twilight. There is no moon, only a swirling nebula of glowing dust that hangs in the zenith, casting a pale, sickly amber glow over the vertical labyrinths. It is cold, dry, and terrifyingly vast. To stand here is to feel like an ant on the edge of a broken mirror, watching as the reflection of the universe slowly, quietly, pulls itself apart.
