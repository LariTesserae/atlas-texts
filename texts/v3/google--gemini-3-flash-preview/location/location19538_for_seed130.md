The ground is not earth, but a vast, sagging quilt of oxidized iron plates and corrugated lead, stretching toward an horizon that refused to remain horizontal. In this low-lying basin, the scale is oceanic; a valley of manufactured planes that have buckled under the weight of eons. There is no soil here, only the "Deep Built"—a crust of dead machinery and architectural foundations so ancient they have achieved a state of geological permanence.

The temperature is a constant, biting chill that never reaches freezing but clings to the skin like a wet shroud. The air is heavy, saturated to the point of collapse. It does not rain in drops; rather, the atmosphere curdles into a pervasive, weeping fog that coats every metallic surface in a shimmering film of grey moisture. Water does not flow so much as it "seeps" through the seams of the world, collecting in shallow, oily lagoons that sit in the depressions of rusted cooling towers.

The erosion here is profound. It is the erosion of intent. The sharp edges of the industrial age have been blunted by millions of years of chemical weeping. Massive cogs, once part of some unthinkable engine, have been worn down into smooth, rounded mounds that resemble river stones the size of cathedrals. The rivets have dissolved into orange weeping sores. The world is settling into its final, flattened form—a planished graveyard of utility.

Everything is made, yet nothing is whole.

Vegetation is a rare, desperate occurrence. It is not organic, but a lithic mimicry: black, glass-like mosses that grow only in the rhythmic pulse of leaking electrolytes, and thin, wiry filaments of copper-colored lichen that crackle when the wind catches them. They cluster in the "low-tide" areas of the basin, sparse and solitary, like survivors of a wreck.

Then there is the sound. It is a deafening, industrial roar that does not come from any one source, but is the collective voice of the landscape itself. It is the sound of a world-sized machine that was never turned off, but was never maintained. It is a dissonant chorus of grinding tectonic plates of steel, the hysterical screaming of steam venting from subterranean ruptures, and the rhythmic, percussive thud of massive, unseen pistons deep within the crust. The noise is so thick it feels like a physical weight, a pressure against the chest that fluctuates with the "weather."

The darkness is nearly absolute, a dim, 0.5-level twilight where the only illumination comes from the "Ghost Glow"—the faint, bioluminescent flickering of short-circuiting power grids buried miles below. These pulses of sickly blue and amber light move beneath the translucent sheets of ice and oil, tracing the veins of the world like the nervous system of a dying god.

The dynamics of this place are not biological, but mechanical-metabolic. There is a predator-prey relationship between the different systems of decay. The "weeping" water erodes the structural integrity of the high-pylons; as they collapse, they provide "nutrient" surfaces for the glass-moss to spread. The wind is a localized pressure differential caused by massive, automated fans that still spin in the dark, miles away, pushing the cold, wet air in predictable, agonizing cycles.

There are no animals. No birds wheel in the sulfurous gloom. No insects skitter over the rusted dunes. There is only the motion of the infrastructure. The sudden, violent shudder of a settling floor-plate. The slow, rhythmic groan of a crane arm swinging in a circle for the ten-millionth year. The landscape is active, vibrant with the energy of a slow-motion collapse, a perpetual-motion machine that has forgotten its purpose and is now simply consuming its own corpse in the dark.
