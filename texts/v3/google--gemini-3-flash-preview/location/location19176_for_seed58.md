The heat is the first thing that asserts itself—a heavy, radiant thrumming that feels as though the air itself has mass. It is not the heat of a sun, but the heat of an engine.

You are descending into the **Caldera Sink**, a subterranean industrial hamlet wedged into a shallow, basaltic depression. Though it sits below the surface, it isn't deep; it is more like a cellar window into the earth’s crust. The walls are young, the stone still holding the jagged, obsidian-sharp edges of a recent tectonic rift. There has been no time for the wind to round the corners or for water to carve its signature. The geometry is brutal and freshman.

Everything here is packed tight. This is a "dense" world, a labyrinthine settlement the size of a city block but with the complexity of a metropolis. Thousands of metal conduits, brass steam-pipes, and swaying electrical cables bridge the gap between the buildings. The structures are built upward despite the ceiling, leaning against one another like tired giants. Corrugated iron, riveted steel, and vitrified brick form a vertical slum of industry.

**The Water:**
It is not wet, but it is never dry. The heat creates a persistent, shivering condensation. Beads of moisture form on the underside of every cold pipe and drip rhythmically onto the hot stone below, where they instantly hiss and vanish. This is the "intermittent moisture"—a cycle of sweating metal and rising vapor. In the gaps between the foundries, there are "cool-pockets" where the damp gathers into oily slicks, reflecting the dim amber lights of the catwalks.

**The Industry and Light:**
The light is dim, filtered through a permanent ceiling of yellowing steam. Most of the illumination comes from the glowing vents of the furnaces and the flickering cathode lamps bolted to the brickwork. There is a sense of desperate, mechanical productivity. Great flywheels turn slowly in the shadows; piston-driven pumps hammer at the earth’s heat, extracting power from the tectonic friction. It is industrial in a raw, Victorian-analog sense—there are no computers here, only governors, valves, and the smell of hot grease.

**The Vegetation and Ecology:**
Life is a persistent afterthought. Because there are no animals—not even the scuttle of a cockroach or the drone of a fly—the world feels strangely sterile despite its grime. Only the hardiest, most opportunistic flora survives in the cracks. Sparse, pale mosses that thrive on the heat-sweat of the pipes cling to the brickwork. Occasionally, a cluster of "boiler-vines"—tough, waxy, translucent stalks—wraps around a steam pipe, drawing warmth rather than light. They don't have leaves; they have radiator-like fins that vibrate in the rising heat.

**The Sound and Dynamic:**
Despite the visual chaos of the pipes and the density of the buildings, the Sink is mostly quiet. The sound is a low-frequency vibration, a collective hum of a thousand small machines working in unison. It is the sound of a sleeping factory. 

But the world is highly active. This is not a graveyard; it is a breathing system. Every few minutes, a pressure-release valve screams in a distant corridor, followed by a heavy "thump" that shakes the floorboards. The "weather" here is the movement of steam. Huge billows of hot fog roll through the narrow alleys, triggered by the cooling cycles of the main generators. It moves with a biological pace, creeping around corners like a predator, changing the visibility from twenty feet to two inches in a heartbeat.

**The Rules of the World:**
In the Caldera Sink, the vertical is more important than the horizontal. To move through the space, one does not walk streets; one climbs ladders, crosses rattling gantry-ways, and squeezes through "crawl-spaces" between the machinery. There is no horizon. The sky is a ceiling of black rock and white pipes. 

The air is thick with the scent of ozone and parboiled copper. Because there is no wind to clear the heat, the temperature remains a constant, oppressive 2.9/3—hot enough to make the skin prickle and the breath come short. 

It is a place of restless stasis. The machines are always working, the steam is always rising, and the water is always dripping—yet nothing ever scales beyond these jagged, young walls. It is a dense, intricate pocket of human-made industry, humming away in the dark, hot belly of the earth, unobserved by anything with eyes.
