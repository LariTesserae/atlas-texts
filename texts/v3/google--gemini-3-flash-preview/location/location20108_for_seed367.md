This is the interior of a hollowed, fossilized seed-pod the size of a cathedral, snagged for eternity on a high, windless plateau where the air is thin and baked stone-dry. 

Inside this amber-hued shell, life has forgotten the concept of rain. There is no open water here; the environment operates on a closed-loop system of metabolic vapors and thick, waxy resins. The world is a vertical town built into the ribbed, inner walls of the pod. It is a humid, airless city of velvet and chitin, perched miles above the flat, eroded plains of the outside world, yet contained entirely within a space no larger than a merchant’s hall.

The terrain is an architectural jungle. Giant, succulent mosses—thicker than a man’s waist and blooming with oily, orange flowers—carpet the inner ribs of the shell. They do not need soil; they feed on the dust of the decaying pod itself. These plants have no leaves, only heavy, water-retaining bladders that pulse like slow hearts. The "lushness" here is a suffocating, crowded green-brown, an overlapping of ferns that feel like dried leather and vines that secrete a sticky, sweet-smelling glue to trap drifting minerals.

The light is a constant, dim ochre. It filters through the translucent, ancient walls of the seed-pod, stained the color of old tea. Because the sun of the plateau is harsh, the interior remains a steady, baking warmth—a kiln-like heat that never breaks. 

Humans, or things like them, have built a dense, primitive metropolis within this organic cavern. They live in "basket-cells" woven from the fibrous veins of the pod, suspended by ropes of braided hair and cured vine. This is an urbanity of extreme proximity. Homes are stacked like cells in a beehive, separated only by thin membranes of pressed pith. There are no streets, only a chaotic network of ladders, hand-carved notches in the cellulose walls, and swinging bridges. The tech is tactile and manual: bone-needles for sewing the thick moss-pelts, stone pestles for grinding dried spores into flour, and clay lamps fueled by insect fat.

The sound is a relentless, vibrating roar. It is not the wind, but the fauna. 

The air is thick with a "biological weather." Hives of heavy, flightless beetles scuttle across the ceilings, their carapaces clicking like a thousand looms. Clouds of tiny, translucent midges—millions of them—swirl in the center of the pod, their collective wings creating a low-frequency hum that vibrates in the marrow of one's bones. In the shadowed crevices between the moss-beds, feathered lizards hiss and scrap over territories no larger than a handspan. The density of life is so high that one cannot move without displacing a living creature. To step onto a bridge is to startle a dozen nesting birds; to reach for a handhold is to touch the warm, leathery skin of a sleeping bat-thing.

The rules of this world are dictated by the scarcity of moisture. There is no drinking; there is only the "Licking of the Walls," a ritualized gathering at dawn when the temperature shift causes a thin, microscopic film of condensation to bloom on the coolest parts of the shell. Survival is a frantic, high-energy scramble. Because the environment is "active," nothing stays still. The mosses grow inches per day, threatening to swallow the woven houses; the inhabitants must constantly prune and harvest the very walls of their city to keep the thoroughfares open.

Every surface is polished smooth by the passage of millions of hands and carapaces over thousands of years. The pod is "ancient"—eroded from within by the sheer friction of so much life. The original ridges of the seed are now rounded nubs, worn down like river stones, though no river has ever touched them. 

It is a crowded, dry, deafening paradise. It is a city-state of heavy breaths and rustling wings, suspended in the amber light of a dying afternoon that has lasted for an eternity. There is no "outside." There is only the climb, the heat, and the endless, rhythmic thrum of the swarm.
