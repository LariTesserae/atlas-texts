The air here is a thick, frigid suspension of silvered vapor, held in place by the sheer density of the biomass. This is a mountain-cradle, a high-altitude basin no larger than a city square, where the world has forgotten how to be stone. 

At a glance, the terrain appears to be a cluster of jagged, gothic spires, but there is no geology here. The "bedrock" is a compressed, fossilizing mass of ancestral roots, hard as teak and slick with permafrost. Everything is green, but it is a green so dark it flirts with black, bruised by the cold.

Because the vegetation is at a maximum (2.9/3), the plants have become the architecture. They do not merely grow in the space; they *are* the space. Great, bulbous mosses have woven themselves into thick, structural walls that mimic the layout of a dense urban slum. There are "streets" here—narrow, winding capillaries of exposed peat—and "buildings" comprised of hollowed-out, living gourds and interlocking fern-canopies. It feels like a town, organized and layered, but every lintel and floorboard is respiring. 

The temperature is a constant, biting chill. It is cold enough that the water (1.5/3) does not flow so much as it weeps. It exists as a heavy, pressurized sweat on every surface. It beads on the sharp, needle-like tips of the frost-hardened succulents and collects in the deep, velvet-lined troughs of the thoroughfares. This is a world of constant condensation; the high elevation means the clouds are not above you, but among you, shredding themselves against the serrated leaves.

The scale is intimate—a "hamlet" of biology. You can walk from one end of this ecosystem to the other in a matter of minutes, but the density (2.7/3) makes it feel gargantuan. There is no negative space. To move is to brush against cold, wet membranes, to duck under low-hanging fruit-sacs the size of barrels, and to step over braided vine-cables that pulse with slow, hydraulic pressure. 

Everything is young and sharp (Erosion 1.3/3). There are no soft, rotted mounds or weathered stumps. The growth is aggressive and fresh, stabbing upward into the dim, grey light. The "buildings" of this plant-city have the crisp edges of new construction, the geometry defined by the tension of young saplings rather than the sag of old wood.

The sound is a rhythmic, muffled percussion. 
*Tlip. Tlip. Tlip.*
It is the sound of heavy droplets falling from one waxy leaf to a broad, resonant drum-leaf below. Occasionally, there is the low, tectonic groan of the plant-walls shifting as they grow, a slow-motion grinding of cellulose against cellulose. 

Fauna is a rumor. Perhaps a pale, multi-legged crustacean scuttles deep within the root-foundations, or a silent, heavy-furred moth vibrates its wings to stay warm against a bioluminescent bud. But for the most part, the plants perform all the roles of the living. 

Despite the cold and the dimness, the place is intensely active (1.6/3). It is a slow, biological engine. You can see the movement if you watch the water-clocks: specialized pitcher-plants that fill to a specific weight, tip over to irrigate a lower tier of "housing," and then snap back into place with a wet thud. The city is self-regulating, a motherboard of chlorophyll and capillary action.

There is no technology here—no metal, no fire, no tools. The "urban" feel comes from the plants’ own drive for order. They have grown into grids to maximize light-capture; they have grown bridges to share nutrients; they have grown lanterns of cold, chemical phosphorus that pulse with a faint, dying-star glow in the perma-mist.

It is a cathedral of ice and ivy, a crowded, freezing metropolis where the only citizens are the ones who cannot move, but who never stop growing.
