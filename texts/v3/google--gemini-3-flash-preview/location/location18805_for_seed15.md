The space is a colossal, flooded chamber, deep beneath a structure of unknown proportions. It is not an ocean, but a cistern—a vast, captive reservoir where the water is so heavy and omnipresent that the distinction between "room" and "sea" has dissolved. 

Everything here is built. There is no silt, no sand, no tectonic rock. The floor, walls, and the distant, unseen ceiling are composed of massive, interlocking blocks of a pale, vitrified ceramic. The geometry is fresh, the edges of the blocks sharp and unweathered, as if the water began filling this void only days after the final brick was laid. It is an infant world of hard angles and drowned architecture.

The light is blinding. It does not come from a sun, but from the very material of the walls. The ceramic glows with a relentless, humming luminescence, a bright and sterile white that turns the water into a thick, luminous gel. Visibility is paradoxically high and low at once; you can see the sharp corners of a pillar a hundred yards away, but the glare of the water itself creates a whiteout effect, a fog of pure radiance.

The heat is significant. The water is a stagnant 95 degrees, a thick, maternal warmth that makes the air—where it exists in small, trapped pockets—heavy and humid. This heat drives the dynamics of the space. Because of the temperature, the water is in constant, violent motion. Great convective plumes rise from the floor, creating shimmering thermals that buffet anything drifting within them.

The sound is a roar. It is the sound of a plumbing system designed on a planetary scale. From hidden apertures in the floor, massive jets of pressurized water scream upward, colliding with the currents descending from the ceiling. It is a booming, mechanical thrum that vibrates in the chest, the sound of a machine that is also a landscape.

Life is an afterthought here, a sparse intrusion. There are no fish, no crustaceans, no moving eyes. The only ecology consists of "The Thready Ones"—long, translucent filaments of vegetable matter that cling to the sharp edges of the ceramic blocks. They look like unspun silk or pale hair. They do not have leaves, only long, rhythmic cilia that catch the minerals suspended in the hot water. They are spaced far apart; a single cluster of white vines may claim an entire hundred-foot wall, swaying violently in the turbulent currents, their pale fronds the only break in the relentless, manufactured perfection of the cistern.

The scale is intimate despite the cavernous potential. Because the water is so dense and the light so blinding, the world feels shrunk to the immediate vicinity. You are always "embedded" within a specific corridor of current, or tucked into the right angle of a massive junction. It is a pocket of existence defined by the reach of one's hands.

The technology is high in purpose but primitive in execution. There are no circuits, no screens, no complex alloys. The entire mechanism of this place is governed by flow and weight. Great wooden-handled sluice gates, bound in simple copper, are the only tools present. They are massive, manual things that require the weight of a body to turn. The world is a clockwork of water and clay, a masterpiece of hydraulic engineering that uses the smartest geometry to achieve the most basic goal: the movement of heat and liquid through a bright, white dark.

There is no decay here yet. No rot, no rust. The water is too fresh, the ceramic too new. It is a loud, bright, drowning cathedral of a womb, waiting for its first real inhabitants, or perhaps simply content to churn its own warm weight in the silence of the deep earth.
