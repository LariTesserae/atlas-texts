The floor of this place is not earth, but a thick, compacted crust of silicate scales that clatter like ceramic shards underfoot. We are deep within a planetary hollow, a geode-chamber the size of a cathedral, held in a state of eternal, warm stagnation. There is no water here; the air is heavy with the scent of hot copper and parched ozone. It is a dry, pressurized womb of mineral and machine.

Because the erosion is so young, the geography is a nightmare of razor-edges. The walls of the cavern have not been smoothed by wind or water, but appear recently crystalline, jutting out in obsidian-sharp fractals that catch the dim, amber radiance of the ceiling-molds. It is a crowded geography. Massive, hexagonal pillars of raw basalt groan under the weight of the crust above, standing so close together that one must turn sideways to pass between them.

Amidst these jagged mineral teeth, the industrial presence hums. This is not a ruin, but an automated, headless factory. Brass conduits, thick as ancient tree trunks, are bolted directly into the rock faces. They do not pulse; they vibrate with a terrifying, constant frequency. Pistons the size of siege engines are frozen in mid-stroke, yet they radiate a dry, blistering heat. There are no operators. Small, cast-iron terminals are scattered throughout—stumpy, squat pedestals with glowing vacuum tubes that display flickering, incomprehensible data. These machines were built to endure a static eternity, their gears ground to such a perfect fit that they make no friction, only a deafening, low-frequency thrum that vibrates the marrow in your bones.

The ecology is a paradox of abundance in a wasteland. Without water, life has turned to the harvest of heat and vibration. 

Sparse, desiccated "glass-weeds" grow in the crevices of the machinery—stiff, translucent stalks that shatter if touched, feeding on the static electricity that crackles along the copper pipes. But if the flora is timid, the fauna is frantic. The cavern is dense with "clickers"—creatures the size of rats with carapaces made of overlapping manganese plates. They move in swarms of thousands, skittering over the hot pipes and the sharp rock, their many legs sounding like a million hailstones hitting a tin roof. They do not eat; they press their bellies against the vibrating conduits to absorb the kinetic hum. Above them, "flutter-shards"—leathery, wing-heavy organisms with no eyes—hang from the stalactites in overlapping layers, their wings constantly twitching in time with the factory’s drone.

The light is a sickly, unchanging copper twilight. It comes from the "phosphor-vents"—small holes in the rock where the high-pressure gases of the deep mantle leak through, glowing with a dim, steady combustion that never flickers and never dies. 

There is no "weather" here. There is no transition from morning to evening. The temperature remains at a constant, stifling 102 degrees Fahrenheit. The noise is a wall of sound—the tectonic groan of the pillars, the infinite hiss of the gas vents, and the rhythmic, metallic skittering of the armored fauna. It is a landscape caught in a perpetual "on" state, a dense, claustrophobic machine-garden where every inch of space is occupied by a wire, a wing, or a crystal edge.

The rules of this world are simple: move slowly, or be cut by the jagged youth of the stone; breath shallowly, for the air is dry enough to crack the throat; and do not expect silence. Silence would mean the end of the world, the cooling of the pipes, and the death of the silver-plated swarms. But here, the clock never ticks forward; it only hums in place, forever.
