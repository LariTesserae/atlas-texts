The ground here is not earth, but an endless expanse of fired ceramic and cast stoneware, stretching to a horizon that curves so subtly it feels infinite. It is a fabricated continent, a world-vessel of kiln-dried plates and porous, interlocking tiles. Because the erosion is so low—the world being in a state of clinical, newborn freshness—there is no sand, no dust, and no soil. The surfaces are sharp, geometric, and pristine, meeting at angles that suggest the hand of a master mason who has long since departed.

Heat is the primary inhabitant. It radiates from the pale, terracotta-colored plains in visible shimmers, pushing the temperature into a constant, baking fever. The light is steady and solar, pinning the shadows of the few standing structures—colossal, hollow obelisks of basalt-like brick—directly beneath them. 

Despite the heat, the world is punctuated by a specific, rhythmic dampness. Because the scale is vast enough to possess its own atmospheric cells, the moisture does not fall from local clouds but arrives as a "sweat" from the architecture itself. In the cooling hours of the high-altitude cycles, condensation beads on the millions of miles of ceramic grooves. This moisture funnels into narrow, shallow gulleys built into the floor of the world. It is a riverine system without mud; clear, distilled water slips over glazed channels, pooling in wide, circular cisterns that act as the only breaks in the arid heat.

Vegetation is a lonely, deliberate presence. There are no forests, only singular, monumental growths. Out of the seams between the massive floor-plates, long, fibrous stalks of a fleshy, wax-coated succulent emerge. They grow with a slow, hydraulic pressure, swaying slightly in the heat-shimmer. These plants are built for the scale of the world—they are tall as towers but spaced miles apart, their grey-green skins reflecting the sun. They do not compete for space; there is an abundance of nothingness between them.

The world is active, yet it lacks the chaotic vibration of life. There are no birds, no insects, and no scurrying things. The "fauna" of this place is the weather and the water. You can hear the landscape working: the low-frequency *tink-tink-tink* of ceramic tiles expanding under the sun, and the distant, rhythmic *rush* of water being siphoned through subterranean clay pipes by gravity alone. The sound is not a silence of death, but a silence of a machine that has just been turned on and is currently idling.

Everything here is "built," but the technology is invisible or non-existent in any mechanical sense. It is a world of pure masonry and hydraulic physics. There are no wires, no screens, no humming engines—only the massive, clever arrangement of stone and moisture. It feels like the floor of a drained ocean designed by a civilization that worshipped geometry and heat.

As you walk, your footsteps ring out with a clear, ceramic percussion. The air is scrubbed clean, smelling only of hot stone and the faint, mineral tang of evaporating water. It is a place of embryonic stability; the first cracks of erosion haven't even begun to mar the perfect edges of the world. It is waiting for a history to begin, yet it provides everything necessary for a slow, botanical persistence. It is a masterpiece of emptiness, a continental-scale garden where the only thing that moves is the sun, the water, and the slow, stretching shadows of the wax-plants.
