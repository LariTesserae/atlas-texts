The world is a single, hollowed-out crystalline geode the size of a cathedral, suspended in a silent void, yet inside, it is a riot of heat and friction. There is no water here—not even in the air. The atmosphere is a pressurized, shimmering haze of translucent silicone-gases and fine mineral dust. 

The terrain is an impossible urbanism of the micro-scale. It is a city of "Hollow-Thorns." Thousands of slender, bone-white towers, carved from a material resembling recycled pumice and hardened sap, reach from the floor toward the ceiling. These structures are not built on the ground so much as they are woven between the massive, jagged quartz-like protrusions that define the geode’s interior. The architecture is dense, layered, and ancient; a vertical labyrinth of walkways made of woven fiber and balconies cut directly into the mineral skin of the world. Despite the complexity of the city, it feels ghostly—empty. The streets are wide, the plazas are vast, but the inhabitants are few and far-between, living in the quiet gaps of a grand, dusty design.

The vegetation is the true master of this dry space. It is lush, but alien. Thicket-vines, dry and brittle as parchment but vibrant in color—electric purples and burnt oranges—choke the gaps between the buildings. These plants do not drink; they feed on the intense, amber light that floods the chamber from an unknown exterior source, and they pull sustenance from the electrostatic charges in the air. They grow in explosive, geometric patterns, unfurling like fractals across the "urban" facades. 

It is incredibly warm, a steady, baking heat that radiates from the walls. This heat powers the "Wind-Mills," primitive but massive wooden-and-fiber fans built by the inhabitants to circulate the heavy gases. 

The sound is the horizon of this world. It is a constant, physical weight. Because the environment is a sealed chamber of resonant crystal and the atmosphere is heavy with gas, every vibration is amplified a thousandfold. The "vegetation" doesn't rustle; it clatters like a million falling glass shards. The wind through the narrow city alleys doesn't whistle; it shrieks. The most dominant sound is the "Thermal Groan"—the sound of the entire geode expanding and contracting against the heat, a rhythmic, deafening boom that sounds like a heartbeat slowed down to the pace of geological time. To speak here is impossible; the inhabitants likely communicate through touch or rhythmic percussion that cuts through the roar.

Technologically, the world is trapped in a sophisticated stone age. There are no wires, no engines, no splashing fluids. Everything is friction and tension. Pulley systems made of braided vine-silk lift heavy stone blocks; obsidian blades are used to shave the flinty surfaces of the buildings. It is a world of manual craft, where every notch in a wall was chipped by hand over centuries of settled, patient existence.

Erosion has softened the edges of this place. The city feels settled, as if it has always been part of the crystal formation. The stairs are worn smooth into shallow bowls by eons of footsteps. There is no decay, only a dry, dusty polishing. 

Occasionally, a rare creature—a "Glass-Skipper"—flits between the towers. These fauna are six-legged, translucent-shelled insects the size of a human torso, moving with jerky, frantic motions. They are the only things that move faster than the slow, baking rhythm of the heat.

In this bright, golden-lit void, the rule is simple: everything is dry, everything is loud, and everything is permanent. It is a crowded city built for a thousand, inhabited by ten, vibrating in the center of a sun-drenched rock.
