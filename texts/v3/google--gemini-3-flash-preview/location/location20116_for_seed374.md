This is the **Labyrinth of the Steam-Cradle**.

The world here is newborn and heavy, a humid, mineral cradle that has never seen a seed or a leaf. There is no green here, only the slick, monochromatic sheen of wet basalt and the dull, matte texture of cooling pumice. Because erosion has not yet begun its work, the landscape is violently jagged, composed of raw, needle-like spires and razor-edged terraced pits that have not been softened by wind or time. The geometry is embryonic—sharp, fresh, and brutal.

A thick, lukewarm condensation clings to everything. The air is localized, heavy with a perpetual, silver-grey mist that never clears, keeping the world in a state of eternal dusk. Water does not fall as rain so much as it breathes out of the earth; it weeps from the pores of the stone and gathers in shallow, obsidian-colored basins. The ground is a rhythmic network of puddles and rising vapors, making the surface look as though it is constantly perspiring.

Across this low-lying, jagged expanse sits a sprawling, primitive megalopolis. It is a city of immense density, constructed entirely of stacked, unhewn stone and mud-caked slabs. These are not buildings as we know them, but a continuous, suffocating crust of habitation—tens of thousands of low-roofed hovels and communal hives pressed so tightly together that there are no streets, only narrow, vertical fissures acting as crawlways. The architecture is recursive and layered; new levels are simply piled atop the old as the base sinks into the soft, volcanic silt. There is no metal here, no glass, no rotating parts. It is a civilization of heavy lifting and manual stacking, a town built by hands that have only just mastered the lever and the knot.

The sheer volume of life is overwhelming. Though there are no plants, the world is saturated with a gargantuan biomass of soft-bodied creatures. Legless, pale-skinned amphibians the size of oxen heave themselves through the narrow corridors between the stone huts, their skins glistening with the constant moisture. Thousands of thick, translucent worms—thick as a human thigh—bridge the gaps between the dwellings, acting as living cables. Smaller, multi-legged scavengers with chitinous shells click and swarm over every square inch of the wet masonry. 

The atmosphere is not silent. It is, in fact, an assault. Because the world is "never changing," there is no wind to disperse sound and no cyclical rhythm to offer a break. The noise is a constant, deafening roar—a combination of the deep, subterranean thrum of rising steam, the wet, rhythmic slapping of millions of soft bodies against stone, and the ceaseless, communal chanting or grunting of the unseen inhabitants within their stone hives. It is a wall of sound that never ebbs, a permanent cacophony of biological and geological friction.

Everything is warm—not hot enough to scald, but the oppressive, unchanging warmth of a fever. The temperature never fluctuates; there is no night-chill and no noon-glare. The dim, grey light remains constant, filtered through a sky that is more steam than air. 

In this place, the concept of "yesterday" or "tomorrow" has no meaning. The city does not grow, it simply persists in its dense, crowded state. The water never drains; the stones never wear down. It is a permanent, bustling, high-occupancy basement of a world, caught in the moment of its creation and refusing to move toward its end. It is a place of heavy labor, wet skin, and the endless, loud thrum of a million hearts beating in the dark.
