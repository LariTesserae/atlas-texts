The land is an endless, sodden shelf of grey schist and black basalt, worn so smooth by the cycles of deep time that not a single jagged edge remains. It is a world of curves and shallow basins, a landscape that has been sanded down by the patient friction of water and wind until it resembles the back of a vast, submerged animal. There is no soil here—only a fine, silvatic silt that collects in the depressions, and the perpetual, heavy presence of moisture.

The air is a thick, cool suspirations of fog, clinging to a light that never fully arrives. The sun is a pale, smudged thumbprint behind a ceiling of permanent slate-colored clouds. Everything is slick. The rocks weep; they do not merely hold water, they seem to generate it from their very pores, sweating a cold, clear mineral sweat that trickles into a labyrinth of interconnected tidal pools and sluggish, slow-moving veins of current.

There is no green. The concept of a leaf or a stem is alien to this plateau. Instead, the ecology is one of calcified minerals and strange, rhythmic vibrations. In place of trees, there are the *Siphon-Spires*: waist-high cones of porous, baked clay that look like oversized termite mounds or the calcified remains of ancient chimneys. These are scattered thinly across the dim expanses, separated by hundreds of meters of bare, glistening rock.

The silence one might expect from such a desolate, stony place is nonexistent. The world is, instead, a theater of roar and resonance.

The sound is a physical weight. It is the product of the "abundant fauna"—not mammals or birds, but millions of *Chime-Crabs* and *Vibration-Leeches*. The Chime-Crabs, creatures with translucent, quartz-like shells the size of dinner plates, possess long, articulated legs that they strike against the resonant hollows of the smooth rock. They move in vast, decentralized swarms, their tapping synchronized into a thundering, percussive drone that vibrates in the marrow of one’s bones. This is layered over the constant, shrieking hiss of the moisture evaporating and re-condensing in the porous spires, creating a cacophony that makes verbal thought difficult.

The "built" elements of this world are humble and strangely integrated. Occasionally, one finds a low wall of stacked, unmortared stones, or a shallow basin carved into the bedrock with blunt, primitive tools. These are the "Way-Stations," small depressions shielded from the wind where the inhabitants—shadowy, slow-moving bipeds dressed in heavy, sodden hides—huddle to escape the most violent swells of the noise. Their technology is limited to the grinding of stone and the weaving of thick, waterproof cords from the tough, gelatinous filaments that grow in the deep-water pools.

There is a primitive industry here: the collection of *Sound-Salt*. The inhabitants place hollowed-out gourds or ceramic vessels near the most resonant fissures in the rock. The sheer intensity of the vibration causes the minerals in the water to precipitate at an accelerated rate, forming jagged, crystalline crusts inside the vessels.

Everything is in motion, despite the ancient, eroded stability of the terrain. The water is always migrating, the crabs are always drumming, and the fog is always rolling in heavy curtains across the flats. The "weirdness" of the place reveals itself in the way the light behaves; because the air is so saturated with mineral-heavy mist and the sound is so intense, the atmosphere occasionally experiences "harmonic refraction." For a few moments, the dim light will shatter into iridescent geometric patterns, reflecting off the vibration-ripples in the pools, making the gray world flash with a sudden, violent violet or sickly yellow before fading back into the gloom.

It is a world at the end of its geological life, yet surging with a frantic, noisy, non-biological energy. It is a wet, cold, echoing graveyard that refuses to be quiet, where the very stones have been taught to scream by the water that dissolves them.
