The world is a vertical catastrophe of unhardened stone. It is a dry, vertical empire where gravity seems to be a suggestion rather than a law, yet the scale of the ascent is so punishing that the lungs ache for oxygen that isn't there.

### The Terrain of Raw Birth
We are at an elevation that defies planetary logic. The ground does not sit beneath the feet; it looms beside them. Great, jagged spires of "prenatal" mineral rise miles into a bruised, dim sky. This is a world under construction, a "just-coming-into-being" geology where the rock is still tacky to the touch, like cooling wax or half-set clay. There is no sand or dust here because nothing has had the time to break down. There is no water to carve canyons; instead, the land is formed by massive, slow-motion upheavals of dry, silicate marrow.

The colors are muted—bruised purples, slate greys, and the sickly white of unexposed bone. The light is a permanent, dim twilight, as if the sun is a guttering candle hidden behind a thick veil of frozen gas.

### The Vegetation of the Void
Despite the absolute absence of water, life has claimed the height. It is not biological as we understand it. Clinging to the vertical faces of the cooling spires are forests of "glass-moss" and "chime-ferns." These are brittle, translucent organisms that feed on the static electricity generated by the grinding tectonic plates. They are lush in their coverage but sparse in their distribution, grouped in hanging valleys that tilt at forty-five-degree angles. 

These plants do not wilt. They shatter. They grow in fractal, crystalline explosions, their leaves—thin as mica—vibrating in the thin air. They are deep indigos and sharp oranges, the only vibrant pigments in a world of grey stone.

### The Deafening Silence
The most oppressive element is the sound. It is a roar that does not come from wind or water, but from the very act of the world’s formation. It is the sound of continental plates screaming as they slide upward. It is a low-frequency, bone-shaking groan punctuated by the "crack" of miles-long fissures opening in the new rock. Because the air is dry and thin, the sound doesn't travel like a wave; it hits like a physical blow. To stand in the open is to be pummeled by the vibration of the planet's birth. It is a deafening, rhythmic thunder that repeats every few hours, followed by a silence so heavy it feels like a weight.

### The Broken Reality
At this extreme elevation, the rules of physics have begun to fray. The "3.0 Weirdness" manifests as localized failures of perspective and weight. You may look across a chasm to a distant peak, only to realize that the peak is actually floating three inches above its base, slowly rotating. In certain pockets of the landscape, shadows fall toward the light source instead of away from it. 

Time is viscous. You might watch a crystal-fern grow an entire limb in what feels like a minute, while the falling of a pebble takes an hour to reach the bottom of a ravine, drifting downward as if submerged in invisible syrup. There are "mirage-structures"—geometry that appears solid from a distance but dissolves into mathematical static as you approach.

### The Scattered Marks of the Primitive
There are signs of intent here, though no creatures move. Balanced on the lips of impossible precipices are small, dry-stone cairns. They are built of the same raw, unweathered rock as the mountains, stacked with a crude, heavy-handed precision. 

Occasional "bridges" made of braided, glass-tough vines span the gaps between floating islands of rock. These are the marks of the "Pre-Animistic"—a culture that left behind tools of chipped flint and heavy bone before the concept of "animal" had even been written into the world's code. There are no homes, only small, circular clearings where the ground has been beaten flat by heavy, manual labor. A single, primitive hammer made of a star-metal alloy might sit atop a pedestal of cooling magma, abandoned mid-strike a thousand years ago.

### The Ecology of Stillness
There are no birds, no insects, no scuttling things. The only movement is the agonizingly slow drift of the landmasses and the shimmering vibration of the vegetation. The air is sterile and smells of ozone and scorched flint. 

There is a profound sense of waiting. The world is too high, too dry, and too loud to be finished. It is a giant, mineral embryo, dreaming of a future where it might finally harden, where the sound might stop, and where the water might finally fall. But for now, it remains a soaring, vertical desert of screaming stone and glass-gardens, hanging in the dim light of a dying or unborn star.
