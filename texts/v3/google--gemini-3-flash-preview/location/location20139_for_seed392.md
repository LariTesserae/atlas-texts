The world is a single, continuous plane of fired terracotta and polished basalt, extending toward a horizon that never curves. There is no soil, no rock that wasn't placed by a hand, yet there is no sense of age. The edges of the bricks are razor-sharp, the mortar is wet and white, and the vast, stepped plazas feel as though they were finished only moments ago. This is a manufactured basin of infinite scale, a masterpiece of primitive geometry where everything is built and nothing is "natural," yet everything is alive.

The heat is a heavy, golden weight, held at a constant, unmoving 2.6 intensity. There is no wind to stir the air, and the sun—which does not travel but hangs perpetually at the zenith—is a blinding, white eye that eliminates all shadows. The light is so total that depth is perceived only through the layering of steam and the vibrant greens of the flora.

Because the world is perfectly flat and low-slung, water does not flow; it weeps. It seeps upward through the microscopic seams in the masonry, coating the colossal terracotta tiles in a thin, shimmering film. It collects in shallow, purposeful depressions—hand-carved basins the size of lakes—where it sits in a state of terminal stillness, reflecting the white sky like a mirror of glass. The atmosphere is a thick, humid bloom, a nectar-heavy mist that clings to the skin and the leaves.

The vegetation is aggressive and lush, yet it grows without earth. Massive, thick-veined vines, the color of bruised emeralds, wrap themselves around basalt pillars. Iridescent mosses with the texture of velvet colonize the vertical faces of the terraces, drinking the condensation directly from the air. Enormous, waxy flowers the size of shields hang from the limestone arches, their petals perpetually dripping a clear, viscous dew that pings rhythmically onto the hard floor below. There are no forests here, only a deliberate, scattered distribution of botanical giants—solitary, magnificent trees with bark like woven rope, standing in the centers of vast, open courtyards.

The fauna is abundant but eerily calm. Golden-scaled lizards the length of a man’s arm bask on the hot kiln-fired tiles, their throats pulsing in the heat. Great, slow-moving insects with wings like stained glass drift through the heavy air, their flight silent and hovering. There is no predation here, only a dense, crowded coexistence. The creatures seem to emerge from the masonry itself, as if the carvings on the walls occasionally decide to detach and walk.

The "weirdness" of the place manifests in its refusal to change. Despite the wetness, the brick never erodes. Despite the heat, the water never evaporates. The world exists in a state of reality breakdown where the laws of entropy have been suspended. A fallen leaf does not rot; it simply stays on the ground, green and turgid, forever. If a brick is chipped, the crack does not widen over centuries; it remains a fresh, sharp wound in the stone, unchanging. Time has no grip here; there is no evening, no season, no decay.

The silence is almost physical. The only sounds are the occasional, heavy *splat* of a raindrop-sized dewdrop hitting a stone floor and the low, vibrational hum of the heat itself. It is a regional stillness, a valley of crafted stone where the "built" environment has become the ecology. 

There are no machines. The world was made with hammers of diorite and chisels of flint, by a hand that understood geometry but ignored physics. The architecture is primitive—massive post-and-lintel structures, endless stairs leading to nowhere, and decorative friezes that repeat into infinity—but the scale is divine. You are walking across a world that is a single, giant, wet, hot, handmade monument, frozen in the millisecond after its completion. It is a masterpiece that will never grow old, never dry out, and never be finished, because it has already reached the terrifying equilibrium of perfection.
