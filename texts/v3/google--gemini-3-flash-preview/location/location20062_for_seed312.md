The scale of this place is not measured in leagues, but in the exhaustion of the lungs. It is a vertical continent, a monolithic rising of slate and basalt that pierces the atmosphere until the air becomes thin, brittle, and freezing. 

The elevation is so profound that the sky above is a deep, bruised navy even at noon, though the light remains sharp and white, reflecting off the jagged edges of the young stone. These are mountains that have not yet learned to be soft; the erosion is in its infancy. Every ridge is a razor, every cliff face a clean, terrifying shear of dark rock. There are no talus slopes or weathered foothills here—only the sudden, violent thrust of the crust against the void.

Because the world is so vast and the stone so fresh, the architecture clings to the rock like lichen. This is an urbanity of density and desperation. Thousands upon thousands of small, square dwellings are stacked one atop the other, carved directly into the vertical faces or assembled from unhewn shale and heavy timber. The city is a hive that spans the entire breadth of the range, a thousand-mile strip of habitation where the "streets" are narrow, treacherous ledges and the "plazas" are mere bridges spanning canyons so deep the bottoms are lost in shadow.

The technology is tactile and heavy. There are no engines, only the rhythmic thud of stone hammers and the creak of enormous hemp ropes. The people here have mastered the art of the pulley and the counterweight. Great wooden cranes, some the size of cathedrals, lean out over the abysses to haul supplies from the lower terraces. Everything is built by hand, brick by cold-shattered brick.

Water is a precious, intermittent ghost. It does not flow in rivers; it arrives as a heavy frost that coats the stones at dawn, or as a sudden, brief mist that clings to the jagged peaks before the thin air drinks it dry. The inhabitants have carved shallow grooves into every available surface to channel the condensation into communal cisterns. The moisture is just enough to keep the rock damp, giving the black stone a slick, oily sheen, but never enough to wash away the grit.

Vegetation is a luxury of the crevices. Small, hardy tufts of iron-grey moss and wiry, black-leafed shrubs huddle in the lee of the wind. They do not grow tall; they crawl, gripping the rock with roots as hard as wire. 

Yet, despite the scarcity of green, the world is teeming with life. The fauna is abundant, adapted to the verticality and the cold. Great thick-furred ungulates, with hooves split into grappling hooks, leap between the rooftops and the precipices. Massive, silent raptors with wingspans that could shade a house drift on the updrafts, their shadows flickering across the stone like passing clouds. In the communal courtyards, tethered herds of smaller, goat-like creatures huddle for warmth, their breath creating a constant, low-hanging fog in the streets.

The atmosphere is dominated by a profound, heavy silence. The scale of the city is so great that the sounds of a million souls are swallowed by the sheer volume of the mountains. A hammer strike three miles away arrives as a faint, metallic tick. The lowing of the herds is muffled by the density of the stone walls. The only consistent sound is the wind—not a roar, but a long, slow sigh that wanders through the labyrinthine alleys, carrying the scent of woodsmoke and wet fur.

Change here is glacial. The dynamic of the world is one of patient endurance. The city grows by one room every few years, as a family chips away at the mountain’s heart. The stone does not crumble; it waits. The people move with a slow, deliberate economy of motion, conserving heat and oxygen. It is a civilization of persistence, a massive, primitive machine of stone and bone, hanging in the bright, freezing thinness of the high places, watching the world far below with the unblinking clarity of a predator.
