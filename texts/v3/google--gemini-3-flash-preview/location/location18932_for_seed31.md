The mountains here are teenagers. They are sharp-toothed, jagged peaks of dark, unweathered basalt and obsidian-flecked granite that have only recently—on a geological scale—pierced the skin of the world. Because they are young, they are steep; because they are high, they are the first to catch the weight of the sky.

The air is warm and heavy with a perpetual, silver humidity. Large, translucent droplets cling to the undersides of every sharp rock ledge and narrow spire. The atmosphere does not rain in the traditional sense; rather, it exhales. A constant, motionless mist hangs in the valleys between the peaks, feeding a network of shallow, crystalline pools that sit atop the high plateaus. The water is everywhere—seeping from the jagged stone, running in thin, vertical veins down the cliffs, and pooling in basins so still they look like solid glass.

Vegetation has claimed this vertical world with a focused intensity. Thick, waxy-leafed ferns and vibrant neon-green mosses carpet the lower slopes and the lips of the rising precipices. Vines as thick as a human torso bridge the gaps between neighboring spires, creating organic tightropes draped in weeping lichen. While the flora is lush, it is not crowded; there are vast expanses of raw, dark stone between each cluster of green, giving the landscape a sense of intentional, sparse composition.

Built into the ribs of these mountains is a town of stone and wood. It scale is significant—it spans three distinct peaks, connected by massive, hand-hewn suspension bridges made of braided fiber and heavy timber. The architecture is sophisticated in its geometry but primitive in its execution. There is no metal here. The buildings are rounded cells of stacked shale, sealed with resin and topped with thatched roofs of giant, waterproof leaves. Terraces have been cut directly into the cliff-faces, forming tiered streets that collect the constant seepage of the mountains into organized stone channels. It is a city of masonry and cordage, a feat of manual labor and communal effort.

But it is a city without a pulse.

There is no sound here but the rhythmic, hypnotic *tink-tink-tink* of water falling into water. No birds circle the high spires. No insects hum in the ferns. No livestock bellows from the stone enclosures. Even the inhabitants, if they remain, make no noise. The streets are clean, the bridges are taut, and the stone basins are full, yet the silence is absolute. It is not the silence of death, but the silence of a clock that has stopped at exactly noon.

The world here is frozen in a state of perfect, wet equilibrium. The light is steady and white, filtered through the high-altitude haze so that shadows are soft and depth is hard to judge. The wind does not blow; the mist does not drift. Even the waterfalls seem to hang like silver threads, their movement so consistent and unchanging that they appear static.

The erosion is stalled. Despite the abundance of water, the sharp edges of the stone do not wear down. The moss does not die or grow; it remains in its vibrant, mid-life flush. The wooden bridges do not rot in the moisture. It is a young world that has refused to get any older, a regional expanse of high-altitude splendor where the architectural triumph of a primitive civilization is preserved forever in a warm, breathless, and lonely humidity. This is a place where the morning never ends, and the first drop of rain never quite hits the ground.
