The inhabitants of this vertical sea prioritize the skin over the limb, and the membrane over the bone. In a world where gravity has lost its resolve, the residents are masters of the "boundary"—the thin, shivering edge where the water meets the mist.

**The Siphons**
The primary shepherds of the vine-bridges are not animals in the traditional sense, but colonial organisms known as the Siphons. They appear as gelatinous, translucent bells, similar to jellyfish but possessing a firmer, rubbery consistency. They do not swim; they "grip." Their undersides are a carpet of velvet-fine cilia that vibrate at a frequency capable of manipulating surface tension. By vibrating, they create tiny ripples that pull them along the surface of the cathedral-sized water-globes. They are the gardeners of the high-altitude nursery, instinctively pruning the massive vines and redirecting the flow of hydraulic sap. They are entirely silent, communicating through rhythmic flashes of indigo light that pulse deep within their bellies, turning the interior of a water-sphere into a flickering, bioluminescent lantern at dusk.

**The Glass-Gleaners**
Living exclusively on the jagged shards of floating pumice are the Glass-Gleaners. These are small, crustacean-like beings with shells made of the same translucent salt crystals that form the islands. They are the only creatures with "weight," and they use it as a weapon. They are flat, many-legged, and move with a frantic, skittering clockwork precision over the sharp-edged rocks. Because they are denser than the water, they must constantly anchor themselves with silk-like tethers to the massive vines; if they lose their grip, they would drift forever into the center of a water-sphere, unable to swim out. They feed on the aggressive minerals of the young rocks, their mandibles vibrating so fast they create a high-pitched hum—one of the few sharp sounds in this muffled world.

**The Lung-Fronds**
In the middle of the "food chain" is a creature that blurs the line between flora and fauna. These are the Lung-Fronds—stationary animals that look like clusters of weeping willow branches made of pale, pulsing flesh. They hang from the undersides of the great floating lilies. They do not hunt with teeth; they hunt with surface area. Their "leaves" are actually highly sensitive respiratory membranes that filter the oxygenated mist. When a Silver-Skinned Ribbon-Eel passes through their trailing reach, the Fronds do not constrict; they simply exchange gases and nutrients with the eel through osmosis. It is a world of parasitic grace where no one is eaten, only "borrowed" from.

**The Shimmer-Lords (The Ribbon-Eels)**
The Silver-Skinned Ribbon-Eels are the true nomads of the vertical ocean. They are the only creatures that have mastered the transition between the liquid orbs and the humid air. Their skin is coated in a specialized, hydrophobic oil that allows them to "skate" on the air itself as if it were a solid floor. They are long—sometimes reaching thirty feet—and move in a slow-motion serpentine dance. They are blind because light in this world is a diffuse, pearlescent fog that provides no shadows to track. Instead, they navigate by sensing the *thrum*—the deep, subsonic vibration of the water-globes colliding. They are the pollinators of the sky, carrying the heavy seed-pods of the giant lilies from one drifting island to another, stuck to their shimmering, oily hides.

**The Ghost-Whales of the Mist**
At the very center of the largest water-cathedrals, where the pressure is greatest and the light is a dim, watery emerald, live the "Anchors." These are vast, slow-beating hearts of muscle and gas-bladders. They are the size of houses, appearing as massive, grey masses of porous tissue. They never move. They stay at the center of the water-spheres, their long, filament-thin nervous systems extending out like a web to the very surface of the globe. They are the intelligence of the place. They sense the approach of other spheres, the growth of the vines, and the shifting of the salts. They act as the gravitational center for the ecology, their steady, rhythmic pulse acting as a metronome for all other life.

In this world, life is not a struggle against an environment, but a slow, wet melting into it. Nothing is solitary. To live here is to be part of a single, continuous, hydraulic machine—a world of soft collisions, shared breaths, and the eternal, quiet labor of staying afloat in a sky that has forgotten how to let things fall.
