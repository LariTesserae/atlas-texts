The inhabitants here do not distinguish between their own bodies and the architecture. To live in the machine-vault is to be an extension of the pressure; to be a slow, deliberate leak in the system.

The most numerous residents are the **Gland-Tenders**. They are not quite organic, yet they possess a terrifying, soft fragility. Imagine a creature shaped like a prayer—kneeling, elongated, and draped in "shrouds" that are actually layers of sloughed-off industrial filter-cloth. They have no eyes; in a world of soot-colored darkness, vision is a wasteful expenditure of calories. Instead, their fingers have evolved into long, multi-jointed needles, sensitive enough to feel the specific vibration of a failing bearing three floor-plates away. 

They move with the same glacial intent as the pistons. A Gland-Tender may take an entire week to cross the chamber, its body molding itself into the shadows of the copper piping. They do not eat in the traditional sense; they press their translucent, vein-mapped bellies against the warm heating coils, absorbing the thermal energy through osmosis. They are the monks of this cathedral, their only "worship" being the application of their own sebaceous body-oils to dry hinges to silence a scream of metal.

In the tea-colored pools of oily water, the **Filigree-Siphons** dwell. These are the "pale vegetation" matured into something more active. They look like bundles of fiber-optic cables stripped of their casing—milky, twitching threads that drift in the stagnant liquid. They are the vault’s nervous system. They grow into the ports of the brass switches, their filaments mimicking the copper wiring so perfectly that the machine cannot tell where the copper ends and the pulp begins. They drink the electricity of the dying relays, pulsed through their bodies in rhythmic, orange flashes. When a Siphon dies, it doesn't rot; it calcifies into a brittle, conductive crust that the next generation grows upon.

Higher up, in the verdigris canopy of the pipes, live the **Clockwork Moths**. They are not insects of silk and dust, but of foil and magnetism. Their wings are thin sheets of oxidized tin, so light they can ride the rising plumes of hot steam. They do not fly so much as they drift on the thermal updrafts. They are attracted to the "scent" of ozone. When a spark leaps from a failing relay, a cloud of these moths will descend, their metallic wings shivering in a frantic, rattling dance to catch the ionized air. They are the scavengers of the vault’s exhaustion, living off the literal crumbs of energy cast off by the machine’s decay.

Deep in the recesses behind the corrugated plates, there is the **Slow-Thinker**. There is likely only one. It is a mass of "gear-moss" that has achieved a collective, rhythmic consciousness through the sheer passage of centuries. It is a sprawled, weeping mound of fungal matter and rusted iron filings that has grown over a central processing hub that failed long ago. It "thinks" at the speed of oxidation. Its thoughts are not words, but the low-frequency pulses that mimic the vault’s own heartbeat. It remembers the surface not as a place, but as a myth of "Cold" and "Dry"—two concepts that are the devils of its theology.

The ecology here is a closed loop of patience. Nothing competes for resources because there is no surplus to fight over. To move fast is to die; to be loud is to waste pressure. Everything that lives here—the Tenders, the Siphons, the Moths—exists to ensure the vault never reaches a state of stillness. 

They are the janitors of a forgotten purpose, tending to a fire that no longer cooks for anyone, in a house where the doors have been welded shut by time. They are the ghosts of the industrial age, having become the very rust they were perhaps originally sent to prevent.
