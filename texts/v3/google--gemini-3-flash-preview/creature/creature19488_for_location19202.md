In this high-altitude stillness, the inhabitants are not defined by movement, but by their endurance of the light and their integration into the pressure. To live here is to be a part of the slow-motion metabolism of the plateau.

### The Silicate Crawlers (The Glass Fauna)
The most visible residents are the "glass-like insects" mentioned in your survey, though to call them insects is to misjudge their chemistry. They are brittle, translucent organisms with shells made of fused mineral salts and trapped condensation. They look like blown glass, cold to the touch and perfectly transparent, save for a core of bruised purple that mimics the color of the succulent plates they inhabit.

Because the air is so thin and the gravity feels so deliberate, these creatures do not scuttle. They move with a frame-by-frame precision. A crawler may take six hours to lift a single, multi-jointed leg, its internal hydraulics struggling against the pressurized atmosphere. They are masters of the "long wait." They do not hunt; they are living filters. They anchor themselves to the lips of the Sump-Engines or the edges of the basalt fissures and extend microscopic, gossamer filaments into the air. These filaments catch the silver droplets of condensation, drawing moisture and mineral grit into their bodies. They are so still that the "creak" of the plants often masks the tiny, glass-on-glass chime of their joints.

### The Marrow-Listeners (The Sub-Surface Clerics)
Tended by the deep hum of the cables, there are things that live within the "crowded" interstices of the machinery and the basalt. These are the Keepers of the Sump, though they are less like workers and more like biological barnacles. 

They are pale, soft-bodied beings, possessing the same "cold silicone" texture as the ferns. They have no eyes—the bleached silver light is too violent for sight—and instead, they have evolved massive, fan-like sensory organs that they press against the cast-iron pistons and the vibrating cables. They live entirely through the "marrow-hum." By pressing their torsos against the industrial artifacts, they regulate their own heartbeats to match the funereal rhythm of the machines. They are the slow-time gardeners of this place, moving with the same agonizing slowness as the horizontal arms of the engines, occasionally reaching out with moist, multi-fingered hands to clear a thicket of bruised-plum succulents that might be clogging a pressure valve.

### The Condensation Motes
Hovering in the stagnant, silent air are the smallest inhabitants: colonial clusters of bio-luminescent vapor. They are not gas, but spherical colonies of microscopic organisms that huddle together within the silver droplets. To a casual observer, they look like drifting sparks of the sun’s bleached light. They are attracted to the "Sump-Engines." They cluster around the glowing, pressurized canisters, feeding on the trace leakages of heavy gas. They represent the only "fast" thing in this world; they vibrate with a frantic, microscopic energy, though they never travel more than a few inches from their chosen machine.

### The Gestalt of the Plateau
The distinction between the "dweller" and the "dwelling" is porous here. Because the terrain is young and the biology is so aggressive, the Sump-Engines themselves have begun to develop a form of mechanical sentience—or perhaps the vegetation has simply learned to use the machines as a nervous system. 

There is a sense that the entire plateau is a single, slow-breathing entity. The "creak" of the plants, the "hum" of the cables, and the "thrum" of the Sump-Engines are all parts of one conversation. Whoever lives here does not have a "life" in the way we understand it; they have a "state of being." They are the living embodiment of the pressure. They do not sleep because the light never wavers; they do not hurry because there is no tomorrow, only the eternal, silver *now*. They are the stewards of a factory that produces nothing but its own persistence, waiting for a transport that—if it ever arrived—would find a world that has forgotten how to move.
