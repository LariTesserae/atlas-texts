The inhabitants of this vertical empire hold no concept of "horizontal." To them, the world is a series of shelves and drops, and their bodies have reshaped themselves to reflect this geometric cruelty.

The people are compact, deep-chested, and barrel-ribbed. Their lungs are disproportionately large, bellows of dense tissue capable of extracting life from air that would leave a lowlander gasping toward a coma. Their skin is the color of the stone—shades of charcoal, bruised plum, and weathered flint—toughened by a sun that burns without the filter of thick atmosphere. They move with a terrifying, rhythmic grace, a "mountain-slouch" that keeps their center of gravity tucked tight against the rock. They do not walk so much as they lean into the world, their movements measured by the pulse of the stone.

The eyes of the populace are their most striking feature. Evolution has granted them a second, translucent eyelid, a nictitating membrane that protects the cornea from the stinging grit and the razor-sharp frost-shards carried by the high winds. At a distance, they appear to have eyes of polished obsidian; up close, they are a pale, piercing amber, capable of spotting the movement of a rope-line miles away across the void.

Hierarchy is a matter of altitude. 

On the **Lower Strata**, where the air is "heavy" and wet, live the *Cistern-Hands* and the *Muck-Sifters*. These are the laborers of the damp, people with permanently stained palms and thickened soles who manage the collection of the dawn-frost. They are the most muscular of the castes, their limbs knotted from hauling the great hempen pulleys. They wear heavy cloaks of felted ungulate hair, greased with animal fat to repel the slick, oily moisture of the dark-rock.

The **Middle Galleries** house the *Lithos-Masons* and the *Weavers*. Here, the clatter of stone-hammers is the heartbeat of the city. These inhabitants are obsessed with tension. Their clothing is a complex web of harnesses, buckles, and braided cords. A child in the Middle Galleries learns to tie a bowline before they can speak. Their social status is displayed through the quality of their gear—brass-capped pitons, pulleys carved from the dense, black wood of the wiry shrubs, and boots with soles made of the corrugated hide of the hook-hoofed ungulates. To lose one’s footing is not just a personal tragedy; it is a moral failing, a breach of the communal pact of gravity.

At the **Highest Pinnacles**, where the sky is nearly black and the light is a blinding, holy white, dwell the *Sky-Watchers* and the *Airs-August*. They are the thinnest of the people, almost skeletal, their metabolisms slowed to a near-hibernatory crawl to conserve the precious, thin oxygen. They wear garments of raptor-down and silvered leather. They do not speak; they communicate through a complex language of hand-signals and long-distance mirrors. They are the navigators of the updrafts, the ones who read the "breath of the world" to predict the coming of the great frosts or the shifting of the tectonic plates.

Between these castes move the **Rope-Runners**, the nomadic youth who ferry messages and small goods across the abysses. They are the most reckless of the living things here, possessing an instinctive understanding of the wind's architecture. They use "glider-capes"—stiffened, ribbed fabrics that allow them to leap from a thousand-foot shear and catch a thermal, drifting like falling leaves until they can snag a lower ledge with a grappling hook.

The animals of this place are not pets, but extensions of the architecture. The **Hook-Hooves** (the great ungulates) are never led by ropes; they are followed. Their intuition for stable rock is infallible. Their milk is thick as paste and grey as slate, providing the dense calories needed to survive the cold. The **Great Raptors**, while wild, are revered as the "Architects of the Void." The people study their flight paths to know where the invisible rivers of warm air flow between the peaks.

Nothing is wasted. When a person dies, their body is not buried—for there is no earth—nor burned, for wood is too precious. Instead, they are "given to the wind" in the highest reaches, or their bones are cleaned and used as structural anchors, becoming a literal part of the foundations of the house they once inhabited. In this way, the city is built not just of stone, but of the ancestors themselves. They have become a civilization of human lichen: patient, silent, and utterly inseparable from the pitiless basalt that birthed them.
