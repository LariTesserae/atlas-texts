The inhabitants of the pod are a people of friction and oil. They are the **Suture-Folk**, a race whose very anatomy has been warped by the verticality and the unyielding, kiln-like dry. 

To look at a Suture-Man is to see a creature designed for the grip. Their fingers are disproportionately long, ending in nails that are not clipped but sharpened into hooked plectrums, stained a permanent mahogany by the resins they climb. Their skin is not moist, but it is not cracked; it is a burnished, toughened hide, similar to the "leather-ferns" they harvest. They do not sweat—water is too precious for such waste—but instead exude a thin, protective wax from their pores that traps their internal moisture and gives them a dull, metallic sheen in the ochre light.

They move with a terrifying, fluid grace, a three-dimensional ballet where "down" is merely a direction of travel rather than a danger. They do not walk so much as they flow over the ribbed interior, their toes as prehensile as their fingers. Because the air is a "biological weather" of midges and spores, they have evolved heavy, translucent third eyelids—nictitating membranes—that flicker shut to protect their vision from the constant velvet abrasive of the swarm.

Their culture is one of the **Great Pruning**. Because the pod-growth is aggressive, the Suture-Folk are eternal gardeners of the void. Every citizen carries a *scythe-shard*, a blade of fossilized pod-wall honed to a molecular edge. They are in a state of perpetual war with the succulent mosses. A home left un-tended for three days will be swallowed; a ladder not scraped will be buried in pulsing orange flowers. This creates a society of frantic industriousness. They are "harvester-architects" who eat the very obstacles they clear, grinding the encroaching spores into a heavy, narcotic paste.

Communication among them is rarely vocal. The "vibrating roar" of the beetle-hives and the midge-clouds renders speech a local, whispered affair. Instead, they speak in **Skin-Tap**, a language of percussive rhythms struck against the resonant cellulose walls of the pod, and a complex sign-language of finger-twitches that can be felt through the tension of the communal ropes.

Living alongside—and often inside—the human basket-cells are the **Wick-Beetles**. These are heavy, palm-sized insects with porous, sponge-like abdomens. The Suture-Folk treat them as living thirsty-tools. A Wick-Beetle is placed against a seam in the pod wall to soak up the microscopic dawn-dew that the humans cannot reach with their tongues. Later, the beetle is gently "milked," its abdomen squeezed to release a single, precious drop of filtered moisture into a child's mouth. These beetles are the true currency of the pod; a man’s wealth is measured by the number of "thirst-servants" clicking upon his shoulders.

Deep in the "Gut"—the lowest, narrowest point of the pod where the debris of eons settles—dwell the **Vapor-Seers**. These are the elders who have grown too heavy or too brittle for the climb. They live in the thickest clouds of midges, their bodies almost entirely encased in the grey, dusty cocoons of sedentary life. They do not move; they simply breathe. They act as the pod’s lungs, inhaling the thickest metabolic vapors and exhaling the "Word"—a low, rhythmic chanting that syncs with the humming of the wings, a sonic glue that keeps the frantic population from descending into madness.

Then there are the **Shadow-Flickers**, the feathered lizards that have grown larger in the dark nooks. Some are the size of hounds, leurred by the Suture-Folk with offerings of insect-fat to serve as guardians of the basket-cells. Their feathers are not for flight but for sensory input; they feel the approach of a thief or a structural fracture in the pod-ribs minutes before it occurs, their crests vibrating in harmony with the cathedral’s groan.

Nothing here dies in the way we understand it. When a Suture-Man’s heart finally stops, he is not buried—there is no earth. He is "woven in." His body is placed into the base of a growing moss-rib, where the succulent, heart-pulsing plants slowly dissolve his waxen hide. He becomes the very wall his grandchildren will climb, his minerals fueling the orange flowers, his legacy a slightly more polished handhold for those who continue the eternal, airless ascent.

In this place, to exist is to be a part of the friction. The inhabitants are not merely living *within* the pod; they are the pod’s internal metabolism, a frantic, churning digestion of heat and dust, turning the fossilized past into a humid, thrumming present.
