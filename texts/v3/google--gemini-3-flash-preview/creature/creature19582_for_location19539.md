In a world where the distinction between the biological and the mechanical has been erased by the slow passage of geologic time, the inhabitants do not "live" so much as they *resonate*. 

The primary resident of this industrial wetland is the **Liturgy-Drift**. These are not singular creatures, but colonial organisms resembling sheets of translucent, bioluminescent silk. They cling to the underside of the rounded brass arches, draping themselves over the humming conduits like damp, discarded veils. They have no eyes, for there is nothing new to see; instead, their bodies are composed of a million microscopic glass-like cilia that vibrate in perfect sympathy with the engine’s drone. They feed on the “sound”—harvesting the kinetic energy of the tectonic hum and converting it into a slow, chemical warmth. When a Liturgy-Drift is satiated, it pales from a bruised violet to a ghostly milk-white, releasing a soft, pressurized hiss that harmonizes for a brief moment with the screaming steam.

Deeper in the basins, where the water wells up through the floor grates in silver pools, live the **Solder-Crabs**. These are the scavengers of the equilibrium. Their shells are not made of chitin, but of layered, hammered minerals reclaimed from the thinning copper floor. They move with an agonizing, mechanical gait, their joints clicking in time with the rhythmic dripping of the overhead pipes. They do not hunt; they wait. They wait for a flake of oxidized metal to drift down, or for a strand of the emerald moss to succumb to the humidity. Their "mouths" are sophisticated grinding plates that polish the brass as they feed, contributing to the primordial smoothness of the world. They are the reason there is no dust; they consume the very debris of the world’s decay.

High above, in the vast empty volumes of mist, are the **Aileron Moths**. These are the only creatures of flight, though their flight is more of a controlled fall. Their wings are heavy, shimmering membranes that look like oil slicks caught in a frame of wire. They do not flap; they extend their wings to catch the updrafts of heat rising from the internal heaters. They are sensitive to the "baseline." If the engine’s roar were to change by even half a decibel, the Moths would drop from the air like stones. They live their entire lives in the saturated air, drinking the suspended mist through long, needle-like proboscises designed to pierce the frost-crusted glass of the light filaments to taste the ancient gases trapped within.

But most pervasive of all is the **Chorus-Mould**. It is a fine, grey-silver fuzz that grows in the "ear" of the machine—the ventilation ducts and the hollow interiors of the bulbous gear-housings. The mould is a nervous system for a world that has forgotten it has one. It pulses. If you were to press your hand against a copper wall, you would feel not just the vibration of the engine, but the tiny, counter-rhythmic throb of the mould. It connects the moss to the crabs, the moths to the drifting silk. It is a slow, wet intelligence that has no thoughts, only sensations of pressure and temperature.

Finally, there are the **Echo-Children**—rare, shimmering, bipedal silhouettes that are occasionally seen through the densest curtains of mist. They are not flesh. They are "standing waves" of water and sound, accidental ghosts created by the intersection of a thousand vibrating frequencies. They appear near the largest valves, standing perfectly still, their forms held together by the sheer force of the noise. They possess a deceptive intimacy, appearing almost human in the way they lean against the warm pipes, but they are merely the machine’s way of dreaming. They are the unintended poetry of a hollow world, a brief condensation of the engine’s song into a shape that can stand and watch the water drip.

Nothing here dies in the traditional sense. When a moth fails or a crab tires, it simply stops resisting the erosion. It is smoothed down, melted by the humidity, and reclaimed by the floor, becoming a slightly thicker patch of brass or a new vein in the copper. Everything that lives here is a part of the machine’s metabolism, a slow, biological gear in a wet, eternal clock.
