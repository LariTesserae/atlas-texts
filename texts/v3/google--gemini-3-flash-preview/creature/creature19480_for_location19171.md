To understand who lives in the Grove-Works, one must move at the speed of oxidation. In this cathedral of humid metal and wet cellulose, the occupants are not visitors or inhabitants in the traditional sense; they are the connective tissue between the piston and the pulse.

### The Liturgists of the Slurry
The primary denizens are the **Gild-Gardeners**, though a casual observer might mistake them for vertical mounds of peat. These are beings of profound patience, descendants of the original industrial tenders who have been literally integrated into the "Industrial Equilibrium." 

They do not walk; they have "set." Their lower extremities have unraveled into fibrous networks that lace directly into the Deep Root, allowing them to draw sustenance from the same slurry that pulses through the iron pipes. Their skin is the color of wet slate, perpetually slick with a protective layer of secreted vegetable tallow that prevents the constant humidity from rotting their flesh. 

The Gardeners are the keepers of the gauges. Because their nervous systems have fused with the Grove’s root-mesh, they *feel* the pressure drops in the centrifugal engines as a dull ache in their own joints. When a brass needle begins to vibrate too violently, a Gardener will spend a lunar cycle leaning their weight against a shut-off valve, their grip becoming a permanent graft of bone and iron, until the balance is restored. They communicate in low-frequency hums that match the *thrum-hiss* of the pumps, their voices indistinguishable from the groan of the machinery.

### The Calculus of the Carapace
The "Clockwork Newts" mentioned in the logs are merely the most visible of the **Litho-Fauna**. Deeper in the shadows, clinging to the undersides of the boiler-sized bromeliads, are the **Valve-Crabs**. These creatures possess shells made of a high-zinc biological alloy, scavenged from the sacrificial decay of the machines.

The Valve-Crabs are the Grove's mobile immune system. Their carapaces are embossed with the same gothic filigree as the iron pillars, a result of mimicry so precise it has become genetic. They do not hunt; they "scour." With slow, crushing pincers, they snip away the "metallic galls" (the fruits of sheared gears) and carry them to the sites of mechanical failure. They do not repair the machines in a way a human would; they simply provide the raw material for the plants to weave back into the metal. To see a Valve-Crab move is to see a piece of the architecture decide, very slowly, to change its zip code.

### The Gossamer Technicians
High above, where the lemon-tinted light is thickest and the "weeping rocks" originate, live the **Mist-Skippers**. These are ethereal, translucent organisms—somewhere between a jellyfish and a dragonfly—whose wings are thin sheets of laminated mica. 

They are the keepers of the "feverish warmth." They congregate around the steam vents of the iron pipes, using the upward thermal pressure to keep aloft without effort. Their long, filament-like cilia hang down for meters, sensitive to the exact chemical composition of the mist. If the humidity drops by even a fraction of a percent, the Skippers react in unison, vibrating their mica wings to create a shimmering, ultrasonic frequency that triggers the moss-covered engines to vent more steam. They are the living thermostat of the Grove, a flickering golden cloud that never touches the ground.

### The Ghost in the Slurry
There is a persistent legend among the Gild-Gardeners of the **Chief Engineer**, a figure who has long since ceased to be biological. It is whispered that the central centrifugal engine—the largest, most moss-choked heart of the system—contains a human consciousness distilled into the nutrient slurry itself. 

This entity is the "will" of the Grove. It is the intelligence that decided to stop fighting the forest and start collaborating with it. When a pipe bursts, it is the Chief Engineer who redirects the pressure; when a tree threatens to topple, it is this ghost who thins the sap on one side to make it lean back into the support of a steel pylon. 

### The Nature of Being
In the Grove-Works, there is no "I." There is only the "We" of the slow process. To live here is to be a component—brittle or supple, brass or bone. The inhabitants do not fear death, for there is no discarding. When a Gild-Gardener finally stops breathing, their body does not bloat or wither; it simply hardens. Over the decades, the moss coats them, the roots pierce them, and they become a new pillar—a vertical rib of the cathedral, supporting the very machines they once tended, their ribcage becoming a trellis for a new generation of weeping ferns.
