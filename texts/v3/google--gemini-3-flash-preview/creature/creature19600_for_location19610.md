In this cradle of crushing density, the "citizens" are not occupants of the architecture, but the nervous system of it. 

The primary inhabitants are the **Stern-Keepers**, a species of sessile, vertebrate-like mollusks that have forfeited the need for shells in favor of becoming the connective tissue of the plant-walls. They look like thick, translucent coils of pale muscle, or perhaps like exposed segments of a silvered spinal cord. They do not walk the streets; they reside within the "building" walls, threading themselves through the hollowed gourds and mossy rafters. They function as organic valves. When a neighborhood becomes too dry or a "building" too cold, these pale, pulsing lengths of flesh contract, squeezing the vascular vines of the plants to redirect the flow of nutrient-rich sap or warm, pressurized water. They are the plumbers of the hamlet, silent and sightless, communicating through the rhythmic thrum of their heartbeats against the cellulose.

Moving more freely through the "capillaries" are the **Vapor-Sinks**. At a distance, they might be mistaken for clumps of detached, roving moss, but they are hexapedal scavengers with coats of fur so fine and white they are nearly invisible against the silver mist. They have no eyes—light is a luxury the basin rarely provides—but they possess oversized, fan-like ears made of a stiff, cartilaginous membrane. They move with an unsettling, fluid silence, their padded feet clicking softly on the hard, frozen roots. They do not hunt in the traditional sense; they "graze" on the heavy condensation. They use their long, prehensile tongues to lick the "sweat" from the needle-tipped succulents, filtering microscopic spores and airborne proteins from the water. To encounter one is to see a ghost of frost that shivers; they vibrate at a high frequency, a biological humming that prevents their internal fluids from freezing solid in the biting chill.

High in the "rafters" of the interlocking fern-canopies live the **Glass-Moths**. These are the only creatures of color in the basin, though it is a color born of chemistry rather than pigment. Their wings are broad and rigid, resembling panes of stained glass or frosted obsidian. They do not fly so much as they glide on the thick, pressurized currents of vapor. They are the pollinators of the "lanterns." At night—or in the deeper dimness of the lower tiers—they descend to the phosphorus buds, inserting needle-like proboscises to drink the glowing nectar. As they do, their own translucent bodies begin to pulse with that same dying-star light, making them look like floating, drifting embers in the cold dark. They are the only "citizens" that seem to acknowledge the verticality of the city, spiraling between the damp "streets" and the jagged, gothic "spires."

Deep within the dark-green, "urban" slums, where the moss-walls are thickest, reside the **Scribes**. These are small, multi-legged crustaceans, pale as bone and armored in a chitin that has the texture of wet slate. They are the only creatures that possess something resembling a "tool." Using their sharpened, serrated fore-claws, they etch precise, geometric patterns into the waxy surfaces of the large drum-leaves. These etchings aren't language in a human sense, but a form of biological record-keeping. They mark the frequency of the "Tlip-Tlip" water-clocks and the slow shifting of the root-foundations. When a leaf dies and falls, it becomes part of the "bedrock," a fossilized layer of history upon which the next generation of the city is built.

There is a sense of profound, collective intent among these dwellers. No one fights; no one preys with heat or hunger. They are as much a part of the cold, hydraulic circuit as the plants themselves. They are the maintenance crew of a machine that never turns off, the quiet tenants of a cathedral that re-builds itself every time a drop of water falls. They do not live "in" the place; they are the place’s way of watching itself grow.
