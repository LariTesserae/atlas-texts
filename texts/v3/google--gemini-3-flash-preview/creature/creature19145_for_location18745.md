The inhabitants of this horizontal realm do not possess the concept of a "step" or a "leap." In a world that has forgotten how to be tall, life has abandoned the vertical. To live here is to be a creature of the interface—the thin, shimmering membrane where the silt meets the saturated air.

The dominant architects of the stillness are the **Silt-Lungs**. These are the heavy-bodied amphibians whose percussive croaks provide the landscape’s rhythmic pulse. They are not shaped like the nimble frogs of younger worlds; they are broad, flattenened discs of muscle and mottled skin, looking like living stepping stones. Their bones have become soft and cartilaginous, unnecessary in a place where gravity has no peaks to pull from. They spend decades in a single "eye" of clear water, their skin mimicking the bruised purple of the mosses. They breathe through an intricate system of external, feathery gills that fan out like delicate lace into the lukewarm pools, filtering both oxygen and the microscopic biofilm that coats the grit.

Co-existing in the slow migration of the water are the **Glass-Gliders**. These are the "thick-winged insects" that provide the low-frequency hum. Their wings are not dry or brittle; they are heavy, flexible panes of translucent chitin, coated in a natural oil that repels the perpetual humidity. They do not fly so much as they row through the thick air. Because the atmosphere is so saturated, the air provides a buoyancy that allows these hand-sized creatures to hover for hours with a single, languid stroke. They have no stingers or mandibles for hunting; they are grazers of the air, trailing long, sticky filaments behind them like aerial jellyfish to harvest the floating spores and humidity-loving bacteria that drift in the "silk" of the atmosphere.

Below the surface of the weeping clay dwells a more reclusive inhabitant: the **Pulse-Worms**. These are the true masters of the 2.9 erosion. They are segmented, translucent tubes, some reaching lengths of thirty feet, though they are no thicker than a human thumb. They do not tunnel so much as they melt through the mud, their bodies secreting an acid that pre-digests the ancient metamorphic grit. They move by osmosis, drawing themselves toward the "eyes" of the pools. They are the reason the ground "breathes." As they move in their millions beneath the silt, the land literally heaves in a slow, imperceptible wave, a geological peristalsis that keeps the water from stagnating.

The life here is characterized by an extreme longevity and a lack of urgency. Predation is not a chase, but a slow, mutual engulfment. The **Marrow-Mats**—the leathery, violet vegetation—are not entirely passive. Should a Silt-Lung remain motionless for too many years on a single mound, the moss will slowly begin to grow over its flanks, not to kill it, but to enter into a slow-motion trade. The moss provides camouflage and moisture; the amphibian provides nitrogen through its secretions. They become a single, composite organism, a living hillock that may persist for a century.

There are no eyes in the traditional sense. In a world of repetitive patterns and shimmering heat-haze, sight is less useful than vibration. The inhabitants "see" through a sophisticated haptic map. They feel the weight of a cloud passing overhead by the slight change in atmospheric pressure; they hear the location of a pool five miles away through the low-frequency thrum of the insects hovering over it.

The "0.8 dynamic" of this world has produced a psyche of total contentment. The inhabitants do not migrate; they do not hoard; they do not build. They are the biological expression of the silt itself—patient, porous, and ancient. They are the custodians of the Earth’s long exhale, living out their slow, warm lives in a state of perpetual arrival, having long ago reached the same "terminal velocity of stillness" as the land they inhabit.
