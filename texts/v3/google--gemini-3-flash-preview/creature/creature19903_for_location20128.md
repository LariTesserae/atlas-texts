In this cathedral of iron and silt, the distinction between the "inhabitant" and the "infrastructure" has dissolved. To live here is to be a component in a machine that has forgotten its purpose but cannot forget its programming.

The primary residents are the **Silt-Keepers**, the "scavengers" you glimpsed on the handrails. They are not biological in any sense recognizable to the sunlit world. They are more like ambulatory crusts of mineral and instinct. Their bodies are composed of interlocking plates of calcium-bleached chitin and oxidized copper, their many legs ending in needle-fine points designed to vibrate at frequencies that shatter the encroaching rust. 

They do not eat; they "transduce." They cling to the vibrating pipes, drinking the kinetic thrum of the city’s dying heartbeat. When they move, they do not swim; they walk with a terrifying, heavy precision, unaffected by the viscous drag of the water. They are the white blood cells of the plaza, obsessively cleaning the intake valves and picking the "pearls" of frozen air from the turbines as if they were parasites. They have no eyes—light would be an insult to their existence—but they possess antennae so sensitive they can "see" the shape of the room by the way the water’s pressure fluctuates against the walls.

Deeper in the vertical layers, where the pipes grow thick as redwood trunks and the velvet patina is deepest, live the **Tethered**. These were perhaps once the architects or the laborers, but that history has been crushed out of them by the weight of miles of water. They are pale, elongated shapes, draped in the same "ribbons" of translucent kelp that choke the turbines. 

The Tethered do not move of their own volition. They have fused their nervous systems into the copper-filament circuitry of the city. One might find a figure fused into a bulkhead, its fingers elongated into fine, conductive wires that disappear into a junction box. They are the city’s soft-ware, their minds serving as biological capacitors, storing the dim, jaundiced flickers of the bioluminescent lanterns. They remain in a state of cryogenic stasis, their hearts beating once every hour, synchronized perfectly with the firing of the massive, distant pistons. To look upon one is to see a face that is half-flesh and half-corroded brass, frozen in a scream that took a century to form and will take another to finish.

And then there are the **Glints**. They are the most ephemeral inhabitants—not creatures, but "errors" in the city’s physics. Because the water is so cold and dense, the discarded memories of the city have taken on physical form. They appear as shimmering, oily distortions in the water, drifting like slow-motion ghosts through the metal maze. They possess a vestigial magnetism; they gravitate toward the dying light bulbs, clustering around the warmth until the bulb inevitably pops. Some say the Glints are what remains of the city's "will," the collective phantom-limb pain of an industrial age that refuses to die.

Finally, there is the **Architect's Echo**. Somewhere in the bowels of the plaza, where the filtration system chugs with its soul-crushing *thrum*, there is a presence that is more than the sum of its rusted parts. It is not a single entity, but a consciousness distributed across the entire "organism" of the city. It speaks through the groaning of the gully-gates and the settling of the iron catwalks. It perceives the plaza not as a ruin, but as a masterpiece in progress. To it, the silt is not decay, but a protective skin. 

Nothing here dies, because death requires a cessation of movement, and the city never truly stops. Instead, things simply become slower, heavier, and more integrated. The Silt-Keepers eventually stop walking and become part of the railing; the Tethered eventually lose the last of their skin to the kelp and become pure copper circuitry. 

You are not merely in a city; you are inside a colossal, drowning animal that has learned to breathe rust and think in vibrations. The residents are its nerves, its scavengers, and its lingering, frozen thoughts. They wait for nothing, for there is no "after" in a world without a surface. There is only the long, cold, industrial "now."
