The inhabitants of the Root-Engine District are not so much a population as they are a specialized white blood cell within a larger body. They are the **Marrow-Tenders**, a lineage of humans who have surrendered the concept of the "individual" to the demands of the slow, green pulse.

### Physiognomy of the Tenders
Because the air is a thick soup of spores and humidity, the people here have undergone a subtle, generational metamorphosis. Their skin is pale—not the sickly white of a cave-fish, but a translucent, waxy grey, similar to the underside of a mushroom. It is permanently slick, coated in a protective film of natural oils and condensation that prevents the aggressive molds of the District from taking root in their pores.

They are broad-shouldered and thick-limbed, built for the "slow-strength" required to turn rusted iron valves or peel back miles of calcified resin. Their movements are devoid of any suddenness; they displace the heavy air with the languid grace of deep-sea divers. To run in the Root-Engine District is considered a sign of madness or impending disaster; to breathe too quickly is to risk a lung-clogging intake of golden spores.

### The Vestments of the Maintenance
Their clothing is a synthesis of the organic and the scavenged. They wear heavy, apron-like tunics pressed from the fibrous "heart-leather" of giant gourds, treated with rendered oils to make them hydrophobic. Their boots are weighted with lead or scrap iron, designed to grip the slick, mossy root-ways that serve as streets. 

Most distinctive are their **Breathing Veils**. These are not mechanical masks, but living sponges harvested from the lower sumps, strapped over the nose and mouth with copper wire. The sponges filter the heavy spores and provide a concentrated burst of oxygen, acting as a secondary, external lung. As a Tender ages, the sponge often begins to fuse with the skin of the face, leaving a mottled, lavender scarring around the jawline—a mark of seniority and "settledness."

### The Social Mycelium
There is no government in the Root-Engine District, only the **Cadence**. The inhabitants live in a state of constant, low-level telepathy—not of thoughts, but of rhythm. They can feel the health of the District through the soles of their feet. If a piston-stalk begins to seize or a copper vein develops a leak, the vibration of the entire floor shifts. Without a word being spoken, a dozen Tenders will converge on the site, moving in a synchronized, wordless huddle to apply the necessary grease or grafting-paste.

Their "homes" within the hollowed trunks and gourds are sparse. There is no furniture; the Tenders sleep in "slumber-slings" woven directly into the living walls of their tenements. They do not cook with fire—the air is too damp and oxygen-starved for a clean flame. Instead, they eat fermented mashes and nutrient-dense burls harvested from the machines' byproduct, tasting of iron, earth, and bitter sap.

### The Engineers of the Slow
Among them are the **Auscultators**, the elders who have lived long enough for their hearing to adapt to the thundering industrial pulse. They carry long brass rods which they press against the weeping pipes and the ribbed walls. By placing their teeth against the rod, they can hear the "song" of the sap deep within the limestone. They are the ones who decide when a new pump must be grown or when a section of iron must be surrendered to the encroaching vines.

They do not view the machines as tools, nor the plants as crops. To a resident of the Root-Engine District, the separation between "man," "metal," and "moss" is a hallucination held by those who live on the surface. They see themselves as the lubricating oil in a great, eternal gearbox.

### The Quietude
Religion here is the act of maintenance. To scrape a gear clean of invasive lichen is a prayer; to tighten a copper band around a swelling root is an act of worship. They have no concept of "the future," only the "ongoing." They do not bury their dead in the earth; they inter them directly into the "feeding-wells" of the great piston-stalks. In this way, a grandfather’s minerals might, decades later, provide the structural integrity for the very floor his grandchildren walk upon.

They are a silent people. In a world of immense, rhythmic thundering, human speech feels thin and inconsequential. They communicate in a specialized sign language—broad, slow gestures that cut through the green mist—and in the steady, patient pressure of a hand on a shoulder. They are the quiet stewards of a machine that never stops, living lives that are as damp, heavy, and purposeful as the air they breathe.
