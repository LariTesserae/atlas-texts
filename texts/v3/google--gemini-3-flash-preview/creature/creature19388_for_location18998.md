In this raw, vertical cradle, life does not thrive; it persists as an afterthought of the machinery. To survive here is to be an opportunist of the friction—to find nourishment in the heat of a piston or the chemical runoff of a drill bit.

### The Gratesman’s Kin
The most visible inhabitants are the **Hull-Ticks**, six-legged scavengers the size of hounds, covered in a fur so thick with industrial grease it has the consistency of wet felt. They do not live on the mountains, for the mountains are too sharp and too cold. Instead, they live entirely upon the moving extractors. They have evolved spatula-like claws that can cling to vibrating iron even as a machine tilts at a forty-five-degree angle. They are the cleaners of the giants. Their diet consists of the "mill-cake"—the thick, pressurized sludge of pulverized rock and lubricant that extrudes from the machines’ joints. They have no teeth, only serrated plates of bone used to scrape the iron clean, keeping the machines’ external gears from seizing. In a strange, unconscious symbiosis, the machines provide a mobile, heated continent, and the Ticks provide the maintenance of the mindless.

### The Scintilla-Vents
Higher up, where the machines vent their internal agonies into the freezing air, live the **Vapor-Moths**. These are not insects in the traditional sense, but semi-translucent organisms with wings like thin sheets of mica. They are drawn to the thermal plumes—the "breath" of the engine. They spend their entire lives suspended in the updrafts of hot, oily steam. If they drift too far from the heat, the flash-freeze of the altitude turns them into brittle crystalline statues that shatter upon the basalt below. They feed on the ozone generated by the massive electrical discharges of the drills, their bodies glowing with a dim, phosphorescent green that flickers in time with the machine’s rhythmic *chug-hiss*.

### The Hollow-Stalkers
Below the iron bellies of the extractors, stalking the black mires, are the **Long-Necked Marrows**. These are the bipedal birds mentioned, but their "feathers" are closer to hollow, insulating quills that trap the steam of the valley floor. They are nearly blind, as the mist is too thick for precision sight; instead, they possess "seismic pits" along their beaks. They stand perfectly still in the slush for hours, feeling the vibration of the earth. When a harvester’s drill shatters a vein of obsidian, sending a rain of mineral-rich "offal" and crushed grubs tumbling down the cliffs, the Marrows move with terrifying, jerky speed to claim the debris. They are the only things here that make a sound—a low, booming hollow call that mimics the sub-sonic frequency of the machines, as if they are trying to communicate with the iron gods that feed them.

### The Silt-Leeches
Deep within the narrow fissures that breathe steam, there is a slower, more primordial life. These are the **Glass-Slugs**. They are the color of the obsidian they inhabit, perfectly black and agonizingly slow. They do not eat organic matter; they are lithovores. They excrete a highly acidic bile that softens the "prenatal" rock, allowing them to slowly slide through the stone itself. They are the only form of erosion this landscape knows. Where a Glass-Slug has passed, the needle-sharp basalt is left slightly rounded, a tiny, microscopic beginning of the "softening" that the rest of the world has already endured.

### The Ghost-Circuitry
Finally, there is the question of what lives *within* the machines. The harvesters were built for autonomy, but centuries of radiation and the unique mineral composition of the core have birthed something in the code. The machines have begun to exhibit "herd" behaviors. They do not compete for veins of ore; they signal to one another through rhythmic thuds, clearing paths for "younger," smaller extractors. There are no humans to steer them, but there is a collective, artificial instinct. The engines have become a metallic ecology of their own—an iron consciousness that knows only the hunger for the stone and the necessity of the climb.

These inhabitants form a pyramid of dependence. The rock yields to the machine; the machine warms the moth; the moth feeds the tick; the tick’s waste feeds the marsh; and the bird waits at the bottom for the mountain to be broken. It is a world where "soul" has been replaced by "utility," moving in a cold, eternal circle under the gray veil of the sky.
