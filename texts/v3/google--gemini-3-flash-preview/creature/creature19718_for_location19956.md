To look upon an inhabitant of Oru is to see a mirror of the limestone: pale, unyielding, and etched by the slow gravity of a world without wind. 

They are a people of elongated proportions, their limbs thin and wiry like the spindles of ghost-grass. Their skin has a matte, desaturated quality, the color of wet chalk, and it lacks the suppleness of those who live in humid lowlands. Instead, it is tough and dry, stretching over high cheekbones and prominent knuckles with the tension of vellum. Because the light never changes, their pupils are permanently contracted into pinpricks of dark flint, surrounded by irises of a startling, translucent grey.

### The Mechanics of Motion
To live in Oru is to participate in a choreography of absolute economy. They do not walk so much as they *place* themselves. Every footfall is calculated to preserve the grip of their sandals—carved from a dense, cork-like fungus harvested from the massif's darker crevices—against the slick gradient of the stone. 

They move with a terrifying smoothness. Because the air is thin and sound carries with such violent clarity, they have developed a secondary language of gesture. They communicate through "the Stillness," a complex system of finger-signs and posture shifts that can convey entire histories without a single vibration of the vocal cords. When they do speak, their voices are dry, raspy whistles—sound stripped of all unnecessary moisture, humming through the acoustic tubes of the town like the mountain breathing through a flute.

### The Weaver-Architects
The social hierarchy of Oru is not defined by power, but by the mastery of the Static. 

At the highest tiers live the **Condensers**. These are the elders, draped in heavy, unwoven cloaks of raw fiber that have been stiffened with mineral salts until they stand up on their own, like wearable sculptures. The Condensers spend decades watching the "tear-tracks." They can predict the arrival of a single bead of moisture by the subtle shift in the stone's temperature against their palms. They are the cartographers of the microscopic, knowing every hairline fracture in the town’s geometry.

Below them are the **Friction-Kings**, the laborers who maintain the verticality of the town. They do not use pulleys or cranes—relying on such things would imply a trust in motion that Oru does not possess. Instead, they move heavy blocks of stone through "breath-timing," a synchronized effort where a dozen people exert pressure in perfect silence, moving the weight an inch every hour until the friction gives way to placement.

### The Biology of Scarcity
The internal chemistry of an Oruan has adapted to the "frozen equilibrium." Their heartbeats are incredibly slow—perhaps twenty beats per minute—and their body temperature sits just above the ambient chill of the massif. This allows them to exist on a diet that would starve a lowland creature. They eat a pale, tasteless paste made from the shadow-lichen and the protein of the obsidian beetles, supplemented by "mineral-salt," which they lick from the walls of the cisterns to retain what little water their bodies hold.

They do not sleep in the way we understand it. Instead, they enter a state of "standing diapause." They wedge themselves into narrow vertical niches in their stone homes, locking their joints and lowering their metabolic rate until they are indistinguishable from the statues that decorate the niches of the lower tiers. They can remain this way for days, waiting for the air to cool enough for the moisture to slick the tracks.

### The Cult of the Ancestor-Object
There is no concept of "new" or "old" among the people of Oru, only "refined." An Oruan does not own a knife; they are the temporary custodian of a sliver of obsidian that has been thinned by three hundred years of careful sharpening. 

When an inhabitant dies, they are not buried—there is no earth to receive them. Instead, they are taken to the "Well of Return" at the very base of the town. Over centuries, their bodies are slowly desiccated by the parched air until they become as hard and light as driftwood. These "Dry Ancestors" are then integrated into the architecture itself. A bridge might be reinforced with the ribcage of a master weaver; a ladder-rung might be the femur of a basin-guard. In Oru, the people do not inhabit the town; they *become* it.

### The Interior Life
Inside their minds, there is no room for ambition or change. They dream in geometry and mathematics. To an Oruan, a perfect right angle is a form of prayer; a dry cistern is a moral failure. They possess a collective memory that is nearly geological in its scale. They remember the placement of every stone as if it were a mole on their own skin.

They are the ultimate curators of the end of the world. They live in the quiet certainty that they have reached the final height, the final shape, and the final silence. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply there, pulse by slow pulse, ensuring that the stillness of the mountain remains unblemished by the chaos of life.
