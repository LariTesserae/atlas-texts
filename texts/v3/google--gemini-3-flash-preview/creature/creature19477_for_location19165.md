The inhabitants of this vertical mire are people of skin and water, their very biology adapted to the "refrigerator-cool" intensity of the heights. They are shorter than lowland kin, barrel-chested to accommodate lungs that must draw deeply from the oxygen-rich, moisture-thick air. Their skin has a subtle, rubbery sheen—an evolutionary gift of natural oils that allow the constant drizzle to bead and roll off them like water from a seal’s back. To look into their eyes is to see the environment reflected; their pupils are wide, permanently dilated to catch the pearlescent glow of the fog, often ringed with a pale, milky iris that grants them sharp definition in the perpetual twilight.

They move with a "low-gravity" fluidity born of generations spent on unstable ground. Because the mountain is a sponge, they do not walk with the heavy heel-strike of a plains-dweller. Instead, they move with a prehensile, splayed-toe gait, their feet as expressive and gripping as hands, capable of finding purchase on a slick fern-trunk or a swaying willow-cable without a second thought.

### The Dynamics of Kinship and Guild
Life here is too dense for solitary existence. The population is organized into *Tendrils*—extended familial guilds that serve as the organs of the city. A Tendril does not own a territory; it maintains a function. 

There are the **Suture-Men**, recognized by the coils of silver-vine looped around their torsos. They are the structural engineers of the abyss, moving toward the sound of a snap with frantic speed. They don’t use nails or stone; they use "quick-grow" grafts and caustic saps that fuse broken wood back into a living whole within minutes. They are the reason the city doesn't dissolve into the depths.

There are the **Well-Singers**, who manage the clay-lined troughs. Their bodies are often stained with the rust-red minerals they divert. They are masters of fluid dynamics, obsessed with the "voice" of the water. They can tell by the pitch of a trickling rivulet if a plateau above is becoming dangerously oversaturated, signaling an evacuation before the peat-slump begins.

### The Symbiotes
The people do not live alone in their hollowed ferns. Every household is a microcosm of the bazaar. 

The loaf-sized beetles—the **Thrum-bugs**—are more than just background noise. They are the domesticated "oxen" of the heights. The inhabitants have learned to lure them with fermented moss-nectar, strapping light wicker baskets to their iridescent carapaces. These insects ferry small goods between the vertical layers, their low-frequency vibration acting as a beacon for those navigating the thickest mists. A home is considered prosperous if it has a "humming rafter"—a dedicated perch where these heavy-bodied insects rest and vibrate, their collective body heat providing the only warmth against the chill silk of the air.

Below the walkways, in the shadowed "under-leaf," live the **Mist-Dogs**. They are not canines, but large, six-limbed amphibians with the long, questing snouts of anteaters. They are the primary waste-disposers of the city, consuming the rot that constant humidity brings. They are kept as pets and sentinels; their skin changes color from pale translucent to a bruised indigo when a storm-front or a predatory "Cloud-Cat" (a spindly-limbed, pale-furred hunter) approaches from the lower mist.

### The Culture of the Instant
Because their world is in a state of high-speed repair, these people have no concept of "antiquity." To them, a bridge that has lasted ten years is a miracle of stubbornness. They value the *becoming* over the *built*. Their art is not sculpture, but intricate, living topiary—shaping the gargantuan ferns into grotesque, beautiful fractals that serve as landmarks. 

Their language is a percussion of wet sounds, mimics of the environment. They have forty different words for "drip," distinguishing between the sound of water hitting a soft moss and water hitting a stiffened lily-pad shingle. They are a people of the "now," possessed by a frantic, cheerful fatalism. They eat standing up, they weave while they walk, and they sing in discordant harmony with the shrill amphibians of the rock-faces.

To stay still is to sink. To stay still is to let the moss claim your feet. And so, they are a civilization in a state of perpetual, graceful franticness—a city of hands and vines, forever weaving itself out of the rot and into the light.
