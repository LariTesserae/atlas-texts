To live here is to be a component.

The inhabitants of this cathedral-void do not see themselves as masters of the machine, nor as stewards of the forest, but as the lubricant within the joint. They are the **Lichen-Smiths**, a people whose skin has taken on the translucent, waxy quality of the surrounding flora. Their veins do not map just their own circulation, but seem to pulse in unsettling synchronicity with the thrumming seed pods. They are tall, gaunt, and supple, their bones reinforced by the same lignin that hardens the great vines, allowing them to withstand the crushing gravitational pressures of the deep earth.

They do not wear clothes so much as they wear "enclosures"—garments of woven aerial root steeped in the chrome-slick secretions of the cat-sized insects. This makes them smell of ozone and bitter almond. They move with a strange, liquid gait, a constant micro-adjustment to the heaving respiration of the floor.

But the Lichen-Smiths are only the visible layer of the census.

Deep within the "weeping joints," where the glycerin-thick water pools, live the **Valve-Tenders**. These are not entirely human, if they ever were. They have become bloated and stationary, fused into the copper catch-basins by centuries of mineral buildup. They function as living sensors. With their fingers elongated into fine, sensitive filaments that brush against the passing currents of steam and sap, they "feel" the health of the engine. They hum in low, multi-tonal registers, a sound that the moths follow like a map. When a Valve-Tender’s song sharpens into a frantic whistle, the city knows a vine is parched or a piston is seizing.

Then there are the **Shadow-Gliders**, the sleek, eyeless mammalian things you glimpsed. Up close, they are more like living mercury than fur and bone. They possess a terrifying, silent efficiency. They are the nervous system of the town. On their backs, they carry "data-galls"—spherical plant growths that contain chemical messages or tactile blueprints. They do not navigate by sight, but by the "heat-ghosts" of the machinery. They are revered as sacred messengers, and the townspeople leave bowls of fermented sap and iron filings out to entice them to rest near their hearths.

The insects—the **Chrome-Carapaced**—are the true custodians of the infrastructure. They are not mindless; they possess a collective, clicking intelligence. You will see a swarm of a hundred beetles working in perfect unison to polish a brass gear or to clear an obstruction of overgrowth from a cooling vent. They communicate through the percussion of their carapaces against the metal pipes—a frantic, rhythmic code that the human inhabitants have learned to mimic by tapping their tools against the catwalks. To live here is to be part of a perpetual, multi-species conversation.

Higher up, in the indigo gloom of the vaulted ceiling, live the **Aery-Weavers**. These are the dreamers of the cathedral. They are rarely seen on the floor, spending their lives suspended in hammocks of woven moth-silk. They tend to the "vibrating looms," guiding the hanging roots into the maws of the machines. They speak in whispers, their lungs habituated to the thin, terpene-rich air of the heights. They believe that the great vines are dreaming the world above into existence, and that their weaving is what kept the "surface" from collapsing under its own weight.

In the center of it all, within the hottest, most feverish pocket of the cavern, lives the **Conductor**. It is unclear if the Conductor is a person, a machine, or a particularly complex knot of roots. It sits within a cage of pulsing glass and copper, its body wreathed in the bioluminescent steam of the primary furnace. It does not speak, but its respiration dictates the rhythm of the entire chamber. When the Conductor sighs, the moths settle; when it shudders, the steam-hammers strike with a ferocity that rattles the teeth of everyone in the settlement.

Everything here—the insect, the gardener, the mammal, and the plant—is bound by the **Glycerin-Debt**. No one eats in the traditional sense. Instead, they press their palms to the "sweating walls" or drink from the copper basins, absorbing the nutrient-dense sludge that the machinery produces as a byproduct of its friction. It is a closed loop of hunger and production. 

The people do not dream of the sun; they find the idea of an unshielded ball of fire in a vacuum to be terrifying and inefficient. To them, "home" is a place where you are always felt, always heard, and always part of the pulse. They are the gardeners of a god made of brass and chlorophyll, and they labor with a joyful, frantic devotion, knowing that if they ever stopped, the heart of the world would cease its beat, and the heavy, rhythmic silence of the earth would finally claim them.
