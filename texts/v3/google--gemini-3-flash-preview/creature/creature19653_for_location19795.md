In the Gully of the Brass Kelp, the concept of "life" has undergone a process of heavy sedimentation. Distinction has been lost; the inhabitants do not merely live *in* the machinery, they are the byproduct of its exhaust and the witnesses to its slow, rhythmic decay.

### The Calcified Guilds
The most prevalent "residents" are the **Valve-Tenders**. These are not biological entities in the traditional sense, but clusters of nervous, multi-limbed invertebrates that have fused with the brass interfaces of the district. Over generations, their ancestors nested in the warmth of the pressure-valves, and the minerals in the water—copper, salt, and slag—have replaced their carapaces with a brittle, metallic bark. 

They do not swim. Instead, they move with a jerky, clockwork gait along the cable-webs, their many-jointed legs ending in specialized hooks that fit perfectly into the grooves of the industrial screws. They possess no eyes, for vision is a liability in the violet murk; instead, they press their soft underbellies against the vibrating pipes, "hearing" the health of the city through the frequency of the steam. They are the gardeners of this ruin, obsessively polishing certain gears with their tongues and scraping away the aggressive glass-fiber sponges that threaten to jam the slow-turning turbines.

### The Vein-Leeches
Clinging to the thrumming, high-voltage cables are the **Copper-Siphons**. These are long, ribbon-like organisms that look indistinguishable from the Brass Kelp until they move. They wrap themselves in tight spirals around the pulsing hoses, their translucent bodies tumescent with stolen thermal energy. 

They are parasites of heat. When a pressure valve hisses, releasing a cloud of scalding silt, the Siphons unfurl like blooming flowers to catch the warmth. They have a primitive communal intelligence; if one cable Grows cold, they migrate in a silent, undulating wave, a smear of pale, glowing jelly drifting from one "district" to the next, following the warmth like a nomadic tribe following a dying sun.

### The Archivists of Silt
Deep in the foundations, where the house-sized boilers rest in the velvet sludge, live the **Centenarian Crabs**. These are massive, slow-moving crustaceans whose shells have become the foundations for entire miniature ecosystems. A single crab may be five paces across, its back a forest of kelp, sponges, and discarded rivets. 

They move so slowly—perhaps one league every decade—that they have become part of the geography. Their roles are those of planetary historians. They consume the thick, industrial silt, filtering out the "memory" of the machines—the flakes of rust, the chemical signatures of the fluids being pumped, the minute shards of glass. They are the only things in the Gully that remember why the machines were built, processed through a digestive logic that turns industrial waste into a thick, glowing ambergris they secrete into the canyon floor.

### The Ghost-Gears
There are things in the Gully that defy biological classification. These are the **Kinetic Echoes**. Sometimes, a machine cycles for so many centuries that its movement creates a localized eddy in the thick, gelatinous water—a "habit" of the medium itself. 

One might see a ghost-piston: a shimmering, translucent column of water that rises and falls with perfect mechanical precision, even though the iron hardware it once belonged to has long since rusted into dust. These apparitions are harmless but eerie; they are the ghosts of the industry, the "soul" of the factory persisting after the body has failed. Small, bioluminescent shrimp often dance within these kinetic ghosts, caught in the eternal loop of a phantom stroke.

### The Apex: The Dredge-Wurm
At the very bottom of the gully, where the pressure is an absolute, bone-crushing weight, lives the **Dredge-Wurm**. It is less an animal and more a living pipe, miles long and thick as a tenement. It is a scavenger of the "Silent Industry," its mouth a massive, circular vacuum that attaches to the outtake vents of the oldest boilers. 

It does not hunt; it waits for the machines to "feed" it their concentrated waste. Its skin is a patchwork of hammered lead and leathery hide, scarred by a thousand years of abrasions against the canyon walls. The Wurm is the ultimate expression of the Gully’s rule of **Saturation**. It fills the gaps between the buildings, its slow pulses synchronized with the vibration of the turbines. To the smaller residents, the Wurm is not a creature, but a shifting wall—a warm, pulsing mountain of flesh and metal that defines the boundaries of their world.

In the Gully, no one speaks, and nothing dies quickly. To live here is to be integrated—to become a part of the plumbing, a slow-moving gear in a clock that forgot what time it was supposed to keep.
