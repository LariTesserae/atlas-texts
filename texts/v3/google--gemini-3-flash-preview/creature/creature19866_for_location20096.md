In the Deep Sump of the Basin, life does not occupy the landscape; it is fused into the structural integrity of the world. Because there is no "empty" space, the inhabitants have evolved to be interlocking components of a singular, pressurized machine.

### The Crustacean Anchors: The Litho-Crabs
The hound-sized, soft-shelled crustaceans mentioned are the **Glandular Anchors**. Their shells are not hard, defensive plates, but absorbent, rubbery membranes that pull minerals directly from the weeping "rope-water." They do not scuttle; they exert a constant, hydraulic pressure against the conduits and the pre-stone floor. 

They are essentially living valves. They clamp their wide, suction-cup undersides over leaking steam vents or fissures in the silt, regulating the pressure of the Deep Sump with their own internal bellows. Their "faces" are arrays of sensitive, vibrating filaments that taste the chemistry of the fog. When they move—which happens only once every few decades—it is an agonizingly slow migration to a new mineral vein, leaving behind a perfectly molded impression in the pre-stone that soon hardens into an industrial socket.

### The Aerial Fluids: The Hum-Eels
The translucent, eel-like predators are the **Vibrational Scavengers**. Because the atmosphere is so dense it behaves like a liquid, these creatures do not fly; they "distplace." They are ribbons of muscle and luminous jelly, three to six feet long, with bones made of a flexible, cartilage-like glass. 

They have no eyes. Instead, they possess a central organ that resonates in perfect sympathy with the 2.7-level roar of the turbines. They hunt by detecting disruptions in the sound field—a "discordance" caused by a moving limb or a falling rock. To see them is to see a shimmering distortion in the amber light, a glitch in the fog that glows with a faint, rhythmic pulse of indigo. They feed on the microscopic fungal spores that thrive on the heat of the iron pipes, scraping the metal clean with abrasive, sandpaper tongues.

### The Micro-Architects: The Silt-weavers
In the gaps between the massive turbines and the floor live the **Silt-weavers**. These are colonial organisms, resembling thick, calcified mats of wet velvet. They are the true bridge between biology and geology. They consume the obsidian-dark silt and excrete a biological cement that accelerates the lithification of the ground. 

They are the reason the industrial conduits seem "grown" into the rock. The weavers climb the legs of the machines, encasing them in layers of living, mineral-rich grey matter. They are the maintenance crew of the Sump, sealing cracks in the iron and reinforcing the foundations of the churning blades. To step on a Weaver-mat is to feel a slow, sucking warmth; they do not bite, but they will begin to grow over your boots within minutes if you remain still.

### The Ghost-Engineers: The Humid Hollows
Deep within the maze of pipes and liverwort-choked ribs, there is a presence that is felt rather than seen. These are the **Pressure-Walkers**, the remnants or descendants of whatever force installed the turbines. They are not biological in any recognizable sense. They appear as humanoid-shaped voids in the fog—disturbances in the steam that maintain a constant, upright posture.

They move with the same glacial intent as the land itself. You might see one standing by a gauge the size of a shield, its "hand" (a blur of high-pressure vapor) resting on a dial that hasn't turned in a century. They do not communicate with sound, as the roar of the Sump would drown them out; instead, they change their internal temperature, casting heat-shadows that the other fauna use as navigational beacons.

### The Silent Flora: The Liverwort Ribs
The massive, rubbery clumps of **Pale Liverwort** are the lungs of the Sump. They lack leaves, consisting instead of miles of translucent, vein-filled tubing that wraps around the high stone arches. They drink the "wet-dark," processing the ozone and sulfur into a thick, nutrient-rich sap that drips like honey from their hanging tips. 

This sap is the primary fuel for the entire ecosystem. The crustaceans feed on the drips; the eels rub their bodies against the sap-streaks to maintain their bioluminescence; and the Silt-weavers use the sticky residue to bind the obsidian mud. The liverworts hum, too—a low, rhythmic thumping that suggests their roots are wrapped directly around the central shafts of the subterranean turbines, drinking the very vibration of the world.

### The Collective Consciousness
Nothing in the Deep Sump is an individual. Every creature is a gear in a wet, dark, and deafening clock. They are the "white noise" of the planet's birth—muffled, heavy, and vital. To live here is to be part of the weight, a single cell in a massive, working organ that is slowly, loudly, turning itself into stone.
