In the damp shadows of the Great Loom, life does not thrive; it persists as a function of the friction. The inhabitants are not masters of this place, nor are they truly its prisoners. They are its **lubricants**.

The first thing you notice about the **Loom-Folk** is their skin. It is the color of unfired slip—a pale, translucent grey-mauve that seems to hold the moisture of the air. Because there is no sun, their skin has never toughened; it is soft and perpetually damp, often stained with the verdigris of the bronze pipes or the orange dust of grinding terracotta. They are hairless, save for fine, colorless bristles on their forearms used to sense the vibrations of the gears. 

They do not walk so much as they *tread*. Their feet have evolved wide, splayed soles with toughened, leathery pads to grip the slick, thumb-printed shingles. To a Loom-Dweller, silence is a sign of catastrophe. They move in a rhythmic sway that matches the *thud-hiss* of the bellows; if a person stands too still, they become nauseated by the lack of pulse.

### The Caste of the Kiln-Keepers
Deep within the "cathedral-kilns," where the air is still warm enough to ache, live the **Sintered**. These are the elders and the engineers. They are easily identified by their hands, which have been burned and calloused into something resembling cracked ceramic. They do not use tools to shape the repair-tiles; they use their palms, pressing their own unique life-lines into the clay that will eventually become the floor for the next generation. 

They speak in low, percussive hums. Their language is not based on vowels, but on the mimicry of the machine. A "sentence" sounds like the clicking of a wooden cog. They believe that the Great Loom is a living creature that has forgotten its purpose, and their only duty is to keep it from seizing up. They "feed" the machine with oils rendered from the Scuttlers and with the pressed cakes of phosphorescent mold.

### The Scuttlers: The Soft Ecology
Below the feet of the humans, the **Scuttlers** represent the only true "wildlife." These creatures are the size of a loaf of bread, their bodies composed of segmented, pale-white plates that feel like damp porcelain. They are the Great Loom’s janitors. 

They possess a strange, frantic intelligence. You will see them in clusters around a leaking bronze joint, their many-jointed limbs (the "fingers") working in a blur to scrape away the oxidation. They do not eat plants or meat; they consume the "bitter sweat" of the machine—the mineral-heavy condensation that leaches out of the clay and metal. 

When a Scuttler dies, it does not rot in the traditional sense. Its soft interior liquefies into a grey paste, while its outer shell becomes brittle. The humans harvest these shells, grinding them down to create the "High-Glaze"—a waterproof sealant used to patch the most vital conduits. In this way, even the fauna is eventually integrated back into the mechanical architecture.

### The Whispering Mold
On the furthest reaches of the Great Loom, where the gears turn slowest and the dampness is absolute, a different kind of inhabitant resides: the **Spore-Singers**. 

These are humans who have stayed too long in the darkness, away from the flickering oil lamps. They have developed a symbiotic relationship with the pale, phosphorescent mold. The mold grows in thin, dendritic patterns across their chests and faces, glowing with a rhythmic, low-voltage pulse. These people act as living lanterns for the maintenance crews. 

They are eerily calm. They claim they can "hear" the friction of the entire world. They can tell when a counterweight three miles away is beginning to fray its leather harness simply by the change in the mold’s vibration. They are the nervous system of the Loom, wandering the charcoal-colored gaps, tracing the veins of the machine with their glowing fingers.

### The Geology of the Living
Because there is no "away" in this world, death is handled with mechanical efficiency. When a Loom-Dweller dies, they are not buried; they are **Inlaid**. Their bodies are placed into the cooling vents of the smaller kilns, where they are desiccated and then crushed. This bone-meal is mixed with the terracotta sand of the industrial valleys to create a "Grave-Brick." 

The "hills" of the world are quite literally built from the ancestors. You might walk across a terrace and see a brick with a slightly different hue—a fleck of calcium or a streak of iron—and know that you are standing on the remains of a master potter. 

### The Great Stasis
Life here is defined by a lack of ambition. There is no concept of "progress," only **Retention**. To the living things of the Loom—human, Scuttler, and Mold—the world is a finished work that is slowly unravelling. Their lives are spent in a beautiful, desperate struggle against erosion. 

They are the keepers of the friction. They live in the narrow crawlways, sleeping in hammocks made of woven copper wire, lulled to sleep by the grinding of stone teeth. They are a people of the damp, of the shadow, and of the rhythmic thump; a civilization that has accepted that they are merely the grease between the gears of a god made of mud.
