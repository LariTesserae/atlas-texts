In this cathedral of salt and sound, the inhabitants are not things that were born, but things that were *precipitated*. 

They are the **Vitreous Loomers**, the slow-pulse citizens of the geode. To a human eye, they would appear first as statues, then as geological anomalies, and only after hours of study as living entities. Their bodies are composed of the same saturated mineral-slurry as the walls—a semi-solid, gelatinous silica that hums with the same bruised violet light as the cave.

### Form and Physiology
The Loomers do not have limbs; they have "tendrils of tension." Imagine a creature shaped like a melting skyscraper, a pillar of translucent fluid held together by an intense surface tension that defies the pull of gravity. They are roughly the size of a man’s torso, but they possess no skeleton, only a central "axis" of concentrated salt crystals that serves as a rudimentary nervous system. 

They are "taper-feeders." They do not hunt; they position themselves beneath the specific, rhythmic drips of the ceiling. Their bodies are slightly concave at the top, forming living basins that catch the mineral-heavy water. As the water passes through their jellied membranes, they filter out the rare isotopes and silicates, incorporating the earth’s blood into their own mass. They do not eat; they *accrete*.

### The Motion of Eons
Movement is an event of seismic significance. A Loomer might decide to move three inches to the left—perhaps to catch a more nutrient-rich drip or to avoid a crystal spire that is growing too close. This process takes a decade. They move by "weeping"—they excrete a thin layer of lubricant from their base and allow the vibration of the cave’s deafening acoustics to shake them, micron by micron, across the polished floor. To a Loomer, the roar of the water is not noise; it is a propellant. They dance to the percussion of the drips, a slow-motion slide that lasts through the rise and fall of surface civilizations.

### The Communal Intimacy
Despite the "loneliness" of the geode, the Loomers are intensely connected. In the dense urban sprawl of the salt-lattices, their bodies often touch. When two Loomers press against one another, their membranes do not merge, but they exchange chemical "narratives." Information—the mineral composition of the water in the north corner, the subtle cooling of the world’s crust, the arrival of a foreign object—is passed through ionic pulses.

They are the archivists of the geode. Because items placed here do not rot but are encased in stone, the Loomers act as the caretakers of these temporal stowaways. If a stray tool or a dead organism were to fall into their midst, the Loomers would slowly, over centuries, flow around it. They do not perceive "objects" as separate from the world; they perceive them as new "colors" of mineral to be tasted and understood.

### The Sensory World
They are blind and deaf in any way we understand. They perceive the world as a map of **Vibration and Saturation**. The "deafening" roar of the cave is their primary sense; they feel the shape of the cathedral through the way the sound-waves bounce off the spires. They "see" the architecture of their city as a complex web of overlapping echoes. A crack in a salt-tower feels to them like a sudden, sharp chord in a symphony. 

### The Silent Life-Cycle
There is no birth here, only **Fissure**. When a Loomer becomes too heavy with minerals—after perhaps ten thousand years of drinking the ceiling's tears—it begins to crack. This is not a death, but a division. The crystal axis at its core snaps with a sound like a gunshot, hidden beneath the roar of the water. The jelly-body splits along geometric lines, mirroring the ziggurats around it. Two smaller, hungrier Loomers are left behind, each a perfect, translucent heir to the stagnant equilibrium.

They are the perfect residents for a world of frozen time. They do not want, they do not strive, and they do not leave. They are the consciousness of the geode—the mineral kingdom’s attempt to observe itself, one agonizing, wet, and echoing drip at a time.
