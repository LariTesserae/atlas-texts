The inhabitants of this world-root are not a populous race, but a sparse guild of **Lignin-Stewards**. They are a people defined by a lack of hurry, their very temperaments having been forged by the glacial expansion of the wood they call home. To look upon one is to see a study in muted copper and deep, mossy textiles. They dress in layers of boiled wool and oil-treated leather, necessary garments for the "cellar-chill" that never lifts. 

Their skin possesses a translucent, waxy quality, pale from a life spent under the amber glow of fungus-jars, often mapped with fine, dark veins that mimic the patterns of the very roots they inhabit. They do not walk so much as they drift with a practiced, heavy-footed stability, their boots tipped with soft lead to prevent sparking against the steel gratings or scarring the sacred, mahogany-hard floor.

### The Surgeons of the Sap
The primary caste of this city is the **Hydraulic Clergy**. They are less engineers and more vascular surgeons. They carry long, thin brass stethoscopes and bundles of glass capillary tubes. You will find them pressed against the walls, ears tilted toward the wood, listening for the "thrum-thud" of a sap-vein. When a sap-engine wheezes or a piston falters, they do not strike the machine with a hammer; they apply a poultice to the wood or adjust a grafting-shim to re-pressurize the flow.

They move with a solemn, rhythmic patience, often standing motionless for hours to observe the fall of a single drip-clock. To these people, time is not measured in minutes, but in the millimeter-per-year expansion of a burl. Their speech reflects this—a low-frequency, resonant murmur, sparse in words but heavy with intention.

### The Scripter-Keepers
While the brassy, many-legged "scripters" are the only fauna, they are not wild. They are the beloved familiars of the **Polishers**, a class of citizens who live in the higher, more precarious galleries. These stewards carry porcelain whistles that emit frequencies human ears cannot hear, guiding the scripters to patches of soot on the light-jars or mineral buildup on the brass pipes. 

The relationship is symbiotic: the Polishers provide the insects with dabs of nutrient-thick resin, and the scripters keep the city’s "eyes"—the amber lanterns—clear. A Polisher is rarely seen without a few scripters clinging to their heavy sleeves, their metallic carapaces clicking rhythmically like a pocket watch.

### The Graft-Architects
If the Clergy are the surgeons, the **Graft-Architects** are the sculptors. They are the ones who negotiate the "peace treaty" between the buildings and the organism. They carry intricate, clockwork drafting tools and jars of caustic "growth-stunts." 

When the world-root begins to groan—a sign that a structural timber is being slowly crushed by a thickening root—the Architects are called. They do not demolish; they redirect. They might install a series of heated copper coils to discourage growth in one direction, or apply a nutrient-slurry to "lure" a root-buttress into a more favorable structural shape. They are the record-keepers of the city’s slow-motion drift, possessing scrolls that map where a wall stood three centuries ago, predicting with eerie accuracy where the floor will buckle in the next fifty years.

### The Sensate Solitude
Because the workshops are separated by "cavernous, vegetable silence," the people have developed a highly acute sense of vibration. In the absence of bustling crowds, they communicate across the distances of the city by striking "tuners"—resonant brass rods bolted into the lignin floor. The vibrations travel through the hard, fibrous wood far better than sound travels through the damp air. 

At "noon"—the time when the overhead drip-clocks are most active—the city hums. Not with voices, but with these tactile signals. A Steward miles away can feel the "shiver" of a neighbor’s greeting through the soles of their feet.

### The Parasitic Ethos
There is no arrogance in these people. They live with the constant, humbling knowledge that they are guests—or perhaps more accurately, useful microbes—within a much larger life. They do not bury their dead; they "re-integrate" them. When a citizen passes, they are placed in a deep fissue of the root, sealed with resin and brass plates, their mineral essence returned to the host that gave them their floor and ceiling.

They are a quiet, meticulous folk who have traded the frantic pace of the sun-lit world for the steady, thrumming heart of the root. They are the keepers of the "damp-cool," moving between the amber pools of light, forever listening to the slow, heavy breathing of the world they serve.
