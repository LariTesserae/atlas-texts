The inhabitants of this vertical sea, the **Syrin**, are as unweathered and translucent as the obsidian they inhabit. They are not creatures of bone or scale; having evolved in a world without the crushing weight of a planet or the resistance of a current, they are elongated, spindly, and strikingly fragile. 

At first glance, a Syrin resembles a ghost caught in a lens flare—a silhouette of pale, milky cartilage and shimmering, vein-mapped skin. Their bodies are long, stretching ten or twelve feet from crown to toe, but they possess almost no mass. Their limbs are like the obsidian needles: long, tapering to points, and jointed with a crystalline fluidity. They do not have feet for walking or fins for swimming; instead, their fingers and toes have evolved into delicate, prehensile hooks, designed for snagging the fiber-ropes of their hanging city.

Movement for a Syrin is a choreography of momentum and patience. To "travel" is to push off a glass pillar with a single, languid extension of a leg and drift for minutes through the gelatinous green dark, eventually catching a kelp-vine with a hooked finger.

### The Anatomy of Silence
Their faces are smooth, devoid of the jagged features required for heavy breathing or mastication. They lack noses and ears. Instead, their skin is their primary sensory organ—a vast, porous membrane that tastes the chemical composition of the water and feels the low-frequency vibrations of the obsidian core. 

Where a human would have eyes, the Syrin have large, recessed pits covered by a reflective film. These do not see "images" in the traditional sense; they perceive the refraction of light through the glass pillars, turning their world into a map of geometric shimmers. They navigate by the "glow" of the core and the "cold" of the vacuum. 

Communication is a physical intimacy. When two Syrin speak, they often press their chests together or hold a shared obsidian rod. The "humming" described is a vibration of their internal air-bladders—vestigial organs that once helped with buoyancy but now serve as instruments. To speak is to make the other person's body resonate. A joke is a light, fluttering frequency; a mourning song is a vibration so deep it makes the kelp-pods shiver.

### The Weavers of the Pressure
The Syrin are defined by their "advanced primitivity." Because they cannot use fire, their chemistry is biological. They are master horticulturalists. A Syrin "blacksmith" does not forge; they graft. They spend centuries breeding specific strains of the pulsating vines to grow in geometric patterns, essentially "growing" their tools and homes.

The "caste" of a Syrin is often determined by their relationship to the verticality:

*   **The Root-Tenders:** They live closest to the scorching obsidian core. Their skin is thickened, tanned a deep, bruised purple by the heat. They are the strongest of the race, tasking themselves with carving the glass and harvesting the raw silicates. They are heavy, slow, and scarred by the razor-edges of the unweathered world.
*   **The Mesh-Makers:** Living in the temperate middle, they are the artists and weavers. They spend their decades-long lives twisting the bio-engineered fibers into the city's infrastructure. They are characterized by their extreme patience; a Mesh-Maker might spend three years perfecting the tension of a single bridge-line.
*   **The Membrane-Watchers:** The most ethereal and strange. They live near the outer limits, where the water turns cold and the sun-void screams through the thin skin of the world. They are almost entirely transparent, their internal organs visible as flickering, bioluminescent sparks. They watch for tears in the world’s surface, wielding bladders of "living glue" to patch the leaks into the vacuum.

### The Philosophy of the Unbroken
The Syrin have no concept of "falling." To them, the center is not a floor, but a furnace; the edge is not a ceiling, but a disappearance. This has bred a psychology of profound "holding." They do not like to let go. Their myths are not about heroes who conquered, but about ancestors who held on the longest. 

They view the sharpness of their world—the razor-edges of the obsidian—as a sacred state. To them, "smoothness" is a sign of decay, a terrifying omen of the end of the world. They fear the day the glass begins to round, for that would mean the water has begun to win, and the embryonic dream is over. 

They are a people of the "Eternal Present." Because the water is viscous and the light never truly changes, they don't count days. They count "pulses"—the rhythmic thrum of the obsidian core settling. A Syrin might sit in the crook of a glass needle for a human week, perfectly still, watching the way a Drifter’s tendril interacts with a purple fern, lost in a state of suspended, emerald meditation. They are not waiting for the future; they are part of a world that is still deciding how to begin.
