In the High Cradle, the residents are not merely inhabitants; they are **The Tension-Keepers**, a people who have internalized the kinetic energy of the rising stone. They do not walk so much as they lean, their bodies perpetually compensating for the subtle, tectonic vibration that hums through the resin soles of their footwear.

### The Physiology of the High-Born
To live in the Cradle is to be physically sculpted by the thinness of the air and the sharpness of the edges. The people are lean, their frames a network of wiry muscle and long, clever fingers. Their skin has a matte, weather-beaten quality, often stained with the faint, charcoal-gray dust of the basalt or the pale green of the resin sap. 

Most striking are their eyes: they are wide and slightly recessed, evolved to squint against the high-contrast glare of sun on wet obsidian. They have a "focus-lock"—a way of staring at a single point for hours, a trait born from the necessity of watching for minute frays in the mooring cables. Because the air is so sparse, they speak in low, breathy hums, often communicating through a complex tactile language. They tap rhythms on the resin walkways or pluck the tensioned vines like harp strings to send signals across the vertical districts.

### The Life of the Loom-Masters
Social status in the High Cradle is not determined by wealth, but by one's relationship to the **Bind**. 

At the top of the social hierarchy are the **Strand-Singers**. These are the engineers and architects who spend their lives suspended in harnesses over the white void. They do not build with hammers; they build with weight and counter-leverages. They are the ones who listen to the "groan" of the city. If a cable screams too high an octave, they know the rock beneath is thrusting too quickly, and the house must be "slacked" or moved. They carry bone-handled adzes to shave the sharpest edges of the emerging basalt, smoothing the path for the city’s slow, upward drift.

Beneath the bridges live the **Moss-Tenders**. Clad in waterproof leathers made from the hides of Drift-Skates, they harvest the bruised-plum mosses and the leather-stiff ferns. They are the chemists of the Cradle, boiling the vegetation in stone vats to extract the resins that make their streets and the acids used to etch their histories into the rock. They are perpetually damp, their hair matted with the condensation of the passing cloud-skeins.

### The Symbiosis of the Void
The people share this verticality with the **Drift-Skates**, but the relationship is more spectral than domestic. The Skates are treated with a quiet, superstitious reverence. On the "Low-Moons" when the mist is thickest, the inhabitants hang cages of phosphorescent lichen over the precipice. This attracts the rare mountain insects, which in turn brings the Skates close to the town’s edge. 

The people do not hunt the Skates with bows; they "fish" for them with silk-fine lines made of gut, hoping to snag a creature that has died of natural causes or exhaustion. A single Drift-Skate hide provides the translucent material for a dozen window-panes or a coat that can repel the sharpest mountain sleet.

### The Culture of the "Now"
Because the land they occupy is "new"—a raw birth of stone—the inhabitants have no concept of permanent burial. When a resident dies, they are not committed to the earth, for there is no soil to receive them. Instead, they are "given to the Wind." The body is wrapped in resin-soaked vines and suspended in the void between peaks, where the thin air and mineral-heavy mists slowly petrify the remains. These "Grave-Anchors" eventually become part of the city’s counterweight system—the ancestors literally helping to hold the houses of the living against the gale.

### The Internal Rhythm
Domestic life is quiet and rhythmic. Inside the hive-like dwellings, the air is thick with the scent of crushed mint and the steam of "Rock-Tea," a bitter infusion made from the acid-excretions of ferns. 

Furniture is nonexistent; the floors are terraced to match the slope of the rock, and the inhabitants sleep in "slings"—suspended hammocks that isolate them from the mountain’s constant, low-frequency vibration. 

They are a patient, unsentimental people. They understand that their city is a temporary agreement between the rising earth and the falling rain. They do not pray for stability—they pray for the ropes to hold, and for their hands to stay calloused enough to feel the pulse of the stone as it screams its way toward the sun.
