In this lightless, weeping crucible, the inhabitants do not live so much as they *persist*, their bodies becoming anatomical reflections of the sump’s oppressive physics.

The "citizens" glimpsed through the steam are the **Pallid-Kin**, a lineage of humans or near-humans whose skin has transitioned into something resembling wet parchment or the translucent flesh of a deep-sea fish. Because the heat is a constant, crushing weight, they have discarded the concept of clothing as protection, wearing only loincloths of treated insect-wing or aprons of heavy, oil-slicked hide to protect their torsos from the caustic splash of the brine. They are long-limbed and terrifyingly thin, their ribcages expanded into barrel-like bellows to wring oxygen from the soupy, sulfurous air. 

They do not walk with the upright confidence of surface dwellers; instead, they move with a low-slung, kinetic economy. They have mastered the "three-point lurch," using long, calloused fingers to hook into the rusted grating of the catwalks while their feet, widened and webbed by generations of wading, find purchase in the alkaline mud.

Their faces are studies in sensory adaptation. Their eyes are enormous, milky orbs, lacking pupils and tuned entirely to the infrared spectrum. To them, the "dim orange glow" of the industrial lights is not a flicker, but a searing, informative geography—they see the heat maps of the turbines, the warm thermal plumes of the insect swarms, and the cold, deadly streaks of the ceiling-water. They rarely speak. In a place where sound is a "textured roar," the Pallid-Kin have retreated into a language of tactile percussion. They communicate by tapping rhythms on the iron pipes or clicking their tongues in a complex, ultrasonic code that cuts through the thrum of the engines.

Living alongside them, in a symbiosis born of necessity, are the **Husks**. These are not quite animals and not quite machines, but heavy, slow-moving crustaceans whose shells have begun to incorporate the industrial waste of the sump. Their carapaces are reinforced with flecks of iron and slag, giving them a jagged, metallic appearance. The Pallid-Kin "farm" these creatures, not for meat, but for their filtration. A Husk tied to a tenement piling will pull gallons of brine through its gills, excreting a concentrated salt-paste that the citizens use as both a preservative and a crude building mortar.

Higher up, in the "spiderwebs" of the gantries, live the **Glimmer-Wrights**. These are the technicians of the sump, a sub-caste that never touches the mud. Their skin is darker, stained copper by the constant proximity to the steam vents and grease. They are the ones who tend to the gargantuan, slow-turning turbines. They move with an unsettling, insectile grace, using hooked prosthetics to slide along the cabling. They view the cavern not as a home, but as a failing lung that they must keep breathing. They are obsessed with the rhythm of the pumps; to a Glimmer-Wright, a change in the pitch of a motor is a portent of a catastrophic flood. 

Then there is the "Over-Life"—the things that truly own the shadows. 

Deep in the alkaline silt, beneath the iron pilings, dwell the **Silt-Lurkers**. These are the "multi-legged things" whose movements ripple the water. They are soft-bodied, segmented horrors, yards long, that feed on the falling dead and the scrap metal alike. They have no eyes at all, only sensitive bristles that can detect the heartbeat of a Pallid-Kin through fifty feet of brine. The citizens fear the "Still-Water," for when the ripples stop, it means a Lurker is rising to the surface to snatch a stray limb from a low-hanging catwalk. 

The most sacred and reviled of the inhabitants, however, are the **Moth-Keepers**. In the highest, hottest reaches of the vault, where the "miles of solid rock" are closest, a few hermits live in cages of woven copper. They tend to the clouds of heavy-bodied moths, harvesting the thick, grey silk the insects produce. This silk is the only fabric that can withstand the rot of the sump. The Keepers are blind and deaf, their senses annihilated by the sheer proximity to the screaming steam-whistles of the upper vents, yet they move with a rhythmic, trancelike certainty.

In this town of iron and clay, life is a slow-motion riot. There is no joy, but there is an intense, vibrating intimacy. Every inhabitant is connected by the hum of the metal and the dampness of their skin. They are a civilization of the "Wet-Breath," a people who have forgotten the sun but have learned to find a terrible, beautiful meaning in the taste of salt and the endless, heavy pulse of the Earth's burning heart.
