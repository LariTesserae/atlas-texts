The things that live here are called the Recorders, though they have no name for themselves. They are the landscape's memory, its method, and its motion. You will not see them at first, because to see them you must unlearn the distinction between inhabitant and environment. They are not *in* the place; they *are* the place, made temporarily distinct.

They coalesce. They do not have a permanent, solid form, much as the water in the air has no single form until it beads on a surface. They begin in the still, silver pools. If you were to watch one for a century, you might see it. A patch on the mirrored surface ceases to reflect the sky. It darkens, growing opaque, the colour of a thundercloud or a bruise. It is the water gathering itself, drawing in the suspended peat and the fine, dark grit of the igneous rock. It is a concentration of substance and, more importantly, of purpose.

Slowly, this patch of darkness will lift from the surface of the pool. It rises not like a creature of flesh and bone, but like a drapery of heavy, wet velvet being pulled up by an invisible thread. It is a being of held water, a figure of colloidal suspension, its skin a shimmering membrane of surface tension, barely containing the dark, mineral-rich liquid within. Light does not pass through them, nor does it bounce cleanly off. It is swallowed, giving them a profound, light-eating quality that makes them appear as holes in the world. Their form is vaguely humanoid only in that it is vertical and bilaterally symmetrical, but it possesses the stark geometry of the city. Their limbs are not rounded, but faceted like raw crystals. Their heads are not featured, but are smooth, oblong monoliths. They look like ambulatory, water-filled versions of the stones they work.

They are the builders. The city was not built and then abandoned; the building is the sole and eternal activity of its inhabitants. They are the reason for the causeways' impossible straightness and the plazas' geometric perfection. This is not a feat of engineering as much as it is a function of their being. They do not measure; they simply *are* the correct measure. When one stands at the end of a causeway, its body is the plumb line. When a group of them lays a stone, they do not communicate with words, but with pressure, with the subtle shift of the water within their forms, a hydraulic consensus. Their movements are infinitely slow, patient, and silent, save for the soft, wet slap of their bases on the stone and the gentle trickle of themselves as they lose water to the air, which they must constantly reabsorb.

They have no tools. When a great block of stone must be moved, they surround it. They press their "hands"—broad, flat planes at the ends of their faceted limbs—against its surface. Then, they simply wait. They are not exerting force in a way a muscle would. They are altering a more fundamental property. The hum that one feels through the soles of their feet on the plazas is their working sound. It is the resonance of the stone being sung to, being convinced to become lighter, to yield its immense inertia. They do not lift the stone; they persuade it to float, and then guide it to its new place in the great, silent pattern.

Their skin, that shimmering membrane, is host to the same thin, emerald film of algae that coats the causeways. This is not a contamination, but a symbiosis. The algae photosynthesize the diffuse, pearlescent light, providing a subtle energy gradient that the Recorders use to maintain their integrity, to hold their water against the pull of the world-sponge. This is the only energy conversion in the entire ecosystem. The Recorders are, in essence, mobile, sentient works of topiary, gardened by algae and filled with the world's own water and stone.

They do not eat, or sleep, or speak. Their thoughts are the slow, geologic pressures of the plateau. Their conversations are the patterns they lay upon the land. The building of a new causeway is a philosophical treatise. The expansion of a plaza is a stanza of an epic poem that will never have a final line. They are recording the state of their world, not in books, but in the very stone of the world itself. The city is a manuscript written in monoliths.

They do not reproduce in a biological sense. They are an emergent property of the plateau's equilibrium. When the immense, slow "thought" of the collective requires a new action, a new Recorder simply condenses from a pool, already knowing its part in the grand, silent work. And when their form is no longer needed, or when their internal cohesion wanes, they do not die. They walk to the edge of a causeway, or a plaza, and simply… relax. The membrane dissolves. The dark water within, rich with the memory of its brief, formed existence, spills out. It runs down the stone, finding the cracks, and seeps back into the peat, rejoining the water table. The individual is gone, but nothing is lost. It is all still there, in the wet earth and the still pools, waiting for the need to rise and be revealed again. They are the patient waiting of the landscape made manifest, a silent, perpetual ritual of stone and water, forever maintaining a stage for a drama that is nothing more than the maintenance itself.
