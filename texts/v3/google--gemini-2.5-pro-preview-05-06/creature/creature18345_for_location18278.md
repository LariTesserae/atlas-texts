The prompt's statement is absolute: "There are no animals." This is the truth, but not the whole truth. There is life here, but it is not animal. It is not life that scuttles or hunts or flees. It is life that *accretes*. It is life for which a year is a short nap and a century is the time it takes to make a decision.

They are easy to miss. At first, they are just part of the landscape. An observer might note a cluster of basalt shards that seems too ordered, a formation of rock that holds a shape slightly too deliberate against the chaos of the cavern floor. Or perhaps they would notice a patch of the slick, purple-leafed vines that seems to coil with a kind of slow, concentrated intent, tracing patterns in the debris that are not merely opportunistic.

These are the **Still-Folk**.

To see one move is to misunderstand the word "move." They do not walk. They *shift*. A process of geological deliberation. Imagine watching a patch of ground for a day. At dawn, it is a heap of jagged, crystalline rubble, glinting silver-blue in the light of the fungal trees. By the time the next slow pulse of steam from the boiler-heart echoes through the vault, the heap has changed. It is no longer a heap. It has become a low, broad pillar. Individual shards have ground against one another with infinitesimal slowness, their facets locking into a new configuration. It is the stop-motion animation of a mountain range being born.

Their bodies are composites, amalgams of the cavern itself. They are primarily a dense, obsidian-like stone, but this is merely the substrate, the bone. Flowing through this matrix, like molten solder in a circuit board, are veins of the raw, unrefined metals that glint in the cavern walls—copper, silver, and something else, something heavier. This metallic network is their muscle and their nerve. When they are still—which is most of the time—they look like natural ore deposits. When they are active, a faint, internal heat makes the metallic veins glow with a dull, cherry-red light, a warmth that can be seen through the finer, more translucent crystals embedded in their forms. They have no face, no limbs, no front or back, unless they have need of one.

Their form is their function. If one needs to cross a chasm, it will slowly extrude a part of itself, a bridge of interlocking stone and metal, flowing across the gap over the course of weeks. Once it reaches the other side, the rest of its mass will follow, a sluggish tide of rock. If it needs to manipulate something, it might extrude a fine, crystalline tendril, sharp and precise as a surgeon's tool. These appendages are not permanent; they are temporary expressions of will, reabsorbed into the main body when their task is done.

They are the tenders of the garden and the machine. They are inextricable from both. The velvet mosses and pearlescent fungi do not just grow *near* them; they grow *on* them. A mature Still-Person is its own ecosystem, a walking monolith draped in glowing flora. The slow, rhythmic pulse of the fungal light is, for them, a form of communication. A slow, steady silver-blue is a state of contentment, of slumber. A quicker, brighter pulse might signal a discovery—a new mineral lode, a weakening pipe. A shift in color to a deeper violet indicates a need, a hunger.

They "eat" by pressing themselves against the mineral-rich walls or the rubble-strewn floor. They do not chew or digest; they absorb. Their internal metallic network acts as a powerful chemical magnet, slowly drawing specific minerals and elements out of the rock and into their own bodies. They drink the condensing rain, letting it seep into the porous parts of their stone bodies, where the symbiotic fungi on their skin filter it and offer up the processed nutrients.

Their purpose, their industry, is the maintenance of the great cycle. They were perhaps not the ones who built the boiler engine—they seem born of this place, not architects of it—but they are its custodians. You might see one pressed against the vast, iron sphere of the engine, utterly motionless for a decade. It is not sleeping. It is performing a repair. It has found a thinning plate, a rivet weakened by the steam. And over those ten years, it has been slowly, atom by atom, depositing a refined metallic compound from its own body onto the weak spot, reinforcing it, becoming one with it. The green verdigris on the bronze flywheel is not just corrosion; it is the slow, patient work of a Still-Person sealing a crack, its own substance becoming the patch. They are not using tools; their bodies *are* the tool, the material, and the artisan.

They perceive the world not through sight or sound in any way a flesh-and-blood creature would understand. They *feel* the world. The slow, thumping beat of the boiler-heart is their pulse, the rhythm of their lives. They can sense the minute vibrations of the dripping condensation, mapping the entire cavern by the echoes in the stone. They taste the air, the metallic broth, to gauge the engine's health and the mineral content of the steam. The light of the fungus is not something they see with eyes, but something they feel on their surfaces, a language of pure energy.

They are born, but not in a biological sense. When a rich vein of minerals is found, a few of the Still-Folk may gather. They will pool parts of themselves, a slow confluence of liquid metal and crushed crystal. Over a century or two, this new mass will begin to develop its own internal network, its own symbiotic flora. It will learn the rhythm of the engine-heart. It will not be taught, but will simply absorb the patterns of the cavern, the logic of the closed system. Its first independent action might be to shift its weight an inch to the left, a movement that takes a full generation to complete.

To be an observer here is to feel an impossible loneliness, but the Still-Folk are not lonely. They exist in a state of slow, constant communion with the rock, the water, the light, and the machine. Their thoughts are not flashes of inspiration but geological pressures. Their memories are strata. They are the cavern's waking consciousness, the dream that the sleeping machine is having. They are what lives here: silent, patient, and as eternal as the slow, iron beat of the world's heart.
