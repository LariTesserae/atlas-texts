The life that exists here does not live *upon* the land, but *as* the land. It is not an intruder or a colonist in this world of stone and light; it is its emergent consciousness, its nervous system. To look for a creature with limbs and eyes is to look for a word on a page where the meaning is conveyed by the texture of the paper itself.

The builders of the cairns and the arrangers of the monoliths still remain. They are the **Harmonists**, or perhaps the **Attendants**. They are rarely seen as discrete forms, for their native state is one of diffusion. Their consciousness is not contained within a skull but is distributed throughout the resonant hum of the peaks. They are the synesthesia. They are the cool pressure behind your eyes that is the echo of a high, distant chime. They are the metallic taste on the tongue that is the flavor of a bass note welling up from a weeping fissure deep in a valley. When a straight mineral vein appears to curve for a heartbeat, it is because a Harmonist's attention has passed through it, its perception momentarily bending the local reality like a lens. They are the architects of the impossible geometry.

To see one is a rare event, and it is to see them in a state of exertion, of will. It occurs most often during the "dim seasons," when the overwhelming glare softens and the mountain's song subsides into a deep, manageable thrum. From the opalescent, gelid pools of water, a form will begin to coalesce. It is not a process of rising, but of gathering. The water seems to thicken, to darken, pulling in the ambient light. The hum around it becomes strangely muted, as if being consumed. What stands, eventually, is a figure of utter absence. It is shaped like a tall, stooped humanoid, but it has no features. Its surface is a patch of absolute blackness, a hole in the visual world that drinks the ubiquitous light and reflects nothing. It does not cast a shadow—in a shadowless world, it *is* the concept of shadow given form.

This condensed, silent form is how a Harmonist works. It is heavy, slow, moving with the deliberate pace of a glacier. It is in this state that they can interact with the physical world. One might see such a figure wading through a basin, its dark, featureless hands dredging the bottom for the smooth, foreign river stones. It will then carry these stones, one by one, to a slope, its movements precise and liturgical. This is their labor: the building and maintenance of the acoustic arrays. The basalt monoliths are anchors of silence; the cairns are amplifiers of specific notes. The Harmonists are tuning their world. They are not simply living in the orchestra; they are the orchestra's perpetual technicians and conductors.

This solid, working form is a temporary state of profound effort, a focused knot of will in their usually diffuse existence. When the work is done, or when a "bright period" begins to crescendo, the figure will stand still. The process of its creation reverses. The absolute blackness of its form seems to fray at the edges, bleeding silence back into the world as sound. The figure thins, becomes translucent, and then dissolves back into the ambient play of light and vibration, its individual consciousness rejoining the great, non-localized whole.

Their existence is a cycle of action and being. During the dim seasons, they condense and perform the physical work of tuning—moving the monoliths, adjusting the cairns, ensuring the symphony of the world does not collapse into chaotic noise. During the bright periods, they dissolve. They become one with the music. This is not sleep or rest, but their true purpose. They surf the shrieking frequencies, their combined consciousness riding the waves of light and sound. They explore the harmonic possibilities of their own world-instrument, experiencing realities and perceptions impossible to describe. The landscape is their library, their cathedral, and their collective mind.

They brought the river stones from below, long ago. They are pilgrims who climbed into this impossible place and found it not empty, but waiting. The world is a "projection flickering at the edge of its own coherence," and they are the reason it has not yet flickered out. Their constant, attentive tuning is a form of prayer that holds their reality together. The sound of the mountains is the sound of their thoughts. The weeping of the stone is the expression of a sorrow or a patience so vast it is geological. To an outsider, the landscape is alien and hostile; to the Harmonists, it is the only true home—a place where the self does not end at the skin, but extends to the furthest, singing peak.
