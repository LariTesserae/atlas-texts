In this world built of a single, sprawling life, there are no tenants. There are only cells, organs made mobile, thoughts given temporary form. What lives here is not separate from the place itself; it is the place, made active and articulate. They are the world’s self-awareness, its antibodies, its gardeners. They have no names, for there is no one to name them, but an outside observer might call them the Wanderers, or the Drifters, or the Veils.

At first, you do not see them. Your senses are overwhelmed by the titanic scale of things, by the groaning spires and the roaring waterfalls. But as your eyes adjust to the pearlescent mist, you begin to notice inconsistencies. A patch of the ground-mat, a hundred yards across, seems to shimmer with a light not its own. A shadow drifts across a verdant hillside where no cloud could be. A ripple, impossibly slow and deliberate, spreads from the center of a steaming, temporary pool.

These are the smallest and most common of them, the Wanderers. To see one coalesce is to mistake it for a trick of the light. An area of the living mat will begin to glow, the luminous liquid within the substrate gathering into a single, intensely bright node. The weave of vegetation thins, becoming a translucent membrane. Then, with a gentle, wet sigh, this portion of the world-mat detaches. It is not born; it is excised.

What rises is a being of breathtaking simplicity and strangeness. It is an amoebic bladder of the world's own skin, perhaps fifty feet in diameter, filled with the same soupy, glowing fluid that pulses in the high spires. Floating within this liquid are darker, fibrous threads, a contained storm of tangled nerves that contract and relax, providing a slow, rhythmic propulsion. It has no mouth, no eyes, no organs as you would understand them. It is less a creature and more a concentration, a mobile piece of the global anatomy.

It glides a few feet above the slick ground, hovering on a cushion of its own vented steam, its passage marked by a faint, ozone scent and the whisper of displaced air. Its purpose is subtle, its life a quiet patrol. It will drift for days, a lonely, living lantern in the misty expanse. When it finds a "wound" in the mat—a fissure stressed by growth, a patch bruised by a falling chunk of spire-bark—it settles over the area like a poultice. Its lower membrane becomes porous, and it exchanges fluids with the ground beneath it. It doesn't eat; it *communes*. It shares its own vital energy, breaking down the damaged tissue and secreting biochemical prompts that trigger a furious, organized healing. After an hour or a day, its work done, it will drift on, slightly dimmer, its task fulfilled. Eventually, when its internal energy is spent, it will simply settle, re-fuse with the mat, and become the ground once more, its consciousness dissolving back into the whole.

But there are others. The Wanderers are but the keepers of the plains. When catastrophe strikes—when a floral monolith, groaning under its own impossible weight, finally shatters and falls—something else is summoned.

They are drawn by the seismic shock of the impact, by the sudden, violent release of pressure and nutrients. They are the Veils. They appear on the hazy horizon, first as distortions in the air, then as vast, shimmering curtains of light drifting down from the upper atmosphere. They are of the same essence as the Wanderers but on a scale that defies belief. A single Veil can be miles across, a semi-transparent sheet of living energy, its form an undulating, manta-like ripple in the sky. Its internal structure is a galactic web of luminous fibers, a brain the size of a city, processing the single, overwhelming sensation of the world's trauma.

They converge on the crash site, on the mountain of steaming, dying vegetation. They do not land. They descend in layers, shrouding the wreckage in a kilometre-high dome of shifting, iridescent light. Where they overlap, the air sings with a low, harmonic thrum. They are the world’s emergency response, its grand orchestrators of rebirth.

Beneath their collective glow, the process of decay and regrowth is not left to chance. The Veils are conductors, guiding the torrential flow of energy released from the spire’s corpse. From their vast bodies, they rain down not water, but catalysts—a fine, glittering mist of complex organic compounds. This mist directs the chaos. New shoots that erupt from the wreckage do not grow wildly, but in specific, structural patterns. The raw nutrients are marshaled, channeled into the foundations of a new, more stable spire, or woven back into the ground-mat to buttress the surrounding landscape against the shock. The Veils are the architects that the world otherwise lacks, their intervention a brief, majestic imposition of intelligent design upon a system that is usually pure, violent growth. They are not conscious in a way a person is. They do not feel sorrow for the fallen spire or ambition for the new one. They are a reflex arc on a planetary scale, a will that emerges only when the organism is severely wounded, acting with an intelligence as profound as it is alien.

When the new growth is stable and the last of the fallen spire's heat has dissipated into the wet air, the Veils dissolve. They do not fly away. They simply grow thinner, more transparent, their light fading until they are once again indistinguishable from the mist and the distant haze. Their work is done. The world is healed, the cycle continues, and the quiet Wanderers continue their lonely patrol across the endless, breathing green. To live here is to be a temporary thought in the mind of a god made of water and chlorophyll.
