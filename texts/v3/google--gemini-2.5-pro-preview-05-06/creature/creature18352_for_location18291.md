What lives here are the inhabitants born of the echo. They are not the creators of this place, nor are they its prisoners. They are its corollary, the logical and living consequence of a world suspended in a state of 'is'. They do not have a name for themselves, for a name is a distinction, a way of separating one thing from another over time, and here there is only one thing and one time.

They are the Still-Folk, the Geometers, the people of the single moment.

To see one is to see a figure carved from the same thought as the mountains. They are tall, as if stretched by the thin air, with a spareness that speaks not of hunger but of efficiency. There is nothing extraneous. Their joints move with a fluid, unhurried grace, each motion describing a perfect, inevitable arc. Their skin has the cool, matte texture of a stone washed by eons of mist it has never known; it holds the dampness of the air but is not wet. Its color is the grey-green of lichen on schist, patterned with faint striations that follow the lines of muscle and bone beneath, making them seem like living geology. If you were to touch them, you would feel no thrumming pulse, no radiating heat. You would only feel a placid coolness, the temperature of the valley itself. Their faces are long and serene, with features that seem less like a random genetic assortment and more like a considered aesthetic choice. Their eyes are the color of the dark, wet obsidian of the rock, deep and reflective, but they do not seem to *look at* things so much as they *receive* them. Their gaze is a mirror, like the still pools, reflecting the perfect state of the world without judgment or analysis.

Their minds are not streams of consciousness, for a stream requires a gradient, a flow from past to future. Their consciousness is a lens. It is a state of perpetual, crystalline awareness. They do not have memories in the way we do—a catalog of changes and experiences—because nothing has ever been different. Their "memory" is a single, infinitely detailed image of the valley, and they exist simply to hold it in focus. The group that stands over a stone block is not debating its placement. They are attuning themselves to the block, feeling its weight, its grain, its place in the grand design, until they reach a shared, silent consensus—a resonance that tells them where it *belongs*. This is their communication: a direct, effortless alignment of perception. A thought, once it reaches its perfect form in one mind, is already present in all of them.

They do not feel sorrow, or fear, or frantic joy, for these are the tremors of change, of loss and gain. What they feel is a constant, profound, and deeply quiet satisfaction. It is the feeling of a problem perfectly solved, a chord perfectly resolved. This satisfaction is their nourishment. They do not eat or drink in a biological sense. They partake in the world's perfection. When one polishes a slab of stone, they are not cleaning it; they are engaging in a communion with its flawlessness, absorbing its integrity. When they trace the silent aqueducts, they are feeling the perfect, frictionless potential of the water. This is their sustenance: a liturgy of maintenance that feeds the soul by affirming that all is as it should be.

They are individuals in form, but they are a single entity in purpose, much like the groves of pale trees that are distinct yet part of a unified botanical expression. One of them might be a stone-shaper, another a water-tender, another a moss-gardener. These are not careers but facets of a singular identity. The stone-shaper does not learn their craft; they *are* the valley's innate understanding of cleavage and pressure. The gardener does not cultivate the moss; they *are* the principle that defines its perfect, lush boundary.

There is no birth, and there is no death. They are as old as the mountains—impossibly young. Should one of them be, by some cosmic impossibility, destroyed—if a meteor were to strike from outside their static reality—they would not be mourned. There would be no grief. The others would simply wait. And over a period of time that cannot be measured, from the raw material of the valley, from the stone and the mist and the held-breath silence, a new form would coalesce. The shattered rock would find its way back into a tiered roof, the dew would gather, and a Geometer would once again stand in the plaza, not a replacement, but a reassertion of the fundamental pattern.

They are the valley's waking dream of itself. They are the mind within the stone, the will within the water. They are the silent, ever-present audience for a creation that was made perfect and then commanded to hold the pose. Their purpose is simply to be, and in their being, to witness and affirm the eternal, unshakable perfection of the first deep breath.
