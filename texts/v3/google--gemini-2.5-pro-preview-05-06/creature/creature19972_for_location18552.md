They are the Tinn. It is not a name they speak, for they have no spoken language of complex words. It is what they are—the sound they make as they move. *Tinn*. A sharp, fleeting percussion against the omnipresent hum of the Geode. It is the sound of a carefully placed heel, the tap of a bone-picking tool, the click of a handhold being tested. In a world where sound travels forever, to make a sound is to declare your existence to the void. The Tinn are economical with such declarations.

To look upon one is to see a being sculpted by scarcity and sharpness. They are not made of flesh and blood, not in any recognizable sense. Their form is a composite, a symbiotic marriage of the Geode's constituent parts. Their bones are a dense, cloudy type of quartz, milky and opaque, possessing a tensile strength far greater than the brittle Glasswood. Their musculature is a tight weave of the same dark, fibrous material they use for wind-breaks, a substance harvested from the "night-side" of the largest spires, where the Spindle’s glare is a remote twilight and the crystalline flora grows slower, more pliable, less vitrified. This sinew is bound to the bone with ligaments that shimmer, catching the light like threads of spun aluminum.

Their skin is their most remarkable feature: a mosaic of tiny, interlocking silicate plates, like the scales of a pangolin or the shell of an armadillo. It is not grown, but accumulated. From birth, they are rubbed with a fine paste of dew and photoreactive dust, and over a lifetime, this outer layer accretes, hardens, and polishes into a dull, pearlescent armor. It carries the history of their life in a topography of minute scratches and chips. It is cool to the touch, always slick with the world’s condensation, and when they move, the scales shift and whisper against each other—a sound like sand settling.

They are humanoid in shape—two arms, two legs, a torso and head—but the proportions are alien. They are lanky and slight, with unnervingly long limbs and fingers. Gravity is a constant threat and weight is a liability. Their bodies are built for climbing, for distributing their mass across fragile surfaces. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, a four-limbed arachnidan crawl across the vertical and horizontal planes of the Glasswood. Their feet are not soled with soft pads but with hard, curved claws that can find purchase in the smallest crystalline imperfections, making that signature *tinn* as they secure a grip.

Their faces are the most unsettling part. They have no visible eyes. Where eyes would be are deep, shadowed hollows beneath a heavy, shelf-like brow of fused plates. The actinic, shadowless glare of the Spindle makes sight as we know it a liability, an agony. They are not blind; they simply see differently. Their entire being is an organ of perception. Through the soles of their feet and the tips of their fingers, they feel the deep hum of the Geode. It is their sight. They can read the health of a bough by its vibration, distinguish the deep groaning of a trunk under torsion from the high-frequency shudder that precedes a fracture. They feel the scuttling of Silicate Grazers miles away. Crucially, they feel the subtle change in the Spindle’s song, the tightening of the world-hum that heralds a coming light-storm. Their recessed ocular pits are not for forming images, but for gauging intensity. They can navigate the deep-root grottos by the faintest gradients of light, and they know, without looking "up," exactly where the Spindle is, its presence a pressure on their being.

They are the Gleaners of the Lightfall. They do not fear the light-storms; they are the world's harvest, and the Tinn are its migrant workers. When they feel the hum of the Spindle shift, they do not hide. They move, climbing with desperate haste into the "lee"—the far side of a colossal spire, the underbelly of a continent-sized branch. They anchor themselves with woven tethers and wait. They listen as the thunder of shattering crystal washes over them, feeling the shockwave through the very structure of the world.

And then comes the aftermath. Silence, and a rain of glittering dust that can last for weeks. This is when they emerge. They are scavengers of energy. The light-storm does not just break the Glasswood; it infuses the newly-exposed surfaces with a massive charge of pure energy. This fresh growth, these razor-sharp edges of "light-choked" crystal, are their food. They use knapped shard-axes not for violence, but for butchery—cleaving off manageable fragments of the new growth. They do not have mouths, but a grinding cavity in their torsos, into which they place the shards. The slow, powerful process of pulverizing and absorbing the stored light provides their sustenance for weeks, even months.

Their culture is one of impermanence and profound quiet. They live in small, familial pods of no more than a half-dozen individuals. They do not build homes, only temporary camps: a wind-break woven in a recess, a collection of sleeping hollows scraped into a root-wall. Everything is abandoned when the hunt for the next storm begins.

They communicate through touch and controlled vibration. A long, drawn-out scrape of a claw is a warning. A series of rapid taps against the Glasswood, a call. When they are at rest, in the deep quiet of their shelters, a family might communicate by placing their hands on each other’s chests, feeling the slow, internal grinding of their bodies processing the light-shards—a sign of contentment and life.

Their art, like the etching in the root, is their philosophy. It is a story of scale. They are small. The world is large. The light is absolute. They have no gods, no myths of creation—the evidence of constant re-creation is all around them. Their spirituality is a form of deep listening. The wisest among them are not chiefs or priests, but the Listeners—Tinn so old their skin-mosaic is a complex map of healed-over fractures. They can lie on the Glasswood for days, unmoving, feeling the slow, geological stress-lines shift, predicting storms seasons in advance, or locating the deep, rare pockets of "fossilized light"—ancient, energy-rich fragments from a storm thousands of years past.

To encounter a Tinn is to witness a ghost in the machine. You might see a flicker of movement on a spire fifty kilometers away, a shape so slight it is lost against the immensity. You might stumble upon an abandoned camp, the shard-tools lying discarded, already slick with new dew, on their way to being reabsorbed into the world. You might even, if you are silent and still for long enough, hear it: the faint, lonely *tinn* of a climber on the glass walls of the void, a sound that says, simply and completely: *I am here. For now.*
