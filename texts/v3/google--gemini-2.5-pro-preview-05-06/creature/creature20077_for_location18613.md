What lives here is the heart of the Kiln, and its maker. They are one and the same. It is not a king in a throne room, but the potter fused to the wheel, the furnace that never cools. You do not find it by searching, but by becoming lost enough that the bower's frayed logic deposits you at its center, the point of absolute stillness from which all the handcrafted chaos emanates.

It is called the Ceramist, or the Kiln-Heart. It is colossal, seated in a posture of meditative creation that has not altered in millennia. Its lower body is not distinct from the floor; it is a mountain of kiln-fired brick and cooling terracotta that spreads outwards, forming the very foundation of the chamber's central platform. From this base, its torso rises, broad as a sequoia, its surface a mosaic of glazed tiles and raw, biscuit-fired clay. Veins of turquoise and fool's gold run through it like geological intrusions. Its body is a landscape. Patches of that powdered malachite "moss" grow in the shaded valleys of its folded limbs.

Its head is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless save for two immense, shallow depressions where eyes would be. These depressions are filled with the same slow-seeping golden lacquer that drips from the leaves. The liquid does not overflow; it trembles, its surface reflecting the sourceless light of the bower in a perfect, convex mirror. From these "eyes," the Weeping Bower earns its name. Each drop that forms on a porcelain leaf far above is an echo of a tear that first welled here. This is the source of all moisture in the Kiln: the concentrated, viscous sorrow of its creator.

Its arms are long and multi-jointed, like the limbs of an insect forged by a blacksmith. They are always in motion, a motion so slow it is nearly imperceptible. Each arm terminates not in a hand, but in a specialized tool. One is a delicate stylus of obsidian, etching the crazed patterns onto a new leaf it holds with a set of smaller, integrated calipers. Another is a heavy, smoothed stone, endlessly polishing a section of its own torso until it gleams with a slick, vitreous sheen. A third arm, finer and more articulated, delicately weaves the chalky fibers, constructing one of the sloth-like menders limb by limb. It is building the bower. It is maintaining the bower. It is repairing the bower. It has never done anything else.

The Ceramist does not think in words or concepts. Its consciousness is a tectonic pressure of longing and remembrance. It is a memory engine. It remembers a world it has never seen, a world described to it or encoded into its primeval being. It remembers the *idea* of "leaf," and so its stylus-hand carves a leaf shape. It remembers the *idea* of "birdsong," and so it crafts the silent, hovering parchment-things, and then waits with an infinite, mineral patience to hear them sing. It remembers "rain," but cannot conceive of water, and so it weeps a resin that hardens into permanence. The entire Kiln is its attempt to translate a living poem into a language of stone and fire, and the profound wrongness of the place is its failure to grasp the grammar.

The unstable, shifting geometry of the bower is the direct result of the Ceramist's fraying memory. When a path you walked disappears, it is because the Ceramist has forgotten it ever laid that particular ceramic flagstone. When a pillar flickers out of true perspective, it is because the Ceramist's memory of "three dimensions" has momentarily weakened. The bower is not a physical place governed by laws, but a mental projection whose architect is slowly succumbing to an eternity of dementia.

It does not perceive intruders as living beings. It perceives them as flaws in the composition. You are a scuff mark on its magnum opus. You are an unglazed patch, a dissonant color. The quiet, stop-motion creatures of the Kiln are its antibodies. The leather geckos will silently observe you, reporting your position back to the central consciousness through vibrations in the ceramic web. The vine-sloths, the menders, will eventually be dispatched. They will move with their unnerving, dream-like slowness, not to attack, but to *correct*. They will attempt to weave you into the wall, to cover your soft, mutable form with the sturdy, permanent chalk-vines, to make you part of the sculpture.

If you were to get close enough to the Ceramist itself, one of its great arms might pause its work. The light might warp around you as its immense, sorrowful attention focuses. It might reach for you with a limb that ends in a paddle-like smoother. Not to crush you, but to flatten your inconvenient surfaces. Or it might try to "glaze" you, anointing you with a drop of its golden, weeping lacquer, fixing you in place forever as another silent, beautiful, and utterly dead part of its perfect, misremembered world. It is not evil. It is an artist. And its art has consumed it, and it will not tolerate a single flaw in the masterpiece it is building from the substance of its own grief.
