The beings who live here are born of the long quiet and the slow drip. They are the Stone-Tenders, though they have no name for themselves that is separate from their work. To be is to tend.

They emerge from the pearly twilight not as creatures of flesh and blood in the way of a sunlit world, but as extensions of the geology itself. Their skin is the first giveaway. It is not pink or brown or black, but a soft, matte grey-white, the precise color of the damp, chalky ground. It is cool to the touch and has a texture like unglazed, wet pottery. On the very old, this skin becomes a canvas. Over a long life of absorbing the mineral-rich dampness, faint, shimmering patterns begin to appear, branching crystalline structures that glitter under the skin like captured frost. An elder is a living geode, a testament to their time and place, their life story told in veins of calcite and quartz that map their arms and faces.

They are tall and impossibly slender, their forms suggesting a life without hard gravity or the need for bulky muscle. They move with a profound, unhurried grace, a liquid deliberation that seems to be learned from the seeping water that is their constant companion. Their limbs are long, their joints subtle, allowing for a sinuous, flowing motion as they navigate the rickety wooden scaffolds and narrow, half-formed passages of their city.

Their faces are long and serene, dominated by their eyes. They are large, dark, and possess no white, only a deep, liquid blackness that seems to drink the soft light. They do not glitter, but rather absorb, making it impossible to read their expression through gaze alone. They blink slowly, a rhythmic, meditative gesture, like a pool settling after a dropped stone. These eyes are not built for a world of sharp edges and bright sun, but for perceiving infinitesimal gradients of light and shadow, for discerning the subtle shift in hue that indicates a wall is curing properly, or the faint shimmer that marks a patch of stone as weak.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They are disproportionately large, with palms as wide as their chests and fingers that are long, spidery, and multi-jointed. Their fingertips, rather than having nails, end in smooth, hardened pads of a darker grey material, like river stones. These hands are not calloused from abrasion but are smoothed and polished by a lifetime of patting, stroking, and guiding the mineral paste. They are instruments of unimaginable sensitivity. A Tender can lay a hand on a half-formed archway and feel, through the subtle resonance and moisture content, its internal stresses, its thirst, its slow, crystalline heartbeat.

They do not speak often. The heavy, damp air smothers sound, so their voices are low and murmuring, rarely used for communication across distances. Their language is one of gesture, proximity, and touch. The true conversations happen in silence: two Tenders working on a wall, their hands moving in a slow, synchronized dance; an elder guiding a youth by placing a hand over theirs, imparting decades of feel and pressure through that simple contact. The primary sounds they make are the purposeful sounds of their work: the gentle *thump-thump-thump* of a wooden paddle compacting a slurry, a sound absorbed almost immediately by the wet stone; the soft, rhythmic scrape of a bone adze smoothing a curve.

Their biology is slow, like their world. Their hearts beat at a pace that is almost imperceptible. They eat the pale crustaceans from the pools and the nutrient-rich mosses, but much of their sustenance seems to come directly from the world itself, absorbed through their porous skin and the water they drink. They are creatures of mineral as much as of protein. This slow metabolism grants them immense lifespans. A Tender might spend their first century as an apprentice, their next three centuries as a journeyman builder, and their final centuries as an elder, a living repository of the city’s history and structural knowledge. Their age is not a measure of decay, but of accumulation, both of wisdom and of the literal crystals forming in their skin.

There is no concept of waste, not even in death. When a Tender's slow life comes to an end, their body is not buried. It is cradled within a new wooden scaffold, placed in a foundational position for a future pillar or a load-bearing wall. Their family and community then tend to this new structure with special reverence. The mineral-rich water is guided over their form, and slowly, patiently, over the course of a generation, the stone precipitates and hardens, encasing them. They become a part of the city, their bones lending their strength, their final form a permanent, silent contribution to the home they spent their long lives building. To walk through the oldest parts of the city is to be surrounded by the ancestors, to feel the deep, quiet presence of those who have literally become the pillars of their society. They do not live *in* the city; they *are* the city, in its present and its past, midwives to a world of stone, and ultimately, its most precious material.
