They are the Geophagi, the Earth-Eaters. They are the source of the thrumming, the architects of the unfinished mountains, the slow, living heart of this high, cold world.

From afar, one is a geological anomaly—a hillock that moves, a great, rounded slab of terrain inching its way across a valley floor. Its passage is imperceptibly slow, a motion measured in meters per hour. It seems less like an animal and more like a localized, ambulatory tectonic event. Approaching, its scale becomes unnerving. The size of a multi-story building, its surface is not a uniform shell but a mosaic of vast, interlocking plates. These plates are a chimerical substance, a litho-organic material that looks like weathered basalt fused with the dark, matte chitin of a colossal beetle. The edges of the plates are worn smooth, rounded by an eternity of slow, grinding friction against one another.

Deep within the meter-wide sutures between these plates, a soft, sullen orange light pulses with the rhythm of the all-pervading thrum. The light is not a fiery glare but a deep, thermal glow, the contained heat of a blast furnace. From these same seams, plumes of dense, hot vapour vent into the freezing air. The steam smells of ozone, hot metal, and wet earth. It is this constant exhalation from tens of thousands of them across the landscape that creates the thick, clinging humidity and the perpetual cloud cover, a self-generated weather system.

They have no face, no eyes, no discernible front or back. Their existence is defined by process. Their entire underside is a single, continuous grinding organ, a vast plane of diamond-hard ridges, mineral-toothed rasps, and pulverizing pads that constantly work against the ground. As the Geophage moves, it scours the soft protolith, scraping up tonnes of the damp gravel, mud, and chalky stone. The sound of this feeding is the occasional, echoing scrape that breaks the monotony of the thrum—the noise of its grinders hitting a pocket of harder material.

The raw geology is drawn up into its body, which is less a system of organs and more a mobile, self-replicating refinery. The thrum is the sound of its internal workings: colossal peristaltic crushers breaking down scree, cyclonic separators sorting materials by density, and cavernous melting chambers rendering down mineral ores. They are not biological in any recognizable sense. They are silicellular, their life process a form of high-pressure archaeo-chemistry that metabolizes rock itself. The weeping dampness of the land is partly their coolant, drawn up and circulated through a capillary network of porous stone, and partly their effluent, a constant ooze of mineral-saturated wastewater.

They are the landscape's primary producers, but they also create their own successors. Periodically, a mature Geophage will slow, its internal thrumming rising in pitch. Over the course of days, it secretes a vast, metallic-sheened egg the size of a small car. But it is not an egg. It is a starter engine, a dense core of prefabricated components and a seed-colony of silicellular organisms. The parent abandons it, and for months the core sits inert, its metallic shell slowly being colonized by the leaden lichens that feed on its subtle energy leakage. Then, one day, it will begin to thrum quietly. Drawing minerals and moisture from the ground directly around it, it will begin to build its first exterior plates, growing slowly from a stationary node into a new, mobile hillock that will eventually join the slow, continent-wide pilgrimage of its kind.

Living in symbiosis with them are the Static-Flies. These are not predators, but opportunists, the sparrows of this industrial ecosystem. The size of a human hand, their bodies are little more than a crystalline capacitor wrapped in coils of what looks like spun copper, with four fragile, mica-like wings that beat with an audible crackle. They swarm in the hot, ionized steam venting from the Geophagi's seams, drawing their energy directly from the thermal and electrical differential. They land on the warm plates, their six hair-fine legs tapping as they absorb the radiant heat, their bodies glowing faintly before they launch back into the mist.

And where there is life, there is a death that feeds. There are the Carapassions. These are the wolves of the rolling plains, though they look more like a fusion of scorpion and demolition machine. They hunt in small packs of two or three. Low to the ground, the size of a crocodile, their bodies are plated in the same obsidian-dark chitin-metal, but shaped into sleek, aggressive angles. They do not eat rock. They are specialists. Their forelimbs are not claws, but heavy, piston-driven rams tipped with a single, massive blade of synthesized carbide.

They stalk the Geophagi, moving with a patient, unnerving speed. They listen to the thrum, searching for an irregularity, a sign of age or internal malfunction. When they find one, the hunt begins. It's a loud, brutal process of attrition. One Carapassion will anchor itself with its rear legs and begin to hammer its carbide spike against a plate seam, the percussive *thwack...thwack...thwack* echoing for miles. The others harry the giant, scuttling around it, seeking purchase, testing for weakness. A successful hunt can take hours, ending when a plate is finally breached with a scream of tearing metal-chitin. The Geophage’s internal pressure is broken, and it dies with a final, cataclysmic sigh of steam that can blast a crater into the soft ground. The Carapassions then climb inside the carcass, their saw-like mandibles tearing not at flesh, but at the silvery, gel-filled conduits and crystalline energy cores within, feeding on the refined and stored power of the dead giant before its internal heat fades into the perpetual cold.
