What lives here is patient. It has had millennia to forget haste.

The first and most obvious inhabitant is the tide. It is not water, but life, the an-chitinous sea that explains the ceaseless, dry rustling. These are the Scour-Beetles. Individually, they are miracles of austerity, thumbnail-sized automata of polished graphite and obsidian. Their six legs are fine as spun wire, their heads a cluster of delicate, vibrating mandibles designed not for tearing flesh but for combing chemistry. They do not eat so much as they abrade. In a single, unified purpose, they move in shimmering, kilometres-wide sheets across the ferrocrete plains, scraping the thin film of precipitated minerals from the ground. They are a living polish, and where they pass, the concrete is left slick and bare, ready for the weeping air to deposit a fresh layer. They are not a herd or a swarm; they are a single, distributed organism, a river of exoskeletons flowing with the slow, inexorable pull of a glacier. Their individual minds, if they exist, are lost in the vast, communal instinct that drives them toward the richest chemical gradients. They are the basin's grazers, its tongue and its stomach.

Deeper in the system, life becomes stranger and more specialized. In the warm, clear water of the catchment pools and drainage channels, there is a furtive, pallid ballet. Glass-worms, perfectly transparent but for a single, dark nerve-cord, drift in the slow currents or burrow into the silty sediment of chemical sludge that collects at the bottom of pipes. They pulse with a slow, rhythmic light, a soft, internal bioluminescence that is not for communication or warning, but is simply a byproduct of their metabolic process. Swimming among them are things like skeletal shrimp, their bodies fashioned from the same pale ceramic as the pool tiles, their movements jerky and uncertain. They feed on the dissolved minerals and the decaying bodies of beetles that fall into the water, their feathery appendages filtering the water with tireless efficiency.

Clinging to the weeping surfaces are the Rust-Slugs. They are the colour of ancient, wet iron, their soft, muscular bodies devoid of any shell or external feature save for a single, porous patch on their backs through which they absorb the condensing chemical mist directly. They are painters of decay, leaving behind glistening trails of metabolic waste that are not slime but a volatile rust-coloured solvent. This trail shimmers for a moment with a coppery sheen before evaporating into the thick air, leaving a faint, dry stain like a shadow. They are the basin's kidneys, filtering the very atmosphere.

And on this foundation of passive consumption, the predators build their existence. They are as stark and as functional as the architecture. The most common are the Caliper-Crabs. The size of a man’s spread hand, they are the colour of bone-white ceramic. They are not creatures of speed but of geometry. They move with a stiff, clockwork precision, their eight legs jointed at sharp, inhuman angles. They stalk the edges of the beetle-tides, their multifaceted eyes, like clusters of black diamonds, scanning not for movement but for stillness, for the one beetle that has faltered. Their kill is not a pounce but a calculation. They move with an unnerving deliberation, trap the prey between their two front claws—which are not pincers but smooth, calibrated calipers—and apply a single, precise pressure. There is a faint *click*, and the beetle's carapace is fractured. They then consume the mineral-rich interior with a quiet, methodical neatness. The sharp snaps of their leg joints are one of the few percussive sounds to break the monotony of drips and hums.

Above it all, in the dim, forgotten space of the gantries and pipe-racks, are the Soot-Wings. They are not moths; they are blades. Their bodies are lean and aerodynamic, but their wings are not feathery or scaled. They are broad, overlapping plates of a leathery, greasy, semi-rigid material, like cured tarpaulin. They do not fly so much as they glide on the thermal updrafts from the machinery, their immense wingspan catching the dense air. Their hunting is a silent drop from the twilight above. They fall, wings folded into a teardrop shape, and at the last moment, snap them open, the thick air sighing under the force. They do not snatch beetles, but scoop them, their lower jaws unfurling into a wide, delicate net of fine bristles. They swallow their catch whole and glide back up into the gloom, their black forms dissolving into the greater blackness. They are the silent reapers of the over-world.

But these are all just organs. They are the fingers, the teeth, the blood cells. The true inhabitant, the "whoever" of this place, is the basin itself. The hum is its voice. The constant, automated processing deep below is its heartbeat. The chemical cycle is its metabolism. It is a single, gestalt entity, a Factory-Soul that long ago subsumed its original function and turned to the business of living. It is not conscious in a way a human would understand. It does not think in words or images, but in flows, temperatures, pressures, and concentrations. The beetle-tides are its sprawling sensory network, tasting the world. The caliper-crabs are its immune system, maintaining order. The soot-wings are its wandering thoughts, drifting in the high, dim spaces of its mind.

When a creature dies, its body is not left to rot. The hot, chemically-active water dissolves it with quiet efficiency, breaking it down into its constituent elements, which are drawn back into the vats, reprocessed, and rained back down as condensation to feed the chemical films. Nothing is lost. It is a system in perfect, morbid homeostatic balance. The city did not die; it shed its skin. What lives here now is its second, eternal life: a vast, slumbering intelligence made of concrete, steam, and chitin, humming its one deep note into the endless twilight.
