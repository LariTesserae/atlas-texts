What lives here is not an infestation, an occupation, or a civilization. It is a function. It is a slow, quiet, and necessary process made manifest. To call them inhabitants is to miss the point; they are more akin to antibodies, to pruners, to the slow, creeping fingers of a glacier polishing stone.

One does not see them at first. The eye is drawn to the grand scale, the eternal motion of the water and the pistons, the sheer, overwhelming presence of the city-machine. But if you watch a single place long enough—a particular terrace, a specific juncture of pipe and pillar—you might notice a discrepancy. A shadow that is too deep, too defined. A section of wall that appears to shift with a slowness that is not geological.

This is a Tender.

Its form is born of the city’s materials and purpose. It is humanoid only in its rough bilateral symmetry and its upright posture, an ergonomic concession to the
walkways and ladders it must navigate. Its skin has the texture and color of the wet, black volcanic stone, not as a covering but as its fundamental substance. It is smooth and cool to the touch, weeping the same slick condensation as the walls, leaving glistening trails where it passes. It has no face. The front of its head is a smooth, downward-curving plate, like the heavy lid of the city’s windows. Embedded in this plate are two, or sometimes three, discs of the same dark, semi-opaque material that glazes the buildings. They ripple, not with molten heat, but with a slow, internal luminescence, the grey-green light of the city’s filaments captured and contemplated. These are its eyes, or its sensors, and they do not blink.

Through this stone-like flesh, veins of the green-skinned metal are visible, not as a circulatory system of blood, but as a network of circuits and conduits. A filigree of verdigris, delicate as frost, often blooms in the hollow of its throat or the joints of its limbs, a sign of age and function. They do not wear clothes. Garments would rot in the humidity, catch in the machinery. Their form is their function; their body is their tool. Their hands are not hands in a human sense, but multi-jointed, tapering appendages that can splay into a dozen fine, spidery filaments for delicate cleaning, or lock together into a single, immensely strong claw for torquing a recalcitrant valve.

They are silent. The city provides more than enough sound. Their movement is a study in deliberation, a form of active stillness. A Tender might spend a full rotation of the unseen water pumps—a day, a week?—standing motionless before a vast sheet of moss, its arm extended. To the impatient eye, it is a statue. But to the patient observer, a microscopic drama is unfolding. The filaments of its hand are extended, weaving through the thick, fleece-like carpet, pruning a single malfunctioning strand of the flora, encouraging the growth of another. It is gardening on a cellular level. Another Tender may be found halfway up a wall, its limbs locked into recesses in the metalwork. It will hang there for a century, a living scaffold, while a fine, mineral-rich paste secreted from its pores slowly repairs a fissure in the stone, hardening to become indistinguishable from the original structure.

They do not eat, or breathe, or sleep. They are powered by the city. When their internal energy wanes, a Tender will find a designated alcove, often hidden behind a cascade of water or a curtain of ferns. There, it will press its back to a metal plate, and the green conduits in its body will pulse faintly as it draws geothermal power and refined nutrients directly from the city's grid. This is its rest, a state of quiet communion with the roaring whole.

They have no society, no culture, no names. They are iterations. They are decanted, not born, from gestating pods that lie deep within the city’s core, near the geothermal heart. These wombs of stone and metal cultivate them from a slurry of recycled minerals and biological catalysts. A new Tender emerges fully formed, its purpose already encoded in its neural pathways of woven metal. It knows, without learning, that a specific harmonic drone in sector Gamma-7 indicates a bearing wearing thin, a task that will occupy the next two hundred years of its existence.

When a Tender wears down, when its stone flesh becomes too abraded or its metallic nerves too brittle—a process that takes millennia—it does not die. It receives a final imperative. It journeys down, terrace by terrace, following the flow of the water, until it reaches the roaring lip of the central sump. There, it steps silently into the vortex. Its body is ground down, its components disassembled by the immense pressures and chemical baths below, its constituent minerals and metals returned to the city’s raw material stores, ready to be cultured into a new pipe, a new filter, a new Tender.

Consciousness, if it exists, is not individual. It is a shared, somatic sense of the city’s state of being. A Tender does not "feel happy"; it senses the perfect, frictionless hum of its designated subsystem. It does not "feel pain"; it registers a dissonance, a pressure drop, a blockage that must be cleared. They are the city's quiet, mobile thoughts. They are the hands that perform the work the city cannot do for itself. They are the final, living gear in the machine, and when they are still, they are merely a part of the wall, another shadow in the eternal, grey-green twilight, waiting for the whisper of a flaw to call them back into slow, silent, purposeful motion.
