The residents are the world's echo, its antibody. They are made of the same patient, inexorable pressure that forms their home, but turned to a different purpose.

They are the Carvers.

To see one is to see a being sculpted by necessity. They are roughly the height of a man, but their posture is a permanent crouch, a state of readiness and low-centered gravity. Their bodies are encased in a segmented, chitinous shell, the color of wet slate or cooled magma. This carapace is not ornate; it is a functional armor against the constant damp, the abrasive grit, and the occasional fall of a mineral slab. It gleams under the diffuse twilight, perpetually slick with the greasy condensation of the air, which traces slow, meandering paths down the curved plates of their backs and limbs. Beneath this shell, their musculature is dense and powerful, coiled for the singular, repetitive motion that defines their lives.

Their limbs are arthropodal, jointed and strong, ending not in fingers but in heavy, two-pronged claws—one thick and blunt for bracing, the other finer and sharper for gripping tools. They move with a deliberate, sure-footed gait, their claws finding secure purchase on the bulbous, uneven mineral ground. Their walk is a careful, rhythmic process, a silent negotiation with a treacherous surface.

Their heads are smooth, downward-sloping shields, emerging seamlessly from the thorax. They have no visible mouths in the conventional sense, but rather a complex set of grinding mandibles tucked beneath the head, used for consuming the nutrient-rich ooze they scrape from the walls. Their faces, if they can be called that, are dominated by two large, compound eyes. These are not the multifaceted jewels of an insect, but smooth, milky orbs of black, like polished obsidian. They do not glitter; they absorb the faint light, offering no reflection and betraying no thought. They are eyes built for a world without shadow or glare, discerning shape and movement in the perpetual dimness.

But their primary sense is not sight. It is touch, or more accurately, *vibration*. Born into the all-consuming drone, their sense of hearing is vestigial, perhaps entirely absent. The D-flat hum of the world is not a sound to them; it is the baseline of existence, the texture of reality itself, felt through the soles of their claws and the rigid frame of their bodies. They are creatures of profound, internal silence, their consciousness unburdened by the cacophony. They communicate through touch—a grazing of antennae that sprout from beneath their head-shield, a tap of a claw on another's carapace, a specific pressure applied to a shared surface. Their language is one of slow, seismic intimacy.

They are also a people without history, for their present is eternal. Each Carver is born from a leathery, grapefruit-sized egg laid in a warm, freshly hollowed alcove. From the moment it hatches, a miniature version of the adult, its life is the same. It learns to brace itself against the growing walls, to wield the heavy tools, to scrape the life-sustaining ooze. There are no artisans, no priests, no leaders in the traditional sense. There are only the young, the prime, and the old. The prime do the heaviest carving on the main thoroughfares. The young maintain smaller tunnels and learn. The old, their carapaces worn and their limbs stiff, are tasked with the most delicate work: scraping the slime for food, or carving the shallowest layers where the rock is softest.

When a Carver dies, its body is not mourned, but utilized. It is a rare confluence of resources. The hard, resilient chitin of its shell is broken down and sharpened into new chisel-tips. The dense bone of its inner structure becomes a mallet-head for a new generation. The soft tissues are consumed. In this closed system, death is simply a transfer of materials.

There is a stillness to their minds, a placidity born of immense and constant pressure. They do not dream of other worlds, for there is no concept of "other." They do not rage against their fate, for the world’s growth is not an act of malice but a simple condition, like the dampness of the air. Their existence is a pure, reactive rhythm: the slow, gathering pressure of the world, met by the sharp, percussive tap of their chisel. They are the metronome counting a beat against the singular, endless note of creation. To watch them is to watch a force of nature in equilibrium with another, a quiet, desperate dance between living maintenance and geological becoming. They are the city's nervous system, its living memory, ensuring that the spaces carved by their ancestors are not forgotten, not erased by the slow, unthinking ambition of the stone.
