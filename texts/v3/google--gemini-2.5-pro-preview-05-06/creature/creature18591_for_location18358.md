What lives here is the reason for the hum. Or perhaps it is a consequence of it, born from the resonance and the manufactured water, a creature that has become the caretaker of its own cradle. It has no collective name, for there is only one. It is the Custodian.

It does not walk among the pylons so much as it emerges from them. You will not see it coming from a distance. You will be staring at a particularly thick nexus of pipes and cable-vines at the base of a condenser pylon, your eyes tracing the path of a weeping rivulet, and you will slowly realize that a section of the machinery is moving. It is a slow, thick, and silent unfolding. What you thought was a junction of several large, moss-covered pipes is, in fact, the flexed joints of a folded limb.

The Custodian is a being of immense scale and profound patience. It stands perhaps thirty feet tall when fully erect, but it is almost never fully erect. Its form is gangly, asymmetrical, and profoundly reflects its environment. It has no skin, only a carapace that is a living alloy of the coppery metal and a chitinous, organic substance. It is covered in the same verdigris, not as a patina, but as its natural color, mottled with patches of velvety, bioluminescent moss that it cultivates on its own body. Its "muscles" are not flesh but thick, fibrous bundles that look exactly like the cable-vines that festoon the pylons, and they contract and relax with a tide-like slowness.

It is a creature of many limbs, none of them identical. Two are thick and columnar, ending not in feet but in heavy, blunt pestles of the same dark metal. It uses these to anchor itself, planting them in the slag with a deep, crushing *thud* that is absorbed into the greater hum. The ground around its anchor points is a finer, darker dust. Other limbs are long and spindly, like the legs of a spider made of rebar, ending in multi-jointed calipers and delicate pincers. These it uses to walk, picking its way through the sharp slag with an unnervingly precise and silent grace, or to brace itself high up against a pylon. More limbs, thinner and more flexible, remain coiled against its torso, ready to unfurl and perform delicate work: clearing a blockage in a pipe with a fine, wire-like probe, or gently transplanting a patch of rare moss from a crowded spot to a bare one.

It has no face. Where a head might be, there is a smooth, vaulted dome of the copper-chitin, shaped like a great bell. This dome is its primary sensory organ. It does not see with light or hear with ears. The dome is a resonating chamber, perfectly attuned to the bass-note hum of the ward. It feels the entire system as a single symphony. A slight dip in pressure in a condenser array two hundred yards away is a discordant flutter in the harmony. The vibration of a Strider-Crane's talons on a walkway above is a brief, percussive trill. It navigates and understands its world through vibration, its mind a deep and constant state of sympathetic resonance. The patterns of the dripping water, the health of the vine colonies, the structural integrity of a joint welded centuries ago—all are known to it as texture and tone.

The Custodian is a slow and methodical creature. It may spend an entire cycle of light and dark attending to a single pylon. It will brace itself against the structure, a motionless sculpture of verdigris and moss, its limbs extended to caress the machinery. With its delicate manipulators, it will prune overgrown ferns, dislodge mineral buildups from condenser fins, and guide the tendrils of young cable-vines towards optimal paths for water and stability. Its movements are not biological; they are geological. There is a sense of immense weight and purpose to every gesture. When a joint in its great body moves, it is not a crack or a snap, but a low, sonorous *thrum-click*, a localized chord change within the unceasing bass note of the ward.

It is indifferent to the fauna. The skittering beetles and iridescent frogs are simply part of the texture of the mosses it tends. The Strider-Cranes, for all their territorial shrieks, give the Custodian a wide berth. They seem to recognize it not as a living creature, but as a fundamental part of the landscape, as immutable as the pylons themselves. When the Custodian moves, they simply stalk to another section of the infrastructure, their metallic calls sounding briefly agitated before they resettle.

At the end of a work cycle, it does not seek shelter. It simply folds itself back into the landscape. It will find a recess at the base of a pylon, draw its limbs in tight, and become once more a dense, unreadable knot of metal and vine. The moss on its back will pulse with a soft, green light, indistinguishable from the thousand other patches glowing in the humid darkness. It becomes part of the machine again, its bell-head resonating with the deep hum, dreaming the slow dream of the water and the metal, listening to the patient, eternal engine of its home. It is the ward's gardener, its mechanic, its sole, silent inhabitant. It is a part of the process, a living gear in the machine, as silent and necessary as the hum itself.
