They who live in Qat Mandar are the Mandari, though they do not use this name for themselves. They have no name for their people as a whole, for they do not perceive themselves as a distinct entity separate from the stone and the cold. They are simply *the people*, in the same way the rock is *the rock* and the wind is *the wind*. To an outsider, they seem carved from the same material as their city, shaped by the same immense, patient pressures.

Their bodies are a testament to the cold. They are short and broad, a people of compact, powerful frames built to conserve heat. Their chests are deep and barrel-like, housing lungs that have adapted over generations to draw sufficient life from the thin, frigid air. To see a Mandari stripped of their layers of hide and wool is to see a living sculpture of dense muscle and thick bone, a form with little softness or extraneous flourish. Their skin has the colour and texture of weathered schist, a palette of greys and dusty browns, mapped with the fine cracks and lines of a life lived in a permanent, wind-blasted freeze. It is tough, chapped, and often coated in a thin, protective layer of Ormuk grease.

Their faces are broad, with wide, flat noses that warm the air before it is drawn into the lungs. Their eyes, dark and deep-set beneath heavy brows, are narrowed to permanent squints against the unfiltered sun and scouring winds. These eyes are doubly adapted: they can bear the blinding glare on the open plateau, where shadows are as solid as objects, and they can discern the subtlest details in the near-total darkness of the city's warrens. They see with a clarity that is unnerving, missing nothing—the faintest shift in a snowdrift, the subtle change in an Ormuk's gait, the microscopic tracery of new ice forming on a cistern wall.

They move with a deliberation that borders on slowness, but it is a slowness born of efficiency and purpose, not lethargy. Every step is placed with absolute certainty on the uneven stone floors. The thin air and the need to conserve energy have taught them a profound economy of motion. They do not gesture wildly or run; they flow through the tight passages of Qat Mandar like water through a crack in the rock, silent and inevitable. Their favoured footwear is a multi-layered boot of soft hide with a thick felted sole, which makes their passage almost soundless, a soft *shuff-shuff* against the stone that is quickly swallowed by the silence.

The Mandari are a quiet people because their world is quiet. Speech is a resource, like heat or water, to be spent with care. Their language is low and guttural, full of soft consonants and long vowels that do not require explosive exhalations of precious warm air. It is a language built for close quarters, for speaking across a small fire or to a child bundled in furs. More is communicated by a glance, a slight tilt of the head, or the pressure of a hand than by a torrent of words. They can sit together for hours in the dim, smoky light of their homes, sharing presence and warmth, with only a few murmurs passing between them.

Their primary sense is not sight or sound, but smell. The air, too thin to carry sound well, is a rich tapestry of odours. Every passage has its own scent: the mineral dampness of a water-seeping wall, the musky warmth of a nearby dwelling, the sharp tang of frozen dung-smoke, the ever-present, gamey odour of rendered fat and curing meat. A Mandari can navigate by scent in the deepest passages, can tell which family lives behind a hide curtain by the unique combination of their bodies, their cooking, and the particular hides they are working. The arrival of an Ormuk herd on the lee side of the city is not heard or seen first, but smelled—a rich, animal fog on the wind.

The concept of the individual, as understood in warmer lands, has been frozen out of them. A person is a component of a family, and a family is a block in the structure of the community. They live and sleep in piled heaps, sharing body warmth in fur-lined alcoves carved from the stone. Privacy is not only impossible, it is incomprehensible, a form of self-imposed exile that courts death. Their consciousness is collective, their thoughts running along familial and communal lines. "I am cold" is a less common thought than "We need more fuel for the dwelling." Identity is defined by function and relation: "I am a bone-carver of the line of Jutt, whose mother was a hide-scraper of the Third Deepening."

Their memory is geological. They do not write history; they live upon it. The stories of their ancestors are not told in epics but are felt underfoot. The lower, crushed levels of the city are their subconscious, a frozen repository of all who have come before. A Mandari understands their place in time by their physical level in the city. To descend into the old, ice-choked passages is a journey into the past, a pilgrimage where one can touch the stones shaped by hands a thousand years dead.

Life revolves around the Ormuk, and this relationship is one of profound, unsentimental reverence. The great beasts are not gods, but partners in survival, the other half of their existence. The hunt is a slow, methodical process, a culling of the weakest animals conducted with an intimate knowledge of the herd's structure. The butchering is a sacred ritual of disassembly. Every part is used, not out of stinginess, but out of a deep-seated respect. To waste a single bone or a drop of fat would be a deep flaw, a dissonance in the rhythm of life. The rhythmic *clack* of a bone needle, the scrape of a flint tool on hide, the steady drip of water into a cistern—these are the hymns of their people.

They are beings of immense patience. They have to be. Their city took a hundred generations to build. An Ormuk takes a decade to mature. The water drips. The ice flows. Change is measured by the slow outward creep of the city's edge and the gradual sinking of its heart. A Mandari's life is a single, slow breath in the endless winter. They are born into the cold, they live by it, they are insulated by its bounty, and when they die, their bodies are taken to the high perimeter of the plateau, where the wind and the cold scour them back into the stone and dust from which they came. They do not rise to a heaven, but return to the world, becoming one with the vast, quiet tableland at the roof of the world.
