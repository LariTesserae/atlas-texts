There are others. They are not part of the great, rustling sea of grazers, but they live within it, an exception that proves the rule. They are called the Weavers, though they have no name for themselves. They are the planet’s vertical thought in a world of horizontal plodding.

To see a Weaver is to see a ghost made of frost and geometry. Where the grazers are low, broad, and dark—compressions of stone made living—the Weavers are tall, spindly, and pale. They stand on four, five, or sometimes six impossibly long and slender legs, multi-jointed and delicate, like the legs of a crane fly scaled up to the height of a giraffe. Their bodies are small, compact teardrops of pearlescent chitin, suspended high above the ground. They are the color of the rime on the rocks, a shifting, non-color of silver and white and the faintest violet taken from the sky. Against the dark tide of the grazers, they are starkly visible; alone on the plains, they vanish into the frosted landscape, becoming just another trick of the gray light.

They move with a slow, hypnotic grace that is utterly unnerving. They are the only thing on this world that seems truly weightless. Their legs fold and unfold with a liquid precision, their feet—wide, soft pads that barely whisper on the stone—finding purchase that isn't there. They walk *over* the grazer herd, their legs high-stepping the car-sized adults, their bodies drifting through the thin, cold air like a thistledown seed. They are a pattern superimposed upon another pattern, a sparse network of walking towers moving within the crushing tide.

The Weavers are blind, like all things here. But their senses are of a different order. They do not feel for heat or taste the metallic dust. They *listen* to the silence. Their entire pale structure is an instrument for detecting vibration and pressure. Delicate, fan-like membranes of translucent chitin stretch between their legs and along their torsos. These are not wings. They are tympanums, diaphragms tuned to the subsonic hum of the planet itself. They feel the steady, voiceless wind not as a pressure, but as a complex map of information. They can sense the edge of a mile-deep canyon a day's walk away by the change in the silent airflow. They can feel the seismic groan of an Apparatus shifting its gears from across the continent.

Most importantly, they listen to the herd. They walk among the grazers, their minds a tapestry of tremors. They can distinguish the steady, rhythmic clicking of a healthy grazer from the faltering, arrhythmic stumble of one that is old or sick. They can feel the frantic, microscopic vibrations of a young grazer struggling in the press of bodies, its shell not yet fully hardened. They are scavengers first, predators second. When a grazer dies, crushed by its kin or simply expiring from its eons-long life, the Weavers are the first to know. A tremor of finality ripples through the stone, and a nearby Weaver will change its deliberate course, homing in on the stillness.

They do not have mouths or claws. From the bottom of their suspended bodies extends a single, coiled appendage: a proboscis as fine as a needle, but capable of extending up to twenty feet. Upon reaching a dead grazer, the Weaver will delicately press the tip of this proboscis against a seam in the creature’s chitinous armor. It does not pierce or tear. It secretes a single drop of a clear, potent fluid. The fluid is not a fast-acting acid; that would be too violent for this world. It is a catalyst, an enzyme that triggers a slow, localized molecular decay. Over the course of hours, the hard, metallic shell softens, dissolves, becoming a thin, organic soup. The Weaver then sips the liquified insides through its proboscis, a slow, patient meal that can last for a week. When hunting the weak or the young, the process is the same. There is no chase, no struggle. There is only the gentle, inexorable touch, the slow dissolution, a death as quiet and patient as the world itself.

Their lives are solitary, spent drifting through the endless herd. But they are not without their own grand, geologic purpose. The Weavers are named for what they do when they leave the herd.

When a Weaver has absorbed enough metallic minerals from its diet, and feels the slow, planetary pulse shift in a way only it can understand, it will abandon its mobile larder. It will walk away from the rustling sea of life and journey to one of the great canyons, the silent, still places where the ice chandeliers grow. Here, in the absolute quiet, it finds a different purpose.

From its proboscis, it ceases to secrete the enzyme of decay and begins to extrude a filament. It is a thread of silvery, bio-metallic alloy, finer than any hair but with immense tensile strength. For months, sometimes years, the Weaver walks the sheer cliffs and overhangs of the canyon, its padded feet clinging to the vertical stone. It walks in precise, geometric patterns, extruding its filament, anchoring it to the rock, weaving it across the vast emptiness. It creates immense, intricate webs that span the chasms—not nets for catching prey, but complex aerial sculptures of shimmering metal, like the diagram of a molecule made manifest. They catch the faint violet light and glitter like a constellation trapped underground, hanging amidst the milky, frozen waterfalls.

These webs are their nests and their cathedrals. Once a web is complete, the Weaver will move to its center and begin to vibrate, its entire body thrumming in tune with the planet's deep hum. The web resonates, converting the subsonic vibrations and the faint atmospheric charge into a complex, melodic field of pure tone. It is the only true music on this world, and it is a love song, a summons broadcast into the immense silence.

Other Weavers, journeying to the canyons, will feel the song through the stone. They will find the web and add their own filaments and their own resonance, until the canyon is filled with a silent, intricate chorus. Here they mate. The young gestate in small, silvery cocoons hung from the web's junctions, absorbing the resonant energy and the minerals of the web itself, growing slowly in their silent, swinging cradles. When they hatch, they are miniature versions of the adults, perfectly formed. They will climb out of the canyon, find the distant, rustling sea of the herd, and begin their long, slow, solitary walk. The old Weavers, their metal spent, often remain in the canyons, their bodies becoming still, their legs locked onto the stone until they are just another part of the frost-covered geology, silent sentinels watching over their shimmering, silent legacy.
