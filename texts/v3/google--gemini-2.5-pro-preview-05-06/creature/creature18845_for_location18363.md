This is almost true. The original assessment, that nothing walks or crawls, is the conclusion of an observer who measures life in heartbeats and nervous systems. It is the truth of a single, dry season. But the world is patient, and what lives here is more patient still. What lives here is a consequence of the flatness, a child of the water, a shard of the world that learned to move.

It has no name, for there is nothing to name it. In its dormancy, it is merely part of the geography. You would not recognize it. It appears as a large, unusually symmetrical sliver of the pan, perhaps twenty feet long and blade-thin, canted at a slight angle as if an ancient pressure ridge had fractured and left a single, elegant shard half-buried in the crystal floor. Its surface is the same milky white, its veins the same mineral blue. It is indistinguishable. It might lie this way for a decade, or a century, a silent monument to a forgotten tectonic stress, gathering a fine layer of saline dust. It is utterly still, fused to the pan by the same biogenic cement that builds the world, its internal temperature matching the searing heat of the ground. It is not waiting. It simply *is*.

When the sky curdles and the warm, mineral rain begins to weep, the change is imperceptible at first. As the water pools, forming the vast, ephemeral lake, it dissolves the saline dust and begins to work on the creature's seal. The deep hum of the awakening bacterial mat is a resonance it feels in its very structure. The water, the lifeblood, soaks into micro-fissures along its base, and a slow, osmotic pressure begins to build.

Over hours, a quiet sound joins the chorus of the growing world: a deep, resonant *creak*, like a great timber under strain. The seal is breaking. Like a tooth loosening in a jaw, the shard detaches from the planetary floor. It rocks, once, then settles in the inch-deep water. It is now afloat. It is free.

This is the Walker.

It is not flesh. It is a single, massive blade of the same biogenic crystal as the world, but it is organized differently. Its internal structure is not solid but a lattice of fibrous, hollow capillaries. As it awakens, it draws the mineral water up into this lattice, and like the Salt-stalks, it begins to live. A faint, internal luminescence ignites within it, a milky blue-white glow that pulses in time with the deep, planetary hum of the bacterial mat.

It has no limbs, no fins, no flagella. Its movement is an act of impossible grace, a masterclass in exploiting the physics of this unique environment. The underside of the Walker is perfectly, impossibly smooth. It rests on the water's surface tension, displacing almost nothing. To move, it manipulates its own internal mass. By slowly, rhythmically shifting the mineral water through the capillaries from one end of its body to the other, it creates subtle shifts in its center of gravity. This, combined with the faintest currents in the shallow water or the gentle pressure of the humid air, is enough.

It begins to glide.

It is a silent skater on a world-sized rink of mirrored sky. Its pace is meditative, unhurried. It might take an hour to cross a hundred yards. It leaves no wake, only the briefest compression of the water's surface that vanishes behind it. From a distance, it is a ghost, a sliver of light sliding across a motionless sea of stars and clouds.

The Walker is a shepherd, or perhaps a predator. Its senses are vibrations and light. Through the crystalline pan, it feels the infinitesimal tremors of the growing world, navigating by a kind of tecto-location. But its goal is revealed by its other sense. It perceives the faint glow of the Salt-stalks, each one a distant, lonely lighthouse in the shimmering dark.

It moves from one stalk to another across the vast emptiness. It does not eat them. Upon reaching a stalk, plump and turgid with mineral water, the Walker aligns its bladed edge with the stalk's base. The two lifeforms are of the same fundamental substance. With a delicate, resonant touch, it makes contact. A connection is formed without a wound. Fine, almost invisible crystalline filaments extend from the Walker and interface with the stalk's osmotic membrane.

And then, it drinks.

It does not suck or pump. It simply presents a zone of lower energetic potential. The super-hydrated, mineral-rich fluid from the Salt-stalk flows into the latticework of the Walker, a slow, silent transfer of vitality. The Walker's glow brightens, the Salt-stalk's dims. It is a parasitic act, but a gentle one. The Walker never takes so much as to kill the stalk; it is not in its interest to destroy its oases. It is a harvester, ensuring its own survival while leaving the garden intact for the next season, decades hence.

It will visit dozens of stalks in the brief wet season, a lonely pilgrimage of light and thirst. Its journey is a silent ballet across the face of the pan, a single moving object in a world of vertical growth and horizontal stasis.

When the weeping sky clears and the sun returns, the water begins its rapid retreat. The vast lakes thin, break apart, and evaporate back into the thick air. The Walker feels this. The hum of the world quiets. The stalks lose their turgor. Its season is ending.

It does not panic. It simply finds a place, an unremarkable stretch of flat pan, and as the last film of water steams away, it settles. The remaining mineral water in its capillaries is repurposed. It excretes a biogenic cement from its base, the same substance that builds the continents. Over the course of a day, it fuses itself back to the floor. Its internal light fades. Its crystalline lattice becomes dormant and rigid. The heat bakes it, the dust settles on it.

Once again, it is just a piece of the landscape, a shard waiting for the sky to weep. It is not an animal. It is an organ of the world that has been given the freedom of movement: a specialized, mobile geology, a patient thought of the planet itself, forever walking the line between stone and life.
