This place is not uninhabited; it is, in fact, teeming. But the life it holds is singular, and inseparable from the Cusp itself. What lives here are the Cusp’s own thoughts made solid, its own cries given form. They are the **Lament-Glass Weepers**.

At first glance, you see nothing. The black, fractured surfaces of the spire seem sterile, slick with the ever-present damp. But then, a subtle shift in the bruised twilight catches your eye. A facet of stone, no larger than a child’s fist, seems to ripple. It is not a trick of the gloomy light. It is a creature.

A Weeper is a being of mineral patience. It has no distinct head or torso, no front or back in the conventional sense. It is an amorphous, semi-liquid body of living, vitreous matter, like a glob of molten obsidian that has cooled just enough to hold a shape, but not enough to become truly solid. Its "skin" is a mirror of its home, a sheath of rippling, iridescent black that perfectly mimics the surrounding stone. In stillness, it is perfectly camouflaged. It achieves this by pulling microscopic flecks of the Cusp’s rock into its own gelatinous form, wearing the mountain as its flesh.

The Weeper has no limbs. It moves by a process of impossibly slow, controlled flow, like a slug made of tar. Its passage across the stone is not a crawl but a creep, a glacial ooze that might cover a few inches in a lifetime. It anchors itself not with claws, but with a deep, molecular adhesion to the rock, its very substance forming a temporary, cohesive bond with the spire.

The sound of the Cusp, that all-pervading chime, is their voice. It is the sound of their bodies resonating. Inside each Weeper’s form are countless, microscopic crystalline splinters, shards of the Cusp’s stone that the creature has ingested and suspended within its being. The constant, subtle vibration of the spire itself—the deep, planetary hum of a young mountain settling—causes these internal shards to resonate. This sound is then amplified and focused by the creature’s own body, which acts as a living resonance chamber. The high, keening cry is the sound of a billion tiny fragments of the mountain singing from within the bodies of the creatures that have consumed them. They do not cry out; they *are* a cry.

They feed on stillness and despair. The Cusp is a place of profound geological tension, a wound held open. This tension, this potential energy, bleeds into the very fabric of the place. The Lament-Glass Weepers are the only things that can metabolize it. They are, in essence, entropy eaters. They slowly, passively absorb the latent stress in the crystalline structure of the stone they rest upon. As they do, the rock beneath them becomes infinitesimally more stable, more "at rest." The process is agonizingly slow and yields almost no energy. It is why their lives are measured in millennia and their movements are nearly imperceptible. They are not parasites on the mountain; they are its therapists, its confessors, slowly soothing its geological pain by drinking it in. The waste product of this process is the pure, cold water that beads on their bodies and seeps into the cracks of the stone, the very water that gives the Cusp its constant, clinging dampness. They weep the mountain's purified tears.

They do not reproduce in a biological sense; they are born of the Cusp itself. When the geological stress in a section of the spire becomes too great, a new fracture will form. In that moment of violent release, of shattering and splintering, a portion of the spire's "pain" congeals. A new Weeper precipitates from the damp air and fractured stone, a small, quivering bead of black glass that begins its own slow, millennia-long crawl. They are not born; they are calamities given consciousness.

There is a final, secret aspect to their existence. While they feed on the silent stress of the rock, they are also drawn to a different kind of pain. On the rarest of occasions, a creature from the smothered world below—a hardy avian, a wind-borne spore, a lost and climbing thing—will find its way up to the Cusp. Here, it will quickly die from the cold, the lack of food, or the sheer sonic pressure.

When this happens, the Weepers will begin to move. Over decades, perhaps centuries, those nearest to the remains will slowly converge. They will flow over the body, enveloping it. They do not consume the flesh. They absorb the fading echo of its life, the psychic residue of its fear, its struggle, and its final moments of despair. This is a rare and potent vintage for them. After such a feeding, a Weeper’s chime will change, becoming lower, more complex, imbued with a ghost of another's sorrow. For a few hundred years, it will carry a unique note in the Cusp's monolithic chorus, a borrowed and fading grief, before it eventually returns to the pure, mineral lament of the stone. They are the archivists of all the pain that has ever touched their lonely world.
