To say that nothing lives here is true, in a way. No creature with a stomach to fill or a nest to build scurries through the root-woven canyons. There are no hunters and no prey. But to say the city is uninhabited is not the whole truth. It is to mistake the hand for a creature separate from the body, or the thought for an entity separate from the mind.

The city has inhabitants. They are as much a part of its biology as the lignified towers or the sap that flows like blood. They are not animals, nor are they truly plants. They are transient, localized expressions of the city's distributed will. They are its hands, its enzymes, its antibodies. They are what happens when the planetary intelligence needs to perform a specific, delicate task that cannot be accomplished by explosive growth or catastrophic collapse alone.

One might call them the Gardeners.

You will not see them at first. You mistake their motion for the rustling of vines or the slow shift of a buttress. But then, in a place where the golden pollen-fog hangs particularly thick, a form will resolve. It is not born, it does not arrive; it *coalesces*. It is a slow gathering, a drawing-in of the ambient matter and energy of the city. Filaments of fungal hyphae braid themselves into sinew. Pulses of light, piped through vascular bundles, are captured and held in place, forming shimmering, unfixed eyes. The viscous fluid that weeps from the walls is drawn up and hardened into articulated limbs of translucent, amber-like resin. Bark-plates shift and lock together to form a torso, and the iridescent mosses that crawl everywhere creep across its surface, creating shifting patterns like a living tattoo.

The Gardener stands, perhaps nine feet tall, impossibly slender. It has no face in the way a human does, but the captured light-pools of its eyes hold an unnerving focus. It has no mouth, for it does not eat or speak, but the entire front of its torso is a lattice of resonant fibers, the same tissue that forms the seed pods. It is through these fibers that it communicates with the city, not with words, but with vibration, with directed, focused frequencies that are its tools.

They are the agents of the city’s eternal redesign. When a tower begins to fail, its internal structure compromised by its own frantic growth, a Gardener may appear at its base. It will place its resinous hands on the bark-like skin of the building and begin to hum. The hum is not auditory in the conventional sense; it is a focused version of the city’s background roar, tuned to a specific resonant frequency. The hum travels up the tower, and the structure that had seemed solid begins to lose its integrity. The Gardener is not destroying the tower; it is singing its bonds apart, encouraging the process of deliquescence, guiding the collapse into a controlled, nutrient-rich cascade of sludge that the root-mat can readily absorb.

Where a new archway is needed to stabilize two leaning spires, a Gardener will stand between them, its own arms outstretched. From its fingertips, it will extrude fine, sticky filaments that it casts across the gap. These are guide-wires, and the thick, thorny vines that race across every surface will find them. They will follow the paths laid out by the Gardener, weaving and grafting themselves together with an intelligence that is not their own, but the Gardener's. The Gardener becomes a living loom, directing the chaos, shaping the raw impulse of growth into functional, if temporary, architecture.

They are utterly silent beings in a world of deafening noise. They do not breathe; the hot, wet air simply passes through their porous bodies. They do not have minds of their own; they are like temporary terminals logged into the central consciousness of the world-organism. Their intelligence is borrowed, their purpose assigned. They might exist for a week, or an hour, or the span of a single, complex task.

If one were to notice you, the experience would be profoundly alienating. It would not turn its head, but the light-pools of its eyes would drift through its anatomy to fix on you. It might approach, its movements impossibly fluid, half-stride and half-growth, its feet dissolving into the root-mat and reforming a step forward. There would be no malice in its gaze, no curiosity as we know it. It would be the analytical stare of an immune system identifying a foreign body. It might reach out a hand—a hand that is constantly, slowly, weaving and unweaving itself—to touch you. The touch would be warm and damp, like fresh-cut wood, and you would feel a faint vibration as it "reads" your biology. It might classify you as an anomaly to be ignored, a strange and inert piece of geology. Or, it might classify you as a flaw, a disease, something to be pruned. It might begin to hum, not a song of dissolution, but a song of integration, attempting to graft you into the living wall beside you, to weave your tissues into the greater whole, correcting what it perceives as an error in the system.

When a Gardener’s task is complete, it does not walk away. It simply stops. The resonant hum fades. The light in its eyes dims and dissipates back into the city's network. Its form softens. The braided hyphae unravel, the resinous limbs liquefy back into weeping sap, the bark-plates crumble into dust. The mosses that covered it reclaim the raw material. Within moments, the Gardener dissolves back into the pollen-haze and the living ground from which it was made, leaving no trace, no memory, no history.

They are not what lives in the city. They are what the city *is doing*. They are the conscious, fleeting moments in a mind the size of a world, and to see one is to witness the thought process of a god made of wood, and sap, and screaming, endless life.
