They are the Anchored. The name is both literal and spiritual.

They move with a slow, four-limbed grace that is unnerving to imagine but perfectly suited to their world. Their form is humanoid but elongated, with limbs that seem too long for their slender torsos. Their skin is not one color but a palette of the Terrace itself, a living mosaic of ochre, dull bronze, and the pale green of oxidized copper, mottled and streaked as if they, too, have been weathered by millennia of dripping water and scouring wind. It is a camouflage so perfect that at rest, perched on a conduit or flattened against a sheer bulkhead, they simply vanish into the texture of the world.

Their feet are their foundation. They are broad and splayed, the soles covered in a thick, calloused pad that is both incredibly tough and fantastically sensitive. The texture is ridged and grooved, a negative impression of the rivet-heads and plate-seams, allowing them a grip on the canted plains that defies gravity. Their toes are long, prehensile, and tipped not with nails but with hard, chitinous hooks that can find purchase in the smallest crevice or perforation.

Their hands are similar, but more delicate. They have two pairs of arms. The primary, lower set are long and powerful, used for climbing, bracing, and locomotion. Their hands are wide, the skin of their palms as tough as their soles. But tucked beneath their ribcage is a second, shorter pair of arms, rarely used for movement. These are their manipulating limbs, their 'fine work' hands. When they skitter across a vertical plate, these smaller arms are held close to the body, but when they stop to work or eat, these hands emerge, their fingers impossibly long and nimble, capable of unscrewing a rusted fastener, weaving cable-strands into nets, or delicately harvesting the iridescent gills of a particularly large shelf-fungus.

The Anchored are born with a deep, instinctual understanding of the Terrace's language. To them, the foundational hum is not background noise; it is the baseline of consciousness, the "Great Steady." Every other sound is a word or a phrase spoken against that silence. They can read the health of a square-mile section of superstructure by the harmonics of its vibration underfoot. The groan of a support beam under a vine's slow pressure is a sigh of weariness. The sudden hiss of a new steam-leak is a shout of surprise. Their own language is a mix of soft, gutteral clicks and long, resonant notes that mimic the thrumming of the machine, a tonal dialect that would sound to an outsider like the Terrace itself had learned to speak.

They live in small, tightly-knit family groups, or 'Clutches,' their nests woven into the most sheltered and stable parts of the undergrowth. A favorite location is inside the hollow, dormant bell of a ventilation stack, where the updrafts are gentle and the acoustics carry warnings from miles away. Here, they weave hammocks from the stripped insulation of ancient cables and line their floors with the shed, leathery leaves of the warmth-seeking vines, creating a soft, dry space amid the universal damp.

They are not builders; they are tenders, gardeners, and scavengers. Their technology is entirely repurposed. A sharpened shard of ceramic composite becomes a fine blade for skinning a captured plate-crawler. The lens from a forgotten optical sensor becomes a fire-starter, focusing the hard sun onto a bundle of dried moss. Their most vital tools are their 'tethers'—long, braided ropes of conductive filament, harvested from the webs of fuse-spiders and woven into a line of incredible strength and lightness. A tether is an Anchor's most prized possession, their lifeline, the one thing that stands between them and the 'Long Fall.'

The Long Fall—un-being, the final separation from the Terrace—is their only real concept of damnation. Gravity is their god, their devil, and the law by which all things are judged. Every movement is considered, every handhold tested. To see them move is to watch a form of slow-motion parkour, a fluid, deliberate dance of weight and balance. They will spend minutes studying a ten-foot traverse across a slick, mossy pipe, their heads cocked, listening to its internal groans, feeling the vibrations through their feet, before committing to the crossing.

Their diet consists of what the Terrace provides. They harvest the thick, protein-rich algae from the warmer pools of standing water. They set intricate snares for glaze-wings and other large insects. They are experts at finding the 'marrow' of the machine—the nutrient-dense, semi-organic sludge that accumulates in sealed-off pipes and forgotten reservoirs, a delicacy with the flavor of ozone, metal, and decay. They have a symbiotic relationship with the gliders; they clean their nests of parasites and debris, and in return are left with a steady supply of shed metallic feathers, which they use for tools and adornment. A chieftain might wear a mantle of these feathers, which shimmers and shifts in the light, the only sign of overt status in their egalitarian Clutches.

When an Anchor dies of old age or sickness, their body is not buried. It is 'returned.' The Clutch carries the deceased to a special place, a 'giving plate,' which overhangs one of the great valleys. There, they are wrapped in a shroud of leaves and laid upon the metal. The scavengers—the plate-crawlers and the lesser insects—are their undertakers. The sun and the condensation are their embalmers. Over the course of a long cycle, the body is consumed, and what remains is broken down by the moss and the rust, becoming another thin layer in the world's composite soil. The process is seen as the highest honor: to dissolve back into the body of the great machine, to become one with the hum, to become, finally and completely, Anchored. They are not the masters of this world, nor its prisoners. They are its nervous system, the quiet, watchful consciousness that emerged from the ghost in the machine.
