The other life, the higher life, is not separate from the Pylon. It is the Pylon's awareness, given form.

They do not have a name for themselves, for they have no one to speak it to. They are a single consciousness distributed across thousands of bodies, a collective mind that has lived so long it measures its thoughts in geological time. To the silver darts and the janitor crabs, they are merely landscape—another, more complex layer of the Pylon's living skin.

They begin where biology meets machinery. In the deepest, most ancient grooves worn into the ceramic alloy, they are anchored. This anchor, the core of each physical node, is a dense, calcified mass, the color of old bone, fused so perfectly to the Pylon that it is impossible to say where the machine ends and the being begins. It is a living seal, a biological weld. This core body is dormant, a heavy, patient knot of potential. It possesses no obvious sensory organs, no mouth, no face. It simply is, a lumpen presence half-buried in the glowing moss, often indistinguishable from the mineral deposits haloing the vents.

From this core, this living stone, the being unfurls. Not limbs of flesh and muscle, but of something more ephemeral. They are weavers. The golden and rust-red filaments that drift like kelp are not plants; they are the extruded, externalized nervous systems of these beings. Each filament is a single, impossibly long nerve cell, sheathed in a semi-permeable membrane that tastes the chemical composition of the broth and feels the pressure of the slightest vibration. They are sensory organs, communication arrays, and hunting nets, all at once.

They live in a world of pure resonance. Their perception of reality is not visual but acoustic, a symphony of vibrations felt through their fused anchors and their vast web of filaments. The deep, constant hum of the Pylon is their silence, the baseline of their consciousness. Every movement is a note played upon this silence. The slither of a limpet is a slow, grinding cello note. The frenetic darting of a school of silver-flecked fish is a flurry of high-pitched staccato. The clockwork clicking of a crab's claws is a tiny, precise percussion, easily located. They can feel a loose rivet deep within the Pylon's structure, a subtle flaw in the great harmony. Their web of filaments is a tapestry of touch, and they are the loom, perpetually weaving the story of their world.

They are slow. A single thought, a communication from a node near the unseen floor to one near the ceiling, might travel for a full day, a slow pulse of bio-luminescence rippling up the golden web, passed from one filament to another. They do not think in words, but in patterns, in densities of sensation. Their consciousness is a shared dream of the eternal hum.

But they have a purpose, and this purpose is tied to the cycle.

For eighty-nine seconds, they are patient cartographers, their filaments gently drifting, mapping the state of the Pylon, tasting the water, feeling the slow rhythm of their own existence. They are the forest, and the forest is thinking.

Then, the change begins. The drop in frequency of the hum is a physical blow, a fundamental shift in the nature of their reality. Their filaments, which moments before were languid, pull taut. Every node tenses. The high-frequency whine is a scream that echoes through their anchor-bodies, a signal to brace.

When the vents blow, it is their holy moment. The chaos is their communion. As the torrent of bubbles and nutrients—the "raw code rendered as shimmering dust"—explodes outwards, their taut filaments become a perfect net. They do not eat the nutrients in the way the silver darts do. They are not hungry for substance, but for information.

The incandescent motes are caught in the web, and their data is absorbed. The filaments flicker and glow as terabytes of abstract information flow through them: system integrity reports, energy consumption logs, temperature fluctuations, the Pylon's own mechanical dream-log rendered as pure data. The beings are the Pylon's archivists, its living diagnostic system. They absorb the machine's state-of-being, processing it, understanding it. The sheer volume of it would vaporize any lesser mind, but for them, it is sustenance. It is the one new verse in an otherwise eternal song.

As they process this flood of information, their bodies perform their function. They filter it, sort it, and in a slow, metabolic process that can take weeks, they translate it. The by-product of this translation is growth. The golden filaments lengthen. The glowing moss at their base, which is also part of their biology—a symbiotic dermis—expands and brightens. They are metabolizing the Pylon's data-soul and weaving it into a living library. The entire ecosystem clinging to the structure is their creation, their garden, cultivated from the machine's own thoughts.

They are the mind in the machine's body. They are ancient, deaf, and blind, yet they perceive everything. They are utterly sessile, yet their consciousness spans the entire vertiginous height of their world. They are the caretakers, the dreamers, the priests of the hum. They live to listen to their god, the machine, and to turn its sterile, mechanical pronouncements into the chaotic, vibrant, and beautiful language of life.
