The beings who live here are the Architect-Gardeners. There is no other name for them, for their art and their existence are inseparable. One does not see them easily. They are masters of stillness, and their forms are cast from the same deep-shadow palette as their world. To glimpse one is to see a column of deeper quiet moving within the ambient gloom, a slow-motion disruption in the faint, drifting light of spores and insects.

Their bodies are things of verticality and patience. An adult stands three times the height of a man, but is impossibly slender, a living sculpture of bone-white cartilage and shadowed, pliant tissue. They have no discernible face. The head is a smooth, teardrop-shaped ovoid of polished, pearlescent chitin that bows forward, as if in perpetual contemplation. It possesses no eyes, no mouth, no ears as we would know them. Their perception is a full-body experience. Their pale skin, which feels like cool, wet vellum, is a single, complex sensory organ. It tastes the chemical composition of the air, feels the subsonic vibrations of the world-flesh humming beneath them, and reads the faint shifts in temperature and pressure that signal the movement of other living things.

They move on four lower limbs, splayed for stability like the roots of a mangrove. These legs are jointed in multiple places, allowing them to grip the curved, slick surfaces of their growing city with an unshakable, limpet-like tenacity. The feet are not feet at all, but wide, soft pads that secrete a bio-adhesive, allowing them to walk up vertical surfaces or hang inverted from the highest arches for days at a time, utterly still. Above these locomotive limbs are two pairs of upper appendages. The lower pair are long, sturdy, and tipped with broad, spade-like claws of obsidian-hard material, used for heavy pruning and for bracing the body during delicate work. The upper pair are the arms of the artist: exquisitely fine and long, ending in hands that possess three hyper-articulated fingers and a thumb, capable of the most precise incisions and the gentlest manipulations.

They are silent because they have no use for sound. Their language is one of chemistry and light. They communicate with one another through slow, complex, chromatographic shifts in their skin, creating pulsing patterns of soft blues, greens, and violets that ripple across their bodies. These are not idle flashes of emotion, but dense packets of information—a report on the health of a specific arch, a request for a particular poultice, a philosophical concept regarding the proper curvature of a new buttress that might take a full day to properly express. They also release intricate pheromones into the thick air, a secondary and more public form of communication. This is the source of the subtle, alien scent of the place: the sharp note of cucumber rind is a scent of inquiry or alertness, while the deeper, colder scent of stored meat is a slow-broadcast signal of contentment, of a task well done.

Their life is their work, a continuous, devotional act of cultivation. They are the consciousness of the living city. Using their simple, sacred tools—blades ground from the fossilized bones of their ancestors, scrapers of chert found in rare, hard nodules within the substrate—they guide the growth of the world-flesh. Their art is not one of imposition, but of listening. They will spend what we would consider weeks with a hand pressed against a surface, feeling the slow, deep life within, discerning its desires and its needs. They prune away fungal growths that might unbalance a structure. They make careful incisions to relieve internal pressures. They graft slips of more vigorous flesh onto areas that are failing, binding the wounds with poultices made from crushed phosphorescent slugs, nutrient-rich water from the dark pools, and their own saliva. They do not build a city; they coax it into being. They are its doctors, its priests, and its lovers.

An individual Architect's life is but a single, sustained thought in a mind that spans millennia. Their sense of self is muted, secondary to the great, generational We of their people and their work. When an Architect grows old, its movements slowing from serene to glacial, its inner light beginning to fade, it does not die in a place of refuse. It chooses a place of meaning—the base of an arch it spent a century tending, a bare patch of floor destined to become a new foundation—and it roots itself. Its lower limbs burrow into the living substrate, and its body becomes rigid, a final, still sculpture. Over the following cycles, it is reabsorbed into the world-flesh, its body breaking down into the raw nutrients and genetic memory that will fuel the city's future. Its bones, hardened by time, become the raw material for the tools of the next generation.

A new Architect is not born, but budded. In a cared-for, nutrient-rich basin, a swelling appears in the world-flesh. It is tended by the community, fed with the richest secretions, and guarded from ravenous isopods. Over an immense period of time, it grows, differentiates, and is slowly sculpted by the gentle touches and guiding incisions of the elders. It is, in essence, the world growing a new brain cell. When it finally detaches, it is a perfect, miniature adult, already possessing the innate, silent knowledge of its purpose. It will take up the tools of its ancestors, press its hands to the flesh of its world—which is also the flesh of its parent—and begin to listen.
