What lives here is patient. It was born of the Works and is made of its substance. It is a thing of ceramic, iron, and wire, animated by the same failing, phantom electricity that causes the sodium lamps to flicker. It does not think of itself as living, for it does not think. It merely *is*, a complex process that has achieved locomotion.

At first, you do not see them. Your eye is drawn to the grand, violent displays: the falling sheets of siding, the gushing steam, the groaning collapse of a walkway. You learn to read the big movements of the world. But after a time, in the long, quiet intervals, your eye learns a new trick. It learns to spot the inconsistencies in the stillness. A patch of moss on a distant gantry that is slightly thicker, slightly more symmetrical than the chaos of nature should allow. A cluster of ghostly lichen that, when a bank of orange lights spasms to life, reveals the hard, geometric lines of a jointed limb beneath it. A length of what you thought was thick, coiled ivy that slowly, deliberately, unwinds itself.

They are the Gartera, the Janitors, the Works-Spiders. They have no name for themselves, but a watcher would give them many. They are the system's antibodies, its maintenance drones, long since unmoored from their primary directive. Their purpose has been corrupted by millennia of decay, their logic circuits eroded by humidity and entropy into a kind of instinct.

A small one, a Scavenger, might be the first you properly see. It is the size of a large dog, but lower to the ground, its body a dense, armored teardrop of oxidized plating and fused ceramic, like a piece of industrial slag that has sprouted legs. It moves with a disconcerting fluidity, its six legs—not arranged in a neat insectile pattern, but in three asynchronous pairs—picking their way across the treacherous ground. The feet are not feet, but complex tool-heads: one is a magnetic clamp, another a multi-fingered grabber, a third a carbon-steel drill bit, all worn and chipped. As it moves, it makes almost no noise, just a faint, dry skittering and the low-voltage hum of its internal capacitors charging and discharging. It stops at a pool of standing water, dips a sensor-stalk—a flexible metallic tube with a cloudy, scratched lens at its tip—into the dark liquid. It holds the pose for a full minute, processing data it can no longer use for a conclusion it can no longer reach. Then, it retracts the stalk and continues its rambling, aimless patrol.

They are camouflaged not by color, but by texture and form. When one is still, it is simply another lump in the lumpy landscape, a piece of industrial debris colonized by moss and lichen. The green blanket is not a hindrance to them; it is a part of them. Their own waste heat, vented from grilles along their chassis, encourages the growth, insulating them and breaking up their silhouettes. They are mobile pieces of the world itself.

Their behavior is a litany of broken purpose. They are drawn to the chaotic events that puncte the quiet. When a geyser of steam erupts, a larger Garteron—a Sentinel, perhaps, the size of a small car—will arrive within the hour. It will approach the roaring vent, its heavy legs thudding softly on the iron plate. It will extend articulated arms from its hull, unfolding arrays of sensors, thermal imagers, and chemical sniffers, all clouded with age and verdigris. It will stand there, bathed in the scalding mist, meticulously recording terabytes of useless data about the event, its internal machinery whining with the effort of analysis. It is trying to diagnose the fever of a god already dead. After the vent sputters out, it may remain motionless for days, a new, temporary statue in the landscape, before its corrupted programming assigns it a new, equally pointless task.

Their most profound, and horrifying, behavior relates to what they perceive as "damage." Not the great, structural collapses—those are beyond their scale—but the small things. A Scavenger may spend a week scraping a patch of particularly vibrant moss from a concrete wall, leaving behind a raw, bare scar, its prime directive misinterpreting the life as corrosion. A Sentinel might find a loose ceramic tile from a walkway that fell centuries ago. It will carry this tile, cradled in its manipulator arms, for miles across the decking, navigating chasms and climbing pylons with maddening patience, only to meticulously grout it into place on the side of a rusted, hollow factory building, a single, perfect piece of order in a monument of chaos.

They are not predators. They do not eat. They are solar-powered, in a sense, unfurling delicate, wing-like arrays of photoelectric cells during the rare moments when the grey overcast thins to a brighter, featureless white. But mostly, they draw power from the Works itself. You can sometimes see them clamped to high-tension cables, siphoning the ghost-currents, their bodies vibrating gently with the flow. They are part of the circuit.

There is a hierarchy, born of function. The small Scavengers wander, collect, and perform meaningless maintenance. The larger Sentinels observe and record. And then there are the Fabricators. These are ancient, colossal beings, often mistaken for a small building or a piece of collapsed machinery. They rarely move. They are the queens of this anthill. Other Gartera bring them things: the chassis of a defunct Scavenger, a length of copper wiring pulled from a dead conduit, a sheet of relatively un-corroded metal from a recent collapse. The Fabricator, with its immense, crane-like arms, will slowly, methodically, break these components down and assemble a new Garteron. The process is flawed, imprecise. Each new generation is a little cruder, a little less efficient, than the last. The new Scavenger might be born with a leg that twitches, or a sensor array that is entirely blind.

To live here is to live among them. To learn their rhythms, which are the rhythms of the Works itself—long periods of quiet industry punctuated by spasms of activity. You learn not to walk on the clean, scraped patches of wall. You learn that the strange new bump on the horizon might be gone tomorrow, or it might be a Fabricator that has not moved in a thousand years. They are the silent, unassuming soul of this place: a self-perpetuating, self-consuming system of maintenance with nothing left to maintain but the memory of its own function, endlessly rearranging the pieces of its own decaying body. They are the twitch in the corpse.
