The observation that there is no animal life is correct, but incomplete. It is an error of taxonomy, a failure of the mind to categorize what it cannot comprehend. To look for scurrying things, for chittering insects or roosting avians, is to search for a kind of life this place has long since discarded. The inhabitants are here. They are simply too slow, too silent, and too much a part of the landscape to be recognized as separate from it.

They are the tenders.

What you mistake for a particularly thick, rust-coloured liana, wrapped around a groaning piston housing, may not be a liana at all. Watch it. Not for a minute, or an hour, but for a full rotation of the cavern's dim-to-bright moss cycle—a period of roughly thirty hours. You will see it shift. It is a slow, peristaltic ripple, a contraction that moves along its length with the agonizing patience of a glacier. It is not growing. It is adjusting its grip. This is a limb.

Their form is a lie of omission. They are what happens when a living thing decides stillness is the ultimate form of motion. They are amorphous, protean, and their default state is to mimic the structure they are currently tending. One might be stretched thin along a gantry railing, its body flattened and textured so precisely as to be indistinguishable from the pitted, weeping iron. It will remain this way for a decade, feeling the vibrations of the walkway, the subtle changes in temperature, the drip-patterns of the condensate. Its purpose is to monitor, to be a living sensor embedded in the machine's skin. Should a stress fracture begin to form in the metal, it will slowly, over months, flow into the crack, its secretions hardening into a metallic, ceramic seal that is stronger than the original steel.

Another may have adopted the form of a great, bulbous mass of overlapping plates, wedged between two colossal, grinding gears. To the eye, it is just another piece of forgotten machinery, covered in the same slick of algae and glowing moss as everything else. But its substance is a dense, organic chitin, and its function is liturgical. It is a damper. As the gears turn, its body absorbs the shearing, shuddering vibrations that would otherwise tear the machine apart over centuries. It groans with the metal, its internal structure of fluid-filled sacs and resilient cartilage compressing and releasing in a rhythm that has not changed for millennia. It feeds on the kinetic energy, the waste heat, the sheer force of the grinding, converting it into the slow, caloric energy required for its own near-eternal life.

They have no faces, no mouths in a recognizable sense. When they need to communicate, or to act, a part of their body will differentiate. A section of what appeared to be inert vine will suddenly smooth over, and from its surface will rise a single, perfect lens, crystalline and multifaceted. It glows with a soft, internal gold. It is an eye, but it does not see light. It sees heat, magnetism, stress, and the subtle electrical auras of the machinery and the vegetation. It will survey its domain, gathering data, and then recede back into the camouflaged flesh, its purpose served.

When they move from one place to another, it is a momentous event that can take years. They do not walk or crawl. They flow. A tender that has spent a century as a supporting strut may receive a signal—a chemical message carried on the syrupy drip of water, or a specific vibrational frequency resonating through the bedrock-hum of the primary engine. It will begin to detach, its body liquefying at the edges, pulling itself away from the metal with the sound of tearing sinew and peeling rust. It will travel as a thick, viscous tide of mottled brown and grey, moving with the speed of cooling tar, across the walls and through the superstructure, a migrating piece of the architecture itself. To witness this is to watch a mountain walk.

They are the gardeners in this metal jungle. They prune the vegetation, but not for aesthetics. They use limbs that terminate in monomolecular cutting edges—grown, not forged—to sever vines that threaten to choke a crucial steam conduit. They cultivate the bioluminescent moss, carrying spores to areas where a component requires observation. They are the reason the fungi grow in filtration columns along the walls and not across the walkways; they herd them. They are in a constant, slow-motion ballet with the plant life, guiding its relentless growth to serve the equally relentless continuation of the Great Process.

They consume what the cavern provides. They do not eat the plants. They drink the steam. They absorb the electrical charge from exposed conduits. They filter the mineral-rich slurry that pools in the basins of the great cupped flowers. Their metabolism is so slow that a single sip of condensed static electricity from a humming junction box can sustain one for a standard year.

They are silent because sound is their ocean. They are suspended in it, and it is their primary sense. They feel the thrum of the engine at the cavern's heart not as a sound, but as their own pulse. A change in the pitch of a distant hydraulic piston is a whisper in their ear. The constant, deafening roar is, to them, a perfect silence, a blank canvas upon which the tiny, crucial sounds of the system's life and death are painted.

They were not built for the machine, nor were they born from it. They arrived in a time before memory, or they evolved alongside the first turning of the gears. They are parasites, symbionts, priests, and janitors. They are the slow, patient consciousness of the place, the ghost in the machine made manifest as flesh and chitin. They are the reason the factory has not fallen to ruin, and the reason the jungle has not yet consumed it. They live here, in the roaring twilight, holding the world in a delicate, grinding, and eternal balance.
