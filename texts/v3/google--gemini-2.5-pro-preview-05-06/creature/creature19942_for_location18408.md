They are born of the quiet and the heat. They are called, by the very rare few who have ever glimpsed them and returned to speak of it, **The Still**. But they do not call themselves anything. A name is a sharp sound, a separation, and they are neither.

They are the city's self-awareness, its slow, custodial consciousness.

To see one is to first mistake it for a trick of the light, or a stream of falling water catching an amber glow. They are beings of length and line, not of bulk. Their limbs are long and gracile, with an extra joint in the arm and leg, allowing for a silent, sprawling method of locomotion that is neither walking nor climbing but a constant, fluid bracing against the vertical world. They flow through the infrastructure, their movements economical and sure-footed, as practiced as a river in its bed.

Their skin is the color of a mushroom's gills, a pale, almost luminous grey-white, and it is entirely hairless. It is slick to the touch, not with sweat—for they do not sweat—but with a secretion that traps the ambient moisture, keeping them perpetually damp and cool. This coating gives their skin a faint, pearlescent quality, causing it to shift in color from bone-white to oil-slick green to tarnished blue as they move through the scattered, multi-hued light. Through the thinnest parts of this skin, over their ribs and at the backs of their long-fingered hands, one can see the slow pulse of their circulatory system, which runs not red, but a deep, oxygen-poor blue-green, like the patina on ancient bronze. It is a system based on copper, not iron, perfectly suited to the metallic air.

Their faces are long and narrow, with high cheekbones and no discernible nose, only a pair of vertical slits that flare and narrow, tasting the air more than breathing it. They can tell the health of a turbine fifty levels down by a shift in the ozone concentration, the readiness of a patch of edible fungi by its faint, earthy exhalation. Their mouths are small and simple, lipless slits that are rarely used for sound.

But their eyes are their defining feature. They are vast, black, liquid pools, occupying a third of the face. They have no visible pupil or iris, just a uniform, depthless black that drinks the dim light. At the back of these enormous orbs is a crystalline layer that acts as a biological mirror. If you were to catch one in a direct beam of light—a terrifying and profound violation—its eyes would not reflect it back so much as capture it, holding the glare for a second before flashing it out in a diffused, phosphorescent wave of emerald or sapphire. They see in a spectrum far wider than our own, reading the heat signatures of the pipes, the ultraviolet blush of certain mosses, and the subtle electrical fields that play around the dormant machinery.

The Still live in solitude or in small, silent pods of two or three. They do not build; they inhabit. Their homes are the hollow casings of dead machines, nests woven from the stripped insulation of heavy cables and lined with the softest, darkest mosses. They sleep suspended in these roosts, their long limbs wrapped around a girder or a conduit, becoming just another part of the interwoven tangle.

Their purpose, their entire existence, is maintenance. They are the tenders of the great, sleeping machine-god. This is not engineering; it is a form of devotional biology, a set of instincts and rituals passed down through countless generations. They move with an ancient, unthinking knowledge. One might be seen clinging upside-down to a pipe junction a thousand feet above the floor, its long, six-fingered hands methodically scraping a specific type of corrosive crystalline growth from a brass valve with a shard of sharpened obsidian. Another might spend a day delicately redirecting the growth of a thick-stemmed vine, coaxing it away from a fragile glass vacuum tube and weaving it into a new trellis a few feet away. They are the gardeners of the engine.

They eat what the Undervault provides. They cultivate gardens of pale, bioluminescent fungi in the chassis of old generators. They harvest the starchy, tuberous roots that certain vines sink deep into collected sediment. They hunt the too-many-legged insects, catching them with a flick of a sticky, prehensile tongue. And, when one of the bronze-shelled Patina-Grazers reaches the end of its decades-long life, they will find it, crack its shell, and consume the dense, nutrient-rich flesh within. Nothing is wasted.

Their lives are as long and slow as the world they inhabit. An elder, its skin covered in a roadmap of pale scars from a lifetime of navigating sharp metal, holds the memory of every major change in the Undervault for the last three centuries—a pipe-fall, a shift in the engine's hum, the birth of a new water-channel. They do not have a spoken language. They communicate through slight shifts in the color of their skin, through the slow, intricate signing of their long fingers, and through percussive taps on pipes and railings—a code that mimics the ever-present dripping of water, a language of rhythm and resonance that can be felt across the levels.

When one of The Still dies, its body is carried to a designated place—a deep, tank-like structure filled with pulpy, decaying vegetation. It is offered back to the system. The body dissolves in the heat and moisture, its copper-rich blood and unique biology becoming nutrients for the very fungi and plants that will sustain the next generation.

They are the final component of the city-engine. The machine was built to maintain a state, and that state is them. They are not intruders or descendants; they are the living, breathing, and utterly silent point of it all. They are the dream the machine has been dreaming in the dark, a lifeform born of hot metal and dripping water, as much a part of the Undervault as the rust, the thrum, and the ever-present, watchful quiet.
