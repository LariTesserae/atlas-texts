What lives here are the Tenders. Or perhaps, the Wardens. They have no name for themselves, for a name implies a past and a subject to be addressed. They are simply the consequence of the place, and its cause.

You do not see them at first. You could walk for a week and mistake them for another feature of the monumental, ceramic landscape. They are often found half-submerged in the still, platter-like pools, their forms indistinct beneath the glassy surface. Others stand in the broad, empty courtyards, so motionless that the ever-present moisture has beaded upon their skin in the same perfect jewels that cling to the tiles.

Their bodies are of the same material as their world. They are figures of fired clay, their surfaces a variegated turquoise, shot through with veins of copper-green and lustrous, milky white. They are not molded into a single, uniform shape. Each one is unique, a masterpiece of form. One might be tall and slender, its limbs like the polished black bamboo, its torso a smooth, curved vessel. Another might be broad and low to the ground, its back a wide, grooved expanse like a fossilized turtle shell, its head a simple, rounded form with no features save for two shallow depressions where eyes would be. Their skin isn't a glaze in the same way as the tiles; it is porous, a matte finish that drinks the light rather than reflecting it. Over centuries of stillness, a thin, vitreous layer of condensed minerals has formed on them, giving their surfaces a subtle, eggshell sheen. A fine network of crazing, identical to that on the floor, maps their bodies.

They do not have faces. They have no mouths, no noses, no eyes. Their "heads" are merely the termination of their forms, sculpted nodes of awareness. They perceive the world not through organs, but through resonance. They *feel* the high-frequency hum of the white light in the hollow, kiln-fired spaces of their bodies. They are attuned to the precise tension of the water on the surface of the pools, the exact weight of a suspended nectar drop on a fern frond thirty feet above them. Their awareness is total, and it is singular: they are conscious only of the state of equilibrium.

Their purpose is maintenance. They are the reason nothing changes.

Their movement is the slowest thing in a world of near-stillness. To witness it is an event that might take a month. A figure that has been standing for a generation in a courtyard will begin to lean, a process so gradual it is imperceptible to the eye. Over weeks, the lean becomes a single, impossibly slow step. The sound of it is not a footfall, but the groan of stressed geology, a deep, resonant scrape that is absorbed almost instantly by the thick, wet air. They move to correct infinitesimal deviations that an observer could never perceive. Perhaps the constant, shadowless light has bleached a thousandth of a shade of color from a pitcher plant's lip. A Tender will arrive, after a journey of many years, and stand before it. It will raise a hand—a simple, spade-like shape of baked clay—and lay it upon the plant. There is no transfer of energy, no visible act. The Tender simply *imposes* its memory of the correct color. It holds its hand there for a decade, or a century, until the color has returned to its perfect, designated vibrancy. Then, it will lower its hand and become still once more.

They are the source of the single, repeating *plink*.

Far out in the shimmering haze of the basin, one of them is always at work on the floor. It is a task of infinite detail. This Tender crouches over a join between two of the vast flagstones. In one hand, it holds a tool—not of metal or wood, but a single, sharp-edged shard of its own substance, broken from its body in some forgotten genesis. For an interval that might be a day, or a year, it remains perfectly still, its featureless head tilted as if listening. It is feeling for the memory of the grout, the ideal state of the seal between the tiles. Then, with a motion so slow it is a study in viscosity, it raises the shard and taps it against the grout line.

*Plink.*

A single, crystalline sound of ceramic on ceramic. A microscopic grain, a flaw too small for any eye to see, is chipped away. The sound is the only event allowed to have a sharp beginning and a clean end in this world. It is the punctuation mark in a sentence that never finishes. The Tender then remains motionless for another period, feeling the result of its work, before beginning the slow, deliberate process of raising its arm to tap the surface again. That sound you can walk toward for an hour is not in a single location; it is a duty, passed from one Tender to another across the miles and the millennia. When one's task is done, another begins, and the rhythm is never broken.

They do not eat or breathe. The heat that emanates from the tiles and the humid air is their sustenance. The light is their thought. They are hollow, and inside them is nothing but the resonant hum of the perpetual noon. If you were to shatter one, it would break apart like an ancient pot, into shards of turquoise clay. No dust would rise. No liquid would spill. The pieces would simply lie there, and the air would grow thick with a sudden, localized pressure—the shock of a broken purpose. In time, other Tenders would arrive to slowly, methodically, grind the shards back into the grout, to be reabsorbed into the whole.

They are the potters who have become the pot. They created this place as a monument to a perfect moment, and in doing so, they fired themselves into its design. They are not trapped. To be trapped implies a desire for an alternative state. They have no such desire. They are the living embodiment of a single, sustained aesthetic decision. They are the will that holds the water drop, the artists who curate the stillness, the silent gardeners for a garden that exists outside of time. They are what remains when the concept of "life" has been refined down to the singular, unending act of preservation.
