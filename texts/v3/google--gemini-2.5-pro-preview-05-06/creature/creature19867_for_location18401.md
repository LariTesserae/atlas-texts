Nothing lives here in the way of a mind. There is no will, no fear, no hunger, no desire. The great engines do not think; they *are*. They are a geological process that has achieved a state of such complex, self-sustaining order that it mimics life. Yet, if you watch the silent architecture for long enough—for a decade, a century—you will see that the stillness is not absolute. Something does move.

They are the Caretakers. Or perhaps, the Geometers. They have no name for themselves.

At first, you do not see one. You see a flaw. On the polished obsidian flank of a load-bearing arch, a patch of light seems to shimmer wrongly, a patch of geometry that does not quite align with the grand, static patterns. It is an optical illusion, a heat-haze, a trick of the alien light. But the shimmer intensifies, resolves. Facets emerge from the flat surface, sliding over one another. A shape is detaching itself from the superstructure, not like a creature from its perch, but like a key being turned in a lock of impossible complexity.

It is a being made of the world's own substance, a kinematic sculpture of crystal and metal. It might be ten feet tall, but it has no discernible front or back, head or torso. Its body is a cluster of interlocking limbs, some fine as needles and made of polished tungsten, others broad and blade-like, carved from smoky quartz. Joints are not biological articulations but gimbal-like pivots and magnetic bearings that shift and rotate with fluid, silent precision. When it moves, it is less like walking and more like a controlled unfolding, a piece of intricate origami reverse-engineering itself through space. Its surface is a mosaic of the world's geology: plates of raw, grey stone; veins of shimmering bismuth with their tell-tale iridescent stairsteps; lenses of flawless crystal that seem to function as its eyes, though they do not see light so much as they sense stress, temperature, and vibration.

It is an antibody of the machine-organism. A mobile, autonomous repair unit.

The Geometer begins to climb. It does not scuttle like an insect; it flows up the vast iron latticework. Limbs extend, anchor themselves into microscopic fissures or magnetically couple to the metal, while the rest of its body reconfigures and follows. It moves with a terrifying and beautiful purpose, its path dictated by a logic you cannot comprehend but can feel is absolute. It is responding to a signal, a dissonance in the planet-wide thrum that is perfectly audible to it. It is journeying toward a flaw.

After a week of travel across a single "branch" of the world-engine, it arrives. Its destination is one of the colossal crystalline gears at the heart of the machine. To an observer, the gear is perfect. To the Geometer, it is dying. A micro-fracture, thinner than a prayer, has formed near the axle-point. In a thousand years, it might cause the gear to shear. For this world, that is an unacceptable emergency.

The Geometer affixes itself to the architecture nearby. It becomes still, a part of the landscape again, and begins its work. From its crystalline body, specialized appendages emerge. One, a focusing lens, projects a needle-thin beam of concentrated sunlight onto the fracture. The temperature rises with pinpoint accuracy, not enough to melt the crystal but to prepare it. Another limb, ending in a delicate, vibrating tip, begins to sing a precisely calibrated frequency into the gear, a harmonic that settles the crystal lattice and prevents the fracture from propagating. Then, a third appendage extrudes a single, perfect drop of the silvery, molten sap that circulates through the world-engine's veins. The drop is guided into the heated fissure, where it is drawn in by capillary action. The light beam adjusts, its energy output changing to perform a microscopic, localized annealing process.

The work takes a month. The Geometer remains motionless, a mere tool fused to the larger machine, utterly dedicated to this single act of maintenance. When it is done, the fracture is gone. The gear is not repaired; it is perfected again. The integrity of the system is assured.

These Geometers are not individuals. They are not born, and they do not die. When a flaw is detected by the host organism—a stress point, a cooling inefficiency, a vibrational anomaly—a signal is sent to a nexus point, a "fabrication node" deep within the structure. Here, raw minerals are drawn up, and a new Geometer is constructed, its form and toolkit precisely tailored to the task at hand. It is imbued with a single, overriding directive and is then released. It is a drone, a purpose given form.

When its task is complete, it does not seek out another. It travels to the nearest reclamation point—a specific patch of structure that glows with a faint internal heat—and flattens itself against it. It dissolves back into the whole, its constituent materials re-absorbed into the system, its brief, functional existence concluded. It is a thought that the thoughtless world-engine has, a flicker of concentrated intention in a universe of being.

To witness one is to see the immune system of a planet at work. They are the living embodiment of the world's core principle: that there will be no change. No decay. No death. They are the silent, tireless guardians of an eternity machine, ensuring that the great, slow heart of their world will continue its imperturbable rhythm, and that the long, static hum of existence will never fall out of tune. They are what lives here: a distributed, transient, and utterly selfless function.
