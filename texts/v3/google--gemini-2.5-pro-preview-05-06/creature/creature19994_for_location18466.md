This city has no citizens. It has custodians. And you do not find them; you only, after a great deal of time, begin to distinguish them from the city itself.

At first, you see only the motion. Against the bone-white face of a flute, a faint, vertical shadow seems to drift upwards, defying the wind. You dismiss it as a trick of the light, an optical illusion caused by the vibrating air and the stark sun. But you see it again, on another spire. A slow, inexorable crawl, like a crack forming in reverse. Patience is the only sense that can perceive them. If you stare at one spot on a mossy wall for an hour, for a day, you will eventually resolve the form of a Weaver.

They are impossibly, unnervingly slender. An adult might be twelve feet long, but no thicker than your arm, a living line drawn against the vertical world. Their bodies are not flesh and blood, but something in between geology and biology. Their core is a semi-rigid rod of the same porous, white calcite as the spires, but it is latticed with living tissue. This tissue runs in veins across their surface, appearing as sinuous patterns in the precise shades of the mosses they tend: emerald, ochre, and a purple so deep it is almost black. They are perfectly camouflaged. Motionless, they are just a feature of the landscape, a peculiar striation in the stone.

They have no face. The tip of a Weaver is a rounded, featureless dome of the same porous white material, but it is covered in a downy fuzz of microscopic crystalline filaments. This is their sensory organ. It does not see or hear in any way a person might understand. It *feels* the song of the city. It feels the thrum of the spire beneath it, the precise frequency of the wind whistling through a nearby pore, the subtle shift in pressure as moisture condenses on its own "skin." It experiences the world as a symphony of vibrations, a tactile music.

They move with a silent, deliberate grace that is deeply alien. From their elongated bodies sprout between eight and ten pairs of limbs. These are not legs for walking but grapples for climbing. Each one is a thin, chitinous strut, ending not in a claw or a foot, but in a soft, padded disc that looks like a disc of solidified gel. These pads secrete a faint, acidic enzyme and a bonding agent. To move, a Weaver presses a pad against the calcite of the spire, gently dissolving a microscopic layer of the stone to create a perfect grip. It then pulls its long body upwards before repeating the process. The movement is so slow it is almost imperceptible, a fluid, multi-limbed crawl that is more like the flowing of sap than the ambulation of an animal. They leave no tracks, only a surface that is infinitesimally more porous than it was before.

The Weavers are the city’s nervous system and its immune response. They are not its builders, but its gardeners, its tuners, its perpetual maintainers. Their entire existence is dedicated to the perfection of the city’s song.

You might see one paused for days beside a gaping hole in a spire, its filamentous head-cluster pressed against the stone, feeling the dissonance of the wind passing through it. Slowly, painstakingly, it will begin to work. From glands near its limbs, it secretes a thick, calcite-rich paste. Using its delicate grappling pads, it applies this paste to the edge of the hole, adding a millimeter of new material. It will remain there for weeks, months, applying layer after translucent layer, shrinking the aperture until the note it produces is pure, resonant, and perfectly in key with the rest of the spire’s chord. Then it will move on.

Conversely, you might find one working to enlarge a hole that time has clogged with mineral deposits. It will press its pads to the blockage, increasing the flow of its acidic secretions, patiently dissolving the stone, grain by grain, until the air flows freely again, restoring a lost high-frequency whistle to the city’s choir.

They tend the mosses, though they do not eat them. If a patch withers, exposing the spire wall to the hard frost, a Weaver will arrive. It will gently stroke the healthy moss at the edge of the patch with its limbs, gathering spores and microbes on its pads, and then methodically press them into the bare stone, reseeding the garden. They sip condensation directly through their porous skin and derive energy from the minerals they dissolve and the subtle geothermal heat of the planet, conducted up through the spires. They are almost a closed system unto themselves.

They are born from the city and return to it. They do not reproduce. When a spire, through some quirk of its internal structure and the ever-singing wind, achieves a state of perfect harmonic resonance—a chord so pure and complex it can last for a century—it buds. Deep within its core, a calcite nodule begins to differentiate, growing veins of living matter nourished by the weeping water. Over decades, it slowly takes on the long, linear form of a Weaver, until it detaches from the spire's inner wall and begins its pilgrimage of silent maintenance. When a Weaver's long life ends, its internal structure stiffens, its living veins fade to grey. It finds a bare patch of stone, anchors itself one last time, and becomes a permanent rib on the spire's face, a new line for the moss to colonize, its body given back to the architecture it served.

They are solitary and silent. They do not communicate, for their purposes are one and the same: the preservation of the song. Each acts according to the vibrations it feels, an autonomous cell in a planetary-scale organism. To see one is to witness a purpose so vast and slow it is incomprehensible, a living echo of the geology and the wind, a creature whose only meaning is to listen, and to tune, and to be.
