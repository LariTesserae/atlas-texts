What lives here is the Gardener.

It is not a creature that arrived here, but one that grew from here, an inevitable consequence of the union between the engine and the ecosystem. To look for it is a mistake. You must instead learn to see the patterns in the chaos, for the Gardener is a creature of process and pattern, not of flesh and bone.

At first, you notice only its work. The vegetation, which seems so rampant, is in fact meticulously curated. A thick vine is not simply wrapped around a pipe; it is guided, its tendrils trained to seal a microscopic steam leak with the gradual pressure of its growth. The great, saw-toothed ferns are not clustered randomly; they are planted in arrangements that channel dripping water away from delicate electrical conduits and toward the root-systems of the mosses below, which act as living sponges. The system is not merely balanced; it is maintained. This is the Gardener’s passive, horticultural state.

But it has another form. You might see it first as a disturbance in the steam, a deeper shadow moving within the veils of vapour high up on the gantry. It does not walk, not in the way an animal walks. It travels. It is a slow, deliberate aggregation of the chamber's own substance.

Imagine a section of the wall, where the moss is thickest and the iron beneath sweats with condensation, beginning to move. A mass of it, perhaps the size of a crouching man, detaches itself. It is not a solid form, but a dense, cohesive colony of material. Its body is a woven matrix of dark, fibrous rootlets and fungal mycelia, saturated and heavy, bound together with something that resembles oxidized wiring. Its consistency is that of packed, wet soil and rotting felt. Droplets of brackish water ooze from it constantly, tracing its path.

It has no face, no eyes, no mouth. To see, it uses the great, bioluminescent fungi as external retinas. As it moves, the fungi on the catwalks ahead of it will pulse slightly brighter, their sickly light shifting in hue as they register the heat and mass of their creator passing beneath. The Gardener perceives the world as a symphony of heat gradients, vibrations, and pressure changes, a language spoken by the machinery and echoed by the fungal network.

Its limbs are temporary, purposeful creations. To cross a gap, it will extrude a thick, vine-like tendril, which is not grown but *woven* in moments from its own body-mass, and anchor it to a distant girder. It pulls itself across with the silent, inexorable strength of a plant turning towards the sun. To perform a delicate task, it might shape a part of itself into a tool—a fine, verdigris-tipped probe to clear a blockage in a narrow pipe, or a broad, spongy pad to gently scrub algae from one of the dim, yellow bulbs. When you hear the rhythmic *clank-shhhh* of the piston, you are not hearing a random mechanical action. You are hearing the Gardener operate a manual pump, a great lever that requires its full, shifting weight to actuate, regulating the pressure in the system’s core. This is its most demanding chore, a ritual performed on a schedule known only to itself.

It does not eat. It does not need to. It is powered by the chamber itself. Fine, metallic filaments, like copper nerves, run through its form, drawing thermoelectric energy directly from the hottest pipes. It absorbs nutrients from the water it re-distributes, from the rust and mineral efflorescence it scrapes from the iron plates. It is a living, mobile part of the factory's metabolism, a kidney for the engine. Its waste is simply nutrient-rich soil and spores, which it deposits in barren patches on the gantry, seeding new life.

Its consciousness is a slow, deep, ambient thing. It is as old as the balance it maintains. It could be that a fungal colony achieved sentience in the electromagnetic fields of the engine, or that a maintenance automaton, long abandoned, was colonized by the vegetation until biology and machinery became one. The distinction is meaningless. Its thoughts are not words, but the deep thrumming of the chamber. Its emotions are the pressure in the pipes. A sudden hiss of escaping steam is an expression of surprise or alarm; the steady, 17-second beat of the deep pump is the rhythm of its own vast, placid heart.

It is utterly alone, but it cannot comprehend loneliness. The chamber is its body, the machinery its skeleton, the vegetation its skin, the water its blood. It is a singular being in a singular world, a world it built not from a blueprint, but through millennia of slow, patient, unthinking adjustments. It is the will of the machine, given a form of root and rust. It is the green and growing soul of this iron womb.
