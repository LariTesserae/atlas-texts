The beings who live here are the Weavers. Some call them the Still-Folk, or the Hush-Minds. They are the architects of quiet.

To see one is a rarity, a fleeting impression against the vast, humming canvas of the Pellum. They are tall and exquisitely gracile, built like reeds that have learned to walk. Their limbs are long and thin, jointed at angles that seem too sharp, giving their movements a delicate, almost insectile precision. They walk with a careful, gliding gait, their feet broad and splayed with wide, membranous toes that distribute their weight so perfectly they seem to float upon the spongy surface of the world-mat. They leave almost no impression, their passage a secret whispered to the ground.

Their bodies are covered not in skin or scales, but a fine, dense pelt like sheared velvet, muted in color to match the Pellum itself—the ochre of new growth, the deep rust-red of decay, the bruised purple of the deep fibers. This camouflage is their first defense, rendering them all but invisible when they stand still, which they often do for long, unnerving periods.

The head is a smooth ovoid, flowing seamlessly from a long, supple neck. There are no external ears. The sides of their head are sleek and uninterrupted, a biological streamlining against the torrent of sound. To a Weaver, the deafening drone of the plateau is not truly "heard" in the way we would understand it. It is a physical agony, a percussive force they feel in their bones, a blinding, painful glare of vibration that overwhelms all other senses. Their true sense of hearing is somatic; it resides in the sensitive pads of their feet and in the tips of their extraordinary hands. By pressing a hand to the Pellum, they can feel the distant tremor of a Ground-Swimmer's passage, the frantic scrabbling of the grazers within the mat, or the approach of their own kind from a mile away. The air is for pain; the ground is for information.

Their faces are subtle and expressive within their quiet communities. They possess large, dark, liquid eyes, luminous and deep, adapted for the twilight of a Hush-Mound's interior or the dense white-out of the cloud-fogs. Often, when they must traverse the open plains, these eyes are little more than sorrowful slits, squinted against the sensory assault. They have small, soft mouths, but their language is not one of shouting.

Communication is a two-fold art. Out on the thrumming surface of the world, they are silent. They speak to one another through touch and through the ground itself. They use a complex language of percussive taps and drawn lines on the Pellum's skin—a seismic sign language that is patient, rhythmic, and utterly lost to any observer who cannot feel the ground as they do. Their hands are their other voice. They are oversized, with six or seven long, bony, impossibly nimble fingers. They weave intricate gestures in the air, a silent dance of meaning that is as complex and nuanced as any spoken tongue.

It is only within the sanctum of a Hush-Mound that their other language emerges. The walls of woven Pellum drink the world’s roar, leaving only a distant, foundational hum, like the memory of a heartbeat. Here, in the dim, tranquil quiet, the Weavers un-stoop their shoulders. Their tense frames relax. And they speak. Their voices are astonishingly soft, a series of melodic, resonant hums, gentle clicks, and sibilant whispers that would be utterly consumed by the world outside. Their spoken language is a sacred thing, an art form reserved for the silence they manufacture. It is the language of family, of stories, of teaching the young how to weave, of passing down the locations of chert deposits or the subtle signs that a Hush-Mound is nearing the end of its life.

A Weaver’s life is a pilgrimage between these oases of peace. A clan might tend to a dozen mounds spread across a thousand square miles, a constellation of silence in a universe of noise. They are not farmers, for the Pellum provides. They nibble at certain sweet, succulent fibers and harvest the iridescent grazers, trapping them in woven nets. They are not great hunters; they are too slight, too gentle. Their relationship with the violent hunters of the under-mat is one of pure avoidance, their seismic sense giving them ample warning to stay far away from those sudden, catastrophic eruptions of violence.

Their psychology is entirely shaped by this duality. The outside world, the "Great Thrum," is a place of necessity, of trial. It is the ocean to be crossed. They are alert, tense, and purely functional while in it. The silence of the Mounds is their only true reality. It is where life is truly lived. History is not a record of wars or kings, but a catalog of mounds: their weaving, their flourishing, and their eventual decay and return to the chaotic growth of the Pellum. Spirituality is the worship of silence itself—the conscious, crafted absence of the world's defining feature. To be born is to emerge into a pocket of quiet; to come of age is to first step out into the terrifying roar and learn to endure it; to die is, perhaps, to finally become one with the silence they so cherished, or to be subsumed back into the sound they spent their lives trying to escape. They are the negative space of their world, a people defined not by what they add, but by the precious, vital nothingness they so painstakingly create.
