They are the Sinter-Folk, though they have no name for themselves. The concept of a name, a unique identifier for a discrete self, is as foreign to them as shade. They are not so much inhabitants *of* the city as they are its mobile, sentient components, the slow, thinking marrow in its ceramic bones.

To see one is to almost miss it. At rest, it is indistinguishable from the infrastructure. Its form is tall and unsettlingly thin, a bipedal sculpture of the same dark, iron-rich ceramic as the pylons. Its skin—if it can be called that—is not a soft tissue but a complex, inorganic integument, mottled with the same deep rusts and verdigris greens that stain the junctions of pipes. It is cool to the touch, a strange paradox in the sweltering heat, its body a remarkably efficient heat-sink. It moves with a torpor that borders on geological, an agonizingly slow deliberation that makes the dreamlike stride of a Stilt-Walker seem frantic by comparison. When it walks, its broad, three-toed feet make no sound on the causeways, their undersides porous and soft, like acoustic dampening pads, allowing them to feel the thrum and pressure of the systems beneath them.

It has no face. The head is a smooth, ovoid shape, tapering gently to a non-existent chin. Where eyes should be, there are instead two shallow, concave depressions, like thumbprints pushed into wet clay. These are not for sight. The uniform, shadowless glare of the white sky makes optical vision a liability. Instead, these depressions, and hundreds of smaller pits all over its body, are sensitive thermoreceptors. They perceive the world as a complex tapestry of heat gradients: the searing signature of a fresh steam leak, the cooler outline of a dormant pipe, the faint, busy warmth of a cluster of Scrapers, the subtle temperature drop that signals a dollop of condensation about to fall. The world is a symphony of temperatures, and they are its grand conductors.

Their communication is silence. They speak through touch and pressure. When two meet, they press their palms together, or lay a hand on the other's shoulder. In this contact, a universe of information is exchanged—the pressure variance in Conductor Pipe 7-Alpha, a subtle weakening in the travertine foundation near Pylon 92, the location of a particularly rich efflorescence of zinc-salts. Their hands are not identical. One is a thing of exquisite delicacy, with five long, triple-jointed fingers that can weave and manipulate fine crystalline filaments or recalibrate the city's most sensitive pressure valves. The other hand is a fused, heavy mass of ceramic and metal, a biological wrench or clamp, ending in a hardened, adjustable bifurcation perfect for gripping pipes or breaking away corroded sections. They are born, or rather *fabricated*, for their purpose.

They do not eat. They do not need to hunt the Scrapers or pick away at the mineral growths. Their sustenance comes directly from the source. At the end of a work cycle—a period that can last for what humans would measure as weeks—a Sinter-Folk will find a designated junction, a nexus where multiple pipes converge. It will press its plier-like hand to a specific valve, causing a panel on its own chest to slide open, revealing a nest of gleaming, metallic filaments. It will then connect these filaments to a port on the nexus, and for a day it will stand motionless, its body gently vibrating as it draws super-heated, mineral-rich fluid directly from the city's arteries. It refuels, recalibrates its internal chemistry, and deposits its own metabolic wastes—processed into a useful ceramic slurry—back into a maintenance receptacle. It is a closed loop. The city feeds them, and they, in turn, repair the city with their own substance.

Their life is a liturgical act of maintenance. They move through their world with a profound, unthinking purpose. They are untroubled by the steamy haze, for they see in heat. They are not lonely in the silence, for they commune through vibration. They are indifferent to the Scrapers and Stilt-Walkers, viewing them as one might view dust motes or rising damp—a natural, predictable side-effect of the system, to be managed only if they interfere with primary function.

Sometimes, one of the great, armored Geophages from the travertine flats below will manage to heave its bulk onto a low-slung platform, its massive grinding jaws threatening a support pylon. The Sinter-Folk do not panic. They do not fight. Several will simply converge, their movements still maddeningly slow. They will surround the creature and place their hands upon its carapace. They do not push. They do not strike. They simply alter the thermal signature of the platform beneath it, creating an intensely localized cold spot. The creature, a being of pure geothermal instinct, recoils from the unnatural cold, its simple mind overwhelmed not by pain, but by sheer wrongness. It will back away and retreat to the hissing flats below.

They are ancient. When a Sinter-Folk's efficiency wanes, its movements becoming less precise, it is not mourned. It will make its way to a 'recycling' creche, a large vat-like structure humming with geothermal energy. It will step in and dissolve, its constituent minerals and metals breaking down to be reformed into new components, or perhaps a new Sinter-Folk altogether. There is no individual death, only a reassignment of matter. They are the city's antibodies, its engineers, and its children, living out an endless, silent, purposeful existence in the bright, wet heat of their immense, self-sustaining machine.
