There, in the long, slow-setting twilight where the light softens from silver-white to a pale, liquid gold, you might see him. To call him a resident feels insufficient. He is more like a feature of the landscape that has learned to walk.

At first, you would not recognize him as a living thing. You would mistake him for an unusual geological formation, a solitary menhir standing near the weeping base of a Weir. He is made, it seems, of the same rosy, flesh-toned stone as the mesas themselves. The surface of his body is smooth and warm, weathered by the same eons, and you can trace the same milky quartz veins running through his broad shoulders and up his long, thick limbs. When he is still—and he is still for very long periods, sometimes for days—the damp air lays a fine dew upon him, and the coppery mosses begin to creep up his feet, trying to claim him as they claim the stone.

He is the Mason. Or perhaps the Gardener. He has no name for himself, for he has no one to speak it to, and no voice with which to speak it.

When he moves, it is not a biological event, but a geological one. A subtle grinding, a shearing of facets, is the only sound. His joints are not flesh and bone but are composed of the same glossy, light-absorbent black material as the Weirs. They are silent, frictionless pivots from which his terracotta limbs move with an impossible, deliberate slowness. He does not walk so much as he reconfigures his place in the world, one ponderous step at a time, each footfall landing with the weight and certainty of a mountain settling.

His head is not a head in any way we would understand. There is no face, no eyes, no mouth. It is a smooth, ovoid shape of the same rosy stone, its front surface polished to a mirror sheen. It does not turn to see; it simply reflects. In its polished curve, you can see the whole of the Weald: the silent, brilliant sky, the distant mesas floating in the haze, the shimmering backs of a sun-grazer herd. To look at him is to see the world he inhabits.

His purpose is intimately tied to the Weirs. He is their tender and their complement. You might observe him over the course of a week as he makes his way across a mesa to a Weir whose subsonic hum has developed a discordant tremor. He will stand before the black pylon, silent and unmoving, a statue contemplating a fellow statue. Then, slowly, he will raise a hand—a great, blocky slab of stone and quartz—and lay it flat against the Weir's cool, weeping surface.

And then you will feel it in your bones. The hum changes. It deepens, clarifies, resolving into a pure, resonant chord that vibrates up through the soles of your feet and into your very marrow. The Mason is not repairing the Weir with tools; he is attuning it with his own body. He is a living tuning fork, a resonating chamber that remembers the perfect frequency of the world. Water weeps from the hairline fractures in his own body, tracing dark paths down his stone skin, mingling with the water of the Weir he tends. He is part of the same ancient, aqueous engineering.

The sun-grazers are not afraid of him. When he moves through their herds, they part slowly, like water around a stone, their pearlescent hides pulsing with soft, curious shades of an almost-white green. They seem to recognize him not as life, but as a fundamental truth of their world, as inevitable and as present as the stone they walk on and the light they consume. He pays them no mind, his reflective face showing only their own shimmering forms as he passes.

He has no interaction with the hunters. He is neither their prey nor their master. The biomechanical predators, things of shadow and angle, treat him as they would a cliff face: an obstacle to be moved around, a piece of terrain. When a hunt occurs in his presence—the silent explosion of the hunter, the soft *thump* of impact, the extinguishing of a single light—he remains utterly still, the violent, silent ballet reflected perfectly on his featureless face. He is the custodian of the system, not of its individual inhabitants. He tends the flow of water and energy, and the hunt is merely a transfer of that energy. His patience is geological; his morality, if he has one, is the morality of a tectonic plate.

Perhaps he is the last of the builders, a being so old that the distinction between organism and mechanism has eroded away. Or perhaps he is the ultimate expression of this environment, a creature that long ago eschewed the fleeting energy of light to root itself in the more permanent power of the Weirs, becoming a slow, deliberate accumulator and regulator. His thoughts, if he has them, are not words or images, but the low, subsonic hum of the world. His mind is a map of pressures and frequencies, a slow consciousness that perceives time in the eroding of stone and the gradual shifting of mesas. He is the world's memory, made manifest in warm stone and cool, weeping darkness, and when his work is done, he will settle into the landscape and you will not be able to tell him from the rock at all.
