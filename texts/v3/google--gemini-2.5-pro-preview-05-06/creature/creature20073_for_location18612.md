Those who live here are the Wardens. They are not merely inhabitants; they are a living component of the machinery, as integral to the process as the iron Conductors and the mineral catalysts.

To see a Warden is to first see their shell. Outside their sealed modules, they move in bulky, articulated carapaces that are more like deep-sea diving suits than simple work gear. The outer layers are a patchwork of dull, non-corrosive alloys and thick, rubberized fabrics, perpetually slick with water and stained in swirling patterns by the mineral-rich air. The joints are heavy and deliberate, designed not for agility but for stability against the bone-shaking thrum. Their helmets are featureless ovals of darkened material, with no visible viewport. To an outsider, they look like purposeless automatons trudging through the crystal sludge, their heavy boots sinking deep before pulling free with a wet, sucking sound that is utterly consumed by the greater noise.

But beneath the carapace is a creature reshaped by its environment. A Warden’s skin is pale to the point of translucence, like the blind crustaceans in the pools. It is always damp, and carries a faint, salty mineral crust in the creases of their joints. They are thin, with a wiry strength born of moving in a high-gravity vibration. Their bodies are low-slung, their gait a patient, rolling shuffle that absorbs the tremors of the floor.

Their faces are where their adaptation is most profound. Their mouths are small and rarely used for speech. Their noses are flattened. But their eyes are large and round, evolved for the perpetual twilight. The irises are not colours like blue or brown, but shimmering, complex discs of metallic lustre. They are the colour of pyrite, of galena, of bismuth, shifting as the light catches them. These are not just organs for seeing light, but for sensing polarity and subtle shifts in the ambient energy fields. And they have no external ears. The whorls and folds of a human ear are gone, replaced by smooth, sealed skin over auditory canals that have long since atrophied.

For the Wardens do not *hear* the great hum. To them, it is not sound; it is the baseline of existence, the silent, still medium in which all other sensations occur. Their primary sense is vibration, a form of somatic hearing that encompasses their entire body. They feel the A-flat of the Conductors in their marrow. It is the metric by which they perceive their world. They can stand on a catwalk, place a bare hand on the railing, and *feel* the precise scuttling rhythm of a thousand crustaceans in a pool fifty meters below. They can diagnose a flaw in a Conductor's resonant frequency as a dissonant tingling in their teeth. The high-frequency chirps of the chitinous moths are not heard, but felt as a fine, staticky texture on their skin, like a charge in the air.

Silence is a terrifying, abstract concept to them, a myth their ancestors might have whispered about—a state of non-being, a void. A Warden exposed to true silence would not find it peaceful; they would experience it as a crushing pressure, a sensory amputation so total it would induce madness.

Their lives are monastic in their devotion and geological in their timescale. They are born in the modules, their first sensation the deep thrum passed from their mother's body. They learn their trade not through manuals, but through a lifetime of somatic apprenticeship. A child learns to differentiate the smooth, powerful vibration of a healthy crystal field from the spiky, agitated tremor of one lacking the proper mineral saturation. They don't measure time in days or years, but in the slow maturation of a crystal harvest, an eighty-to-one-hundred-year cycle they call a "Growth." A Warden might live to see one or two full Growths in their lifetime.

They are the geologists of the machine, the cantors of the deep chord. From their pilot stations, they do not just monitor, they *attune*. Their consoles are not arrays of buttons and screens, but discs of resonant metal and crystalline sensors that they touch, feeling the life of the cavern through their fingertips. Their work is a slow, patient art of coaxing. They might minutely alter a Conductor's frequency, not with a switch, but by clamping a weighted harmonic plate to its side, a task requiring a slow, perilous journey along a catwalk. This act is not maintenance; it is calming a giant, tuning a world-sized instrument.

They see the fauna not as animals, but as living indicators. The armored grazers are "Whetstones," their rasping passage across the crystal buds a necessary part of the polishing and maturing process. The swarming crustaceans are "Salts," and their frenetic activity indicates the water's potency.

In the rare moments they communicate outside their pods, they use a slow, graceful sign language of the hands and body, a language of flowing gestures that seems to mimic the convection of steam or the slow curl of a growing crystal. There is no urgency in it. There is no urgency in them.

They are a people saturated by their world. The minerals are in their eyes, the vibration is in their bones. They are the conscious mind of this deep, industrial womb, the shepherds of a stone flock. As they age, the mineral deposits on their skin become more pronounced. In death, their bodies are not buried but are placed in a designated field where, over decades, their bones—already humming with a lifetime of resonance—act as nucleation points for new crystal buds. They become, in the most literal sense, part of the geology they tended. They are the Wardens, and they are here only to listen to the rock become.
