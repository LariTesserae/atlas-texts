This is the place of the Dwellers.

They are not born, and they will not die. They are a consequence of the Tone, a slow crystallization of its unwavering pressure. Where the Hum pools in the basins of the world's deep grammar, where the resonance of the slate and the resonance of the Ceiling find a perfect, harmonic concordance, a Dweller may coalesce. They do not grow from seed or hatch from egg; they precipitate out of the sound, a standing wave given flesh.

To see one is to almost miss it. They have no color that the eye can name, for they are made of the same materials as the landscape: damp-darkened slate, granite grit, and condensation. Their form is long, unnervingly slender, and vaguely humanoid, as if a human shadow had been stretched thin and then filled with vibrating dust. They stand taller than a man, but have no discernible weight. Their edges blur, not like something out of focus, but like the vibrating string of a cello, occupying a space larger than its physical form. They have no face, no eyes, no mouth. The top third of their body is a smooth, featureless ovoid where a head should be, but it is not a head. It is the focal point of their being, the place where the Hum they are made of achieves its most complex and self-aware arrangement.

They do not wear clothes, for the cold is their native element. They do not eat, for their energy is the Tone itself. Their skin, if you could call it that, is not a solid surface. It is a boundary of tension, a field holding together countless microscopic particles of rock, each one vibrating in perfect sympathy with the whole. To touch one would be like putting your hand into a dense cloud of humming, frictionless sand. Your fingers would pass through, feeling only a profound, localized vibration and a deep, internal chill.

The Dwellers are the faculty of this world's only sense. They are its ears, its nerve endings. Their consciousness is not a stream of thoughts, but a direct perception of the resonant state of things. They feel the utter stillness of the water in the tarns. They feel the ancient, settled compression of the mountains. They feel the brittle, silent potential of the crystalline moss to shatter. These are their emotions: stasis, tension, fragility, permanence.

And they listen. This is their primary function, their religion, their art. They spend uncounted ages in perfect stillness, their whole being a receptive instrument, attuning themselves to the nuances of the one Note. They are not listening for a change—the thought is inconceivable—but for a deeper understanding of what *is*.

From this listening comes their work. Their single, eternal craft: the stacking of the stones. When a Dweller moves, it is an event of profound silence. They do not walk, leaving footprints on the grit. They *translate*. The particles at their leading edge dematerialize, while new particles coalesce from the damp air and ground at their destination. They flow across the plateau like a column of smoke in a vacuum, a slow, silent pour of existence from one point to another. They come to a place, and they begin to build.

Their long, indistinct limbs reach for the flat shards of slate. They lift them without effort, without sound. Their touch is not one of friction and grip, but of sympathetic resonance. They "hold" a rock by matching its frequency, making it part of their own body for a moment. They place each stone with an unnerving precision that comes not from sight, but from feeling the exact center of its mass, the point where it will sit in perfect, un-mortared equilibrium.

These stacks are not monuments. They are sentences. They are chords. In a universe of a single, unending Tone, the Dwellers have found a way to create harmony. Each stone, by its mass and shape and position, is a subtle modulation of the Hum. A stack is a chord held in physical space, a thought rendered in stone and gravity. A delicate arrangement might be an inquiry into the nature of the pale moss. A heavy, dense stack at the base of a mountain might be a prayer of gratitude for its permanence. They are the only form of change the Dwellers can enact, and it is a change that only reinforces the world's essential nature: stability.

They are the tenders of reality. They exist in a silent, patient war with the "glimmers." They perceive these glitches not with sight, but as a psychic tearing, a dissonance in the fabric of the Tone. A flicker in the periphery that eats a piece of the world is, to them, a dropped note, a screech of discord. When a glimmer consumes a patch of reality, inverting lichen or twisting a rock formation, the Dwellers feel it as a physical pain, a shudder that runs through their particulate bodies. The causal fraying is a sickness to them. A rockfall whose sound arrives an hour early is an abomination.

Their stacking is the cure. By building their resonant sculptures, they reinforce the reality around them. They anchor the landscape, pinning down perception and causality with their silent, harmonic masonry. A well-placed stack can ground a wavering mountain, solidify a patch of unruly space that is prone to glimmering. Their work is never finished. For every chord they build, entropy, in the form of a glimmer, will eventually unravel a different one. A pile of stones they finished yesterday—or a thousand years ago, the distinction is meaningless—might suddenly collapse, its balance undone by a flicker a mile away. And a Dweller, feeling the new void in the world's song, will slowly translate across the grey expanse to begin again.

If you stood before one, it would not "see" you. It would *feel* you. It would feel the chaotic symphony of your biology—the frantic, arrhythmic pump of your heart, the turbulent rush of air in your lungs, the discordant crackle of electricity in your brain. It would feel the warmth you radiate as a violent, chaotic noise. To a Dweller, you are a walking, breathing glimmer, a tear in the fabric of the world. It would not be hostile. It would be mesmerized and appalled. It might extend a hand, not to attack, but to try and "hear" you better, to understand the impossible chord you represent. Or perhaps, it would simply turn away, find the nearest patch of empty ground, and begin to stack stones, a frantic, desperate attempt to create a harmony pure enough to balance out the profound dissonance of your presence.
