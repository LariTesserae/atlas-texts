The beings who live here are the Caryatids. The name is a misnomer, an archaic term for a supporting column carved in a human form, but it has a resonant truth. They are not slaves to this place, nor are they its masters. They are its mind, its hands, and its nervous system, inseparable from the living architecture they inhabit.

To see a Caryatid is to see an echo of the Boles themselves. They are tall and unnervingly slender, with limbs that seem too long, jointed with a slow, fluid grace. Their skin is the primary source of color in the basin, a pale, almost translucent canvas that shows the faint, blue-green tracery of their own circulatory system. It is smooth and cool to the touch, always slick with the same condensation that coats every other surface, as if their bodies are a natural part of the basin’s water cycle. They have no hair. Their faces are narrow and serene, dominated by large, dark eyes that seem to hold no iris or sclera, just a profound, liquid blackness, perfectly adapted to the perpetual gloaming beneath the canopy. They do not blink often.

They move with a staggering economy of motion. There are no sudden gestures, no wasted steps. When one walks the rhizome floor or ascends a grafted staircase, their movement is a form of slow-motion parkour, their long limbs finding perfect balance, their soft-soled feet making no sound. They have a quality of being both solid and ethereal, like figures in a dream that are palpably real but operate on a different plane of physical law.

The Caryatids are the final destination of the Boles' industry. They do not eat in any conventional sense; there are no kitchens, no mess halls, no scent of cooking. Their sustenance comes from the refined, glowing liquids produced in the high burls. They consume it in small, private chambers, sometimes directly through dermal absorption, their skin becoming momentarily luminous as they take in the potent bio-energy. This fluid is the culmination of the entire ecosystem's purpose: sunlight filtered through the canopy, converted by the Boles' immense metabolism, drawn up as sap, refined by the quiet machinery, and finally delivered to animate their consciousness. They are, in a very literal sense, powered by the light of a sky they can barely see.

Their society is one of silent, focused collaboration. They do not seem to speak. Communication appears to occur through subtle shifts in the faint bioluminescence that sometimes pulses beneath their skin, or perhaps through a direct, low-frequency resonance they can feel through the rhizome floor—the same way they can sense the deep, slow pulse of the Bole's life. An individual Caryatid may tend to a centrifuge for a cycle, oversee a section of osmotic filters, or pilot a team of the beetle-like drones on a repair mission high on the trunk. These are not jobs; they are functions, as natural and necessary as a lung drawing breath. There is no hierarchy of labor, only the flow of necessity.

They are individuals. A single Caryatid might pause on a high walkway, its dark eyes fixed on a god-ray painting a slow path across the basin floor, and in that stillness, there is a sense of private thought, of a unique perspective. Yet this individuality is submerged in a vast, collective awareness. Each Caryatid feels the health of the five Boles as a part of their own well-being. A blockage in a sap conduit a kilometer away is felt as a dull ache; a new bud on the canopy is a shared, subtle thrill. Their consciousness is not a chorus of distinct voices, but a harmony of sensation, a shared experience of being the single, sprawling organism that is the basin itself.

They do not seem to age, but rather to weather. Over immense spans of time, a Caryatid's skin might take on the hairline crazing reminiscent of the Bole's bark. They grow slower, more still, until one day they find a final place in a quiet alcove, cease to move, and over centuries, become one with the living wood, their form eventually softening into an indistinct bump in the wall. Their constituent matter is reabsorbed into the whole. There are no graves, only re-integrations.

They are the gardeners of their own bodies, the engineers of their own blood. The Boles are not their home; they are the Caryatids’ communal, macro-organismal form. The factories are their digestive system, the cables their arteries. They, the slender, quiet beings moving within, are its thoughts made manifest—a slow, ancient, and ceaselessly working consciousness living inside a body the size of a city.
