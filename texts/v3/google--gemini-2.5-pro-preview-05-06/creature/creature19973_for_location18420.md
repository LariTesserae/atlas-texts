They are called, by the few who have ever conceived of them, the Weavers. But they do not call themselves anything. A name is a sound, a fixed point in the rolling thunder of the world, and they have no use for such things. They are the still points in the roar, the quiet, deliberate consciousness of the Sunken Canopy.

To see one is to almost miss them. Their physical form is a masterpiece of environmental pressure. They are long of limb and lean of body, all corded muscle and wiry strength, built for brachiation and balance. Their skin is the color of deep shadow, a mottled grey and green that makes them vanish against the lignic plateaus and the fibrous canyon walls. It is a tough, leathery hide, perpetually damp, and covered in a fine, almost invisible down that traps a thin film of moisture, giving them a faint, silvery sheen in the shafts of light. Their hair is not like hair at all, but more like the silvery vine-fiber from which they weave their homes— coarse, strong, and growing in thick, tangled mats that they keep bound close to their skulls with strips of insect gut.

Their hands and feet are their most remarkable features. They are broad and dexterous, with long, grasping fingers and toes. The pads of their digits are not soft but are instead covered in a thick, ridged callus, like the pads of a gecko but infinitely more rugged. These calluses give them a phenomenal, prehensile grip on the smooth-worn wood and the fractured bark-stone. They move not by walking, but by a silent, four-limbed flow, a kind of controlled fall across vertical and horizontal surfaces. They are born climbers, their infants clinging to their mothers' backs with an instinctual, unbreakable grip moments after birth.

But to truly understand the Weavers, one must understand their senses, which have been radically re-sculpted by the all-encompassing sound. They are not deaf. To be deaf would be to be blind and dead in this world. Instead, their entire sensory apparatus has re-calibrated. For them, the ceaseless, grinding drone *is* silence. It is the canvas, the baseline of nothingness. True sound, for a Weaver, is any deviation from that norm. Their hearing is an instrument of breathtaking acuity, capable of filtering the million-layered roar and isolating the one specific alteration that matters: the shift in a flyer-swarm's drone that indicates a change in direction; the subtle, scraping rhythm of a Bark-Grazer that means it is turning; the almost sub-aural *thrum* that precedes the critical condensation of a leaf-fall.

This auditory acuity is twinned with a sensitivity that extends through their entire body. Standing barefoot on the warm, petrified ground, a Weaver feels the world. They can feel the deep, slow pulse of the biological heat in the wood. They feel the distant footfalls of a Grazer as a rhythmic tremor in their bones. They feel the coming of a Predator not just by the sudden shadow and the cone of silence, but by the shockwave of panic that travels through the air and ground, a pressure change their skin registers long before their mind does. They lean against a World-Trunk and feel the slow, geologic life within it.

Because of the noise, they have no spoken language. To shout here is as useless as screaming into a hurricane. Their communication is a silent ballet of gesture and touch. It is a language of hands, a complex grammar of finger-positions, palm-rotations, and subtle pressures when they make contact. They can have entire conversations in the gloom of a canyon, their calloused hands speaking volumes in the dark. They also communicate through rhythm, tapping out signals on the woody ground with a stone or a bone tool, messages that travel for miles as vibrations, felt by any other Weaver with their feet on the same plateau.

They are beings of immense, unhurried patience. A Weaver might spend an entire cycle of light and shadow—a "day"—motionless on a canyon ledge, simply observing. They watch the rivers of iridescent flyers, noting their seasonal paths. They track the slow, meandering pilgrimages of the Grazers whose shed fungal plates they need for armor and vessels. They map the sky-less sky, learning which of the continent-sized leaves weeps most reliably. They are philosophers of a single, immense text: the Canopy itself.

Their society is one of solitary family units or, at most, small "scatter-clans" of two or three families, their tether-huts anchored within sight of each other across a chasm. The concept of a crowd, of a city, is not only alien but viscerally terrifying to them. To be surrounded by so many other bodies would be to lose the feeling of the world, to have one's senses deafened by the proximity of others. Their lives are spartan. A hut contains only what is necessary: hooks and lines for traversing and securing themselves; caches of dried pustule-flesh and smoked flyer-meat; bladders made from Grazer organs to catch the syrupy water of a leaf-fall; sharp-edged tools flaked from the glassy wing-shards of the great predators.

They are not warriors, but they are ultimate survivors. They do not hunt the great winged predators; they follow them. The silence cast by a predator's shadow is a dinner bell. It means a rain of stunned or dead flyers will soon fall, and the Weavers will be there, silently flitting through the gloom, to gather the bounty. They revere the predators not as gods, but as a fundamental principle of the world, a source of both terror and sustenance. The silence they bring is the holiest thing they know.

To a Weaver, the world is not empty space; it is a web of paths and anchors, dangers and resources. There is the "firm-wood" and the "tremor-skin" that covers nests. There is the "weeping-leaf" and the "sun-scalded stretch." Their cardinal directions are Trunk-ward, Void-ward, Up-stalk, and Down-root. To stand on a ledge is not to feel undone by scale, but to read a map of existence. The distant flicker of a tether-hut is not an improbable testament to life; it is a known anchor, a friendly point in the silent noise. They are the synapses of the Sunken Canopy, the quiet awareness living within a body of incomprehensible size, each one a tiny, perfect nerve ending, feeling the beat of a wooden heart and the roar of a chitinous soul.
