The beings who live here are the Carvers. But to call them "inhabitants" is to misunderstand their nature. One does not inhabit one's own bones. They are a part of the Sump-City, a living, mobile component of its geology.

Their bodies are what remain when a thing is scoured by water for a lifetime. They are tall, but it is a height of extension, not of strength. Gaunt figures whose limbs are long and thin, like reeds grown in darkness. Their skin has no color we would recognize, but a pallid, greyish translucence, like river-quartz. It holds a permanent, cool dampness and a faint sheen, as if it has evolved to be the perfect surface for water to pearl and run, rather than to soak and chill. You can almost see the darker map of their veins beneath the surface, mirroring the black mineral that runs through the sump-stone. Their joints are pronounced, knobby knuckles and ankles that look like worn river stones.

They are barefoot, and their feet are the most specialized part of them. They are wide and splayed, with long, dextrous toes. The soles are thick but pliable, covered in a skin that is almost like dense, porous cartilage. They grip the slick, mossy stone with an unnerving Cenozoic certainty, sensing the texture, the stability, and the subtle vibration of the water flowing within the rock beneath them. They move without sound, their weight distributed with a grace that is less a choice and more a fundamental law of their being. A fall, here, is not an accident; it is a failure of attention, a lapse in communion with the stone.

The Carvers do not age in the way a mammal does; they weather. A young Carver has a form as sharp and clean as the newest blocks at the city's apex. Their skin is smoother, their movements possessing a certain quickness. As they grow old, they become more like the city's foundations. The ceaseless moisture rounds their sharp edges. Their skin develops a patina, a mottle of darker grey and faint, luminous green that echoes the verdigris moss. The oldest among them move with an immense, geological slowness. They are often found on the lower levels, not carving, but simply sitting, their forms barely distinguishable from the weeping walls they lean against. It is said they are listening to the city's slow dissolution, feeling the grit of its foundation passing through the water.

They are a species without a voice. Their throats can make no sound, and their faces are placid, impassive masks. Expression is not found in the face, but in the hands. Their language is a slow, complex semaphore of long-fingered gestures, a ballet of silent commands, observations, and acknowledgements. A hand held palm-up might mean *stabilize*. The slow tap of two fingers on the forearm, *the stone is weak here*. A gradual closing of a fist, *end of work, the light is failing*. This silent tongue is as rich and nuanced as any spoken language, capable of conveying engineering principles, communal needs, and something akin to emotion—a shared sense of gravity, purpose, and the quiet awe of their perpetual task.

Their life cycle is the city's life cycle. They do not seem to reproduce in any familiar, biological sense. There are no children, no families. Instead, the Carvers emerge. In the deep, black pools at the city's base, where the dissolved sump-stone gathers as a fine, mineral-rich silt, new life coalesces. Here, where the rumored blind custodians of decay churn the slurry, a Carver gestates. A form takes shape in the silt, slowly hardening, drawing minerals from the water, until it is solid enough to pull itself from the pool. It emerges fully formed, a young adult, with the innate knowledge of the stone and the silent language already imprinted upon its being. They are born of the city's decay.

They eat the city, too. Their sustenance comes from the glass-worms they gently scrape from the watery films on the stone and the soft, fleshy roots of the pale reeds. But their primary nourishment is the water itself. They drink deeply of the city's weeping, their bodies uniquely adapted to filter the high mineral content. They are, in a very real sense, drinking their own foundation, participating in the erosion that makes their upward toil necessary.

To see a Carver is to see a being entirely in its place. Their thoughts seem to mirror the city's state: a constant, quiet awareness of balance, of pressure, of flow, of slow decay and necessary growth. There is no joy in their work, nor is there sorrow. There is only the work itself, the rhythmic *thump… clink* of mallet on chisel. They are building their future and their tomb simultaneously. They are the antibodies to the city's slow sickness of gravity, quarrying the healthy stone above to replace the dissolved stone below. Each Carver is a single, temporary thought in the Sump-City’s long, quiet, and unending mind, a sentence made of stone and water that is never, ever finished.
