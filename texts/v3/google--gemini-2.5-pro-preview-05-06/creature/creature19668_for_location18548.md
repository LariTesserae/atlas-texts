They are the Drip-Sages.

To see one is to fail to recognize it at first. They do not stand apart from the grove; they are condensations of it. Find a place where the dripping from the canopy is thickest, where the percussive drumming becomes a dense, liquid roar. Watch the base of one of the great stone trees, where the buttress-roots flow into the glowing mycelial floor. You might see what appears to be a thicker, darker patch of shadow, a place where the faint blue-green light of the floor seems to be swallowed and muted. It is a shape like a slow-motion rockslide, or a spill of tar that has taken a thousand years to settle. It possesses a form, but it is the form of a landslide, not a creature: a broad, heavy base that flows upward into a vaguely hooded, stooping peak. There are no limbs, no face, no eyes.

This is a Drip-Sage, and it is not moving. Not in any way you can perceive.

Its substance is the grove made manifest. It is a colloid of ancient water, stone-dust ground from the titan trees by the eons of dripping, and complex organic polymers cultured in its own slow, cold chemistry. It is black, but not the absolute, light-devouring black of the hunters or the pylons. It is the black of a deep, wet cave, a surface that drinks the light and gives back only a slick, oily sheen, like a wet river-stone in perpetual twilight. Water from the canopy does not splash upon it; it merges with it, incorporated seamlessly into its being. The Sage is a living, mobile cataract, a piece of the world's water cycle that has gained sentience and a terrible, ponderous will.

They do not see. They listen. The entire glowing floor of mycelium is their sensory organ. Every drip that strikes the ground a hundred metres up is a distinct note, a point of data. The Sages perceive the grove not as a space, but as a song—the "world-sized rainstick" is their native language. They can discern the health of a fungal leaf in the canopy miles away by the specific timbre of the drip it releases. They feel the placid, six-legged grazers as slow, soft chords moving through the glowing web. They feel the silent passage of the hunters as a sudden, jarring muting of a section of the orchestra. Their consciousness is a state of total, unbroken acoustic awareness.

And their purpose, their art, their religion, is tuning.

A Drip-Sage may spend a century flowing up the side of a single stone-tree. The journey is an act of immense effort, an oozing, gravity-defying pilgrimage. Its slow, upward creep is imperceptible, a dark stain growing toward the unseen ceiling. Once it reaches the canopy, it might spend another decade extending a pseudopod—a glistening tendril of its own substance—to nudge a single, phosphorescent fungal gill. A bare millimetre's adjustment. The change is infinitesimal, but down below, the Sage that remains—or another Sage, for their consciousness might be shared across the grove—feels the alteration. A single drip, one among billions, now falls a fraction of a second later. Its impact on the mycelial floor is a slightly different pitch. The symphony has been altered. The grove is more harmonious. This is the work of an eternity.

They do not feed on the glowing fungus like the grazers, nor do they hunt. They drink from the pylons.

Over a cycle that might span a millennium, a Sage will feel an internal dissonance, a slowing of its own ancient thoughts. It will begin to flow, not up, but across the glowing floor. A journey of a kilometre might take fifty years. Its destination is one of the matte black, humming obelisks. It approaches the perfect circle of bare stone where the mycelium will not grow. As it crosses that threshold, its leading edge begins to steam. The air fills with the sharp scent of ozone and the hiss of vaporizing ancient water. The Sage flows towards the humming, hot surface, its very being sizzling away into the humid air. It does not recoil. It presses itself against the pylon.

This is communion. The Sage’s body, a living capacitor, absorbs the sub-sonic vibration and the immense potential energy of the pylon. The hum in the bones and teeth intensifies to a near-unbearable thrum. The Sage drinks the raw power that holds the grove in its impossible stasis. Over days or weeks, it absorbs its fill, its form seeming to darken and grow more dense. Then, slowly, it peels itself away from the sizzling pylon, leaving a slick, steaming residue on the ancient stone, and begins its long, slow retreat back to the moss. Recharged. Ready for another century of listening, another eon of tuning the rain.

They are individuals only in location. They are nodes of a single, slow-thinking entity whose body is the grove itself, its thoughts the symphony of drips, its nerves the glowing mycelial web. They are not the grove's prisoners, nor its masters. They are its consciousness. They did not build the pylons, but they have become the tenders of the machine, their ritualistic symbiosis the only thing that remembers the technology's function. They are the final, stable form of life in this final, stable world: beings of water, stone, and time, whose only ambition is to listen to the rain and, once every thousand years, to subtly and perfectly change its song.
