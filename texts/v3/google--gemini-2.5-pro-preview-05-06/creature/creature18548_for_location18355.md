They are The Slow.

To see one is to mistake it for the landscape. They are a trick of the light in the milky haze, a shape that might be a buttress or a geological formation, until it moves. And that movement is almost too slow to be perceived by a faster eye. It is the ponderous, inexorable shift of a glacier, the creeping groan of tectonic plates. They are beings of geological time, their lifespan measured not in the frantic cycles of the lesser fauna, but in the ponderous respiration of the city-machine itself.

Their form is one of convergent evolution with the stone. They are tall, but their posture is rarely erect. They are more often sprawled across a wall or braced within a conduit, their bodies conforming to the architecture. They are hexapedal, with two heavy, load-bearing limbs at their rear that anchor them to the slick surfaces, and four more slender, multi-jointed limbs at their front for manipulation and finer locomotion. Their feet and hands are not soft, but are tipped with growths of sintered carborundum and diamond-hard silicates, allowing them to find purchase on smooth, weeping stone by micrometrically fusing and fracturing their grip. They climb kilometre-high walls not with speed, but with an absolute, unshakeable certainty.

Their flesh is not flesh, not as softer worlds know it. It is a dense, bio-lithic composite. The outer layer is a shell, a carapace that is grown, not built, incorporating the very metals and minerals of the city. It is mottled in the same shades of oxidized iron, verdigris, and igneous grey as the world around it. Over centuries, this shell grows thick, rounding their forms, blurring the line between biology and geology until an ancient one, resting, is indistinguishable from a natural outcrop. Beneath this mineral husk, their tissue is a dense, fibrous colloid, saturated with the same metallic tang of the air, its purpose to withstand immense pressure and insulate against the deep hum of the world.

They do not have faces, not in a way that is meant to be seen. On a smooth, prow-like head, six small, milky lenses are arranged in a panoramic arc. They do not see colour or crisp form in the perpetual gloom, but perceive the world in gradients of heat, barometric pressure, and polarized light, allowing them to read the weather of the air and the health of a machine from miles away. Protruding from the head are a pair of long, delicate antennae, like a machinist's feelers, which they trail across surfaces. These are their primary organs of perception. Through them, they *listen* to the city. They read the chittering flow of the beetle-tides, the thrum of a distant pump, the harmonic resonance of a causeway under strain. Their world is a symphony of vibrations, a constant stream of diagnostic data.

They are the shepherds of the machine-organism. They are its nervous system, its immune response, its will.

They do not speak with voice; there is no air for such frivolities in the constant drone. They communicate through touch and percussion. When near, they press their antennae together, exchanging complex data in a silent, chemical-electrical rush of information. At a distance, they speak by striking the stone with their hardened limbs. A single, sharp *tap* that echoes into the haze is a warning. A slow, rhythmic drumming, deep and resonant, is a call to convene, a message that travels for leagues through the city's stone skeleton.

They feed as the city feeds. They have no mouth to chew the nutrient paste. Instead, they find the arteries, the great slurry-pipes that lattice the walls. With tools they have fashioned–levers of dense, metallic alloy, chisels of focused vibration–they can unseal or create ports. They press a specialised organ, a recessed socket in their chest or abdomen, against the opening, and allow the ruddy, proteinaceous ooze to be pumped directly into their bodies. They are a part of the circulation, taking their share not as predators, but as shareholders.

To observe one at work is to witness an act of geological husbandry. An ancient one might spend a standard decade tending to a single, colossal valve that governs the flow of nutrient to a plateau the size of a continent. With micrometric patience, it scrapes away the calcified husks of sessile creatures, it chips away the mineral deposits left by centuries of weeping water, it coaxes the beetle-swarms into more efficient feeding patterns by striking resonant frequencies into the floor-plates. Its movements are slow, deliberate, and have the force of a mountain behind them. The work is never finished, and that is the point. They are not building towards a conclusion; they are maintaining an equilibrium.

They are born from the city. In vast, warm, geothermal caverns deep in the core, the city grows them. They bud from living, bio-mechanical matrices, emerging as small, almost soft versions of their adult forms. They are tended by their elders for a century or two, learning to listen, learning the rhythms of their particular sector, before being assigned a territory and a function. They never gather in great numbers; they are solitary by nature, distributed throughout the city as a nervous system is distributed through a body. When one dies, its slow metabolism finally ceasing, it finds a quiet place and becomes still. The water weeps over it, the beetles scour it, and the mineral shell, over millennia, simply becomes another feature of the endless, breathing stone. They do not see this as an end, but as a reassimilation. They were never separate from the city to begin with.
