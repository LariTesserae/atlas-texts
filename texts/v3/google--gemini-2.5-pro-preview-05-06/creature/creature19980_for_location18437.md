They are the **Stillborn**, though their name for themselves is a series of soft, resonant clicks that means, roughly, *Those Who Listen*. The name "Stillborn" was given to them by an off-worlder, a ghost story now, who saw their quiet, unnervingly calm nature and their home in the dim twilight and mistook their peace for a kind of death. The name has an unintended accuracy: they are not born in the screaming, violent way of other creatures. They are grown.

A mother does not carry a child within her womb. Instead, she and her partner select a place on the Braid, a node of soft, nutrient-rich pith, and with their obsidian tools, they make a precise, shallow incision. Into this wound, they place a fertilized seed-egg from the mother's body. Over the course of what would be two years, they tend the spot, pruning it, murmuring to it, guiding the Braid’s flesh to swell and form a translucent, pulsing pod, a living amnion. The developing child is nourished directly by the Braid's lifeblood, its nascent mind imprinted with the slow, deep rhythms of the world-tree. When it is time, the pod does not burst; it thins and becomes porous, and the child, fully formed and eyes wide open, is gently peeled from the living architecture by its parents. They are born of the Braid, and so their first, deepest allegiance is to it.

Physically, they mirror their environment's verticality and gloom. They are impossibly long and slender, with limbs that seem to have one too many joints, allowing them a silent, fluid range of motion. Their bodies are hairless, their skin the color of wet river stones—grey, green, and brown in mottled patterns that shift slowly over their lifetime. It is smooth, cool, and always slick with a thin film of moisture, a personal symbiotic layer that allows them to absorb oxygen directly from the saturated air when their activity is low. This makes them appear perpetually dewy, as if they, too, are weeping.

Their hands and feet are nearly identical: long, delicate digits ending not in nails but in soft, yet incredibly tough, pads of cartilage that can grip the ridged bark of the Braid without a sound. Three fingers and a thumb, and three toes and a gripping "heel-toe," allow them to use all four limbs for climbing with the disconcerting grace of a praying mantis. They do not walk; they *flow* over surfaces, their bodies held low, their movements economical and serene. To see one move is to watch a living vine extend itself.

But it is their heads, and most of all their eyes, that define them. Their skulls are narrow and elongated, with no visible outer ears, only sealed orifices that can flutter open to reveal a membrane of extreme sensitivity. They hear the Braid's internal hum as a language, the scuttling of a climber miles away as a clear signal. Their faces are dominated by enormous eyes. These are orbs of absolute, liquid black, so large they seem to hold the emptiness of the Voids within them. They have no pupils or irises, but in the faint blue-green pulse of the Braid's arteries, a silver, crystalline layer deep within catches the light, making their eyes shine like twin moons. They are adapted to see in near-total darkness, to perceive the subtle shifts of the bioluminescent tides, and to gauge impossible distances across the chasms.

Their society is one of quiet gardeners and patient observers. They live in small family clusters called Nests, which are not built things but hollows they have carefully guided the Braid to grow over generations. A Nest is a collection of smooth, warm, interconnected pods, each one a living room, a sleeping chamber, a storehouse. Their only property is their few, precious tools—obsidian blades passed down for centuries, honed to a monomolecular edge; ropes woven from the sinews of a dead branch; baskets crafted from shed glider-wing membranes.

There is no concept of waste. A creature they hunt for food is entirely consumed: its chitin becomes tools, its meat is eaten, its fluids are returned to the Braid as a nutritional offering. They harvest the great, soft, fruit-like sacs that grow from the Braid, and farm the translucent shrimp-creatures in the pools of weeping water, tending to them as shepherds.

Their language is threefold. The primary form is a series of soft clicks, whistles, and humming vibrations produced deep in their chests, sounds that mimic the patter of water and are easily absorbed by the Braid, keeping their communications localized and private. For distance, or when absolute silence is required for a hunt, they use a language of hands and posture, a slow, balletic sign that is as much a dance as it is a conversation. Their third language is unspoken: it is the direct, empathic bond they share with the Braid itself. A senior member of a Nest, a "Listener," can press their hands and face to the Braid's skin and feel its state of being—where it is growing, where it is hurt, where a predator lairs. This is their map, their scripture, and their oracle.

The Voids do not frighten them; they are the counterpoint to existence, the silence that gives the Braid its meaning. To fall is not a terror but a "dissolution," a gentle return to the whole. The dead are not buried; they are wrapped in a shroud of woven fibers and given back to the Void, a slow, graceful descent into the mist and dark until they are gone. The Stillborn believe they simply become part of the air, part of the water, to be wept back onto the Braid once more.

They are a species without ambition, without a desire to conquer or change. Their deepest drive is to maintain the balance. They are the Braid's immune system, its consciousness, its gentle, tending hand. They prune parasitic growths, hunt creatures that damage the great branches, and guide the water into channels to prevent erosion. In return, the Braid is their home, their god, their parent, and their child. They live within the body of a living deity, and their worship is the quiet, ceaseless, and patient act of living in harmony with it, suspended forever in the warm, dim, silent cathedral of the Grand Braid.
