They are the Calashites, though they do not have a name for their people as a single entity, any more than a hand would have a separate name from the body. They are simply the tenders, the listeners, the woven. They are the conscious thought of the Basin.

To an outsider, their first noticeable trait is their constant, subtle motion. Even when standing still, a Calashite sways, a gentle, unconscious oscillation born from a life spent on breathing wood and shifting liana. Their bodies are cords of wiry muscle wrapped around light, flexible bones, built for grip and balance, not brute force. Their feet are broad, the toes long and dextrous, able to grasp a root or a rope almost as well as their hands. They walk with a low center of gravity, their steps not falling, but being placed, testing the give of the ground or the sway of a bridge before committing their weight. Their skin, protected from the harsh direct sun by the canopy, has a smooth, almost polished texture, perpetually damp to the touch. It doesn't tan but carries a pale, greenish or tawny undertone, like new leaves or wet bark, and is often decorated with intricate, dark-blue tattoos that mimic the root-patterns or the webbing of a spider-crab's legs. These are not mere decorations; they are maps, lineages, and records of skill.

Their senses are tuned to a different spectrum. Their eyes are large and dark, with pupils that drink in the soft, green-gold light of the Basin. They see in shades of texture and moisture, able to distinguish a healthy, load-bearing vine from a deceptively lush, rotting one by a subtle dimming of its sheen, or to spot the tell-tale iridescence of a ripe sun-globefruit deep within a thicket of leaves.

But their primary sense, the one that defines their reality, is hearing. A Calashite's mind is a filter, trained from birth to navigate the auditory storm. They can isolate the sound of a single grub chewing deep within a rotting log from fifty paces away. They can diagnose the stress in a support pillar by the change in the pitch of its groan during a high wind. Their language reflects this. Spoken aloud, it is a complex, melodic series of clicks, whistles, and liquid, vowel-heavy notes that are difficult for outsiders to distinguish from the calls of the native birds. It is a language designed to travel, to cut through the percussive rain of drips and the drone of insects. To them, silence is not peace; it is a terrifying void, a sign of sickness in the Basin. A silent patch of the floor is dead, barren, and useless. True communication often happens in the spaces between words, with an intricate grammar of hand-gestures, postural shifts, and subtle changes in the rhythm of their swaying.

The Calashites live with an awareness of life and death that is fluid and practical, not philosophical. They do not fear decay; they manage it. They are born in woven pods, slicked with the same water that slicks the trunks, and their first sensation is the rhythmic rocking of their home. As children, they learn the names and properties of a thousand plants, a hundred insects, the subtle signs of rot and growth. Their "toys" are living things: the shed carapaces of iridescent beetles, the empty chrysalises of canopy moths, the game of guiding a stream of ants with a drop of nectar.

Their minds are not geared toward permanence, but toward process. They would not understand the concept of a monument built to last forever. To them, beauty is a well-guided branch that produces the sweetest fruit, a knot that tightens under strain, a dwelling-pod that, after a generation of use, can be re-woven into a burial shroud. Their elders are not simply repositories of wisdom, but living libraries of sensory data. An elder can close their eyes and recount the sound of a branch that fell thirty years ago, the taste of the fungi that grew on it, and the children who were conceived under the new light that its fall created.

When a Calashite dies, there is no tomb. The body is wrapped in a soft, fibrous cloth, its pockets filled with seeds and spores of beneficial organisms. It is then taken to the Basin floor and laid in a shallow depression in the humus, often at the base of a young support pillar. They are not buried *in* the earth; they are given back *to* it. To be consumed, to rot, to become the rich substrate from which new life will spring is the final, most honored act of tending.

They are a people who do not distinguish between self and environment. A tremor in the Basin floor is a tremor in their own gut. A new fungal bloom is a change in their own skin. They are the nervous system of this great, green organism, a race of gardeners whose own bodies are part of the garden, living and dying and living again in the constant, wet, noisy, and beautiful chaos of the Calash.
