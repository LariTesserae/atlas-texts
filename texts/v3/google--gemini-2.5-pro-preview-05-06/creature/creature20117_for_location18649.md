They who live here are the world's own thoughts, given form. They are not conquerors or inhabitants in the way a man is an inhabitant of a house. They are more like the deliberate, complex patterns that frost grows upon a windowpane, an emergent intelligence born from the very substance of the place. They call themselves nothing, for they have no language of sound, but an observer might call them the **Scribes**.

A Scribe is a creature of profound quiet and exquisite fragility, yet perfectly adapted. Its body is long and slender, anywhere from four to seven feet in length, built like a calligrapher's brush handle. Its form is segmented, encased in a flexible, chitinous armor the color of aged parchment, polished smooth with a faint, waxy luster. They possess three pairs of limbs. The rearmost pair are thick and muscular, ending in wide, soft, adhesive pads that can grip any surface—the springy vellum ground, the horn-like plates, the vertical, porous walls of the spires—without making a sound. The middle pair are more slender, used for stabilization and swift, spider-like locomotion through the three-dimensional city.

It is the foremost pair of limbs that gives them their nature. These are not hands, but intricate clusters of delicate, filament-like appendages, each no thicker than a thread, tipped with chemoreceptors and microscopic barbs. These are their tools, their mouths, their sense organs, their means of expression. They are almost never still, constantly tasting the air, tracing the texture of the walls, testing the ripeness of a fluid-bladder.

A Scribe does not eat in the way the beetle-shredders do. To feed is an act of art and communion. It will approach one of the great, swollen hut-bladders and spend a long time caressing its surface with its filaments, reading its turgor, its temperature, its chemical composition. When it is satisfied, it does not tear the skin. Instead, it inserts a single, needle-fine proboscis from a sheath beneath its head, a tool of surgical precision. It pierces the waxy hide with a barely perceptible pinprick and siphons the vital fluid, drinking its sustenance and the world's water in one slow, meditative act. It never drains its host. It is a gardener, not a ravager, taking only what is needed, ensuring the plant-well will continue to thrive.

Their intelligence is not individual. They are nodes in a silent, distributed consciousness mediated by chemistry and touch. A Scribe's "thoughts" are waves of complex pheromones released from glands along its segments. A "conversation" is a slow dance of two or more Scribes, their antennae and frontal filaments touching, exchanging intricate chemical data—the location of a new, sweet-fleshed bladder growth, a structural weakness in a spire wall, a resonant frequency that indicates a predator's burrow. The pheromonal storms are their global congress, vast outpourings of consensus and directive that guide the entire civilization.

Their purpose, their entire existence, is the tending of the Vellum Sea. They are the living nervous system of the world-organism. They are architects of the highest order, but they use no tools but their own bodies. A Scribe will crawl into the porous framework of a living spire and secrete a specific cocktail of enzymes and catalysts from its filaments. These chemicals don't build; they *persuade*. They encourage the trillions of microscopic organisms that form the spire to grow in a new direction, to thicken a wall, to open a new ventilation shaft to catch the thermal breezes, or to seal a passage that has become unstable. They sculpt their city by guiding its life.

And they are Scribes in the most literal sense. On the great, open plains of the vellum mat, they practice a form of silent, geological history-keeping. A Scribe will drag its filaments across the living ground, not scratching it, but secreting a unique trail of biocatalysts. In its wake, the mat responds over days and weeks. The texture changes. The color deepens from ochre to a rich, permanent umber. They write in the skin of their world. These are not letters or words, but vast, intricate glyphs, fractal patterns that encode knowledge. A pattern might represent a lineage of a particular bladder-plant, another might be a star-chart mapping the slow drift of the sun, and another a warning, a memory of a blight that swept through a region a millennium ago, written into the ground for all future generations to read with their sensitive feet and filaments. They walk upon their libraries.

When a Scribe's life runs its course, there is no ceremony, only a final act of belonging. It finds a quiet place, often a new area of ground that needs strengthening, and it simply ceases. Its body becomes still, its internal fluids congealing. Other Scribes, or the simpler scavenging organisms, do not consume it. Instead, they gently nudge and cover it with secretions that accelerate its fossilization. The Scribe's body, its parchment-like shell and all the knowledge it carries in its chemical signature, is pressed into the vellum mat. It becomes another layer in the biocement bed, another stroke in the living record. To be a Scribe is to live as a thought of the world, and to die is to be committed to its memory, becoming the very foundation on which the future will quietly walk. They are the stillness within the quiet, the deliberation within the growth, the slow, golden, and unending consciousness of the Vellum Sea.
