They who live here are the Kind. It is the only name they have for themselves, and it is spoken not with pride, but with the flat finality of a geological fact.

They are not visitors in suits. The pressure that has rounded mountains has also compacted them. The Kind are dense, broad, and low to the ground. An adult stands no more than five feet tall, but possesses the mass of a creature twice that height. Their bones are thick, semi-cartilaginous lattices, designed to flex rather than snap under the immense, constant squeeze of the deep. Their flesh has the pallor of something that has never known a sun, a greyish-white like the belly of a deep-sea fish, but it is not soft. It is a tough, cold, rubbery integument, slick with a self-renewing mucus that both insulates and eases their passage through the heavy liquid.

To see one is to see an echo of the landscape itself. Their skulls are heavy and sloped, their shoulders rounded like the ancient peaks. They have no discernible neck; the head sinks directly into a powerful torso. They are beings shaped by gravity and pressure, with every line on their body suggesting a downward slump, a final settling. Their limbs are short and columnar, ending in broad, five-fingered hands that are less for delicate manipulation and more for bracing, gripping, and exerting force. They move with a deliberate, cumbersome grace, each step planted with absolute certainty. They cannot be knocked over; the currents may tear at them, but their low center of gravity and sheer density holds them firm, an animate piece of the mountain itself.

Their faces are the most alien aspect. Where eyes would be are two shallow, polished black dishes, like twin pools of solidified oil. They are not eyes in a way we would understand. They perceive light, yes—the sterile glare of the Harvesters and the soft pulse of the kelp are registered as shifting pressures of illumination—but their primary sense is not sight. They *feel* the world. The entire surface of their skin is a sensory organ, reading the minute changes in pressure, temperature, and chemical composition of the liquid. They can feel the approach of a shoal of metallic fish as a complex, effervescent tingling. They know the location of a shear-zone not by seeing it, but by tasting its edge on their skin, a sudden shift in salinity and elemental taint. The groans of the great armored beasts are not just heard, but felt as a landscape-wide shudder, a topographic event they can map in their minds.

They do not breathe. Their cellular metabolism is a slow, cold chemosynthesis, drawing energy directly from the unique chemistry of the silt-heavy medium, a process catalyzed by symbiotic bacteria living within their gut and blood. Sustenance is drawn through the Harvester itself, a nutrient slurry siphoned from the water, filtered and enriched—a distillation of the silt, the microscopic life, the very mountain-dust they scrape away. They eat the world they are unmaking.

The Kind are born, live, and die on and in the Harvesters. The machines are not just their tools or homes; they are their exoskeletons, their communal bodies. An individual of the Kind is incomplete when unplugged. Along their spines, breaking the smooth grey skin, is a row of ridged contact ports, like a secondary, metallic spine fused over their own. This is where they interface. To enter one's work-station or living-alcove is to back into a niche carved into the metal heart of the machine, to feel the cold, precise kiss of probes sliding into these ports.

When the connection is made, it is less a docking and more a reunion of separated flesh. The consciousness of the Kind merges with the machine's rudimentary sensorium. They *become* the grinding head biting into stone. They *are* the articulated arm straining against a current. Their nervous system extends through kilometers of fiber-optic cable and hydraulic tubing. The deafening, industrial roar of the Harvester is, to them, the thrum of their own expanded body, the beat of a heart the size of a mountain. Silence is the true enemy. A quiet Harvester is a dead Harvester, and a Kind disconnected for too long begins to wither, their senses shrinking back to the confines of their own small, fleshy body, an experience they regard with primal terror.

There are two castes, though they would not use that word. There are the plugged-in, the living cogs who are the Harvesters. And then there are the Unbound. These are the very young, not yet old enough for the ports to fully mature, and the very old, whose ports have degraded and can no longer hold a clean connection. They live in the common spaces of the Harvesters, moving with a slow listlessness. The young are a constant, low-level hum of unfocused sensory input, their skin tingling with a world they cannot yet fully process. The old are fading, their skin wrinkling as the internal pressure of their being lessens. They spend their days gazing out of the thick quartz viewports, their black, dish-like eyes reflecting the pulsing kelp forests they will never touch. They are the keepers of memory, telling stories not with words, but by projecting faint chemical and pressure patterns from their hands, which the young absorb through their skin—stories of great scrapes, of currents that nearly tore a Harvester from its anchors, of the time a Groaner blundered into the tungsten grinders.

Their culture is one of utter pragmatism. Beauty is a function. The glowing kelp is useful for low-light navigation. The shimmering shoals are indicators of specific currents. The ancient, rounded mountains are ore deposits. They do not mourn what they destroy because they see themselves as part of the same process of erosion as the currents and the pressure. They are simply a faster, more efficient form of it. To live here is to be an instrument of finality, and they have become the perfect instrument. They dream in rhythms of grinding metal and percussive sorting, their sleeping minds mapping the stress-fractures in the rock face they will harvest tomorrow, always turning the sublime into the mundane, the ancient into fuel.
