What lives here is singular. It is the Custodian.

It is not the builder of this place, nor its prisoner. It is a component, as integral as a gear or a root. You do not see it at first. Your attention is on the grand scale, the chittering rivers of light, the breathing machinery. You only become aware of it when a piece of the landscape detaches itself from a pillar and begins to move.

Its form is one of utter adaptation. It is long, impossibly so, a being of ten or twelve feet, yet it seems to have no fixed height, capable of extending and compressing itself with a boneless fluidity. Its body is a fusion of the Weft and the Machine. Its skin has the same dark, petrified, grey-brown texture as the pillars, worn smooth in some places and grooved with age in others. It is not flesh but a living, woody fiber, cool to the touch and perpetually slick with the same film of condensation that covers everything.

Through this ligneous body run veins of the same dark, corroded metal as the factory’s gears. They are not armor but a circulatory system, and in the dim amber light, you can sometimes see a sluggish, internal luminescence moving within them, a pulse of energy that is not biological but electrical. Patches of green verdigris bloom on its joints, like lichen on a branch, marking the seams where organic and inorganic meet.

It has no face, no eyes in a way a surface creature would understand. Its head is a smooth, tapered ovoid of the same dark wood, featureless save for a series of fine, vertical slits near the bottom. These are not a mouth, but sensory organs. They flutter delicately, tasting the air, reading the chemical composition, the density of the Cinder-mite swarms, the ozone signature of a specific piston. It navigates not by sight but by a total, immersive perception of the chamber’s resonance. It feels the thrum of the Weft through the soles of its feet, the precise rhythm of the distant machinery as vibrations in its metallic skeleton, the flow of the mite-rivers as shifting pressures against its skin. The entire cathedral of industry is its nervous system.

Its limbs are the most alien part of it. There are six of them, long and spindly, jointed like a spider's but moving with a slow, hydraulic grace. They don't end in hands, but in an array of specialized tools. One might terminate in a cluster of fine, chitinous picks, sharp enough to clear fungal rot from a larval basin. Another ends in a socket-like appendage that can grip the rounded, worn edges of a gear to test its torque. A third might split into delicate, metallic filaments that it can insert into a corroded vent, using them to sense and clear unseen blockages deep within the Weft's hollows. It is a walking, sentient toolkit.

Its purpose is maintenance. It is the living regulator of the closed system. The place is old, and things go wrong in subtle ways. The pitch of the chittering might change, indicating a sickness in a new generation of Cinder-mites. A piston's rhythm might falter, its *thump-hiss* becoming a *thump… thump-hiss*, a minute imperfection only the Custodian can perceive.

When this happens, it moves. With geologic patience, it will cross the vast, slick floor, its many-jointed legs distributing its weight, finding purchase where there is none. It might climb a pillar, its limbs finding ancient holds in the bark, moving up into the hazy, glowing mist of the upper vaults. There, it will tend to the fungal nurseries, its filament-tools delicately adjusting the composition of the substrate, or pruning a section of the Weft that is growing in a way that might one day interfere with the machinery.

At other times, it will descend into the machine itself. It will unfold itself into a maintenance crawlspace where the woody flesh has been peeled back, disappearing into the deafening shriek and groan of the inner works. It will brace itself against a living wall and use a limb to apply a lubricant it secretes—a fluid that shares the crushed-flower scent of the air—to a complaining axle. Or it will find a chipped gear tooth and, over the course of a day, a week, a month, gently work it, smoothing the metal with a limb that acts as a polisher, its body absorbing the resonant shrieks until the machine's tone returns to the proper, familiar rhythm.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. It draws its energy directly from the system. When it is still, it will sometimes press itself flat against one of the pillars, and the metallic veins in its body will glow more brightly as it draws power from the Weft’s own life force. It is not a parasite; it is part of the circuit. The energy it takes is what it uses to maintain the whole.

It is solitary, and has likely always been so. It possesses no language, no art, no history. Its mind, if it can be called that, is a map of the entire geode: every gear, every mite, every drop of water is a known quantity. It does not feel joy or sorrow, only equilibrium and deviation. The constant, harmonious hum of the running machine is its state of peace. A discordant shriek is its call to action. It is utterly unburdened by 'why'. The cycle is, and its function is to ensure the cycle continues.

When its work is done, it will retreat. It will fold its long limbs, compress its body, and press itself into an alcove in one of the great pillars, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture once more. It will wait, motionless for decades if need be, a silent, living statue in the warm, buzzing darkness, listening to the great, ancient engine run.
