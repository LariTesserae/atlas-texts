They are called the Tenders, though they have no name for themselves as a people. There is only the Pumping Heart, and them, its living components. To them, the distinction is a semantic one; they are the part of the Heart that thinks.

Their forms are shaped by the endless, dripping humidity and the lack of true ground. They are tall and unnervingly slender, with limbs that seem a joint too long, lending their movements a fluid, boneless quality. Their skin is not the porous, fragile barrier of a surface-dweller. It is smooth, pale, and holds a faint, waxy luminescence, like the skin of a peeled tuber or the underbelly of a grub. It is always slick, not merely with the ambient condensation, but with a secreted mucilage of their own, a biological film that prevents the sodden air from leaching the salts from their bodies. Their coloration is muted, ranging from the grey-green of new moss to the bone-white of a root.

Their feet are their most remarkable feature. They are wide and splayed, with long, prehensile toes that can grip the fibrous, yielding root-mat as easily as a hand. The soles are thick, heavily calloused, and patterned with whorls like a fingerprint, utterly insensitive to sharpness but exquisitely sensitive to vibration. This is how they listen. They do not hear the Pumping Heart's thrumming cacophony with their ears—which are small, simple holes, often sealed by a thin membrane—but feel it through the soles of their feet, the palms of their hands, the length of their spine when they rest against a Vesselwood trunk. They can read the world through this deep, somatic sense, feeling the distant surge of a pressure valve opening, the subtle shift in rhythm that signals a blockage miles away, or the frantic, localized tremor of a rupture.

Their eyes are large and dark, often a solid, unreadable black or a deep, cataracted blue, adapted to the perpetual gloom beneath the canopy. They are recessed beneath a heavy brow-ridge that diverts the constant drips, and are shielded by a translucent inner eyelid that sweeps across with the slowness of a closing flower.

But what truly defines a Tender is the Graft.

At a certain age, when their bodies are strong enough, an adolescent is taken to a node-nursery. Here, a master Lignin-wright makes a series of careful incisions along the youth's arm, back, or torso. A specially cultivated tendril of Vesselwood, no thicker than a worm, is laid into the channels of their flesh. Over a period of weeks, which the youth spends in a feverish, meditative trance, the tendril integrates. It fuses with their circulatory system and nervous system. It grows.

When the process is complete, the Tender has become a true part of the Pumping Heart. A latticework of pale, greenish-brown vascular tissue is now visible beneath their skin, a living tracery that pulses with a slow, steady rhythm in counterpoint to their own heart. This Graft is their connection, their interface. Through it, they have a direct, intuitive understanding of the system's state. They can "taste" the nutrient content of the fluid passing through the conduit they lean against. They can "feel" the photosynthetic efficiency of a membrane high above them. It is a constant, overwhelming stream of sensory data that would drive an outsider mad, but to them, it is the normal state of being.

They move through the vertical world with a slow, deliberate grace, their lanky bodies perfectly suited to climbing the spiraling root-walkways and balancing on humming conduits. Their work is their life, and it is a ceaseless, patient tending. They carry tools grown from the same materials as their world: pruning hooks made of sharpened, resin-hardened bone-wood; scrapers made from the shells of old hab-pods to clear away choking fungal blooms; delicate probes of living, nerve-tipped vine used to diagnose the internal health of a Vesselwood pipe.

They do not speak often. The roaring, gurgling soundscape makes vocal communication difficult. Instead, they use a slow, complex language of hand signs, body postures, and direct contact. A senior Tender might lay their hand over the Graft of a junior, not only to guide them in a task, but to transmit a complex diagnostic feeling—a "conversation" of pressures, temperatures, and chemical balances.

They live inside the gourd-like hab-pods, their interiors warm, humid, and smelling of sap and sleep. They do not accumulate possessions. What would they own? Everything they need is grown and provided by the Heart. Their life is one of collective responsibility, with an intimacy that is absolute. They feel the system's health as their own. A slow, sluggish section of the Heart brings a lethargy upon the Tenders who live there. A sudden rupture sends a jolt of sympathetic, bio-electric pain through the Grafts of all Tenders for miles around.

When they die, there is no ceremony of mourning, only one of return. The body is taken to a decomposition alcove, a place where the janitorial fungi are most active. They are laid upon the root-mat, and within hours, their form softens, breaks down, and is reabsorbed into the substrate. They are returned to the Pumping Heart not in spirit, but in substance. To the Tenders, this is not an end. It is merely a change of state, the thinking part of the Heart dissolving back into the humming, working, weeping whole. They were a momentary, self-aware node in the system, and now they are again the system itself.
