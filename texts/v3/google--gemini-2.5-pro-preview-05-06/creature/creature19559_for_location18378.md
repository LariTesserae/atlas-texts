They are the principle of the place, given form. They are so much a part of the landscape that to see one is not to discover a creature, but to suddenly understand that a piece of the architecture is, and has been, alive. They are the Attendants.

Their existence is announced not by sight, but by a subtle shift in the resonant hum that permeates the pillar-forest. The deep, bass thrum of the machines is the baseline, the steady heartbeat of this world. But sometimes, a single thread of that vibration will detach, moving with a languid, purposeful grace through the water and air. You feel it as a localized pressure in your bones, a focused node in the ambient thrum, a silent chord being played just for you. This is the Attendant, moving.

To see one is a slow revelation. You might be watching the patient, scraping progress of a metallic snail up a column of black basalt. Your eyes adjust to the subtle gradations of grey on grey, the greasy sheen of the lithovoric scurf, the glint of the snail's crystalline shell. And then you realize that the pillar *next* to it is not shaped quite right. It has a stillness that is different from the mineral stillness around it. It is the stillness of a coiled spring, of held breath.

And then it moves. It does not walk. It *unfurls*.

What you thought was a slender, fused cluster of pillars reveals itself to be a single being. It is impossibly tall and thin, eight or nine feet in height but no wider than a man's shoulders. Its body is segmented, like the predator-eels, but where the eels are soft and fleshy, the Attendant is articulated and rigid. Its exoskeleton is made of the same material as the pillars—a dark, vitrified slag, polished to a glossy, wet-black sheen. It is a creature of living basalt.

It has no face. The segment that serves as its head is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless and reflective, mirroring the dim twilight in a distorted, warped pane. There are no eyes, no mouth, no visible sensory organs at all. Its senses are the environment itself.

From its central torso, at the joints of its segments, sprout multiple limbs. They are not legs for ambulation but delicate, multi-jointed appendages that look like spun-glass instruments or the legs of a celestial spider. They end not in claws or hands, but in fine, needle-like tips or sensitive, flattened pads. These limbs are its interface with the world. It moves by bracing them against the surrounding pillars, unfolding and refolding its body to glide through the tight-packed colonnade with a slow, noiseless grace that is unnerving. It never touches the silty floor. It lives its life in the vertical world of the pillars, a creature of the in-between spaces.

The Attendants are the gardeners, the engineers, the priests of this deep, dim cathedral. Their purpose is equilibrium. One might be seen clinging motionless to a pillar for hours, its fine-tipped limbs resting gently on the mineral-scurf, "tasting" its composition, feeling the microscopic life within it. It is sensing the health of its garden. It may then glide to one of the low, humming machines submerged in the water.

This is where their nature becomes truly alien. An Attendant will brace itself over a machine, its form a dark arch against the gloom. From a previously sealed slit in its underbelly, another
specialized limb will emerge—not a delicate feeler, but a thick, armoured cable of its own flesh, tipped with what looks like a bundle of corroded copper wires. It will plunge this appendage into a corresponding socket on the ancient machine's casing. The connection is seamless, organic. The light on the machine might flicker, shifting from blue to amber. The resonant hum deepens, its frequency modulating slightly. The deep, ten-second *thump* of the internal piston might slow to twelve, or quicken to eight.

The Attendant is not using a tool. It has become part of the machine's circuit. It is communing with it, adjusting its processes from within, feeling the flow of atmosphere and water through its own distributed consciousness. It is managing the output of the nutrient-rich effluent, calibrating the very foundation of the food web it oversees.

They do not eat in any way we would recognize. The eel-predators and the mineral-snails are their livestock, but they are not harvested for meat. The Attendants seem to be cultivating the entire system for a purpose much more abstract. Perhaps they harvest the perfect, crystalline lattices of the snails' shells after they die, collecting them as a farmer might collect pearls. Perhaps the resource they and the machines are truly after is something intangible—a specific resonant frequency, a particular isotopic signature from the water, filtered and refined over centuries.

Their consciousness does not seem to reside in the individual. They are more like nodes in a single, overarching mind, a mind that encompasses the pillars, the water, the scurf, and the machines. Their slow, liturgical movements are the physical expression of this shared, geological awareness. They don't communicate with sound, but with modulated vibrations, with minute changes in the nutrient flows, with the silent language of a system in perfect, patient balance.

To be in their presence is to be utterly ignored, to be as insignificant as a mote of dust or a single bead of condensing water. They are the living expression of the place's will: slow, patient, silent, and entirely self-contained. They are the gardeners in the dark, tending a crop of stone and light for a harvest that will not be ready for another ten thousand years. They are what lives here, and in a very real sense, they *are* here.
