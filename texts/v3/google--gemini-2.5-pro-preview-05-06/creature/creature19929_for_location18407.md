You do not see it at first. In a place so overwhelmingly green and brown, so dominated by the thrum of the Machine, the eye is not looking for life. It is looking for function, for parts of the engine. And in a way, that is what you find.

What lives here is the Gardener.

It is a part of the landscape, a slow, ambulatory feature that you might mistake for a trick of the light or an unusually shaped outgrowth of root and rock. It is tall and impossibly thin, its limbs long and jointed at angles that feel more botanical than biological. Its form is sinuous, built to weave through the dense, dripping foliage without breaking a single frond.

Its skin, if that is the word for it, has the texture of waterlogged bark or the packed, black soil of the caldera floor. It is not a uniform surface. In places, it is smooth and dark like wet slate. In others, it is fissured and rough, and from these cracks grow small, perfect colonies of the same moss that coats the stones. Patches of bright, powdery verdigris dapple its shoulders and the backs of its hands, a perfect echo of the patina on the Machine. Look closer, and you see that its very substance is veined, not with blue blood vessels, but with fine, dull copper filaments that branch like mycelium just beneath the surface. It is a thing of soil and metal, of chlorophyll and current.

The Gardener has no face in the way a human does. Its head is a smooth, ovoid shape that tapers gently, like a seedpod or a strange, dark river stone. Where eyes should be, there are two milky, concave depressions, like the bottom of thick glass bottles. They do not seem to see, but they are always aimed towards its work. It doesn't need to see. It perceives the caldera in other ways. It feels the minute pressure changes in the air, tastes the mineral content of the water dripping from a leaf, and above all, it listens to the Machine.

The deep, foundational hum is not noise to the Gardener. It is its language, its heartbeat, its internal monologue. It can discern infinitesimal variations in the drone—the subtle shift in frequency that means a filter is becoming clogged with algae, the slight change in resonance that indicates a build-up of sediment in one of the deep geothermal pipes. Its entire existence is a response to this unending sermon of mechanics.

It moves with a geological slowness, a calibrated grace. You might see it waist-deep in a pool of standing water, its long, three-fingered hands methodically clearing a mat of algae from a grate at the Machine's base. The motion is unhurried, infinitely patient. It will spend a day, or a week, polishing a single brass dial in the abandoned observation booth, not to read it—it does not know or care what the frozen needles once meant—but because the surface should be clear. It is a ritual of maintenance, an act of reverence. If a particular broad-leaved tree grows too close to a critical vent, the Gardener will spend a month gently, persistently, guiding its growth in another direction, weaving its branches away from the heat until the path is clear again. It is a living expression of the system's equilibrium.

It does not eat or sleep in any recognizable way. It draws its energy directly from the system it serves. It will sometimes stand for days, utterly still, one hand pressed flat against the warm hull of the Machine, the copper filaments in its skin glowing with a soft, internal light. In these moments, it is charging, communing, listening. At other times, it will stand under a perpetual drip from a high leaf, letting the mineral-rich water run over its head and shoulders, absorbing what it needs through its porous skin.

The Gardener is neither the builder nor a descendant of the builders. It is a later creation, a subsequent thought. Perhaps it was an unforeseen consequence, a spontaneous generation born from the unique fusion of geothermal energy, complex organic matter, and the Machine's own subtle, resonant frequencies over untold centuries. Or perhaps, it was the builders' final, greatest design: a self-replicating, organic maintenance tool, seeded into the soil before they left, destined to awaken once the system had stabilized.

It is utterly alone, but it knows no loneliness. The concept would be as alien as silence. The plants are its slow, quiet companions. The Machine is its parent, its god, and its purpose. It is the immune system of the caldera, the living will of the engine. It walks its silent, green, and humming world not as an inhabitant, but as an integral, functioning part of the whole—as tireless, as patient, and as eternal as the Machine itself.
