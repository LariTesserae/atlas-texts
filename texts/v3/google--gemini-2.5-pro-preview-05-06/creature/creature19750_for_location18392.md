They are the Tenders, though they have no name for themselves. The concept of a collective identity is as lost to them as the memory of a stable sun or a straight line. They are the quiet, inevitable consequence of Odem, grown from its substrate as surely as the resin-walled factories.

To see one is to almost miss it. Against the vast organ-towers and the sweeping, bruised purple of the hololith, they are little more than vertical strokes of shadow, a momentary interruption in the glistening landscape. They are tall, unnervingly so, with a gauntness that speaks not of starvation but of efficiency. Their limbs are long and slender, their movements possessed of a slow, jointless fluidity, like kelp turning in a deep current. They do not walk so much as they flow across the living ground, their pace unhurried, as if they know that haste is a lie in a world where distance is a variable.

They are encased, not clothed. What they wear is a form of secondary skin, a carapace woven from the fibrous ligaments that hold Odem together. This suit is not put on; it seems to have grown with them, or onto them, its texture somewhere between hardened leather and the bark of an insect. It is the same muted purple-grey as the world-pelt, but slicker, darker, as if perpetually wet. It is form-fitting, outlining the impossible thinness of their bodies, a frame of bone and minimal sinew. The suit is a complete seal, an isolation. No skin is ever exposed to the damp, nutrient-heavy air.

Their heads are the most alien feature. They possess no face. The carapace rises smoothly from the collar, forming an ovoid helm, featureless save for a single, broad lens of a material like clouded amber that wraps around the front. This is not glass; it is a slab of the same translucent resin that forms the factory windows, polished by years of existence. It betrays nothing of what lies behind it. There are no eyes to be seen, no mouth, no hint of expression. It is a sensory plate, designed to perceive the world not through focused light, but through the overwhelming vibrations and chemical shifts that define Odem. Through this plate, they *feel* the city's thrumming heartbeat as a physical presence, they *taste* the subtle change in the vapor that signals a metabolic imbalance a kilometer away, and they *see* the world as a shimmering, heat-and-pressure map.

The Tenders are Odem's quiet antibodies, its somnambulant physicians. They carry tools, but these too are grown things, not forged ones. One might carry a long, crescent-bladed polearm, not for combat, but for scraping choral-like mineral deposits from the inner walls of a great artery. Another might hold a device like a many-pronged tuning fork, which they press against the chitinous hull of a factory-heart, its hum a counter-frequency to soothe a violent arrhythmia. They work in profound isolation. One might see another Tender on a distant ridge, a lonely silhouette engaged in a similar, incomprehensible task, but they do not convene or communicate. Their coordination is reactive, guided by the world-organism itself. A blockage occurs, and a Tender is simply… drawn there, compelled by a pressure change they feel in their bones, a sour note in the endless industrial hum.

They do not eat or drink in any conventional sense. Their sealed carapaces are porous on a microscopic level, designed to absorb the ambient, protein-rich steam exhaled by the spiracle vents. They are sustained by the world's breath, metabolizing its feverish exhalations. They are, in this sense, parasites, but parasites that work to keep their host alive, a living dialysis machine for a body that is poisoning itself.

Their inner life, if it can be called that, is a mystery. In a place where cause and effect have decayed, memory is a liability. They do not seem to possess it in a narrative sense. They do not recall yesterday's work or plan for tomorrow's. Their knowledge is instinctual, cellular, a set of inherited rituals encoded into their being. They know how to soothe a spasming valve in the same way a spider knows how to weave its first web. Their existence is a state of pure, continuous presence, a meditative focus on the symptom directly before them. They are not sad or content; they are merely occupied.

When a Tender's function ceases, it simply stops. It might stand motionless for days on a high plateau, a new statue in the shifting landscape, until the world's slow cellular processes eventually reclaim it, dissolving it back into the hololith from which it grew. And somewhere else, in a quiet, uterine fold of the city's architecture, a new one will finish growing, detach itself, and begin its slow, fluid walk toward the nearest discordant thrum. They are not a people. They are a process, the last, lingering shred of logic in a world deep in the throes of a dream, trying to smooth the blankets on a restless, continent-sized god.
