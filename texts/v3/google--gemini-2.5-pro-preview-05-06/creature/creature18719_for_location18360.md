The beings who live here are called the Dwellers, though they have no name for themselves. The concept of a separate identity, distinct from their function and their home, is as alien to them as the forgotten sun. They are the human component of the machine, the sentient expression of the geological factory.

They are beings of accretion. Their bodies, like the walls, are things that have grown slowly, layered and patient. A Dweller's skin is not soft flesh, but a pale, waxy, semi-translucent integument, slick with the same briny condensation that coats the pipes. It has the mottled, milky quality of the nascent quartz, with grey, calloused patches over the joints like dull calcite deposits. It is cool to the touch, despite the oppressive heat, a feature of their biology which uses the temperature differential to help process the thick air they breathe. They do not sweat; the air is too saturated. Instead, the humidity of the chamber simply beads on their skin and runs in slow rivulets down their limbs.

They are tall and impossibly thin, their forms defined by a wiry strength that comes not from bulging muscle but from dense, fibrous tissue, evolved for slow, constant effort and endurance. They move with a profound economy of motion, a liquid deliberation that seems to perfectly match the ponderous rhythm of the pumps and the slow weeping of the walls. To see a Dweller climb a service gantry is to watch a tendril of some strange, pale vine finding its way upwards. Their hands and feet are wide and spatulate, the skin on their palms and soles a tough, dark, heavily ridged pad that allows them to grip wet metal and slick, lumpy stone with absolute certainty.

What passes for a face is a placid, unsettling mask. They have no hair. Their skulls are smooth and gracefully elongated. Where a nose would be, there are two vertical slits that flutter almost imperceptibly, membranes within filtering the mineral-heavy, oxygen-poor air. Their mouths are wide, thin, and lipless, rarely opening. They do not speak in complex vocalizations; communication is a series of low, resonant hums that harmonize with the machinery's drone, punctuated by slow, deliberate gestures. Their most striking feature is their eyes. They are large, perfectly round, and possess no visible sclera or iris, appearing as solid orbs of polished obsidian. They do not reflect the sickly orange light so much as swallow it, giving them an unfathomable depth. They blink with a slow, vertical nictitating membrane, a languid wipe that clears away the ever-present film of grit and moisture.

Their primary sense is not sight, but touch, or more accurately, vibration. They are born into the drone, and it is their native language. A Dweller can stand on a metal walkway, bare feet planted wide, and understand the health of the entire system. They feel the subtle shift in a pump's rhythm three levels down, the minute stress-fracture forming in a pipe on the far side of the chamber, the specific frequency of a particular swarm of isopods cleaning a condenser coil. They rest their long, pale hands on the warm, growing rock, not in reverence, but to read its progress, to feel the infinitesimal shifts of its crystallization.

They are inextricably linked to the chitinous fauna. The constant, skittering presence of the arthropods is no more bothersome to them than the feeling of their own pulse. They move through the rivers of oily, black creatures without pause, the insects parting before their slow, steady steps like iron filings before a magnet. The cloud of metallic gnats around a lamp is merely a textural part of the light. They observe the thumb-sized isopods as they methodically scour mineral scale from the machinery, recognizing them as a vital, self-replicating maintenance crew. Sometimes, a Dweller will pause and slowly extend a finger to a passing silverfish-analogue crawling on a control panel, not to crush it, but to let it investigate their skin, its antennae twitching, a moment of silent communion between two different forms of life perfectly adapted to this place. When they eat, they harvest the larger, slower arthropods, cracking their calcified shells to get at the nutrient-rich flesh within, their slow, methodical chewing almost inaudible against the chamber's thrum.

They are beings of immense, almost geological, patience. The concept of "hurry" does not exist. They perform their tasks—calibrating a pressure valve, replacing a corroded filter, extending a new gantry to a recently solidified outcrop—with the same unhurried pace. A single task can take hours or days. They work, they consume, and they rest in their stained habitation pods, their life cycles tied not to day and night, but to the long, laborious cycles of the factory.

An old Dweller is not marked by wrinkles, but by the subtle thickening of the calcified deposits on their knuckles and elbows, and by the depth of their understanding of the drone. They are the librarians of a sonic history, the keepers of a process. They are born in the hot, wet dark, and they will be reclaimed by it, their bodies eventually becoming just another mineral deposit, another layer in the slow, eternal construction of the chamber they serve. To an outside observer, they might seem like prisoners or slaves. But from within, they are simply part of the system, inseparable from the rock, the water, the heat, and the hum. They are the living, breathing, patient soul of the factory.
