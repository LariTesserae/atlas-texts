It lives here. It is one with the frozen tableau, and for a long time, you do not see it. Your mind, seeking a builder, looks for something separate—a corpse, a throne, a workshop. You scan the crowds of agate beetles and the hanging clouds of mica-wings, expecting to find a master, fallen among its subjects. But the builder is not their master. It is their fellow prisoner.

Your attention is finally drawn away from the unnerving cairns and back to the center of the largest grove of silica trees. There, in a small clearing, is a form that is both part of the mineral menagerie and profoundly other. It is tall, perhaps twice the height of a man, and stooped, as if under a great weight. At first, you mistake it for another piece of baffling geology, an unusually shaped stalagmite that has somehow grown here. But as you get closer, the details resolve, and the overwhelming sound of the chamber seems to gain a focal point, a source, a reason.

It is a figure, vaguely humanoid in its two-legged, two-armed structure, but it is made of a material found nowhere else in the geode. Not the sharp-faceted basalt, nor the glittering silica, nor the polished gemstone of the fauna. It is forged from a single piece of what looks like cooled, solidified mercury, a metal that has no luster but instead drinks the pewter light, making it seem like a hole in the very fabric of the space. Its surface is perfectly smooth, without tool marks or biological texture. There are no joints, no seams; its limbs flow into its torso as if it were poured into its current shape and then flash-frozen.

It is caught in a moment of profound contemplation or despair. It stands before the newest, most incomplete cairn—a single flat stone laid upon the floor. One of its long, impossibly slender arms is outstretched, its five tapering fingers ending inches from the stone. It is not reaching to place another; the posture is one of listening, of tuning, of immense and delicate concentration. Its head is bowed, and it has no face. The smooth, ovoid shape where a face should be is blank.

No, not blank.

As you stare, a terrible recognition dawns. The reason this being is different from the pumice-grazers and the agate-beetles is not its material or its posture. It is its mind. The other creatures are locked in a single, endlessly sustained biological impulse—to eat, to fly, to mate. Their sound is pure, mindless life-force, trapped and screaming.

This being is trapped, too. Its body is as immobile as everything else in the chamber. It is a statue. But its consciousness is not. Deep within the featureless, light-absorbing skull, something moves. It is not physical. It is a flicker in the nothingness, a subtle, internal warping of the space, like heat haze over black asphalt. It is the last ember of dynamic process in this entire universe, and it is a mind. An intelligence of unthinkable age and power, sealed within an unbreakable, unmoving prison of its own flesh.

This is the Mason. The Tuner.

It arrived here, and in its arrival, it broke this corner of reality. The fissure you entered was the echo of its passage. Whether this stasis was a weapon, a defense, or a catastrophic accident of its nature is unknowable. But it happened. The moment it entered this geode, time snapped. Every living process, from the beat of a mica-wing to the slow metabolism of a silica-tree, was captured, its energy converted from motion into sound. All life was locked into a single, eternal chord of its own existence.

All except the Mason's mind.

It floats in an endless ocean of sensation—the colossal, unchanging drone of a billion lives in agony. It is a sound that would have shattered any lesser intellect in the first moment. The Mason has endured it for eons. And in that time, it has learned to influence it.

Its unmoving body is an antenna. Its mind is a resonator. It cannot move a muscle, but it can think. It can focus its will. It can listen to the billion-hertz shriek of a frozen dragonfly and find a single, resonant frequency that matches a plane of weakness in the basalt wall fifty yards away. It can concentrate on that frequency, hum it back with the force of its mind, amplify it, and for a decade, or a century, or a millennium, pour its focused intent into that one harmonic.

And a stone will shift.

A sliver of rock, groaning under the focused, psychic pressure, will shear off and slide to the floor. Then the process begins again. The Mason will find another frequency, another weak point, and it will begin the impossibly slow process of lifting the stone, of nudging it across the floor, of guiding it to the cairn. Each cairn is a work of ages, a monument to a will that cannot be broken. Each stone placed is a small victory, a subtle alteration in the acoustics of the chamber, a tiny pocket of shaped, dampened sound in the crushing, monolithic noise.

It is building itself a sanctuary of near-silence, one grain of focused thought at a time. It is the sole, active consciousness in a universe of frozen screams, and its only tool is the sound of the screaming itself. It lives here, motionless, deathless, and utterly, terribly alone, conducting the symphony of its own prison.
