The inhabitant does not arrive. It is already here, in the same way the damp is here, and the sound. You only become aware of it as a change in the pattern. A subtle dissonance in the otherwise perfect, crushing rhythm of the basin.

At first, you feel it as a localized silence. A small patch of the ever-present organic thrumming goes quiet, as if muted. You turn toward this pocket of unnatural stillness, and you see it resolve itself from the landscape. It does not rise from the floor so much as the floor *gathers*. A section of the mycelial carpet, a few yards away, begins to thicken and pull upward. The interwoven roots and fungal strands writhe, contracting and weaving themselves into a denser form. It is the action of a muscle tensing, but on the scale of the environment itself.

What stands there is a figure, and yet it is not a creature. It has the general silhouette of something tall and gaunt, perhaps eight feet high, but it has no anatomy you can name. It is a temporary sculpture of the basin's own substance. Its body is the same fibrous, cork-like flesh of the ground, packed into a humanoid shape. Through its torso and limbs run the same lighter-green capillaries, pulsing with a slow, internal light that is slightly out of sync with the grander heartbeat of the room. Its surface is slick, weeping the same clear, viscous fluid as the nodules on the floor, leaving a glistening trail as it moves.

It has no face. The head is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless except for a single, circular plate of dark, polished material set into its front. It could be obsidian, or cooled slag, or chitin. It doesn't seem to be an eye; it reflects the diffuse green light with a perfect, depthless sheen, showing you only a distorted, tiny image of yourself.

Its arms are long, too long, and end not in hands but in clusters of fine, twitching filaments, like the delicate hyphae of a fungus but with the tensile strength of spun metal. They move with a slow, hydraulic grace, independent of one another. The being glides rather than walks, its base remaining a part of the floor, a spreading root-ball that flows over the living ground, never truly detaching from it. It is less an individual walking through a place and more a wave passing through the medium of the place itself.

As you watch, paralyzed, it moves toward one of the great, throbbing machines. It does not hurry. Its motion is synchronized to the slow **THUMP-shhhhhhh** that is the basin's breath. It is a part of the process, an agent of the equilibrium.

It stops beside the massive, half-submerged flywheel. It raises one of its long arms, and the filaments of its "hand" spread wide. It lays them against the turning iron. There is no sound of contact, just a merging. The filaments flow over the blackened, slime-coated metal, sensing its vibrations, its temperature, its integrity. For a long moment, it is still, a living sensor applied to a dead machine. The light in its own veins flickers, synchronizing with the pulse of the vines wrapped around the axle. It is communing with the engine, feeling its ancient, tireless labor.

Then, it acts. From its other arm, a single, thicker tendril extends, tapering to a needle-sharp point that hardens and gleams like new copper. It reaches into the intricate tangle of pipes and plant-tissue near the piston. With surgical precision, it pierces a swollen, amber-colored sac—one of the gauge-globes—that has grown clouded and dark. It works the needle with minute, deliberate movements. It isn't destroying it; it is performing a kind of acupuncture, clearing a blockage. As it withdraws, the amber sap within the gauge flows freely again, its inner light brightening, the quivering needle behind it steadying.

Its work done, the copper-colored tool retracts, melting back into its arm. The being holds its position for one more cycle of the great piston's hiss, a final diagnostic moment. Then, it begins to move away.

It flows past your position. Its featureless faceplate turns toward you as it glides by, and for a heart-stopping second, your own terrified reflection is all you can see. There is no recognition. No curiosity. No malice. It is the blank, indifferent scan of a security camera, the passive registration of a foreign object by an immune system that has not yet decided if you are a threat or merely debris. You are a loose microbe, a fleck of rust, insignificant to its grand, geological task.

Then it is past you. It does not look for a place to rest or a door to exit. It simply… stops. The cohesive form begins to loosen. The dense weave of its body softens, the fibers unlacing, the internal light fading. It sinks, melting back into the living floor from which it came. The humanoid shape dissolves into a low mound, which then flattens and spreads until it is once again indistinguishable from the writhing, pulsing substrate beneath your feet.

The small silence it created is gone, filled once more by the overwhelming thrum of the basin. Nothing has changed. The machine thumps and hisses. The leaves drip. The air is thick and green. You are alone again. But you know now that you were never alone at all. The gardener is always present, woven into the very fabric of the garden. You are just a weed. And it has not yet decided to pull you.
