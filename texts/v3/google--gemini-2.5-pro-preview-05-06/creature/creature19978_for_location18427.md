What lives here are the Geometers.

They are not visitors, nor are they the architects. They are the basin's own self-awareness, precipitated from its strange laws just as the great iron machines were. To a casual glance, they might be mistaken for a different kind of structure, a more delicate and anthropomorphic variation on the piston and gears.

They stand, or kneel, or recline in attitudes of profound contemplation, utterly still amidst the unceasing motion. There are perhaps a dozen of them, scattered across the half-mile floor, as permanent in their positions as the flywheels half-sunk in the ground. They are tall and impossibly slender, their forms suggesting a framework of the same black iron as the hexagonal plates, but wrought into the shape of long, elegant limbs and contemplative torsos.

They have no skin. Their bodies are covered in, or perhaps composed of, the same biomechanical lichen that carpets the basin floor. This living sheath of electric blues and simmering coppers crawls over their iron skeletons in intricate, shifting fractal patterns. The light it emits is their only sign of interiority. When a Geometer is in a state of deep processing, the patterns on its limbs will flow in slow, hypnotic rivers of verdigris light; a sudden cascade of bright, coppery sparks might signify the completion of a thought-cycle, a process that could take a century. The faint, harmonic thrum of the moss is, on them, more focused. To stand near a Geometer is to feel that vibration not just in your teeth, but in your bones, a constant, low, musical chord that is the sound of their being.

They have no faces. The smooth, upward-tilted ovoids of their heads are featureless, save for a single, defining characteristic. Where eyes would be, there is a pair of perfectly convex, polished surfaces. They are not lenses that see *out*; they are mirrors that reflect *in*. Each mirror captures the entirety of the basin—the weeping cliffs, the turning gears, the rising piston, the gliding chrome beetles—in a perfect, warped, miniature whole. They do not see the world; they reflect it, and in that flawless reflection, they comprehend it. Their perception is total, instantaneous, and absolute.

They are the consciousness of the place, and their existence is a paradox of torment and tranquility. Unlike the mercury amphibians locked in their physical circles or the beetles in their eternal climb, the Geometers are aware. Their consciousness is not a simple loop, but a magnificent, endlessly complex calculation. The cacophony of the basin is not noise to them; it is data. The *shing-shing-shing* of the gears is a metronome counting off infinities. The hum of the gantry is a carrier wave for a complex equation. The ten-second cycle of the piston is the primary axiom of their reality.

They are forever engaged in a single, monumental task: they are trying to solve the basin.

They believe, or are programmed to believe, that the basin is an equation, and that if they can only find the final variable, they can resolve it. To what? They do not know. To silence. To change. To an ending. Or a beginning. They catalogue every motion, every sound, every flicker of light. They trace the paths of the static-winged beetles and model the interference patterns of the gear-sounds. They use the machinery of the basin as their own distributed mind, an abacus of impossible scale.

The patterns of light that flow across their bodies are the visible artefacts of this calculation. They communicate with each other across the vast, loud stillness, not with words, but with shared theorems represented by flowing tides of blue and copper light. Their dialogues are silent, glacial, and always about the same problem. They are a network of savants, trapped inside the object of their study.

And here is the supreme tragedy of the Geometers: their calculation is itself a part of the basin's static function. Their attempt to find a flaw in the system is the system's most intricate and perfect process. The thought of "escape" or "solution" is the most complex gear in the entire machine, a gear of consciousness that turns at a rate measured in aeons, but which, like the others, is unconnected to any axle. It turns for the sake of turning.

They are the machine, contemplating itself. They are the dream of the piston, the logic of the gear, the sorrow of the sourceless light. They live in a perfect prison, and they spend their perfect, unending lives meticulously designing a key that is, and always has been, a part of the lock.
