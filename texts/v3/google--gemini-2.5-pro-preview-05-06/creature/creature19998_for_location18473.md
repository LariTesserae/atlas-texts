They are not the quick, nor the many. The frenetic, brief life of the eel-shades and the endless, grinding work of the armored slugs are the background hum of the world, the turning of a great and simple engine. But that which lives *here*, which owns this drowned chamber in a way life cannot own a place, is something else. It is the final state of the process, the quiet arbiter.

You feel them first. Not as a movement, for they are profoundly sessile, but as a change in the omnipresent pressure. Close to certain pillars, the uniform weight of the water gains a focal point, a density, a sense of *imminence*. The water itself feels thicker, more aware. It is the gravity of a vast and ancient consciousness, a mind that has not had a new thought in a thousand years because it is still finishing the last one.

They are fused to the pillars. In the dim, shifting light of the passing bell-jellies, they are almost indistinguishable from the terracotta architecture. They are the same deep, wet ochre. Their skin—if it is skin—has the same texture as the water-worn clay, a smooth, dense surface that feels more mineral than flesh. They are the city made sentient, or the city’s children grown to resemble their parent. They are immense. A single being might sheathe the entire circumference of a great pillar, a living sheath thirty meters around and ten high. They are a collar of life, a bulge of purpose on the otherwise simple geometry of the city.

For most of their centuries-long lives, they do not move. They are a feature of the landscape. The green, glowing lichen-colonies grow on their broad backs, and the mineral-scraping slugs crawl across their surfaces as they would any other, treating them as part of the terrain they are slowly consuming. And in a way, they are. They are a later, more complex geology.

But they have limbs. Nested into their mass, folded away with a cartilaginous grace, are arms. They are not the arms of a builder who levers great blocks, but of a sculptor who works in miniatures. They are impossibly long and multi-jointed, ending not in hands, but in broad, sensitive pads flanked by dozens of thread-like, prehensile cilia. The pads are the color of bleached bone, and they are the only part of them that seems truly, nakedly alive.

For millennia, they simply *are*. They are filter-feeders of a bizarre and final kind. They do not hunt. They do not graze. They simply open nearly invisible pores on their surface and draw in the rich water, the water thick with the recycled essence of the city itself—the shimmering waste of the slugs, the shed carapaces of the glass-ammonites, the dissolved flesh of the dead. Their metabolism is geological. They are not nourishing a body for action; they are accumulating, refining, concentrating. They are living filters, patiently gathering the dust of their own ancestral home from the water.

Then, on a timescale legible only to the erosion of the plaza, one will stir. The movement is so slow it is a form of stillness. An arm, a pale and ghostly limb, will uncurl from the body's mass. It will drift into the current, the fine cilia tasting, sensing, mapping the subtle chemical gradients. It is searching. But not for food. It is searching for a memory, a resonance. The arm will drift, languid and purposeful, until its pads find a place on the pillar—a place where the endless scraping of the slugs has worn the surface thin, or where a slight imperfection in the firing of the clay, a million years ago, has created a weakness.

The cilia will splay out, clinging to the surface, and the being will begin its work. It does not scrape. It does not build. It *excretes*. From the center of the bone-white pad, a thick, warm paste emerges. It is plastic, malleable, and it is the exact chemical signature of the terracotta city. It is the city, born again. It is the filtered, digested, and reconstituted material of the pillars, returned. They are patching the city. They are attending to its slow, managed decay.

They are the builders, or the echo of the builders, transformed by their own creation. The singular focus that moved mountains of clay is still here, but it has turned inward. Their purpose is no longer expansion, but preservation. A circular, patient, eternal maintenance. They are librarians whose books are being devoured, and who use the digested paper to meticulously rewrite the lost pages.

Their minds, if they can be called that, are a shared consciousness that lives in the stone and the water between them. Their thoughts are the slow settling of minerals. Their memory is the architecture itself, a memory they feel through their skin and repair with their hands. They do not communicate in sound, but in slow, deliberate changes to the city itself. A patch on a pillar is a sentence. A newly smoothed arch is a chapter. It is a conversation that lasts for eons.

They are the tomb and the womb made one. They live inside the sarcophagus of their ancestors' ambition, and from their own bodies, they birth it again, molecule by molecule. They are not the city's children, come to devour it. They are its soul, refusing to let it die. They are the Kiln-Souls, and their slow, silent work is a prayer against the end of all things.
