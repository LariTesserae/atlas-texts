The people who live here are the Caldera Folk, and they are as much a feature of the geology as the mosses that carpet it. They are born of the world's constant hum and the slow, patient yielding of the calciform.

Their physical form is a study in adaptation to this resonant, damp, and vertical world. They are human in shape, but with a palpable density. Their bones are thick and heavy, their musculature less for explosive action and more for tireless, sustained pressure—the kind needed to guide a bone scraper against living stone for a full turn of the sun. Their skin is smooth and curiously poreless, ranging in color from the pale, milky green of new calciform growth to the deeper jade of moss-shaded stone. It holds a constant, cool moisture, never truly dry in the misty air, and it offers excellent purchase on the slick, living surfaces of their city.

They are almost always barefoot. Their feet are broad and the soles are tough, yet exquisitely sensitive. Through their feet, they read the world. They feel the deep, groaning bass of the planet's growth, the subtle shifts in vibration that herald a new lobe swelling from below, or a structural weakness in a distant arch. Their hands are their other primary sense organ: wide, strong, with fingers that end not in soft pads but in hardened, calloused ridges from a lifetime of carving.

Their faces are serene and broad-boned. Their most striking feature is their eyes. They are large and dark, the pupils permanently wide to gather the soft, diffused light of the caldera. They have no need for squinting against a harsh sun. The iris is often a startling, vibrant green, the exact shade of the mosses they tend, or a deep, misty grey. They see the world in a thousand nuances of green, white, and grey, and can spot the subtle change in a plant's health or the faintest mineral discoloration on a wall from a hundred paces.

But to truly know them is to understand how they listen. Their ears are not especially large, but they are perfectly shaped, the whorls of the cartilage elegant and complex, like the harmonic series they are built to decipher. For the Caldera Folk, the great chord of the world is not background noise; it is language, it is thought, it is existence itself. They do not think *over* the sound; they think *with* it. A child’s first lesson is not in words, but in stillness—to learn to separate the hum of their own blood from the deeper hum of the world, to pick out the high, glassy overtone of a newly formed bridge from the guttural groan of a dome settling.

They rarely speak aloud. To shatter the world's harmony with loud, percussive speech is considered a deep vulgarity, a mark of deafness to the true music. Their communication is a quiet murmur, a form of melodic, tonal humming that weaves itself into the existing soundscape. They pitch their voices to harmonize with the note of the space they are in—a lower, more sonorous tone inside a great dome, a lighter, airier one on an open bridge. A conversation between two of the Folk sounds like a subtle, intricate duet played against the grand symphony of the caldera. Great emotion—joy, grief, love—is expressed not with volume, but with harmonic complexity. A funeral dirge is not a wail, but a group of gathered kin humming a chord of such profound and aching dissonance that it makes the very air feel heavy and wrong.

Their society is one of profound collectivism, born of their shared purpose. There is no concept of a king or a chief in the way an outsider might understand. The elders are Conductors. They are individuals whose long lives have allowed them to memorize vast passages of the world's score. They do not give commands; they make suggestions based on their listening. "The high arch sings sharp," an elder might hum to a team of carvers. "It needs deepening on the western face. It wants to be a G, not a G-sharp."

And the carvers, whose tools of bone and obsidian are their instruments, will go to the arch. They don't strike violently. They press, they scrape, they tap and listen, tap and listen. Each removal of calciform is a tuning. The fine, pale-green dust that falls is washed away by the weeping stone, feeding the mosses below. Their work is a meditation, a slow, generational process of shepherding the ever-growing city toward a more perfect harmony.

Life is work, but the work is art, and the art is worship. They live on the thickest of the mosses, on steamed fern fronds, and on the strange, white, bell-shaped flowers that taste of mushrooms and minerals. The slow, six-legged Hum-Grazers are their only livestock. They are sacred. One is harvested only when the population is strong, and its slaughter is a solemn affair. The being is not killed; it is quieted. An elder finds the creature's resonant frequency and hums it, slowly and deeply, until the grazer’s own internal vibrations overwhelm it and its great, slow heart comes to a stop. Every part is used: the meat for a rare feast, the cartilage for tools, the smooth skin for insulating wraps and pouches.

For the Caldera Folk, there is no real separation between the self and the world. When one of them dies, their body is carried to a freshly-swelling lobe of calciform, a place of new generation. It is laid upon the damp ground and covered in a blanket of the greenest moss. There is no marker. The Folk believe that the body’s moisture will be absorbed, its minerals will join the weeping of the stone, its decaying form will feed the life that carpets the world, and its own quiet, resonant frequency will be subsumed back into the great chord. They do not cease to be; they simply change their part in the song. To be one of the Caldera Folk is to be a note, fleeting but essential, in a symphony that has no beginning and no end.
