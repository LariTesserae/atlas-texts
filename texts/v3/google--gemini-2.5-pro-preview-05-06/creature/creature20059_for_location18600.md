This city is inhabited by the Murmuring Choir. They do not call themselves this, for they have no names. The name is an exhalation of thought, a concept that forms in the mind of any who might, by some impossible chance, behold them. It is a name born of paradox, for they are silent.

They are beings of fungus and flesh, creatures that have evolved not merely to survive in this environment, but as the ultimate expression of it. To see one is to see the city in miniature, in motion. They are tall and unnervingly slender, their forms echoing the tapered, fluted spires that surround them. Their skin is not skin at all, but a smooth, seamless layer of living chitin, the color of old ivory, cool and slick to the touch like a damp mushroom cap. It is semi-translucent, and beneath its surface, one can see the faint, pulsing blue light of their own internal mycelial network, a slow, rhythmic thrumming that matches the glow of the city's walkways.

They are humanoid only in the loosest sense. Their limbs are long and delicate, possessing an extra joint in both arm and leg that allows for a strange, fluid, and multi-angled grace. Their hands and feet are not clearly delineated; they are elegant, spade-like appendages, webbed with a thin, tough membrane between long, tapering digits. These digits end not in nails, but in soft, porous pads that can adhere to the slickest fungal wall and, more importantly, can feel and interpret the city's vast, slow life. They can taste the health of a buttress, feel the memory of a thousand water drips, and listen to the silent, biochemical conversations of the network beneath their feet.

Their heads are smooth and ovoid, featureless save for two enormous, perfectly round eyes. These eyes are pools of liquid-black, depthless and devoid of pupil or sclera. They do not reflect the city’s light so much as absorb it, drinking in the chartreuse and silver and blue glows with a consuming, placid intensity. They have no mouths. Sustenance is absorbed directly through the porous pads of their extremities, drawing nutrient-rich water and dissolved biomatter from the city's streams and living floors. They do not breathe air, but rather engage in a constant, slow gas exchange over their entire bodies with the damp, spore-thick atmosphere. What would be a nose or ears on another creature is replaced by a crown of delicate, feathery hyphae that drift from their scalp, pale and white like spun glass. These filaments are their primary sensory organs, catching spores on the still air, detecting infinitesimal shifts in humidity, temperature, and chemical composition. They can smell a predator from a kilometer away, taste the promise of decay on the other side of a plaza, and feel the slow, inexorable approach of another of their kind.

Their silence is absolute. They have no vocal cords, no language of sound. Their communication is the city's own. When they wish to converse, they press their palps to a wall or the floor. The blue light in their veins brightens, and a series of complex, patterned pulses—a language of living light—flows from them into the mycelial network. It may travel for miles in an instant, a silent, luminous sentence received by another member of the Choir through the soles of their feet or the palm of their hand. They do not speak *to* each other; they exchange information *through* the shared medium of their world. Their thoughts, their feelings, their intricate understanding of the ecosystem are all encoded in this slow, beautiful chroma-syntax. To watch them gather in a glowing plaza is to witness a silent symphony. Dozens of them stand, connected to the city's skin, and the entire plaza becomes a canvas of shifting, interlacing, and responding patterns of light—a council, a poem, a history being told.

They are not builders, but cultivators. They are the emergent consciousness of the metropolis, the gardeners of their own flesh. They move with a meditative slowness, a purpose that can span a day or a decade. One might stand for a week with its hands pressed against a fungal tower, not building it, but *persuading* it. Through a slow release of pheromones and bio-electrical signals, it encourages the fungal growth in a certain direction, reinforces a weakening arch, or guides a stream of water into a newly-formed channel. They prune with decay, initiating the gentle crumbling of old structures by introducing a specific decomposer spore, making way for new growth. They are the city's thoughts made manifest, the shepherds of its eternal, cyclical life.

Their life and death are indivisible from the city's. They are not born, but fruited. In certain hallowed, dark recesses, the mycelial mat will thicken and swell, forming a pearlescent sac-like pod that is nurtured by the entire network. Over time, a new member of the Choir gestates within, fed by the collective. When it is ready, the pod wall becomes translucent and dissolves, and a smaller, but fully-formed, being steps out into the dim light.

And when one grows old, its inner light fading, it does not die in a conventional sense. It will find a quiet place, lie down upon the mossy ground, and in a final act of communion, release the cohesiveness of its own form. Over hours, its body softens, deliquesces, and is peacefully, gratefully subsumed back into the floor. Its chitinous structure becomes food for the moss, its internal hyphae merging back into the great network, its memories and experiences filtering into the city's slow, cold consciousness. To die is simply to go home. They are not citizens of a city made of fungus. They are the city, dreaming it is a choir.
