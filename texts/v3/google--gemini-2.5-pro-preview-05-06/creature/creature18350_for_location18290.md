They are the Oallan. They do not have bones. Their bodies are elegant, elongated structures of tough, pearlescent cartilage and dense, fibrous muscle, shaped by the immense pressure into forms of perfect hydrodynamic grace. An adult Oallan is perhaps three meters long, but length is a misleading term, as they spend their lives in a vertical posture, suspended in the gloom. Their primary mode of being is to hang, still and attentive, like a living plumb line.

Their form is vaguely cephalopodic, but with a grace and intentionality that suggests something far more ancient. The main body, or "mantle," is a tapered teardrop of opalescent flesh, its surface smooth and cool to the touch. This mantle houses the organs, the vast, slow-beating heart, and the complex system of gas bladders and dense lipid reservoirs that they use to minutely control their buoyancy. With an imperceptible flexing of inner muscles, an Oallan can rise or fall a few centimeters, adjusting their position with the precision of a master sculptor.

From the wider, upper end of the mantle emerges the sensory cluster, which could be called a head. There is no neck, only a seamless continuation of the body. Two enormous eyes, seeming to be made of polished obsidian, dominate this cluster. They are unblinking, lidless, gathering the faint, phosphorescent light of the Precipice. They do not dart or focus in the way a predator's might; they absorb, they witness. They see not just light, but the subtle polarization of it, allowing them to perceive the stresses in the growing crystalline rock and the faint shimmering of nutrient-rich currents. Between these eyes are a constellation of small pores, the true mouths and nostrils of the Oallan. Through these, they filter the soupy medium for sustenance—planktonic life, mineral dust, and organic detritus—and taste the chemical composition of their world with a subtlety that is their primary sense. They can taste the age of water, the approach of a leviathan from the scent of its bacterial cloak hours before it arrives, the health of a kelp-frond by the unique chemical signature it exudes.

Below the mantle, six long, articulate limbs unfurl. They are not tentacles; they are arms, structured with hundreds of small cartilaginous plates that allow for both incredible flexibility and surprising rigidity. The upper two are their "fine" arms, used for the delicate work. Their tips split into a dozen slender, prehensile digits, each one a master of touch, able to feel the microscopic roughness of a nascent crystal and guide its growth. These are the hands of the artist, the gardener. The lower four limbs are thicker and stronger, ending in broad, padded lobes. These are used for anchoring to the spires, for bracing against a strong current, or for the heavy work of scraping and clearing sections of rock with their shell-tools.

The skin of an Oallan is their voice. While they can produce low, resonant hums used for communal "singing" that encourages mineral accretion, their primary language is light. Their entire bodies are laced with a complex network of chromatophores and photophores, controlled with the same nuance a human uses for speech. A slow, gentle pulse of soft amber from the mantle is a sign of contentment, a greeting. A complex, rippling wave of blue and violet down the arms communicates a detailed technical instruction for guiding a crystal's growth. Alarm is a sudden, sharp flash of cold white. A conversation between two Oallan is a silent, hypnotic dance of shifting colors and flowing patterns, a language of pure light that is as profound and slow as their thoughts.

Their consciousness is not a rushing river but a thickening gel. An Oallan does not "decide" so much as "accrete a conclusion." Thoughts are slow, deliberate processes, built layer by layer from sensory input, memory, and communal understanding. Rushing is a foreign, violent concept. To them, the growth of the city is not a project with a deadline, but a continuous, sacred act of being. It is their art, their religion, and their life, all one and the same. They feel the spires as extensions of their own bodies; the health of the glowing moss is their own health.

When a god-beast leviathan glides through their valley, the Oallan do not hide. They anchor themselves to their spires, dim their lights to a respectful, deep violet, and turn their great, black eyes upward. They are not watching a mere animal; they are observing a fundamental process of the world, like watching a sunrise on a world that has a sun. The passing of the leviathan is a solemn event, a reminder of the grand, slow scale of which they are but a small, thinking part. Their own lives, which can last for several centuries, are but a flicker in the time it takes the leviathan to make its great circuit of the globe.

When an Oallan’s life nears its end, its light begins to fade. The internal processes slow even further, and its body becomes denser, its buoyancy failing. In a final, communal ritual, its kin guides the dying one to a designated place within the spire. There, it anchors itself for the last time. Its body, rich in cartilage and complex organic compounds, becomes a part of the city. The tough sinews are woven into new tethers, the hard parts of its tools are passed down, and its very substance becomes a nutrient-rich foundation upon which new velvet-moss and kelp-fronds will grow. They do not die and leave the world; they simply change their form, continuing their endless work of building the Precipice from within. They are the slow, patient, luminous soul of the mountain.
