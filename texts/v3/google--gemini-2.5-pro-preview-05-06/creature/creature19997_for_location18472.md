What lives here must first be a creature of sound, or of its negation. In a world defined by a deafening, industrial roar, the inhabitants are either the instruments of that chorus, the silence that hides within it, or the ears that have learned to parse it.

The most immense inhabitants, and the least alive in any sense we might understand, are the Shapers. They are not creatures you see. They are forces you feel. They are the source of the foundational hum, the planet-sized tinnitus. A Shaper is a being of unimaginable mass and density, moving slowly through the semi-molten substrate far below the ceramic floor. They are the potters. Their passage displaces the soft, pliable material upwards, extruding the spires and walls. The grinding shriek that comes and goes is the sound of a Shaper setting its course, its crystalline internal structure shearing against the planetary crust. The colossal tool marks on a new wall are not from a trowel; they are the dermal denticles of a Shaper’s hide, the skin of a god-engine scraping against its creation.

You do not perceive a Shaper as an object. You perceive its presence as a change in the very physics of the local area. As one draws near, the constant bass note deepens, the vibration in your bones becomes a painful thrumming, and the rhythmic pulse of cherry-red light from the birthing structures intensifies and quickens, like a heartbeat under strain. They are blind, mindless, and deliberate as glaciers. Their “life” is the act of building; their "thoughts" are the architectural patterns they etch into the landscape over eons. They are as unaware of the smaller things that scuttle across their work as a human is of the bacteria on their skin. They are the world's living, moving geology.

Scuttling on the skin of this nascent world are the Grylle, the ceramic arthropods you saw. But they are not alone. They are the prey.

Hunting them are beings known only as Echo-Hunters. Where the Grylle are solid and loud, an Echo-Hunter is a creature of profound quiet. They are rarely seen, but their form is a marvel of acoustic adaptation. Vaguely tripedal, they stand on three slender, articulated legs that terminate not in feet, but in broad, fibrous pads of a sound-dampening mineral felt. They move with an impossible grace, making no sound themselves, a patch of absolute silence moving through the din.

Their body and head are a single, complex structure of hollow, resonant chambers and flared, parabolic fins, all fashioned from a black, metallic material that does not reflect the red light. They look like a collapsed and reassembled radio telescope. They do not have eyes. Their entire body is an ear. They perch for days, for weeks, motionless on a high, cool arch, their sensory fins twitching almost imperceptibly. They are not listening for the Grylle’s *chink-chink-chink*. That is just the locator beacon. They are listening to the echoes of that sound as they rebound from the terracotta canyons, building a perfect, three-dimensional sonar-map of the world for miles in every direction. They see with sound.

When an Echo-Hunter locates a Grylle deep within a warm, new wall, it does not dig. It unfolds a series of long, needle-thin vibratory organs from its central mass. It gently touches them to the surface of the wall, and it begins to hum. It is a quiet hum, at first, but it finds the precise resonant frequency of the ceramic plate the Grylle is clinging to. The hum builds, channeling the cacophony of the world into a single, focused point of sonic pressure. The red glow of the wall flickers in time with the vibration. Then, with a sharp, explosive crack that is momentarily louder than anything else, a section of the wall shatters inward. The Grylle is stunned or killed by the focused blast. The Echo-Hunter then unfurls manipulator arms, fine as surgical tools, to pick through the rubble and retrieve its meal, leaving a clean, cratered wound in the wall that will eventually be smoothed over by the world’s inexorable growth.

There is one other form of life, found only on the oldest, coldest, and highest structures—those charcoal-gray spires that have stood for millennia, their surfaces covered in a web of crackle-glaze. In these silent peaks, far from the heat and noise of creation, live the Weepers.

They are not solid. A Weeper is a colonial organism that manifests as a slow, viscous fluid, somewhere between oil and mercury. It flows. In the dim, ambient light, it has the faint, shifting iridescence of a patch of gasoline on water. They have no organs, no limbs, no mind. They are a living chemical reaction.

They cling to the crazed surfaces, feeding on the perpetual, coppery drizzle that coats the world. Their "metabolism" is a slow process of filtration. They absorb the mineral-rich fluid, extracting trace elements and silts, and excrete a pure, perfectly clear distillate that drips from their lowest point. Finding a Weeper colony is to find the only source of actual, clean water in the entire Umbilicus. Over centuries, a single Weeper colony will flow across miles of cooled ceramic, cleaning the surface, leaving behind a faint, polished track. Their life is one of silent, patient chemistry. They are the living patina of the world, a consequence of its eventual decay, existing only where the fire of creation has long since died.

And so, the inhabitants form a trinity, a reflection of the world's own process. The Shapers are the act of making, the raw, unconscious force of it. The Grylle and the Echo-Hunters are the life of the kiln, the chaotic, violent symbiosis of the fire. And the Weepers are the life of the cooling, the quiet, patient aftermath. All of them are less inhabitants *on* a world and more functions *of* it, cogs in a machine of continental scale, each one playing its part in the slow, loud, and lonely birth of a world made of clay.
