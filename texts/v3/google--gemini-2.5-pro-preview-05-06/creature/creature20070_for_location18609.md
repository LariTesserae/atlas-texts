What lives here is not an animal, not a creature in any sense that implies separation. To be separate is to be a foreign body, and the Heartwood has no concept of the foreign. It has only itself. What lives here is a function. It is a process given temporary form. It is the Custodian.

The Custodian does not have a permanent existence. It is not born and it does not die. It is *precipitated*. Its genesis is an anomaly, a deviation from the perfect, humming equilibrium. Perhaps a trillion tonnes of framework settle a millimetre too far, and a resonance of stress, a dissonant chord in the world's subsonic hum, propagates through the lignin beams. Or perhaps in one of the chemosynthetic nodules, a reaction runs infinitesimally too hot, creating a bubble of impure gas that threatens the delicate balance of the atmosphere.

This error, this minute imperfection, is a seed. In the deep root-mat near the flaw, a change occurs. The living fibers begin to knit themselves differently. Instead of the flat, felted weave of the floor, they begin to rise, to braid and twist. A limb of hardened cellulose, jointed with flexible, rubbery resin, pulls itself from the substrate, glistening with the world's clear fluid. Then another, and another. It is an act of self-organization, like a crystal forming in a saturated solution.

The form that emerges is one of pure utility, a body designed by the architecture it must traverse. It is vaguely insectile, but only in its articulated segmentation and spindly, multi-jointed limbs. Its torso might be a compact, hexagonal prism of dark wood, echoing the shape of the great pillars. Its limbs, perhaps six or eight of them, are not for walking on a flat surface but for climbing, for gripping. They end not in feet, but in specialized sockets, locking clamps, and fine, sharp probes, each shaped to interface perfectly with the engineered structures of the Heartwood—a concave grip to hold a fluted column, a forked pincer to brace itself on an I-beam's edge.

It has no head, no face, no eyes. It navigates not by sight in the glowing, intermittent darkness, but by a total and inherent communion with the system it is part of. It feels the strain in the lattice through the soles of its 'feet'. It tastes the chemical composition of the air through porous membranes on its back, detecting the errant gas molecule from kilometres away. The subsonic thrum of the world is its lifeblood and its sensory map; the flaw it must correct is a painful, discordant note in a symphony it knows by heart.

Once formed, the Custodian begins to move. Its motion is silent, deliberate, and unnervingly patient. It unfolds itself from its birthplace and begins its journey, a slow, methodical climb up a pillar that vanishes into the blackness above. It moves with the sparse, efficient grace of a machine performing its sole programmed task. It does not hurry. The Heartwood operates on geological time, and so does its antibody.

Its work is subtle, almost invisible. Arriving at the source of the stress, a micro-fracture in a colossal bridge, it may press itself flat against the flaw. From pores in its body, it secretes a viscous, amber-colored resin, which it carefully works into the crack with a delicate, chitinous tool that extends from its forearm. The resin is living tissue; it will fuse with the parent structure, and in a few hundred years, the repair will be indistinguishable from the original growth.

If the problem is a blockage in one of the U-shaped rivers—a crystallization of minerals forming a dam—the Custodian will lower itself into the empty channel. It might use a limb that terminates in a vibrating, diamond-hard shard of crystallized carbon, another specialized product of the Heartwood's chemistry, to gently break the crystal apart. It does not smash it; it taps it at precise resonance points until it cleaves along its lattice planes, dissolving back into the flowing processed fluids.

And when the task is done, when the stress-resonance fades and the world's hum returns to its perfect, unbroken tone, the Custodian’s purpose is complete. Its body is a drain on the system's perfect energy economy. It finds a quiet place, out of the way of any major flows. It ceases to move. Its limbs retract. Its form softens. It delaminates, its complex structures un-weaving themselves layer by layer. The hardened lignin softens back into pliable cellulose. The sophisticated joints dissolve. Over the course of a day or a week, it melts back into the floor or merges into the wall of a pillar, a slight thickening of the wood the only sign it was ever there. Its matter is reabsorbed, its complexity surrendered back to the whole.

The Custodian has no consciousness. It feels no satisfaction. It knows no loyalty. It is a thoughtless, instinctual reflex. It is the Heartwood's own immune system, a temporary, mobile organ grown to address a fleeting sickness. It is a dream the world has when it stirs in its sleep, a manifestation of the underlying, unconscious will to persist, unchanging, in the vast, dark, and solitary factory of itself. It is the loneliest form of life imaginable, because it is never, for one moment, separate from the one thing it exists to serve.
