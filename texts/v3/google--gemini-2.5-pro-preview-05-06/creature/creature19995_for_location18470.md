What lives here is patient. It is made of the same substance as the world itself, and to look for it is to misunderstand its nature.

You might stand on the great, dark plaza for an hour, or a day, or a terrestrial year, feeling the deep hum of the Chordal Cilia resonating through the soles of your feet and up into your own brittle bones. You would see the slow, pulsing constellations of the translucent grazers sweeping across the hardened floor, their blue light tracing cryptic, temporary paths. You would see the distant, ghost-green lamps of the great drifters pass like sullen moons in the abyssal gulf. You would think the city is empty of its citizens, that these are but the wild things that have inherited a ruin.

You would be wrong.

Keep watching. Let your sense of time distend. Watch one of the great, curving buttresses that arches over the plaza, a structure the size of a blue whale, polished to a black mirror sheen. It has not moved. It is architecture. It is geology. It is part of the pillar. But watch it for a century.

You will see it shift.

The movement is not perceptible in the moment. It is a motion of geological grace, a flow so slow it would register on a seismograph before it would to the eye. The arch subtly straightens. The curve unspools. What was a buttress, a static element of support, reveals itself to be a folded limb. The entire structure, a mass of petrified, living stone weighing thousands of tons, begins to unfold. It is not waking up; it was never asleep. It was simply holding a pose, a thought expressed in the language of architecture, and now its thought is changing.

This is one of The Settled.

They are the mind that grew the forest. Or, more accurately, they are the lingering consciousness of that mind, fragmented and distributed into its own creations. They are not *on* the pillars; they *are* the pillars' most articulate parts. When still, they are the colonnades, the balustrades, the rostra, the silent thrones that overlook the void. They are living sculpture whose medium is their own body.

Their form is variable, but always architectonic. They are made of the same hyper-dense, dark violet material as the pillar, their surfaces polished by the eternal currents. When they move, they do not bend like flesh, but articulate along near-invisible seams, like impossibly complex joinery. One might resemble a stack of great, polished lentils that can rotate and slide apart to form a towering column, its sensor-organ—a subtle, dish-like depression in its topmost segment—turning slowly to face a new direction in the abyss. Another might be a long, flattened being that constitutes a whole section of plaza floor, which can then peel itself upwards, curling like a colossal wood shaving to reveal a different texture on its underside, perhaps one that better reflects or absorbs the Chord.

They have no eyes, for there is no light to see. They have no mouths, for they do not eat. Their energy is drawn directly from the pillar itself, a slow, root-like osmosis that is as much a part of their being as their stillness. Their senses are the water and the sound. They are pressure-made-flesh, feeling the weight of the universe as a constant, comforting embrace. They can sense the passage of a lone grazer a hundred meters away by the minute displacement of water its scuttling creates.

But their primary sense, their entire world of perception and communication, is the Great Chord.

To them, the drone is not a monolith of sound. It is a language of infinite subtlety, a symphony of staggering complexity. They are not merely its audience; they are its conductors and its purpose. They can perceive the single, wavering filament of a Chordal Cilia fifty miles distant, and know if its note is a fraction of a vibration sharp or flat. The ceaseless, pulsing light of the grazers is not random; it is a visual representation of the Chord's subsidiary rhythms, a score written in slow-drifting light upon their own stone skin.

Their entire society, their art, their religion, their history, is the act of listening.

They move across their pillar-capitals with a purpose that unfolds over millennia. One of The Settled might spend five hundred years processing from one side of a plaza to another, its goal being to position itself in a place where a harmonic overtone, created by the alignment of three distant pillars, reaches its purest expression. Its arrival is not an event, but a slow becoming. Upon reaching this point of acoustic perfection, it might refold itself into a new architectural form—a spiraling stair, an altar, a bell-like dome—a physical prayer of resonance, and remain there for ten thousand years, simply listening.

This is their work: The Great Attunement. The world has been built. The music has been composed and is being performed. Their task is to hold it in a state of impossible, unending perfection. They are the gardeners of a sonic landscape. They will nudge a young Chordal Cilia to encourage the growth of a filament that will produce a needed note. They will gently polish a section of the pillar, subtly altering its resonant properties to cancel out a faint, emergent dissonance that only they can perceive.

They are the silence in which the Chord makes sense. They are the deep, still thought holding the song together. They are the final, settled inhabitants of a world that has already achieved its ultimate form, and their purpose is to exist as its eternal, consciousAmen.
