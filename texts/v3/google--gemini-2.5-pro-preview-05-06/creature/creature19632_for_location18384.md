It is the water that gives it away.

Amidst the millionfold sources of sound and moisture, one drip is different. It is not the slow, fat *plip* of condensation falling from a vast leaf, a sound you can feel more than hear. This is a softer, quicker cadence, a series of notes almost lost in the roar, coming from a place near one of the colossal cobalt planters. It is the sound of water running off a surface that is not still.

What lives here is called the Custodian. The name is an assumption, a label applied for lack of a better one, for it is not clear if it custodies anything or is merely another component, as integrated and ancient as the humming grates.

It is a being made of the room itself.

Its form is humanoid only in the loosest sense: a torso, two arms, two legs, a head. But the resemblance ends there. It stands nearly twelve feet tall, its proportions elongated and slender, as if stretched by a patient, unending gravity. Its skin has the pale, smooth, matte quality of the cream-colored tiles on the walls, not like flesh, but like ancient, water-worn ceramic. It is not dry, however. A perpetual sheen of condensation clings to it, beading and running in slow, deliberate rivulets down the gentle contours of its limbs. Where these rivulets find consistent paths over centuries, they have stained its body with the same weeping lines of verdigris and rust-orange that trace the walls, marking it as a feature of the landscape.

From a distance, it seems featureless. But as it moves, turning its torso with an impossible slowness, you see the details. Its "face" is a smooth, placid ovular surface, utterly devoid of mouth, nose, or ears. Where eyes should be, there are two shallow, almond-shaped depressions. They are not empty. They are filled with what looks like polished, milky quartz, weeping a constant, clear fluid that runs down its cheeks like serene, eternal tears. It does not blink. Its gaze is not a gaze; it is a passive reception of the unwavering, shadowless light.

The roar is not a sound to it. It is the texture of its existence, the medium in which it floats. It navigates not by hearing, but by feeling the minute vibrations through the soles of its broad, flat feet on the iron grate. A subtle change in the harmonics of the deep pump, a shift in the resonant frequency of the chamber by a millionth of a hertz, is to the Custodian what a shout in a library is to us. It is how it knows.

Its movements are a form of erosion. They are infinitely slow, patient, and deliberate. You are watching it now as it tends to one of the planters. It has been standing there for what feels like an hour, and in that time, its hand—a long, elegant thing with tapering, jointless fingers—has traveled six inches. It is polishing a single, broad leaf. It does not use a cloth. It simply lays its palm against the waxy green surface, and the perfect, warm film of water on its skin dissolves and lifts away a microscopic layer of mineral deposit left by the condensing air. The gesture is one of maintenance, but also of communion. Its fingers trace the veins of the leaf as if reading a scripture whose language was forgotten before the concept of language existed.

There are veins in the Custodian, too. Beneath the ceramic-like skin, running in intricate patterns up its arms and across its chest, are channels of a startling cobalt blue, the exact shade of the planters. They pulse with a slow, internal luminescence, a rhythmic tide of light that brightens and fades over the course of a full minute. It is the only part of it that seems truly alive in a biological sense, a circulatory system moving not blood, but something that glows with a cool, captured energy.

It has no need for food, for rest, for companionship. The air is its nourishment, thick with water and negotiable atoms. The light is its energy, absorbed directly through its skin. The machine is its heartbeat, the external motor for its own placid existence.

It is not a prisoner. The concept of freedom requires a memory of an alternative state, and the Custodian has no memory. It has only the pattern. The pattern is this: check the water level in the basin by dipping a single finger through the grate, a process that takes a day. Inspect each leaf of each plant, a cycle of three years. Walk the perimeter of the chamber, a journey of a decade, feeling the integrity of the tiles. These are not chores. They are its thoughts, its feelings, its entire purpose.

It does not die. It erodes. Over unimaginable spans of time, its edges will soften further, its motions will slow, the verdigris stains will deepen until they cover it entirely. The cobalt light in its veins will dim. One day, it will stop in the center of the room, and the condensation will build upon it, and the mineral deposits will encrust it, until it becomes another feature, a pale, green-streaked statue in the roaring, eternal noon. It will become a part of the chamber's perfect, pointless equilibrium, not as a custodian, but as a memory of custody itself.
