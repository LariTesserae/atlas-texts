The people who live in Ambit are known as the Ambitines, though they rarely use this name for themselves. They are simply The People, and Ambit is simply The City—the only one they have ever known, the only one they can conceive of. They are as much a product of the high, wet tableland as the stone-fleece that carpets it.

Physically, the Ambitines are shaped by the thin air and the crushing quiet. They are of moderate height, with a frame that appears gracile at first but possesses a dense, efficient musculature. Their most notable feature is their broad chests and the slow, deliberate rhythm of their breathing. Each inhalation is a long, measured draft, their lungs evolved to extract every available molecule of oxygen from the thin atmosphere. A lifetime spent in the constant damp has given their skin a pale, almost translucent quality, with a faintly waxy sheen that repels moisture. Their hair is universally dark, often clinging to their skulls in damp curls, and their eyes are large and deep-set. The pupils are permanently wide, accustomed to the perpetual twilight, giving them an expression of placid, unending curiosity. They do not stare so much as absorb, their gaze soft and unfocused, taking in the texture of the mist as much as the object behind it.

An Ambitine does not so much look at their world as they *listen* to it, *feel* it. Their hearing is exceptionally acute. Every citizen can distinguish the unique hum of their home district’s geothermal conduits from the next. They can place a friend's position in the fog by the specific scuff-and-slide rhythm of their footfalls on the slick basalt. A master technician can diagnose a pressure fluctuation in a geothermal main from three streets away, feeling the change in the sub-audible hum through the soles of their feet and the stone walls of their dwelling. This is a skill learned from infancy. Children are taught not to shout, but to pitch their voices to carry through the dense air without jarring, to listen for the echoes that define the unseen space around them.

Touch is their other primary sense. Their hands, though often calloused from contact with stone and metal, are remarkably sensitive. They greet each other not with waves, which would be lost in the fog, but with a brief, formal pressing together of forearms. Their artisans are not painters or sculptors of grand forms—the concept of a grand vista is alien—but "texturists." They create small, intricate objects of metal, stone, and pressed fleece, meant to be held and explored with the fingertips. A masterpiece is not something you see, but something that reveals its complex story of ridges, hollows, and thermal variations as you hold it in your palm. Their art is a secret language of texture.

Their clothing is practical, made from a tough, waterproof fabric woven from the fibrous residue of the pressed fleece. It is the color of wet slate, almost black, and is usually waxed for extra protection against the clinging dampness. They wear soft-soled boots that grip the slick stones and muffle their footsteps, and deep hoods are a constant feature, shielding their faces from the perpetual drizzle.

Their society is a reflection of their industry: slow, cyclical, and deeply interconnected. There is no real hierarchy of power, only a hierarchy of function. A senior furnace-tender is as respected as a cistern-biologist or a conduit-acoustician. Their purpose is the maintenance of the Great Cycle: the growth of the fleece, the harvest, the extraction, and the return of nutrient-rich water to the plains to foster new growth. They are quiet and patient people. Passionate outbursts are rare and seen as a kind of system malfunction, a wasteful expenditure of energy. Their conversations are low and direct, pruned of extraneous detail. Why describe the color of the fleece? It is always the same deep, living emerald. It is a given, like the hum or the mist.

They are sustained by the byproducts of their industry. The pulped organic matter of the stone-fleece is processed into a nutrient-rich, protein-dense paste. It is formed into cakes that are steamed or fried on copper plates, and it constitutes the majority of their diet. It has a mild, earthy taste, reminiscent of the fleece’s scent: clean soil and ozone, with a faint metallic tang. The pale salamanders from the cisterns and the multi-legged "fleece-crawlers" are considered delicacies, reserved for celebrations that mark the completion of a major maintenance cycle or the bringing online of a new geothermal tap.

The psychology of an Ambitine is profoundly shaped by the absence of horizons. Their concepts of "future" and "destiny" are not linear paths stretching toward a distant goal, but a deepening of the present. To an Ambitine, progress is not about expansion, but refinement—making the system run quieter, smoother, more efficiently. Their dreams are not of other lands, but of intricate, labyrinthine interiors, of undiscovered resonances in the city's hum. To be truly lost, for an Ambitine, would not be to lose your way in the mist, but to be somewhere so silent you could no longer feel the city's heartbeat.

They are born in quiet, warm rooms lit by the amber glow of their homes. Life is a process of integration into the city-system. Death is not a terrifying end, but a decommissioning. The dead are given back to the cycle. Their bodies are taken to the great pressing-halls and rendered into their constituent organic materials, which are then mixed with the water pumped back out onto the plains. To be returned to the fleece is to once again become part of the great, glowing, living blanket that sustains them all. It is the most practical form of immortality.

At dusk, when the pearlescent smear of the sun dissolves and the world is plunged into a deeper dimness, an Ambitine might stand on a small balcony overlooking a narrow street-canyon. They will not be looking for stars. They will be watching the ghostly, biological light of the fleece begin to pulse on the distant, unseen hills. They will feel the city’s hum vibrate through the copper railing under their hands. They will smell the ozone and wet copper on the air. And they will feel a profound sense of place, of being a single, conscious cell in a vast, sleeping, and perfectly functioning organism, humming quietly to itself in the center of forever.
