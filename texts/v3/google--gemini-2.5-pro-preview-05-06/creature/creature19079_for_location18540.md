They are the Hum.

It is not a name they would know, for they know nothing of names. It is what an outside mind, reeling from the sensory assault, might call the single organism that poses as a population. They are the inhabitants, the environment, and the god of this place, all at once. They are the Schism-Born, the children of the break.

They do not seem to have emerged into this cavern; they seem to have been revealed by it. When the world-stone cracked, it did not create an empty space to be colonized, but sheared through the single, solid body of their sleeping form, waking it into a million-million pieces that have been scrabbling to find their place ever since. They are living geology.

At the lowest level, forming the very texture of the ground, are the **Fractals**. They are the source of the highest, most pervasive register of the Hum—the sound of a billion styluses scratching on a record of stone. They are the size of a splayed hand, their bodies a jet-black, vitreous chitin that perfectly mimics the obsidian scree. They are not merely on the rubble; they *are* the rubble, until they move. Their form is a nightmare of practicality: six needle-thin legs, two hooked forelimbs for gripping, and a flat, low-slung body that allows them to vanish into the thinnest of cracks. They are the cavern's nervous system, its sense of touch. They swarm over anything new, anything that falls, anything that ceases. When a larger creature dies, the Fractals are the first to arrive, a flowing, rustling tide of black that coats the body, their chittering mandibles disassembling it with an efficiency that is horrifyingly swift. They do not eat for hunger, but for balance. They are the agents of dissolution, ensuring nothing lingers outside the system for long.

Flowing through the Fractals in wide, slow-moving rivers are the **Porters**. These are the dog-sized creatures, the blood cells of the megacosm. Their carapaces are a duller, more worn grey-brown, marred by the constant, gentle friction of their fellows. They move in channels of their own making, grooves worn into the rock over millennia of identical journeys. Each Porter carries something. Some grip pale, pulsing sacs of fungal matter in their forward claws, transporting the 'food' that is also the cavern's light. Others carry mineral nodules, prized chunks of rock heavy with crystal, their purpose unknown but their destination unwavering. The most solemn of these processions carry their own dead, the intact husks of other Porters or even the immense plates of a fallen Hulker. They move without deviation, their antennae locked onto the scent-trail of the one before, their gait a synchronized, rippling wave that creates the low, thrumming bass of the Hum. To fall into a river of Porters is not to be attacked, but to be absorbed, to be trampled into the floor by a force that cannot distinguish an obstacle from the ground it walks on.

And then, there are the **Hulkers**. They are the cavern's thoughts, slow, heavy, and profound. The size of oxen, they are walking monuments. Their chitin is not a single shell but a layered, slate-like plate-mail, thick and grey and seamed with scars. These are not battle scars; they are work scars, a geological record of every sharp edge they have ever leaned against, every mineral vein they have ever worked. Veins of the same green, light-giving lichen that traces the cavern walls grow in the deep fissures of their shells, so that each Hulker carries its own dim, moving constellation.

Their intelligence is a deep and terrifying thing, for it is an intelligence without curiosity. A Hulker will spend a century in a hundred-foot radius, methodically breaking down a single wall of rock. It will find a shard of obsidian, its form a near-perfect match for the grasping claw it has evolved over eons. This claw is not a hand; it is a living wrench, a socket that expects a tool. The Hulker will pick up the shard, feel its weight and its edge, an act of communion rather than assessment. It does not think, "This will be useful." It registers a state of rightness: *the shard is in the limb*. Then, it turns to the wall and begins to shear away stone with immense, patient force. The flakes fall, and the Fractals swarm to investigate, tasting them for their mineral content. When the Hulker has shaved the vein down to nothing, it will drop the tool, which has been worn to a nub. It will stand, motionless, sometimes for a decade, a living statue amidst the flow, its internal chemistry shifting, its muscles rebundling. Then, it will turn, lumber a hundred feet, and begin the process anew. It does not innovate. It does not learn. It *perfects*. It is the physical memory of the system.

These are not three species, but three castes of a single being whose true body is the cavern itself. At the absolute center of the shatter-zone, where the force of the initial cataclysm was most focused, lies the **Axle**. It is not a creature one can look at. It is a geographical feature, a mountain of living flesh fused with the rock, a kilometer wide and half as high. Its surface is a knot of the same grey, layered chitin as the Hulkers, but on a scale that defies biology. The milky-white fungal shelves grow directly from it, and their synchronized pulse is the Axle's heartbeat, slow and inexorable. The Axle does not move. It does not rule. It simply *is*. It exudes a cocktail of complex pheromones, the cool, ozone-scented breath of the cavern, which permeates everything and locks every creature into its designated role. It is the governor on the engine, the thought that can never be un-thought. It is the source of the stasis.

Life and death here are accounting errors. There is no birth, only production. The Axle buds off nascent Fractals from its lower folds, which detach and join the swarm. Porters emerge fully formed from deep, uterine chasms at its base, already seeking their place in the living rivers. When a Hulker has finished its millennia of service, its internal chemistry finally depleted, it does not die in the field. It turns, for the first and only time in its life, from its work, and begins a final, ponderous pilgrimage back toward the Axle, its personal light show dimming with every step. Upon arrival, it collapses against the living mountain, and over the course of centuries, is reabsorbed into it, its matter and its memories recycled.

They are the Hum. They are a single, continuous action. Their sound is the vibration of their one and only purpose: to hold this world in the exact state it was in a moment after its creation. They are the cells of a body straining with the effort of holding a breath that has lasted for eternity, their deafening drone the groan of a billion-year-old muscle that can never, ever relax.
