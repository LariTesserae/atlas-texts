Here, in the embryonic city of Weft and Spire, live the Lucid.

They do not walk upon the Weft, for they understand its treachery and its sacredness. To them, it is not a floor but a scripture, the living code from which all else is manifest. They move through the vast, cold emptiness between the structures, their forms so aligned with the aesthetic of the place that they are often mistaken for it. At a distance, a Lucid might appear as a shard of the architecture that has detached and is now drifting on some imperceptible current, a sliver of a spire or a peel of an onion-pod's skin given temporary, aimless life.

As one resolves from the gloom, its form becomes clearer, and no more comprehensible. It is a being of impossible verticality, ten to twelve feet tall but no thicker through the torso than a fallen branch. Its body is a composite of the city's own materials: long, overlapping plates of dark, iridescent chitin, like the bark on the pods, are segmented by flexible joints of the same plum-purple vascular tissue as the Weft. Through translucent patches in this living armor, a slow, cold, internal light pulses—the same blue-green as the fungal colonies, but synchronized with the deep, metabolic thrum of the grove-city itself. Their light is their blood and their breath.

They have no face, no head in any recognizable sense. Their upper end tapers to a smooth, featureless point, like a sealed ceramic vessel. Their lower end frays into a half-dozen slender, prehensile tendrils, each tipped with a patinated, coppery barb that looks identical to the material wrapping the factory-spires. These tendrils are their instruments of interaction, but they rarely touch anything. They are most often held loosely, drifting a few inches from a surface, sensing, guiding. The Lucid do not walk; they float, levitating a foot or so above the Weft, propelled by a force that has no visible mechanism and makes no sound. Their movement is a languid, unhurried glide, a slow-motion ballet through the cathedral of darkness.

The Lucid are not builders; they are conductors. They are the nascent consciousness of the city-organism, the nerve endings of its vast, dreaming mind. When a new spar needs to be extruded from the Weft, a Lucid will drift to the junction point. It will not touch it. It will hover there for hours, perhaps days, its tendrils arranged in a complex, static gesture, like a musician holding a chord. Its internal light will brighten and modulate, pulsing in a complex rhythm that directs the flow of nutrients and genetic information through the Weft below. Under its silent command, the new growth emerges, hardens, and takes its place in the grand design. They are gardeners tending a single, continent-sized plant that thinks it is a city.

They perceive the world not through eyes or ears, but through a total, systemic resonance. They feel the deep thrum of the city in their very substance, and any deviation—the scuttling of a maintenance-crawler on the underside of the Weft, the subtle shift in pressure as a great, unseen lung exhales in the void below—is perceived as a distinct note, a change in the texture of reality. Their consciousness is as fragile and provisional as the environment itself. They do not experience time as a linear progression. An event's sonic echo arriving minutes before its visual component is, to them, the natural order. They perceive the *entirety* of a process at once: the potential for a crystalline shard to break, the act of its breaking, and the lingering resonance of its fall are all part of a single, holistic sensory event that has no distinct 'before' or 'after'.

They are individuals, but only in the way that the fingers of a hand are individuals. They operate with a silent, shared purpose that requires no communication. One might be tending a nascent spire in the east, while another harvests the shimmering spores from a vent miles to the west, catching them in a net of faint, biotic energy woven between its tendrils before releasing them into a specific air current to 'pollinate' a new habitation-pod. Their tasks are components of a single, city-wide metabolic process.

When a Lucid's cycle ends, it does not die. It drifts towards one of the great onion-pods and, on approach, the cloudy quartz-like wall becomes permeable. The Lucid passes through it without a sound, dissolving back into the undifferentiated, organic substrate within. Its light goes out, and its consciousness is reabsorbed into the whole. Later, perhaps centuries later, when a new conductor is needed, a new Lucid will differentiate itself from the wall of a spire, peel away from the chitinous bark, and float out into the cold, quiet dark, its internal light beginning its first, slow pulse in time with the heartbeat of the world it has been born to serve. They are the dreamers, but they are also the dream, and their silent, patient work is the slow, inexorable process of their world waking up.
