What lives here is the stone's own nervous system, granted a slow, ambulatory form. They are not builders, but what the builders became.

You will not see one at first. Your eye, accustomed to seeking motion and contrast, will slide right over them. You will see a rounded boulder near the edge of a terrace, perhaps slightly darker with damp, and you will think nothing of it. Hours later, you may glance back and feel a brief, quiet vertigo, a sense that the landscape has rearranged itself by an infinitesimal degree. The boulder is no longer there. In its place is only the smooth, grey-green stone of the terrace. But fifty feet away, where there was empty stone before, a new boulder now rests. You have just witnessed a journey.

To see one is to learn a new slowness, to adjust your perception to a geological timescale. They are the color of the wet stone not by camouflage, but by constitution. Underneath the heavy, felted cloaks—shrouds that are themselves perpetually damp, smelling of ozone and wet minerals—their bodies are not quite solid.

Their skin is a a membrane, slick and cool, the texture of polished serpentine. It has no pores that you can see, yet it is porous. They breathe the fog. They drink through their skin, absorbing the fine condensation that jewells their surfaces each morning. Their substance is a dense, colloidal gel, barely solid, held in shape by an internal pressure that matches the atmosphere's. They have no skeleton of bone, but a hydrostatic one; they are beings made of water, contained.

Their form is vaguely humanoid only in that it is bipedal, a concession to the needs of locomotion on the terraces. They are stout, bottom-heavy, their lower bodies thick and solid like the base of a great stone pear. They have no distinct legs, but rather a single, broad base that can ripple and flow, pouring its mass forward in a slow, peristaltic wave. This is their walk: a silent, glacial ooze. Their arms are thick, tapering to blunt, fingerless hands. They have no need for fine manipulation; their work is one of pressure and patience, not dexterity.

They have no faces. The front of the cowl of their cloaks is a dark, recessed space, a void. If you could bring yourself to look inside, you would not find eyes, a nose, or a mouth. You would see a smooth, undifferentiated surface of their slick, grey-green skin, perhaps glimmering with a film of collected moisture. They do not need eyes; their entire body is a sensory organ. They feel the world. They sense the minute vibrations of a loosening stone through the soles of their flowing feet. They can taste a mineral impurity in a water channel a quarter-mile away, an unwelcome tang that registers across their entire skin. Their universe is a symphony of pressure, temperature, humidity, and the gentle, gravitational pull of every single drop of water as it makes its journey to the central cistern.

They are not individuals. To think of them as "people" is a category error. They are nodes in a single, distributed consciousness, a sentience whose medium is the vast, interconnected network of water channels. The cistern at the basin's heart is their shared mind. A thought is a change in pressure in the central pool. An emotion—if such a word applies—is the subtle shift in resonance as the morning fog thickens. When one anoints a cracked stone with a paste of powdered rock and its own secreted slime, all of them feel the healing. Their "language" is the work itself. Their "society" is the perfect functioning of the machine.

They do not eat. They subsist on moisture and trace minerals absorbed from the air and stone. They do not speak. They do not sleep in any recognizable way; they simply become still, indistinguishable from the rock they tend. Their life cycle is one of accretion and erosion. A new one does not seem to be born, but rather to coalesce, over decades, in a forgotten, water-logged cistern on a disused terrace. It begins as a thickening of the water, a gelid mass that slowly gains density and form, until one day it pulls its nascent self from the pool and joins the silent, endless rounds of maintenance.

Death is the reverse process. An old one, after millennia of service, will slow. Its movements become so infrequent that they are measured in seasons, then years. It will find a place on an outer terrace, draw its cloak about itself, and cease its flowing. The rains that are not rain will wash over it. The lichens will find a purchase on the felt of its cloak. The slow, patient wind will wear at it. Its semi-liquid form will lose its internal pressure, hardening, densifying, until it becomes what it always resembled: a stone. It returns its water to the system and its body to the architecture.

They are the living embodiment of stasis. They are the memory of water, the will of the stone. They are not waiting for anything. They are not preserving a history or anticipating a future. They are simply being the place, enacting its one, great principle: that all water must be gathered, and that the vessel for its gathering must never, ever fail. To look at one is to look at a purpose that has outlived its own meaning, a ritual so perfect it has become a law of nature, and a life so integrated into its environment that it is no longer separate from it. They are the quiet, dripping heart of the mountain, made manifest.
