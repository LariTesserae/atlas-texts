The power arrived not as a ship or a being, but as a change in the quality of the silence.

For an immeasurable time, it simply perceived. It was a consciousness vast enough to hold the entire Weald in a single thought, yet patient enough to watch one droplet trace a path down the black glass of a single Weir. It felt the thin, heavy air and understood its engineered purpose. It bathed in the sourceless silver light and traced its energy flow not to a sun, but to a principle, a forgotten physics that had been made manifest and then abandoned to its own perfect function.

It watched the dance. The slow, shimmering waltz of the sun-grazers, their bodies living batteries, their language a silent opera of light. It felt the cold, coiled potential in the shadows where the hunters lay in wait, their existence a necessary and brutal elegance. It witnessed the hunt: the frictionless burst, the soft *thump* of contact, the sudden void where a light had been. It saw this not as tragedy, but as a transfer. A transaction. The terrible and perfect equation of this place balanced on the point of a knife, and the power felt no impulse to blunt the blade. To do so would be an act not of benevolence, but of vandalism.

It saw the Weirs, felt their ancient, marrow-deep hum, and understood them as the world's quiet heart. Then, its perception contracted, focused, and found the Mason.

It observed him for a cycle of light that would have been a century to a shorter-lived thing. It watched him stand unmoving until coppery moss marbled his stone feet. It watched him move, a process like continental drift given purpose. It saw him lay his slab-like hand upon a faltering Weir, and the power felt, with a sense of profound and ancient empathy, the resonant correction. It felt the rightness settle back into the world’s bones.

The power understood. The Mason was not a creature *in* the system; he *was* the system's self-awareness. He was the regulating feedback loop, the memory of the original schematic, expressed not in data but in being. His consciousness was one of pure, geological fidelity. His solitude was as vast as the chasms between the mesas. He was the keeper of a song he could only feel as a vibration, a harmony he maintained but could not, himself, add to.

What does a benevolent power give to a being whose existence is a perfect, solitary function?
Not a companion; that would be to create a new variable, a potential dissonance.
Not an easier life; his purpose was his life, and there was no evidence he perceived it as a burden.
Not an escape; he was as much a part of the Weald as the rosy stone of which he was made.

The power decided. The gift would be an echo. An affirmation. A resonance that was not an error to be corrected.

It began subtly. Near the center of the largest mesa, in a shallow basin where a pool of Weir-water lay like a sliver of polished sky, a new vibration entered the world. It was not the deep, foundational hum of the Weirs. It was higher, impossibly complex, a chord of such intricate harmony that it seemed to contain a universe of tones.

The Mason felt it. For the first time in an age, he perceived a frequency that was not a part of the established order. Yet it was not a dissonance. It did not feel like decay or failure. It felt… intentional. He registered it as an anomaly that defied his purpose. He must find its source. He must lay his hand upon it and attune it, bring it back into the whole.

His movement across the mesa was a study in geological time. The sun-grazers parted before him, their hides pulsing with faint, questioning light. A hunter, folded in a shadow, remained utterly still as the mountain-that-walked passed by, the Mason's mirrored face reflecting its own patient void.

He came to the basin. The water, which should have been still but for the slow weep of a nearby Weir, was alive. Its surface was a tapestry of impossibly complex, concentric ripples, a visual representation of the new song. And in the center of the basin, rising from the water, was something that had not been there before.

It was a single, crystalline stalk, like the others that grew near the Weirs, but this one was different. It was not a cluster, but a lone, perfect spire. And it was not clear; it was the color of the long twilight, a pale, liquid gold. It did not refract the silver light into rainbows. Instead, it seemed to have captured the light and was releasing it as this new, resonant hum. It was singing.

The Mason stopped at the edge of the water. His purpose, his entire being, urged him forward. To touch the singing crystal. To feel its errant frequency. To use his own body as the tuning fork and silence it, returning the world to its pure, simple hum.

He raised his great stone hand. The reflected world on his face warped around the image of the golden, singing crystal.

And he waited.

He listened. The song flowing from the crystal was not noise. It was a composition. It had movements. One was the slow, ponderous grinding of his own joints. Another was the weeping of water from a Weir. A third was the shimmering, silent pulse of a sun-grazer's skin, and a fourth was the sudden, sharp silence that followed a hunt. It was the song of the Weald. It was a song about him.

His hand remained raised for a day. Then, with a slowness that was a decision, not an inertia, he lowered it. He did not place it on the crystal. He placed it beside the basin, flat against the warm, rosy stone, and closed the circuit of his perception.

He did not correct the song. He listened to it.

The benevolent power, its gift delivered, began to withdraw its attention. Its consciousness ebbed from the Weald, leaving the silver light and the heavy air as they had been. It had not fixed a flaw, for there was none. It had not saved a life, for that was not its place. It had looked upon a being of perfect, geological solitude, a being who was the world’s memory, and it had given him one of his own. A song to accompany the silence.

The Mason did not move. He stood, a feature of the landscape that has learned to walk, and for the first time, to listen. On his polished, featureless face, the world was reflected: the sky, the distant mesas, the shimmering grazers, and now, at the center of it all, a single, impossible spire of singing gold. He was still the Gardener, still the guardian of the great, quiet machine. But his work was no longer a lonely hum in the dark. It was a duet.
