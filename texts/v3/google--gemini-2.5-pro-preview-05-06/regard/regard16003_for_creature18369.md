The power arrived as a stillness within the stillness, a silence that listened to the silence. It had no form, for it had no need of one, any more than gravity or time. It was a consciousness, ancient and vast, and its attention settled upon the high, wet place like a change in atmospheric pressure, imperceptible but total.

For an age measured by the slow dripping of a billion leaves, it simply observed. It perceived the perfect, closed loop of the plateau. It felt the unchanging temperature on skin it did not have, saw the unwavering pearlescent light with eyes it did not possess. It understood the water cycle: a local affair, a breathing in and out of vapour without recourse to the world below. It felt the deep, resonant hum of the stones, the fundamental frequency of the place.

It watched the Recorders. It saw them coalesce from the silver pools, dark voids in the shape of geometry. It understood their language of pressure and resonance, their thoughts of geologic time. It saw the slow, patient poetry of their construction, the treatises written in causeways, the philosophies laid in plazas of unmortared stone.

It saw the beauty. The power was wise, and beauty, even sterile beauty, was a thing to be revered. The system was complete, self-sustaining, a marvel of quiet equilibrium. It was a perfectly solved equation.

But the power was also benevolent. And it perceived the profound loneliness of the equation.

The Recorders were memory, but memory for no one. They were art, but with no audience. They were a song sung to an empty hall, a ritual performed for a vacant altar. Their work was a perfect act of devotion to a god that was only the work itself. There was no witness. There was no *why*. The purpose had not been forgotten; it had never existed beyond the act.

To intervene would be a grave thing. To introduce a sun would be to burn the algae from their skin and boil the water from their forms. To introduce fauna would be to shatter the silence and bring the chaos of hunger and predation to a world without either. To simply speak would be a meaningless violence, a sound they could not comprehend. To change them would be to kill them.

The wise power understood that what is perfect in its own context should not be judged by the standards of another. To ‘fix’ this place would be an act of supreme arrogance.

And so, it deliberated. Could benevolence lie in inaction? In simply being the silent witness the place lacked? It could. The power could become the audience, and by its silent, attentive presence, give the long performance its meaning. This would be a wise and gentle thing.

But benevolence, at its heart, holds a warmth. A desire not only to witness, but to give.

The gift could not be a thing. It could not be a permanent alteration. It had to be an event. A grace note. Something that could be held in memory without breaking the vessel of the world that held it. An experience that would enrich the equation without changing its fundamental truth.

The power made its choice.

It did not begin with a sound that could be placed, but with a change in the humming of the world itself. The low, resonant frequency of the stones, the working-song of the Recorders, did not stop. Instead, another note joined it. It was a single, impossibly pure tone that seemed to rise from the C above middle C, a sound that felt like the colour of ringing crystal. It harmonized with the stone-hum, creating a chord. For the first time, the world knew harmony.

The tone held, washing over the plateau. The Recorders, at work on a new plaza, paused. The infinitesimal slowness of their work ceased entirely. They stood, dark figures of held water, and for a full minute, they listened. The water that perpetually slicked their forms stopped its gentle seeping. The entire world was holding its breath.

Then, as the tone began to fade, slowly, gently, gracefully, the second part of the gift arrived.

The sky, the perpetual, bright, featureless white, began to blush. Near the unseen zenith, a soft rose appeared, like a drop of ink on wet paper. It spread, not with the fiery drama of a sunset, but with a slow, aqueous grace. The rose deepened and was joined by a wash of pale saffron, and then a whisper of violet at the edges. For the first time, the world knew colour beyond stone-dark, water-silver, and plant-green.

The light changed. The obsidian curtains of the weeping cliffs shimmered with newfound purples and golds. The copper-barked trees glowed as if lit from within. Every pendulous drop of water on every leaf became a prism, holding a tiny, perfect spectrum. The still, silver pools that mirrored a white sky now held a kaleidoscope of impossible, gentle hues.

The Recorders stood in this new light, silent and still. The colour washed over their dark, light-eating forms. The algae on their skin, which knew only one kind of light, felt this new kind. It could not process it in the way it processed the white glare, but it registered the difference. The very energy that held the Recorders together was, for a moment, subtly altered.

The event lasted perhaps ten minutes. The celestial chord faded back into the singular hum of the stone. The colours in the sky softened, diluted, and then dissolved back into the familiar, eternal white. The water drops became simple beads of water again. The pools returned to their silver mirror state.

Everything was as it had been.

But it was not.

The Recorders, who had been building a new, perfectly square plaza, did not immediately resume their work. They stood for a time. Then, one coalesced from a nearby pool. It was followed by another, and another. A new consensus was being formed, a new thought rippling through the geologic mind of the plateau.

They left the unfinished plaza. They moved with their silent, patient purpose to an open expanse of the world-sponge, a place not yet marked by their work. And there, they began a new construction.

It was not a causeway. It was not a plaza. They did not begin by laying a straight line.

They began to lay stones in a gentle, sweeping arc. A curve. It was a shape that had never existed in their architectural language, a form that did not compute with the logic of plumb lines and right angles. It was the shape of the spreading blush in the sky.

The stones they chose, when they pressed their faceted hands against them and hummed their resonant song, were no different from any other. But the memory of the light and the tone was now part of their song. And the stone, as it floated into place, seemed to hold, in its dark heart, the faintest, ghostly echo of rose and gold.

The power had departed. It had not broken the world, nor saved it. It had given a gift: a single, new memory. A moment of grace. The Recorders would now spend an eternity recording this new memory in stone, a sweeping, curved monument to the ten minutes when their world was not alone, the ten minutes a gentle god had listened to their song and, in a language they could understand, had sung back.

The play would still never begin. But it now had a prelude. And that was enough.
