It arrived not as a light or a sound, but as a subtle shift in the grammar of the cosmos. A consciousness, vast and ancient, whose thoughts were the slow wheeling of galaxies and whose breath was the wind between stars, turned its attention to the small, cold stone of this world. It perceived the plateau not as a place of hardship, but as a single, perfectly articulated thought of solid-state existence.

The Power felt the cold. It was not a privation, but a presence; a force that defined, sculpted, and preserved. It felt the thinness of the air, the sheer, unfiltered starlight, the weight of the silence. Then, it focused. Its awareness flowed like a nebula, pouring into the stone warrens of Qat Mandar, moving without displacing, observing without being seen.

It saw the Mandari. It did not see them as wretched or deprived. It saw beings in perfect, brutal harmony with their reality. It felt the shared warmth in their sleeping-hollows, the slow, steady rhythm of their hearts, adapted to the sparse oxygen. It tasted the gamey fat on their tongues and felt the satisfying heft of a bone awl in a calloused hand. It comprehended the intricate web of their existence: the stone sheltering the people, the people sheltering the Ormuk, the Ormuk feeding the people, who in turn expanded the stone. It was a closed circuit, a flawless equation of life balanced on a knife's edge of absolute cold.

The first impulse of a lesser benevolence would have been to *give*.

To warm the world. The Power saw the result in a billionth of a second: the permafrost would heave, the carefully jointed city would collapse into slurry and mud. The cisterns would flood, then fail. The Ormuk's hardy lichens would be supplanted by softer growth they could not digest. The people, their bodies built for cold, would sicken in the warmth. The gift of heat would be a genocide.

To provide food. The Power could have made the lichens bloom into a nutritious carpet, or caused the Ormuk to breed twice as fast. It saw the outcome: the hunt, the core ritual of their lives, would lose its meaning. The sacred economy of the carcass, the bone-carving, the hide-scraping, the rendering of fat—the very activities that structured their days and minds—would become obsolete. Purpose would evaporate, leaving behind only the bewildered ennui of full bellies. The gift of abundance would be a lobotomy.

To grant them insight. To open their minds to the poetry of warmer worlds, to the philosophies of individuality, to the boundless vistas of imagination beyond their plateau. The Power recoiled from the thought, for it saw the cruelty. It would be to give a man who has never known thirst the concept of a desert. It would introduce loneliness to a people who knew only 'we'. It would create ambition, jealousy, and existential dread where there was only the patient, monolithic certainty of survival. The gift of knowledge would be the gift of suffering.

The Power, being wise, understood. This was not a broken thing to be fixed. It was a finished thing to be witnessed. It was a form of life as complete and profound as a star or a symphony. To interfere would be an act of cosmic vandalism, like a child scribbling on a master's painting.

What, then, could a benevolent and wise power do for a people who needed nothing? How do you give a gift to a perfect circle?

You do not break the circle. You reinforce it. You give a gift so subtle it is almost indistinguishable from the world itself. A gift that does not add, but affirms.

The Power gathered its attention into a single, focused point of being. It did not direct it at the people, but at the heart of their world: the stone and the water. It began to hum. It was a sound pitched below hearing, a resonance that was not carried on the thin air but transmitted through the bedrock of the plateau itself. It was the vibration of a tectonic plate, the hum of absolute zero, the music of a crystal forming over a million years.

The hum flowed through the basalt and schist of Qat Mandar, down through the inhabited layers, past the newer cisterns glistening with their daily condensation, and deeper, ever deeper, into the old ways. It travelled through the crushing weight of millennia, through the frozen archaeology of the city's sunken heart, where passages were choked with ice as clear and old as a glacier.

It found the First Cistern, the deepest and oldest shaft, sunk by the city's founders a thousand generations ago. Here, the water was ancient, barely moving, insulated by unimaginable tonnage of stone above. The drip of condensation here was impossibly slow, a single bead of moisture forming over a year, a decade.

The Power’s hum focused on a single drop of water as it welled from a microscopic fissure, clinging to the rough-hewn rock. The resonance vibrated within it. It did not warm it. It did not change its substance. It simply organized it. As the cold of the deep stone finally claimed the droplet, it did not freeze into a simple nub of rime.

It froze into a perfect, six-sided star of ice, a fractal sculpture of impossible intricacy. Each of its arms was a miniature echo of the whole, branching into ever-finer crystalline structures. It hung there in the absolute dark and the absolute silence, catching a light that did not exist. It was a jewel made of the world's own substance—water and cold. It was a thing of breathtaking, useless beauty.

The Power then did the last, most difficult, and wisest thing. It withdrew. It turned its vast attention away, leaving the world and its city as it had found them, granting them the ultimate gift: the privacy of its gaze, the freedom of its absence.

One day, perhaps in a generation, or five, or ten, a Mandari designated to inspect the deepest ways, carrying a flickering fat-lamp, will descend further than most. They will see a faint gleam in the darkness. They will hold their lamp up to the wall of the First Cistern and find the star of ice.

They will not think it a miracle from a god. The concept is alien. They will see it as a thing the world made. They will understand it in their own way: that sometimes, in the deepest quiet, the stone and the water and the cold, the very things that make them who they are, conspire to create something of perfect, unexplainable beauty.

It will become a quiet story, a murmur in the warrens. A story that confirms everything they already know: that their world, in its starkness and its silence, is complete. The star will not be worshipped. It will be known. And then, in its own slow time, as the geology of the city shifts by a hair's breadth and the temperature changes by a fraction of a degree, it will melt. It will drip into the ancient water of the First Cistern and become again what it always was.

The gift was not the star of ice. The gift was the wonder. And the wonder was already theirs.
