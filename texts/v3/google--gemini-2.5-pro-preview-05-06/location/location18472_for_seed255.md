This place announces itself first with sound. Not a single sound, but a chorus of immense, industrial groaning. It is a constant, foundational hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a bass note so deep it feels like the world’s own unconquerable tinnitus. Layered over this is the percussive, brittle shattering of cooling material, the sharp hiss of escaping steam, and a high, grinding shriek that comes and goes, like immense teeth being set. The air is loud enough to make conversation a shouted ordeal, and silence an impossible memory.

It is a world of utter darkness, punctuated by a slow, rhythmic pulse of dull, cherry-red light. The light does not come from a sun or a moon, but from the landscape itself. It is a vast and open basin, perhaps miles across, but it is not empty. The entire expanse is a dense, near-impenetrable forest of terracotta.

There is no soil, no rock, no substrate that has not been made. The floor is a solid, fused-ceramic plate, slick with a constant, cold condensation. From this floor, growing in chaotic profusion, are the structures. They are spires, walls, fins, baffles, and arches, all extruded from the ground itself. They are packed together so tightly that in many areas they create winding, claustrophobic canyons, but seen from any high point, they form a sprawling, regional pattern, like a city built by geological forces.

This is a world under construction. The "erosion" is reversed; it is a landscape of accretion. The forms are raw, embryonic. You can find sections of the floor that are soft, like wet clay, pliable and warm. In these areas, new structures are being born. They rise slowly, pushed up from below by some unimaginable, primitive mechanism. The process is not delicate. The surfaces of the new spires are scarred with colossal tool marks: the gouges of a planet-sized trowel, the combing lines of a rake hundreds of feet wide. The technology is simple, but the scale is incomprehensible.

As a new wall or pillar ascends, its core begins to glow with that sullen, cherry-red heat. This is the source of the light, the pulse that illuminates the condensation beading on the surfaces of older, cool structures nearby. This is also the source of the dampness. The intense heat of formation boils some hidden, internal fluid, which vents from fissures as plumes of hissing steam. The steam billows into the cool, dark air, then condenses and falls as a perpetual, chilling drizzle, coating everything in a slick, greasy film of moisture that is not quite water. It has a faint, coppery taste. The air smells of wet clay, ozonic discharge, and hot metal.

Everything is a shade of fired earth: ochre, sienna, umber, charcoal gray. Some of the oldest, tallest structures, those that have been cool for millennia, have developed a crazed, crackle-glaze surface from eons of thermal shock. Water—or the local equivalent—weeps from these cracks, tracing dark paths down the massive ceramic faces. In the lowest crevices, where the mist pools, shallow, stagnant basins form, their bottoms coated in a rust-colored silt. There is no life in them. No algae, no bacteria, nothing. It is a sterile, mineral soup.

Life here is exceptionally rare, and it is not biological in any familiar sense. For days, the only movement is the slow extrusion of the land and the billowing of steam. But then, a new sound joins the cacophony: a rhythmic *chink-chink-chink*, sharp and clear. High up on the face of a colossal terracotta wall, a section of the surface flakes away. A creature emerges, perfectly camouflaged. It is a Grylle, an arthropod-like being whose carapace is made of the same ceramic material as the world. It is not grown, but assembled, its body a mosaic of interlocking plates. It moves with a stiff, clockwork gait, its multi-jointed legs chipping at the wall as it climbs. The Grylle is a lithovore, feeding on the raw, unfired clay-paste deep within the active extrusion zones. It carves tunnels into the nascent structures, its mandibles, tipped with a material harder than diamond, leaving the same gouging marks as the titanic tools that build the world. Seeing one is an event. Their populations are small, their lives slow, and they spend most of their time sealed inside the walls, hibernating for centuries between feeding cycles.

The rule of this place is simple: it grows. It is a single, monolithic artifact, a piece of pottery the size of a continent, caught in the perpetual act of being thrown and fired. Everything is a consequence of this one, dynamic process. The sound is the friction of its making. The darkness is the absence of a star. The light and heat are from the kiln. The water is its sweat. The fauna are the parasites that have learned to eat the clay before it is hardened by the fire. You are standing in the Umbilicus, the place where a world is being born not of fire and rock, but of craft and clay. And it is not finished yet. It will not be finished for a very, very long time.
