The air is a hot, wet weight. It does not feel like air but like a fluid you have to push through, a fine-grained steam that clings to you instantly, beading on any exposed surface and soaking through fabric in seconds. The heat is not the bright, dry heat of a sun; it is a close, geothermal warmth that rises from the ground itself, a planetary fever. It smells of hot stone, of ozone, and of a deep, metallic tang like old blood.

You stand on a surface of slick, black, obsidian-like material, worn smooth as a river stone but impossibly vast. It is not ground, not soil. It is a pavement. Below your feet, you can feel a deep, slow, and almost infrasonic thrum, the patient pulse of a world-sized engine.

The landscape is a great, shallow basin, a city slumbering in a dark, eternal swamp. But this is not a city that was built. It is a city that grew. Towers of the same black, glassy material swell up from the pavement, their forms slumped and rounded, as if they were once molten and have been cooling for millennia. They are not separate buildings but fused nodes in a single, sprawling entity, connected by low, broad arches and causeways that span the channels between them. There are no sharp corners, no straight lines. Every edge has been softened by eons of condensing steam and weeping fluid. The architecture is organic, exoskeletal, like the carapace of some colossal, sleeping beetle. Some towers are hollow, their cavernous interiors echoing with the slow, resonant *plink... plink...* of heavy droplets falling from a great height.

Through this city run the "streets," but they are not for walking. They are shallow, wide canals filled with a warm, viscous fluid. It is not water. It is heavier, clearer, and moves with a syrupy lethargy. Where it pools, it shimmers with a faint, oily iridescence. This fluid is the city's blood. It seeps from hairline fractures in the pavement, flows slowly through the channels, and is drawn up into the bases of the great, hollow spires in a silent, continuous circulation. The city is drinking. The atmosphere is its respiration, a constant exhalation of steam that condenses and drips back onto its own skin.

There is almost nothing that could be called vegetation. Here and there, growing from the edges of the fluid channels, are sparse clusters of delicate, crystalline filaments. They are a brittle, translucent amber color, and they glow with a faint, internal light, like fiber optics powered by the basin's geothermal heat. They are the only life, and they are more mineral than plant. On the sheer faces of the towers, metallic, rust-colored sheens spread like lichen, patterns that shift and rearrange over the course of days, a form of geological chromatography.

This is a world of near-total darkness. There is no sun, no moon. The only illumination comes from the faint, warm glow of the crystalline growths and, more profoundly, from the city itself. The black, vitreous material of the structures is not entirely opaque. Deep within it, vast and complex patterns of soft, blue-green light pulse with the same slow, deep rhythm as the thrumming underfoot. It is the city's nervous system, a map of its metabolic processes, visible but incomprehensible. The light ebbs and flows, creating shifting, mobile constellations deep within the stone.

The silence is a presence. There are no animal calls, no rustle of leaves, no wind. The air is too thick and heavy to move with any speed. The soundscape is one of drips, of the thick, quiet gurgle of the fluid moving through its channels, and the deep, pervasive hum that is felt more than heard. In the hollow towers, the acoustics are strange; a single drop falling can create a cascade of chiming echoes that lasts for minutes, a form of slow, accidental music.

And the place feels wrong. Distances are deceptive in the dim, pulsing light. A causeway that appears to be a stone's throw away might take an hour to reach, its arching path subtly refusing Euclidean geometry. The sense of perspective shifts, and the colossal scale of the structures can suddenly collapse, making them feel like intricate models, before swelling again to horizon-spanning immensity. There is a sense of being observed, not by any creature, but by the city itself. Its slow, pulsing light feels like a state of consciousness, a dream so deep and so old that the presence of a fleeting, warm-blooded thing is a novelty noted without interest, a mote of dust settling on a sleeping god. The single, unifying rule of this world is the city's own metabolic process, a dynamic equilibrium of heat, fluid, and slow, crystalline growth that has been in place for longer than mountains have existed, and will continue long after they are gone.
