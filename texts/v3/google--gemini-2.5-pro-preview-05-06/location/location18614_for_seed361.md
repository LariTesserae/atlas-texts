The first thing you feel is the sound. It is not something you hear, but something you are *in*, a medium as physical as water. It has pressure. It is a dome of a million chittering, vibrating, clicking, and whistling sources, a wall of granulated noise so dense it feels warm against the skin. You have to shout to hear yourself think, but there is no one else here to shout to.

This place is a cup in the palm of the world. A basin, perhaps a mile across, sunk into a high, ancient plateau. The rim of the basin is not a sharp cliff but a weary, rounded shoulder of stone, worn smooth by aeons of wind that no longer seems to blow. The rock here is a deep, dark basalt, polished to a dull satin finish, showing the texture of its cooling not as sharp crystals but as slow, knuckled whorls, like petrified muscle. It is so old it feels like the planet's skeleton, finally exposed. There is no soil.

And yet, it is lush. Life does not grow *from* the ground, it clings *to* it. Great, swollen mats of vegetation sprawl across the stone floor and up the curved walls, like thick green pelts. They are islands of intense life in a sea of bare, damp rock. The plants have fat, waxy, paddle-shaped leaves, many of them cupped to catch the air's ambient moisture. There are ferns with fronds like iridescent peacock feathers, and mosses that glow with a faint, oily chartreuse. Nothing is delicate. Everything is thick, rubbery, and engorged with water it seems to drink directly from the humid, heavy air. The air itself is a perpetual mist, a warm tropical breath that condenses on every surface, leaving the dark stone perpetually weeping.

Between these verdant, sprawling colonies are expanses of the smooth, dark, empty stone. This is where the sparseness lives. You can walk for a hundred yards on nothing but slick, resonant rock before reaching another riot of growth.

And stretched between them, almost invisible, is the technology. They are filaments, like strands of a spider’s web spun from glass, but impossibly thin and perfectly straight. They run from anchor points on the basin floor to others high on the rim, forming a vast, complex lacework ceiling just below the level of the open sky. In the full light of day, you only see them when the sun glints off a particular angle, revealing a cat’s cradle of pure geometry laid over the organic chaos below. These are the Conductors. Studded into the ancient basalt are their anchors: small, off-white ceramic-like discs, barely a handspan across, cool to the touch and utterly unmarked. They are the only built things here, scattered and unassuming.

The Conductors hum. It is a sound so low and so pure it is almost below hearing, a vibration you feel in your teeth and bones. This hum is the one constant note beneath the overwhelming orchestra of the fauna. It is the hum of the world's engine, and it is doing something. The air near the filaments tingles, smelling of ozone and wet metal. It is these Conductors that pull moisture from the sky, that maintain the impossible warmth at this altitude, that manage the atmosphere of this hidden terrarium.

This place is ruled by cycles. For hours, the sound of the fauna builds. The space is a frenzy of life. Swarms of beetle-like creatures with carapaces of polished chrome and gold take to the air in shimmering, liquid clouds. Tiny, fluting amphibians, no bigger than a thumbnail, their skin the color of poison-dart frogs, call from the bellies of the cupped leaves, their combined chorus a piercing treble that drills into your ears. Avian mimics with drab, brown feathers flit from one vegetation mat to another, perfectly replicating the clicks of the insects and the chiming of the filaments, creating a disorienting, repeating echo that makes it impossible to locate any single source of sound. The sheer biomass is staggering, a roiling, deafening expression of life's will to exist.

Then, with no warning, a new sound joins the symphony: a sharp, rising chime that emanates from the Conductor filaments themselves. The mimics fall silent, instantly. The insect swarms become frantic. The note climbs in pitch until it is a knife's edge of sound, and then a section of the filament network flashes with a brief, silent, blue-white light. Where the light touches the swarms, they vaporize into a puff of grey dust that drifts down to feed the waxy plants. The sound wall collapses by half, a sudden, shocking quiet that leaves a ringing in your ears.

For a few moments, the only noises are the dripping water, the deep hum of the Conductors, and the terrified rustling of the survivors. This is the cull. It’s an active, engineered process. The basin is a farm, a reactor, a garden, an engine—and the fauna is its fuel. Slowly, tentatively, the chorus begins again. A single clicker-beetle tests the air. An amphibian resumes its whistling call. Within an hour, the noise will be rebuilding toward its next crescendo, the pressure will be rising, and the cycle will continue, ancient and strange, in a place hidden from the world but filled to the brim with a life and a purpose that is not its own.
