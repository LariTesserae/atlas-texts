The world is a tableland of stone. It is a continent-sized plateau, a tectonic plate recently and violently forced upwards, so new that the mountains at its perimeter are still shedding rock in sharp, angular sheets. The air at this height is thin and carries a permanent, biting cold. There is no warmth here, only degrees of freezing. The sun is a bright, clear disc in a sky of deep, crystalline violet, its light unfiltered by haze or humidity, casting shadows that are as sharp and black as obsidian shards.

This is the setting for the city, Qat Mandar. It is not a city that rises, but one that grips. It sprawls across hundreds of square miles of the plateau, a dense, low-slung mass of construction that seems almost geological in its scale and texture. From a high vantage point on a neighbouring peak, it might be mistaken for a particularly complex scree field or a fossilized moraine.

The city is built of the world's most abundant resource: stone. Countless quadrilaterals of dark basalt and grey schist are fitted together with staggering precision. There is no mortar; the cold would shatter it. Instead, the blocks are hewn with such care that they join in near-seamless lines, relying on weight and expert jointing for their stability. It is a monument to patience, the work of a thousand generations. The buildings are rarely more than two stories tall, with thick, battered walls and low, heavy roofs made of overlapping stone slates caulked with a mixture of mud and animal fat that freezes into a solid, waterproof seal. The density is immense. There are no grand plazas or wide boulevards. The city is a warren, a labyrinth of narrow, winding alleys and covered passages, some barely wide enough for two people to pass. This design is a concession to the cold; the tightly packed structures shelter one another from the ceaseless wind that scours the plateau, and the narrow passages trap what little warmth escapes from the interiors.

Water is scarce, but ever-present. It doesn't flow in rivers, but weeps from the stone. The slight geothermal heat generated by the dense population and their activities, trapped beneath the city's mass, causes a slow, constant melt at the base of the permafrost. This moisture percolates through the rock and condenses on the cold interior walls of the deeper passages and lower levels. The walls of these old ways glisten with a permanent dampness, beaded with liquid that freezes into intricate traceries of rime ice overnight. Inhabitants have carved small, winding channels into the stone floors to guide this condensation into communal cisterns, deep shafts drilled into the rock where the water collects, insulated from the surface cold. In the briefest part of the year, when the sun's angle is just right, a weak seasonal melt may create slushy flows on the surface, but for the most part, the city's water is harvested from its own cold breath.

The overwhelming quiet is the first thing an outsider would notice. The thin air doesn't carry sound well, and the stone walls swallow it. The wind is a constant, low moan in the high places, but down in the alleys, a thick hush prevails. The sounds are small and intimate: the scuff of a felt-soled boot on stone, the muffled murmur of voices from behind a heavy hide curtain, the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of a bone needle weaving through tough leather, the faint, wet drip of condensing water in a cistern.

Life here is possible because of the fauna. The plateau is home to titanic herds of great-bodied ruminants, beasts called the Ormuk. They are shaggy, slow-moving creatures with thick, oily wool and layers of blubber, adapted to the extreme cold. They graze on the sparse but hardy vegetation—mats of lichen that cling to the rocks like verdigris, and tough, bladed grasses that find purchase in the gravelly soil, drawing moisture from the seasonal frost-thaw cycle. The Ormuk are the city's sole reason for being. The people of Qat Mandar are not just hunters; they are tenders of the herds. The city itself acts as a massive windbreak, creating a vast lee on its downwind side where the Ormuk can shelter and the snow accumulation is different, exposing the tough vegetation.

The people live in a slow, symbiotic rhythm with these animals. Every part of the Ormuk is used. Their meat is the primary food source, often eaten frozen in thin slices or slow-cooked in sealed pots over faint embers. Their thick hides are used for clothing, blankets, and as heavy, insulating curtains over doorways. Their bones, dense and strong, are carved into tools: awls, needles, scrapers, and the load-bearing columns for the covered passages. The rendered fat provides the fuel for the city’s lamps—small, smoky stone bowls that give off a dim, flickering light and a thick, gamey smell that permeates everything. Even their dried dung is collected and used as a precious, slow-burning fuel for warmth.

The city grows downwards and outwards, not upwards. New construction occurs on the ever-expanding periphery, while the oldest sections at the city's heart are slowly sinking. Over millennia, the lower levels of the original city have become subterranean, crushed by the weight of new layers, their passages filled with slow-flowing ice and the detritus of ages. They are the city's memory, a frozen archaeology.

Life is dense and communal. Families live in close quarters, their bodies sharing warmth in sleeping alcoves piled with furs. There is no concept of privacy as it is understood elsewhere; there is only the collective, a single, sprawling organism hunkered down for a winter that never truly ends. Change comes not in years or decades, but in the slow, glacial creep of the city's edge across the stone, and in the gradual shift of the Ormuk herds as they follow their own ancient, patient cycles across the vast, cold roof of the world.
