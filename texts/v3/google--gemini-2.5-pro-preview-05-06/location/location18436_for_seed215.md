This is a landscape of wet cold and immense, unfinished surfaces. It is a high plateau the size of a continent, a vast tableland still in the early stages of its own creation. The ground underfoot is not rock, not yet. It is something prior, a substrate of uniform, gunmetal grey material, slick with a perpetual film of condensation. It has the texture of fine-grained ceramic, cold to the touch, and it rings with a faint, low hum that seems to emanate from kilometers below.

This is not a world shaped by erosion, but by extrusion. The geology is raw, embryonic. Across the plains, which stretch to a horizon blurred by distance and mist, features are actively, if slowly, emerging. Low, angular ridges push up from the substrate like the bones of a skeleton surfacing through skin. They have no scree slopes, no weathered faces; their edges are as sharp as fresh-broken flint, their surfaces smooth and unmarked. In other areas, the ground swells into vast, shallow domes, miles across, their surfaces covered in a perfect hexagonal crazing like the bottom of a dried-up lakebed, but on a tectonic scale. These features are not yet mountains or hills; they are proposals for them, geological sketches waiting to be filled in.

The atmosphere is a constant participant. The temperature hovers just below freezing. A perpetual, high-altitude mist, born from the weeping substrate and the condensing air, blankets everything. It is not a dense, vision-blocking fog, but a luminous shroud that diffuses the light of a pale, distant sun into a soft, shadowless pearlescence. The world is lit as if from within a cloud. This constant wetness clings to every surface, freezing into an exquisite, crystalline lacework of rime. Every sharp edge of the emerging ridges is furred with it; every strand of vegetation is sheathed in ice. The air is still and heavy, and sounds are muffled by the moisture, creating a profound, absorbent quiet. The loudest sound is often the crunch and squeak of one’s own boots on the frosted ground.

Vegetation is sparse but tenacious. It consists almost entirely of two types. The first are fields of crystalline ‘grass’, glassy, needle-like stalks that grow in scattered clumps. They are not green but a pale, milky blue, and they draw energy not from the weak sun but from the low hum of the substrate itself. When a rare, dry wind finds its way across the plateau, these fields of glass chimes with a delicate, dissonant music that can be heard for miles in the otherwise silent landscape. The second plant form is a kind of techno-organic lichen that grows in fractal patterns across the warmer surfaces of the emergent structures. Locals call it ‘syntax lichen’. It glows with a faint internal light, pulsing in slow, complex rhythms, and it is said that these patterns are not random, but a physical manifestation of the system’s underlying code, the world writing itself into existence.

Despite the desolation, life is abundant, though spread thin across the vastness. Great, lumbering herds of six-legged grazers, the size of elephants, roam the plains. Their bodies are covered in thick, white, insulating hair caked with ice, and they move with a slow, deliberate gait, their broad, metallic feet distributing their weight over the brittle substrate. They are called Plateau Striders, and they feed by scraping the syntax lichen from the geofacts with specialized, tungsten-hard teeth.

Hunting them are packs of Rime-Stalkers, lithe, quadrupedal predators that look like a cross between a greyhound and a reptile. Their skin is slick and black, providing no purchase for the ice, and they hunt using hypersensitive thermal pits arrayed around their snouts, spotting the Striders' body heat through the clinging mist. But the true apex presence here is rarely seen. The entire plateau is crisscrossed by subterranean entities known as Conduit Worms. These are not truly fauna, but colossal, semi-sentient maintenance drones, a part of the world’s fundamental infrastructure. They are kilometers long and move through the substrate like a whale through water. Their passage is the only true geological violence this place knows. The ground will groan and heave, and a long, rolling wave will visibly travel across the plains as a worm passes beneath, extruding a new ridge or recalibrating a power conduit, its purpose unknowable. To be on the surface during a 'wormfall' is to experience a localized earthquake.

And this is a city. It is called Aethelburg. It is not built *on* the plateau; it is an expression *of* it. Habitation towers, data hubs, and transit lines are not constructed from imported materials but are extruded from the same substrate as the ridges and domes. A tower might rise from the plain as a smooth, featureless spire of the same gunmetal grey material, tapering to a point a thousand meters up. From a distance, it is indistinguishable from a natural feature. Only up close does one see the pinpricks of light from windows, set flush with the surface, and the silent, glowing portals that serve as doors.

The city is urban in its complexity and population, but sparse in its physical density. These towers and hubs are scattered miles apart, islands of activity in the vast, quiet plain. They are connected not by roads but by lines of pure light that run at ground level—transport beams that carry matter and people from one node to the next at near-instantaneous speeds. Life is lived mostly indoors, in the warm, stable environments within the towers. To go outside is to enter the wild, to step into the cold, wet, active process of the world's own birth. The rules of this place are simple: the ground beneath your feet is not a finished product. The weather is a byproduct of planetary engineering. The life is part of the system, and the city is its nervous system. Everything is in motion, part of a single, vast, technological organism awakening into being.
