This place exists as a single, immense chamber, a geode of industry cracked open in the deep crust of the world. The air is warm, thick, and tastes of wet metal, ozone, and something like crushed flowers. It's never silent. The dominant sound is a high, statistical wash—the sound of a trillion tiny lives in motion, a constant, crystalline chittering that fills the upper registers. Beneath it, a slow, industrial heartbeat: the rhythmic *thump-hiss… thump-hiss* of massive, tired machinery.

The space is a cathedral, but a living one. What would be stone pillars are colossal, woody trunks, their surfaces a dark, petrified grey-brown that is worn smooth as river stone. They are not trees, but something else—a singular, sprawling organism that forms the entire architecture of the chamber. This vast plant-structure, the Weft, rises from the slick, dark floor and branches out into gothic arches that merge with the unseen ceiling hundreds of feet above. There are a few dozen of these major pillars, widely spaced, creating vast open volumes of humid air between them. This sparseness of structure is deceptive, for the air itself is not empty.

It is filled with the fauna. They are the source of the chittering, and the source of the only light. Swarms of beetle-like creatures, each no bigger than a thumbnail, drift in shimmering, riverine clouds through the open spaces. They are the Cinder-mites. Their abdomens pulse with a weak, dying-ember glow, a soft amber light that is constantly in motion. These clouds of light coalesce and disperse, flowing like smoke on invisible currents, their collective bioluminescence providing a dim, shifting illumination that throws the colossal pillars into deep, soft-edged shadow. The light never holds still.

Water is a constant presence, but not in pools or rivers. The warmth generated by the industrial processes radiates from the Weft itself, meeting the cool, deep-earth reality of the cavern's outer shell. The result is a perpetual, heavy condensation. Water beads on every surface. It traces slow paths down the sides of the woody pillars, gathering in the ancient, eroded grooves carved into their bark before dripping heavily to the floor. The air is so saturated that a fine mist hangs in the upper vaults, catching the amber light of the Cinder-mites in a diffuse, hazy glow. The ground is not soil but the same slick, non-porous material as the pillars, covered in a treacherous, glassy film of moisture.

This is a city, or what remains of one. Built *into* the living architecture of the Weft is a vast and ancient factory. This is not a human place; the logic is alien. Great, corroded copper vents, bloomed with green verdigris, open like monstrous gills on the sides of the pillars. From these vents, a powerful inflow of air sucks in the swarming Cinder-mites. You can see the shimmering rivers of light narrow into funnels as they are drawn inexorably toward the intakes.

Inside the pillars—which are not solid, but hollowed-out with a labyrinthine complexity—is the machinery. You can see it in places where the woody flesh has been peeled back or worn away. Massive, rust-pitted pistons, slick with condensing water, punch slowly in and out of organic sleeves. Toothed gears of an unknown, dark metal are enmeshed directly with the fibrous, living tissue of the Weft, turning with a groaning reluctance. The sound is deafening up close: the shriek of metal on metal, the hydraulic sigh of pressure being released, the percussive impact of the captured mites being crushed and processed.

The entire ecosystem is a closed, dynamic loop. The Weft, this plant-like substrate, seems to cultivate the Cinder-mites. In shallow, basin-like hollows high up on its branches, fungal blooms provide the larval stage with sustenance. The mites mature, take flight, and fill the cathedral with their light and sound. Then, the integrated machinery—the city's industry—harvests them. The factory is the apex predator.

What does it produce? From the base of a few of the most massive pillars, a single, thick pipe of the same dark metal juts out. From its end drips a slow, viscous, and utterly black paste. It falls with a thick *plop* onto the slick floor, where it pools for a time before slowly being reabsorbed into the living material of the Weft's base, a nutrient cycle of baffling inefficiency. The process seems to be the entire point. The city exists to power the factory, the factory exists to harvest the mites, the mites exist to be harvested, and the final product exists only to feed the city.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute. There is no outside. There is no sunlight. There is only the Weft, the Cinder-mites, and the Machine. Everything is old, established, locked in its cycle. The edges of the gears are worn round. The intake vents are polished smooth by the passage of trillions of bodies. The water-carved channels in the pillars are deep and settled. The place is not growing or dying; it is simply *running*. A vast, automated, biomechanical engine built by someone long-forgotten, for a purpose now lost, churning endlessly in the warm, dim, buzzing dark.
