This is a place caught in the first deep breath after its creation. The land is a high, sprawling basin, a valley cradled between mountain peaks so sharp they seem to have been carved yesterday. The rock is a dark, glassy schist, fractured into knife-edged plates and torrential scree slopes that look perilously unstable. Every edge is crisp; every crack is a fresh wound in the stone. A geologist would call this range impossibly, violently young. But there is no dust in the air, no grumble of settling earth. The feeling of instability is a photograph, not a process.

The air is thin and cool, carrying the clean scent of wet stone and cold chlorophyll. It doesn't move. There is no wind here, only a silent, pervasive dampness. Water does not so much fall as it simply *arrives*. It manifests as a fine, condensing mist that clings to every surface, beading on the leaves of ferns and slicking the dark rock until it gleams like polished obsidian. In the lowest parts of the valley, this moisture gathers into still, clear pools and slow-moving sheets of water that lip over stone ledges without a sound, their surfaces perfect mirrors for the flat, grey-white light of the sky. This water is fundamentally inert; it lubricates but does not erode. It wets but does not wear away.

Across the valley floor and up its gentler slopes are the structures. They are not clustered but scattered with immense space between them, giving the impression less of a city and more of a grand, unfinished architectural plan. These are low-slung, powerful buildings made from the same dark, shattered stone of the mountains. Walls are composed of enormous, flat plates of schist, stacked with impossible precision, their edges so tight that no mortar is needed. Roofs are tiered layers of the same material, angled to guide the condensing dew into channels and cisterns carved directly into the bedrock. Broad, empty plazas paved with carefully fitted stone connect clusters of buildings. There is infrastructure—aqueducts that are more like shallow, silent canals; wide, stepped paths—but it all feels ceremonial, oversized for its use. The technology is one of leverage, patience, and an intimate understanding of the stone. Chisel marks are visible on some blocks, the work of copper or perhaps meteoric iron tools, but the scale of the construction speaks of a collective effort spanning generations, even if time here does not seem to pass.

Vegetation is not uniformly distributed but grows in lush, discrete gardens. In the spaces between the stone plazas and at the feet of cliffs, life thrives. Great, heavy-fronded ferns uncurl in arrested motion. Mosses, thick as fleece and saturated with a dozen shades of green, blanket entire rock faces but never creep an inch beyond their established borders. Hardy, broad-leafed trees with smooth, pale bark grow in solemn groves, their leaves perpetually slick with moisture, each one a perfect specimen without a single tear or blight. Nothing is overgrown; nothing is withered. Every plant exists in its perfect state of maturity.

The quiet is the most unsettling feature. It is a deep, profound silence, not of emptiness, but of held breath. The fauna is abundant, yet contributes almost nothing to the soundscape. Herds of white, shaggy-furred creatures, like muskoxen with the graceful horns of ibex, move with a slow, deliberate gait across the high meadows. They graze on the un-diminishing mosses, their footfalls muffled by the damp turf. In the sky, huge, dark birds with vulturine bodies and the iridescent heads of magpies hang motionless on invisible currents that do not exist. You can see the twitch of a head, the blink of a nictitating membrane, but they never seem to beat their wings. On the rock faces, marmot-like animals with stony, patterned fur sit upright on their haunches, watching with black, unreadable eyes. They live, they breathe, they move, but they do so within a framework of absolute equilibrium. There are no sudden cries, no territorial disputes, no panicked scurrying.

And among the stone structures, the people. They move with the same dreamlike slowness as the animals. They are tall and spare, wrapped in woven fabrics of grey and green. They perform tasks that seem to be rituals of maintenance. One might spend an entire day polishing a single stone slab with a piece of leather and a paste of fine grit until it shines. Another might trace the carved channels of an aqueduct with a long, wooden rake, clearing away no debris, for there is none. A group might stand around a newly quarried block, tools in hand, seemingly in discussion, but no words are exchanged. They are the builders, but they are also the curators of a static creation.

This is the rule of the place: nothing truly changes. The dynamic processes of the universe—erosion, growth, decay, entropy—are suspended. The mountains are young because they are frozen at the moment of their violent birth. The rain falls, but the water has no power to wear stone. A leaf can be plucked from a tree, and in the morning, a new one will have materialized in its place, indistinguishable from the last. The people and animals do not seem to age. It is a city and a valley locked in a single, perfect, unshakable moment. It is not dead; it is simply finished. The only sound is the gentle, impossibly soft dripping of condensed water from a fern frond onto a stone that will never feel its impact.
