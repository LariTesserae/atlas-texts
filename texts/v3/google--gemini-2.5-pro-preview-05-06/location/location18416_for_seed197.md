This is the Sunken Oratorio.

From the dry, scrubby lip of the basin, it looks like a single, colossal banyan tree has filled the entire depression, a tangled knot of growth perhaps a mile across. The air grows thick and warm as you descend the crumbling, ancient path, and a sound begins to build—not the chirping of a jungle, but a solid, foundational hum, a chord that presses against your teeth.

You are not entering a forest. You are entering a building.

The vegetation here is absolute. There is no soil, no rock, no ground. You walk on a floor of compressed, springy humus woven from millennia of shed leaves and bark, layered like felt. What you thought were tree trunks are living pillars, buttresses of fibrous, bark-like material that soar hundreds of feet upwards to link into a vaulted ceiling of translucent, green-gold leaves. This is a single, interconnected organism, or a colony of organisms so anciently symbiotic they have become one. It has grown itself into a city. The "streets" are vast, echoing canyons between the pillars; the "districts" are wide, open plazas where the leaf-ceiling is thinner, letting down shafts of buttery, pollen-hazed light. The scale is that of a grand cathedral, but one that has grown, not been built. The air is damp, not from rain, which rarely penetrates the canopy, but from the constant, slow respiration of the place itself. Water beads on every surface, catching the light, and drips with a sound that is utterly lost in the cacophony.

And the sound. It is deafening. It is a physical presence. The entire plant-structure is hollow. The great pillars, the soaring buttresses, the arching vaults—they are all tubes and chambers, a lung and a larynx the size of a town. The wind, sighing across the basin's rim, is funneled into this immense biological instrument, creating a constant, overwhelming series of bass notes and resonant harmonics that vibrate through the floor and into your bones. This is the foundation of the Oratorio's song.

Layered over this are the sounds of its inhabitants. Life is riotously abundant. Creatures swarm and thrive in the Oratorio's self-made ecosystem. The sound is a chorus of a billion lives: the high, glassy clatter of insectoid swarms that flow like rivers through the upper vaults; the wet, percussive bellowing of immense, slug-like grazers that rasp nutrients from the living walls; the sharp, shrieking calls of winged predators that flicker in the hazy light. The sound is so dense and constant that it ceases to be information. It is simply pressure, an ocean of noise with no surface. Silence is an impossible, unimaginable concept here.

The space is full, yet it is empty. The sheer volume of the open air within the plant-cathedral is immense. Between the colossal, load-bearing pillars, the distance can be a quarter-mile of open, resonant space. You can see a creature on the far side of a plaza, its movements clear in the green light, yet it feels impossibly distant, a phantom in a void.

This is where reality begins to fray. The Oratorio does not obey the rules of the world outside its rim. The emptiness is not just a matter of distance, but a property of the space itself. Perspective warps. You might walk towards a pillar for ten minutes only to find it has receded. An echo might arrive before the call that made it. The deafening sound is not omnidirectional; it is subject to the same strange laws. The thrumming of the structure itself might seem to come from inside your own head, while the shriek of a pouncing hunter a dozen feet away sounds as if it is on the other side of the basin.

The flora and fauna are caught in this instability. Shimmer-wing moths the size of dinner plates flicker, their wings appearing to be in several places at once. Patches of the wall will suddenly erupt in a colony of bioluminescent fungi that bloom and decay in the space of a breath, casting strobing, chaotic shadows. The active, brutal dance of predator and prey is constant, but sometimes a hunter will lunge and its prey will simply de-resonate, fading into a visual static for a moment before reappearing several yards away, unharmed. The hunt is not just about speed and strength, but about navigating, or perhaps manipulating, the unstable physics of the place.

There is no technology here. Everything is biological process, from the growth of the architecture to the strange, localized breakdowns in causality. The Oratorio is ancient, established, and in a state of continuous, dynamic flux. It grows, it sheds, it repairs itself. Its inhabitants are born, they hunt, they die, their bodies feeding the compost floor that will, in turn, feed the living city. It is a closed system, a self-playing instrument, an urban ecosystem whose organizing principle is not intelligence, but a deep, resonant instability. To be within it is to be a note in its endless, deafening song.
