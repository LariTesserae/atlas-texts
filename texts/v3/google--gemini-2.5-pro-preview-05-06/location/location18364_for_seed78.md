This is a world of scaffolding, a cathedral of hollows. The dominant sound is the first and last thing you perceive: a deep, harmonic moan that is not one note but a thousand, the song of wind tearing through a continent-sized architecture. It is a place of profound, echoing darkness, cold enough that your breath turns to a cloud of glittering ice-dust that does not fall but is instantly snatched away by the gales.

The sky is a void of absolute black, starless and unlit. The only light comes from the world itself. Colossal, arching structures sweep up from an unseen nadir, so vast they create their own topography of peaks and valleys. They are not mountains of stone but of life, a form of vegetation that is more like living crystal. Think of it as ligno-silicate: a material with the tensile strength of wood, the hardness of quartz, and the slow, inexorable ambition of a plant. These are the Cantilevers.

They grow in sharp, geometric formations, evidence of their recent and ongoing genesis. They are not pillars but immense, branching lattices, like the skeletons of diatoms magnified to the scale of mountain ranges. They are hollow. Their surfaces are webs of interwoven, crystalline stalks, some as thick as ancient trees, others as fine as wire, creating a treacherous, semi-permeable ground. Between the major stalks are gaps, some just a handspan wide, others chasms that drop into the shrieking darkness for miles. The scale is planetary; these arches and buttresses span horizons, their distant forms wavering in the thin, frigid air, their bioluminescent cores pulsing a faint, cold blue and viridian green.

This internal light is the world’s sun. It is not bright, more of a deep-tissue glow that reveals the veins and arteries of the vast organisms. It paints the undersides of higher arches in shifting patterns and gives the ever-present mist a ghostly luminescence.

Moisture here is a cycle of breath and ice. The metabolic processes of the Cantilevers release vast plumes of water vapor from vents deep within their hollow structures. As this vapor meets the near-absolute-zero of the high-altitude air, it flash-freezes into rime, coating every surface in a thick, feathery crust of ice. The world glitters. But where the internal bioluminescence is strongest, or where friction from the ceaseless wind generates a pocket of relative warmth, this rime melts. The water doesn't rain; it condenses and runs. Rivulets of pure, cold water trace paths along the crystalline stalks, flowing downwards, carving temporary channels, and pooling in concave junctions before freezing again into lenses of black ice. Deeper inside the hollows of the largest Cantilevers, out of the wind, this meltwater forms great, slow-moving internal rivers that flow in total darkness, a hidden riverine system within the world’s skeleton.

The world is loud and empty. The emptiness is one of density; the vast majority of the volume is air, the voids between and within the Cantilevers. But this void is filled with sound. The Great Moan of the wind is the baseline. Layered over it is the constant crackle and pop of freezing condensation, the deep, resonant groans of the Cantilevers themselves as they grow and settle, stresses releasing like calving glaciers. And then there are the sounds of life.

Fauna is abundant, but rarely seen. To cross the voids, "skimsails" unfurl vast, membranous wings, catching the updrafts to glide for hundreds of miles from one Cantilever to the next. They are like giant, skeletal bats, their passage marked only by the sharp crack of their wings catching the wind and their piercing, sonar shrieks that they use to navigate the darkness. Clinging to the icy, vertical lattices are the "rime-crawlers," six-legged creatures with crystalline claws and adhesive pads, meticulously scraping away the softer, nutrient-rich frost for sustenance. Their chittering and the scrape-scrape-scrape of their claws on the ligno-silicate can be heard for miles on a clear day. In the internal rivers, blind, eel-like predators hunt by sensing pressure changes in the water. The air is never silent; it is a tapestry of calls, clicks, and wing-beats echoing in the immense emptiness.

There are traces of another presence. Not grand cities, but subtle marks of habitation. On sheer faces, a series of notches are carved into a crystalline stalk, too regular to be natural—a ladder. In a sheltered nook where three Cantilevers meet, a fine-spun net of skimsail sinew has been stretched across a gap, creating a windbreak and a collection point for drifting frost. These are "brittle-nests." Here and there, a shard of the densest ligno-silicate—a black, glassy substance like obsidian—has been flaked into a sharp-edged tool, a blade or a scraper, and then discarded.

The beings who made these are rarely, if ever, seen by outsiders. They are a people of the scaffold, climbers and listeners. They dress in the cured hides of skimsails and weave ropes from the fibrous inner bark of the Cantilevers. Their world is vertical. To them, "ground" is a meaningless concept. There is only the grip of their hands and feet, the song of the wind telling them of weather to come, and the pulsing map of the world’s light beneath their feet. They build no permanent structures, for the Cantilevers are always growing, always shifting. A safe haven one cycle might be a point of catastrophic fracture the next. They live in a state of dynamic transit, their lives as sharp, precarious, and transient as the rime-ice on which they depend.

The fundamental rule of this place is that nothing is solid. Every surface is a membrane over a void. Every substance is in a state of becoming—growing, freezing, melting, or breaking. To survive is to understand this lack of permanence, to read the stress-groans of the living world, and to know that a fall is not a fall to a floor, but a fall into the singing, endless dark.
