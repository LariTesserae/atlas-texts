The air is heavy enough to drink. It is cool and carries the taste of ozone and wet, cold iron. Water does not so much fall here as it simply *coalesces*, beading on every surface until the drops grow too fat and heavy, sloughing off in thick, syrupy runs. The ground, if it can be called that, is a shallow, unbroken basin of still, black water, no more than ankle-deep, whose bottom is a fine, silty mud that feels like velvet and chilled grease.

Rising from this universal pool is a forest of pillars. They are not trees. They are a dense, near-touching congress of what might be basalt, or some form of dark, vitrified slag. They are worn unnaturally smooth, as if polished by a million years of slow, non-abrasive flow, their surfaces glossy and perpetually slick with condensing atmosphere. The space feels small and contained, like a single, vast, flooded chamber in a cathedral built for insects. The pillars are close enough that you could often reach out and touch two or three at once, creating a disorienting, crowded verticality in the gloom. The light is a uniform, sourceless dimness, a permanent state of deep twilight that muffles vision and turns distance into an uncertain gradient of grey.

This is a world without plants. No moss, no lichen, no leaf. Yet, the pillars are not bare. They are coated in a thin, living film. It is a biological sheen, a kind of mineral-scurf that shines with a faint, greasy luminescence, like a patch of oil on water. In some places it is the colour of verdigris, in others a dull, coppery rust. This scurf is the bedrock of the ecology, a slow-metabolizing lithovoric slime that draws its nourishment directly from the minerals of the pillars and the super-saturated water that slicks them.

And upon this foundation, life is abundant. The dominant grazers are a species of heavy, snail-like creature, their shells not calcium but intricate lattices of metallic crystal that glitter with captured light. They move with painstaking slowness up and down the pillars, their file-like radulae making a faint, rhythmic *shfff... shfff... shfff...* sound as they scrape away the mineral-scurf. This is one of the few distinct noises in the mostly quiet space, a sound of patient consumption.

Their predators are stranger still. Long, multi-segmented beings, like a cross between a centipede and a moray eel, glide silently through the shallow water and coil with impossible grace around the pillars. Their bodies are a pale, almost translucent white, and they hunt by scent and vibration, their eyeless heads weaving back and forth. When they find a snail, they do not crush the metallic shell, but instead excrete a solvent from glands around their mouths, a clear, viscous fluid that audibly sizzles as it dissolves a single point on the crystal lattice, allowing the predator to sip out the creature within.

Scattered amongst this mineral forest are the machines. They are low, squat, enigmatic structures, mostly submerged in the black water. Their casings are made of a pockmarked, dark ceramic or a deeply corroded bronze, so old and weathered they seem to be a natural part of the landscape, subject to the same slow erosive forces as the pillars. They are industrial in design, with thick, armoured conduits snaking from them, looping over the water or disappearing into the silt. There are no fast-moving parts, no clanking pistons. Instead, they emit a deep, resonant hum that you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears. A single, small, shielded indicator light might glow with a steady amber or placid blue on the side of one, a lonely point of artificial colour in the monochrome gloom.

These machines are not idle. They are the heart of a slow, active process. They seem to be atmospheric precipitators or mineral condensers. They pull something from the air, or filter something from the water. A low, perfectly regular *thump... thump... thump...* echoes from the largest of them, the sound of a deep, internal piston cycling once every ten seconds. From outflow pipes just below the water's surface, they release a slightly warmer, nutrient-rich effluent—the waste from their process—which encourages thicker blooms of the mineral-scurf.

A strange symbiosis has formed. The machines seem to be farming the environment for some unknown resource, and their waste product is the fertilizer for the entire food chain. Small, chitinous creatures like metallic water-striders skate on the surface film, congregating near the outlets. The eel-like predators sometimes rest coiled atop the machine casings, absorbing their faint warmth.

The world here operates by its own rules. Nothing is fast. The cycles are geological, biological, and industrial, all intertwined and moving at a pace of deep, established equilibrium. The silence is the sound of water cohering. The activity is the patient scrape of a mouth on stone, the silent glide of a hunter, the unwavering thrum of a machine that has been running for a thousand years and may run for a thousand more. It is a sealed terrarium of slick stone, strange flesh, and forgotten engines, dense and breathing and utterly alien.
