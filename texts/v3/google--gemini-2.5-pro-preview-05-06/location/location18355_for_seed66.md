The air is thick enough to drink, warm and heavy with the taste of hot metal and wet rock. Light, diffuse and milky, filters down through a high, perpetual haze, illuminating a landscape that is a city, and a city that is a mountain range. This is a place of geological architecture, of constructions so vast and ancient they have become their own topography.

There are no sharp edges here. Millennia of a slow, persistent drizzle have rounded every corner, smoothed every surface. The city is built in layers, a cross-section of epochs exposed on a continental scale. The lowest levels are lost in a gloomy murk, strata of forgotten industry and crushed dwellings forming a foundation as wide as a mountain range. Above this, sprawling plateaus ofhabitation and machinery stretch for horizons that are themselves just other, higher tiers of the same construction. Causeways wide as glaciers span valleys that are not valleys at all, but simply gaps between megalithic structures. The material is a uniform, dark, igneous-like stone, but it's latticed with the dull glint of corroded alloys and the verdigris of colossal bronze fixtures.

Water is the blood of this place. It does not rain in storms, but the atmosphere is so saturated that every surface weeps. Water condenses and coalesces, tracing shimmering paths down the faces of kilometre-high walls. It pools in shallow, te-coloured basins on the city's plateaus and drips in a steady, maddening rhythm in the under-levels. There is no soil, no plant, not a single leaf or blade of grass. The world is stone, metal, and water.

Yet, it is overwhelmingly alive.

The lack of vegetation is not a sign of sterility but of a different metabolic path. The city itself is the primary producer. Deep within its core, geothermal and gravitic engines of incomprehensible size and age hum and groan. Their purpose is singular: to process the rock, air, and water, breaking them down and reconstructing them into a nutrient-rich slurry. This life-giving paste, a ruddy, proteinaceous ooze, is pumped upwards through a billion arteries of pipe and conduit. It coats designated feeding plains, flows in controlled canals, and is aerosolized into the dense air by shrieking atomizers.

And so, life swarms. The sound of the city is a duplex chord: the grave, hydraulic sigh of continental-scale pistons and the vast, grinding hum of the core machinery is the bass note. Over this, a constant, high-pitched descant of biotic noise: the chittering, clicking, and scraping of a trillion creatures.

The surfaces are never still. A shimmering, iridescent tide of beetle-like organisms, ranging from the size of a fingernail to the size of a fist, flows in dense rivers along the conduits, their hardened mandibles scraping the nutrient paste. Their carapaces, in every shade of oxidized metal, form seas of mobile colour. In the air, which is layered with temperature gradients and nutrient mists, float shimmering clouds of skittering motes, creatures that are little more than gas-bladders with vibrating cilia, filtering their sustenance from the fog.

Where the water drips, the walls are encrusted with sessile life. They resemble barnacles or anemones, with silicaceous shells peeling open to reveal soft, filter-feeding mouths that pulse in slow, hypnotic unison. They are the stationary grazers, their forms so dense they create a living, breathing texture on the ancient stone.

And there are hunters. Armoured, crab-like predators, some as large as carts, stalk the causeways with a slow, deliberate patience. They tap the ground with sensitive antennae, listening for the sclerotic clatter of the beetle swarms. Their movements are infrequent but decisive, a sudden clamp of massive pincers, a crunch, and then stillness. In the deep, pooled water of ancient reservoirs, long, pale things drift, blind and patient, waiting for any creature to fall from the levels above.

The world changes, but only on a geological timescale. A machine might complete one cycle every century. A new layer of the city might be completed over ten thousand years, its construction so gradual as to be imperceptible. The fauna lives and dies in its billions, but the overall population remains in a state of overwhelming, stable saturation. The ecosystem is in a long, slow balance with its mechanical heart. The city isn't a habitat for the fauna; the city and the fauna are a single, symbiotic organism. The stone and metal is the bone, the pipes are the veins, the factories are the gut, and the teeming life is the flesh, all of it wrapped in a skin of warm, wet air. It is a world without green, a world of rust and chitin, humming its loud, eternal, monotone song.
