This place is not found, but entered. The approach is a descent, not of steps, but of pressure and heat. You feel it in your sinuses first, a dry, baking pressure that robs the air of all moisture. The world outside, whatever it was, is forgotten, sealed away behind a final, narrow passage in the rock. You are now inside.

You stand on a ledge in a vast, enclosed chamber, a city carved into the interior of a geode the size of a mountain. It is a basin, low-slung and bowl-shaped, its scale both intimate and unnervingly vast. The air is dim, lit not by a sun but by the sullen, thermal glow of the rock itself. Hazy columns of light, thick with shimmering dust, beam from unseen cracks in the vaulted ceiling far above, but they are weak, more like moonlight than daylight. The dominant light is umber, ochre, and deep crimson, bleeding from the ground and the walls.

It is hot. A profound, enduring heat that radiates from every surface. The air itself feels viscous, a medium you wade through rather than walk. And it is loud. A constant, layered drone fills the space. It is the bass hum of immense thermal energy, the high, steady hiss of superheated gas escaping through a thousand unseen fissures, and the ceaseless, delicate chime of fracture. It is the sound of the world’s bones grinding.

This is the Kiln-Garden.

The floor of the basin, acres wide, is mostly empty space—a polished, heat-glazed obsidian that reflects the dim glows above like a black, still sea. But this emptiness is punctuated by islands of impossible lushness. This is the vegetation, though no earthly botanist would name it so. There is no soil, no water, no green. Instead, growing directly from the superheated stone in clusters around thermal vents, are forests of crystalline flora.

They are lifeforms born of pressure and heat. There are groves of vitreous stalks that resemble spun glass, branching into delicate, fractal canopies that capture the ambient light and refract it in shimmering, internal rainbows. Fronds of hexagonal basaltic needles, sharp and perfect, grow in slow, silent explosions. Carpets of what look like mineral moss, made of feathery, salt-like filaments, coat the hotter patches of rock in fields of electric blue and searing white. These are not plants that seek water; they are plants that *feed* on geothermal radiation, their metabolism a slow, solid-state chemistry.

This flora is the source of the place’s dynamism. The heat that fuels it is not constant. The basin operates on a slow, rhythmic pulse, like a slumbering heart. Over a period of hours, a thermal wave will swell from the deep earth. The drone deepens, the hissing sharpens. In response, the kiln-gardens stir. A crystal frond will visibly unfurl, its edges extending by millimeters. The basaltic needles will grow a new, razor-thin layer. Flowers of pure, fragile silica, like frozen puffs of smoke, will bloom at the tips of glassine branches, only to shatter into a fine, chiming dust when the thermal tide recedes. This constant, slow-motion growth and disintegration provides the tinkling, percussive layer to the cavern’s oppressive hum.

And built into the curving walls of this great chamber is the city. It is an urban arrangement, tiered and organized, but its technology is that of the stone and the hand. Terraces spiral up the walls, connected by sweeping ramps wide enough for processions. The structures are not built *on* the rock but carved *from* it. Dwellings are hollows, their entrances smooth and ovular, their interiors glazed a deep, lustrous black by some ancient, focused application of heat. There is no wood, no metal, only the shaped stone of the basin itself and a kind of fire-hardened ceramic, red as rust, used for smaller partitions, channels, and urns.

The city's design is one of brilliant, primitive climate control. It’s a passive engine for managing heat. The lower levels, closer to the basin floor, have fewer openings; they are shelters from the most intense radiation. The higher you climb, the more the city opens up, with balconies and broad platforms designed to catch the cooler (but still searing) air that circulates near the ceiling. Intricate channels are carved into the walls, not for water, but to divert streams of hot gas, perhaps for crafting or for some form of communal heating in a forgotten age when the basin’s pulse was weaker.

There is no sign of the builders. No tools, no bodies, no inscriptions. It is a metropolis without a soul, an architecture left behind for its own geological processes to inherit. The emptiness is profound. You can walk for a minute across a bare, polished terrace and the only presence is your own echo, swallowed by the drone. The vast spaces between the kiln-gardens on the basin floor are pure desolation. Yet the density of construction in the walls, the clear organization of districts and public spaces, speaks of a thriving, populous past.

This is a world without animals. There are no insects flitting between the crystal flora, no beasts of burden to walk the wide ramps, no predators stalking the dim archways. The only movement is the slow dance of geology and its thermal botany. The only sound is the planet breathing. The rules here are simple and absolute: heat is life, heat is time, heat is the only weather. Coolness is death. Water is a myth. The city remains, not as a ruin, but as a perfectly functional, empty vessel, waiting for inhabitants who understood the profound, sonorous, and utterly waterless rhythm of their world.
