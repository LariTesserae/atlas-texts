This is a landscape of sound. Before sight, before touch, there is the noise. It is not a simple roar of wind, but a chord of impossible, geological scale, a deafening and constant music played on the bones of the world. It is a bass drone that vibrates up through the soles of the feet, a pressure in the chest that makes breathing a conscious effort. Layered over this are shrieks, fluting whistles, and a shattering, crystalline hum that seems to come from all directions and none. The sound is not an event; it is a geology. It has always been and will always be.

You stand on the Great Plateau, a continent-sized tableland lifted miles into the sky. The mountains that once existed here have been abraded by eons of wind, worn down into immense, gentle swells and domes of stone. They are not peaks but slumped shoulders, their surfaces polished to a dull sheen, like river stones the size of cities. The erosion is so profound, so ancient, that the world seems to have settled into its final, most stable form. The rock underfoot is a seamless, pocked, and scoured surface, shades of ochre and grey and bruise-purple, with no soil to soften it. It is the bedrock of the world, naked to the sky.

The space is incomprehensibly open. From any of the low, broad rises, you can see for a hundred miles. There is no haze, only a terrifying clarity. But the space is not truly empty. It is populated. Rising from the stone, separated by vast distances, are the Chorus Domes.

This is the source of the lushness, and the sound. They are the vegetation of the plateau. They are not plants in a terrestrial sense; they bear more resemblance to colossal, calcified sea sponges or sound-sculpted crystals. A Chorus Dome can be the size of a hill, a single, hollow, vaguely bell-shaped organism rooted deep into the plateau's core. Its "skin" is a lattice of silica and tough, fibrous filaments, ranging in color from milky white to a deep, resonant amber. The surface is arrayed with countless petal-like flanges, apertures, and tuned channels.

And the wind—the true sovereign of this realm—screams through them. The dynamic here is not geological, but atmospheric. The air itself is a permanent, turbulent storm, a river of unimaginable force flowing constantly across the plateau. It is this river of air, moving at hurricane speeds, that plays the Chorus Domes. Each dome is a unique instrument. The deep, planetary drone comes from the vibration of their massive, anchored bodies. The high, fluting whistles are the wind tearing through their smaller pores. The shattering hum is the resonance of their crystalline structures, excited to a constant, near-breaking vibration. The landscape is a cathedral, a workshop, and a chaotic orchestra, all at once.

The air is warm and heavy with moisture, but it never rains. The wind is too fast. Instead, the moisture is driven horizontally, a perpetual mist that condenses on every surface. The Chorus Domes glisten, their windward sides slick with running water that is immediately torn away and atomized back into the gale. The lee of every dome, and every rock, is a place of relative calm and profound dampness, where water pools in ancient, smooth-edged hollows. In these quiet pockets, a secondary ecology exists: velvety, phosphorescent mosses and thick, gelatinous films that feed on condensation and airborne minerals.

There are no animals. Nothing can fly in this wind, and nothing can walk a thousand miles between the scant shelter of the domes. Life here does not move; it endures. It anchors and sings.

Yet there are marks of passage. In the lee of the great domes, sheltered from the worst of the sonic and kinetic assault, are the faintest signs of building. They are not houses. They are better described as "wind-graves" or "listening posts." Low, circular walls of carefully fitted, unmortared stone rise no more than a few feet from the ground, just enough to disrupt the wind and create a bubble of near-silence at their center. Inside one, the deafening roar of the plateau drops to a complex, intricate whisper. The ground within these circles is sometimes carved with geometric patterns, spirals and lattices that mimic the structure of the Chorus Domes.

Stretched between rock anchors are tethers woven from the fibrous husks shed by the domes. These act as guidelines, humming with a low, thrumming frequency that can be felt by a hand placed upon them—a tactile map for navigating the overwhelming soundscape. Scattered at the mouths of the largest apertures on the a Chorus Dome, you might find a simple, polished stone or a carefully braided loop of fiber—an offering, perhaps, or a tool left for a musician who is yet to arrive. The technology is one of resonance, of listening, of weaving, and of carving stone with stone. It is a technology of profound patience and attunement.

The light is normal, a familiar sun that arcs across the sky. But its effect is made strange. The constant, atomized mist refracts the light into fleeting, racing rainbows that flare and vanish in the blink of an eye. At dawn and dusk, the light catches the high silica content of the domes, and for a few minutes, the entire landscape ignites, the hill-sized bells blazing with internal fire as the low sun shines through their translucent bodies. It is the only time the world is visually louder than it is audibly.

To be here is to have your sense of self eroded. You are an insignificant presence in a place of immense, stable masses and hyperactive, chaotic energies. Your own thoughts are drowned out by the world's song. The rules are simple: anchor yourself. Listen. Understand that you are standing inside a single, living instrument that has been playing one turbulent, unbroken song since the beginning of time.
