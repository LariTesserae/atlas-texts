The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a collection of noises but a single, solid object of sound that has replaced the air. It is a deafening, multi-layered, grinding, and chirping roar that presses on the skull and vibrates in the teeth. This is the chorus of the world, the baseline of existence, so total that moments of relative quiet—the passing of a shadow that mutes a million throats at once—are more startling than the noise itself.

This is the Sunken Canopy. The ground beneath your feet is not soil or stone, but a vast, undulating plateau of petrified wood. It is a continent-sized fusion of ancient, incomprehensibly large boughs, trunks, and root systems, weathered over eons into a landscape of its own. The texture is like sclerotized bark, worn smooth in places into glossy, dark highways by the passage of countless things, and fractured in others into deep, fibrous canyons. The "ground" is warm to the touch, a constant, deep heat radiating from the slow, biological processes within its core. It smells of hot decay and baffling blossom.

Look up, and there is no sky. There is only a higher, greener layer. Immense leaves, each the size of a principality, hang miles above, held aloft by titanic green-grey stalks that rise from the woody plateaus like the pillars of a failing temple. These are the world-trunks, so vast their curvature is barely perceptible. They are spaced miles apart, creating a profound sense of sparse, vertical emptiness between them. Light from a distant, ordinary sun filters through the world-leaves above, falling in hazy, god-like shafts that are solid pillars of gold in the thick air. Where stripes of light hit the woody ground, the heat intensifies, shimmering and alive. Where shadows fall, there is a deep, blue-green gloom.

This upper canopy is its own weather system. There is no rain from the true sky. Instead, water condenses on the underside of the colossal leaves. As the day's heat builds, this condensation gathers until it reaches critical mass, and then it *weeps*. A "leaf-fall" begins not as a drizzle, but as a cascade of heavy, syrupy droplets that can create a moving, temporary river across the lignic plains. These flash-floods carve temporary channels, polish the ancient wood, and feed the pockets of life that cluster in depressions. The air is always damp, thick as broth with humidity, but the world is only truly wet where a leaf-fall is in progress.

This is a world of total vegetation, yet it feels empty because the scale is broken. The architecture is plant, but the spaces between are voids of air and sound. And these voids are not empty. They teem. The overwhelming sound comes from the fauna. Shimmering, iridescent shoals of chitinous flyers, each the size of a human hand, move in glittering, river-like swarms that can block out the shafts of light. They are the base of the food chain, and their constant, vibrating drone is the foundation of the sound.

Larger things move on the woody ground. Tripodal "bark-grazers," armored in thick, fungal plates, slowly scrape away at the petrified surface for nutrients, their grinding mandibles adding a low, bass note to the chorus. In the vast spaces between the world-trunks, winged predators with leathery, translucent membranes soar on thermal updrafts. You know them only by the sudden, terrifying silence their shadow casts on the droning swarms below, a silence followed by a piercing shriek that momentarily cuts through the din. The ground is littered with the shed carapaces, bony fragments, and fibrous husks of this overwhelming life, providing the raw materials for anyone who knows how to use them.

And someone does. Here and there, clinging to the sides of the great woody canyons or suspended from the more stable, lower-hanging vine-webs, are the faint marks of building. These are not towns, but scattered anchor-points. A small hut, woven from silvery vine-fiber, is tethered by braided gut-lines to immense, fossilized thorns that jut from the canyon wall. A rope bridge, so fine it is almost invisible, spans a chasm a thousand feet deep. You see a cache of tools: hooks carved from the leg-spurs of some great hunter-beast; knives flaked from the glassy, sharp edges of shed insectoid wings; mallets made from the dense, mineral-rich core of a fallen "screaming fungus."

This is a primitive existence defined by verticality and sparseness. There is no ground to till, no ore to mine. There is only the living and the dead of the Canopy. The rules of this place are simple and brutal. The sound is a map; you learn to read the change in pitch that signals a shift in the swarms, which might herald a predator or a leaf-fall. You learn which of the spongy, pulsating pustules growing on the wood is a food source, and which is a "Resonating Pustule" that will amplify the world's sound to a physically destructive level if disturbed. You never, ever stray from the established paths, for the un-walked surfaces might be thin carapaces over a hollow, a nest, or a pit of digestive resin.

To stand on the edge of a lignic plateau is to be undone by scale. Before you, a canyon of air two miles across and four miles deep separates you from the next world-trunk. A shaft of light illuminates a swarm of beetle-kin, turning them into a momentary, blinding cloud of gold. The air is so hot and wet you feel you could drown in it. The sound is a physical weight, a force that seeks to break you down into your component parts. And in that vastness, you can just make out a flicker of movement: a tether-hut, swaying gently in the humid breeze, a tiny, improbable testament to existence in a place that is all life, and no room.
