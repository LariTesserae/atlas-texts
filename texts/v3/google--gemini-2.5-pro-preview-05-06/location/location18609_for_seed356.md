This world is a single organism. There is no ground, only growth. The name for this place, if one were to give it one, might be the Heartwood, for it is a forest and a machine and a heart, and its scale is that of a planet.

The floor is a low, sprawling basin, a continent-sized depression woven from living, woody fibers. It is not soil, but a dense, springy substrate of interlaced roots, as thick and tightly knit as industrial-grade felt, saturated with a cool, clear fluid that perpetually weeps from it. The terrain is a landscape of biological machinery. What might be hills are vast, swollen nodules of cellulose, ribbed and gilled like the undersides of titanic mushrooms. They pulse with a warmth that radiates into the humid air, the slow, metabolic heat of a process that has run without change for millennia.

From this floor rise the true architectures of the place. They are not trees, but something subsequent. Great, smooth pillars of a material like dark, hardened lignin soar into the absolute blackness above, so far that they vanish. They are not round but are extruded in precise, geometric shapes: hexagonal, fluted, or shaped like massive I-beams. They are connected by bridgework of the same material, sweeping catenary curves and rigid trusses that are not built, but *grown* into place. The entire space is a lattice, a three-dimensional scaffold that is also the sole inhabitant.

This is the plant-as-factory. The "tech" of this world is biology perfected into industry. Deep below the root-mat, unseen taproots, kilometres thick, have bored into the planet's mantle, drawing up superheated, mineral-rich water. This geothermal energy is the furnace. The pillars are vascular conduits, drawing this primal soup upwards. The swollen "hills" are reaction vats, vast chemosynthetic chambers where extremophilic flora, integrated into the greater organism, catalyze reactions. There is no photosynthesis in the crushing dark; there is only the quiet, relentless chemistry of the deep.

The air is warm, thick, and heavy, saturated with moisture that condenses on every surface, leaving them slick and glistening. It carries a complex odor: the clean scent of ozone, the peaty smell of damp wood, a sharp, metallic tang from aerosolized minerals, and an underlying sweetness like fermenting fruit. The atmosphere is a product, an exhaust. It is thick with a fine, pollen-like dust, a golden particulate that is one of the system's outputs. It coats everything in a soft, non-reflective layer. This dust is inert, the final, powdered waste of a billion years of processing.

The wetness is a constant. Water drips from the high arches and framework, a slow, steady percussion in the profound quiet. Larger flows of processed fluid, viscous and slightly warmer than the ambient air, move through deep, U-shaped channels in the root-mat. These are the world's rivers: controlled, purposeful flows of industrial effluent, moving from one processing node to the next. Sometimes, a blockage far upstream will cause one to cease, the fluid draining away to reveal the channel's perfectly smooth, secreted-lignin bed, sharp-edged and uneroded. This is the "youngness" of the world: its features are not worn by time because the system is in a state of perfect, frozen equilibrium. The parts are fresh because they are never subjected to undue stress. They are eternally new.

There is no sound of life, for there are no animals. There is no wind, for the dense architecture of the Heartwood breaks all air currents. The silence is broken only by the quiet hum of the world-machine itself. It is a sound so low and pervasive it is felt more than heard, a subsonic thrumming in the bones. It is the vibration of the planet's core being tapped, the resonance of fluid moving through conduits the size of skyscrapers. Occasionally, there is the slow, ponderous creak of a structural member settling under its own continent-spanning weight, or a soft *hiss* as pressure is equalized in some distant, unseen valve-sac.

Weirdness permeates the logic of the place. Giant, chitinous structures like gears, fifty metres across, are meshed together, unmoving. They are not metal, but are grown from a substance like beetle shell, and they are motionless, locked in a single position, their purpose fulfilled and now held in stasis. In other areas, vast, translucent bladders hang from the ceiling, filled with glowing gases, byproducts of the chemical digestion. They are the only light source, casting a faint, sickly green or violet luminescence over the immediate area, revealing the intricate fractal patterns of the woody fibers around them. They do not illuminate; they merely make the darkness visible.

This world is not changing. It is not growing or dying. It reached this state of industrial-biological perfection an eon ago and achieved a perfect, homeostatic balance. The outputs precisely match the inputs. The waste is recycled or rendered inert. It is a machine that has finished building itself and is now simply *running*, its purpose forgotten or perhaps never existent beyond the act of its own continuation. To be within it is to be a ghost in a factory that operates without workers, producing for a market that will never exist, powered by a fire at the heart of a world that is now nothing but the factory itself. It is a monument to a process, silent, dark, and eternal.
