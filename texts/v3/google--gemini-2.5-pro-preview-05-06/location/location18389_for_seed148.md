The ground is not ground. It is a floor of interwoven, vascular reeds, thick as a human arm, pulled taut like the strings of a loom. Each fiber is a dark, plum-purple, slick with a cool, perpetual condensation. They form a vast, springy lattice, and through the gaps between them—gaps just wide enough to swallow a careless foot—is a darkness so absolute it feels like a physical substance. This is the Weft, the substrate of the grove-city. The air tastes of wet stone, cellar rot, and the faint, sharp tang of ozone. It is cold enough to see your breath, a pale ghost that quickly dissolves in the deep gloom.

From this living floor, the city extrudes itself. These are not buildings of stone or steel, but structures that appear to have been grown, coaxed out of the Weft’s own genetic template. Immense, half-translucent bulbs, like the hearts of titanic onions, swell upwards, their skins a patchwork of chitinous bark and areas of what looks like cloudy, stressed quartz. These are the habitation-pods, the dwellings. They are silent and dark, their interiors suggesting vast, hollow chambers. Between them rise slender spires that are both factory and flora, wrapped in spiraling conduits of a patinated, coppery material. From grilles and vents in their sides, they do not belch smoke, but exhale slow, rhythmic clouds of shimmering spores and leak a viscous, nutrient-rich fluid that drips down their flanks to be re-absorbed by the Weft below.

This is a city under construction, but it is not being built. It is emerging, still embryonic. You can see the process in real time, if you have the patience for it. A new spar, slick and pale, might be pushing itself out of a junction in the Weft, elongating by an inch an hour, its tip hardening into the familiar copper-like bark. A wall on a distant pod might be thickening, its quartz-like sections clarifying as the internal structures differentiate. This is not erosion by wind and water; it is ontological formation, the raw geology of a world inventing itself from biological blueprint.

And for all its vegetative and architectural fullness, the place is defined by emptiness. The Weft itself is a mesh over a void. The great pods are mostly hollow. The space between the major structures is immense, a cathedral of darkness punctuated by columns of plant-tech. The density of *things* is low, and the quiet is a presence.

The only consistent sound is a deep, impossibly slow pulse, a thrum that is felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. It is the synchronized, metabolic rhythm of the industrial flora—the city’s heart, pumping fluids through its vascular infrastructure, filtering the dark air. This deep thrum is the canvas for other, smaller sounds: the wet, chitinous click of something unseen scuttling inside a wall; the soft, almost inaudible beat of leathery wings moving through the high, empty spaces; the drip, drip, drip of condensation falling from a metallic frond into a pool of sap a hundred feet below, each impact echoing for a long moment.

Fauna is abundant, but it inhabents the voids. Translucent, spider-like creatures, the size of dinner plates, walk the underside of the Weft, their dozen legs moving in perfect sequence. They are the maintenance crawlers, grooming the fibers. Flocks of things like moths, but with bodies of spun, phosphorescent glass, drift on the slow air currents between the pods, their movements tracing languid, glowing patterns in the dark. They are the pollinators of this machine-ecology. Deeper, in the hollows of the largest structures and in the infinite blackness below the Weft, live larger things. Their presence is known only by a sudden, localized shudder in the floor, or a low, mournful sigh that seems to come from the architecture itself, as if a great lung has just exhaled.

Light is a currency, and it is scarce. The primary illumination comes from colonies of pallid, blue-green fungus that grow in patches on the Weft, and from the cold, internal glow of the factory-spires, a light like a dying television screen visible through their semi-transparent walls. Occasionally, a flock of the glass-moths will congregate, creating a brief, swirling nebula of soft light before dispersing back into the gloom.

Reality here is a fragile scaffold. A pathway of Weft might seem solid, but a step reveals it to be an afterimage, your foot plunging into the cold void before the fiber reasserts its existence a moment later. A sound can be heard—a sharp crack, like ice breaking—and ten minutes later, you see the event that caused it: a small, crystalline shard flaking from a high conduit. Cause and effect are drifting apart. The city is a process, not a place. It is a single, vast techno-organism dreaming itself into being in a cold, dark void, and its dream is full of pulsing engines, hollow spaces, and the quiet, constant scuttling of its own attendant ghosts. You are not a visitor in a city; you are a particle floating within a thought that is just beginning to form.
