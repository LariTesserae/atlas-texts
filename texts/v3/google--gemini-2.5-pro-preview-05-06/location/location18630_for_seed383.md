This place is a pause in the deep rock, a cavity of impossible scale hollowed out by a forgotten geology. You stand on a floor of basalt-like stone, worn so smooth by aeons that it reflects the light like a dark, wet mirror. The air is cool and carries the metallic, clean scent of ozone and damp stone, the kind of chill that seeps into your bones not with aggression, but with a deep, patient indifference. It is endlessly damp. Water does not flow here in rivers, but manifests as a universal sweat upon the rock. Every surface is slick with a thin film of condensation, which gathers in the countless shallow depressions of the floor, forming millions of still, black pools that range from the size of a hand to the size of a house.

Above, there is no sky, yet the cavern is awash in bright, unwavering light. The ceiling, miles high, is a living membrane of chemiluminescence. It is not a single, uniform glow, but a vast, mottled lacework of pale blues and soft greens that drift and marble like oil on water, slow-shifting patterns that take centuries to resolve. This cold, diffuse light is eternal, casting no sharp shadows, only a deep, profound clarity that extends for miles in every direction.

The silence is the first thing you would notice, and the last thing you would believe. It is a profound, pressurized quiet, broken only by the almost subliminal-soft *tink* of a water droplet falling from a great height into a pool, or the whisper-faint hiss of air moving across the vast, open space.

And it is this silence that makes the scene so utterly mad. Because this is a city. And it is in a state of pandemonium.

Stretching across the great, dark plain are structures. They rise from the smooth stone in scattered, chaotic profusion—towers, archways, walls, and bridges that connect nothing to nothing. They are built of a material that looks like nacre, or spun pearl. They are translucent, opalescent, catching the cold light from above and refracting it into soft, internal rainbows. The architecture is organic, alien. Towers spiral upwards like unicorn horns, their surfaces whorled and fluted. Walls are not flat planes but undulating curtains of solidified liquid. Archways sag and loop with a dreamlike fluidity, seeming to defy a gravity they only partially obey. The city is sparse; between great clusters of these pearlescent structures lie immense stretches of the bare, wet stone.

This city is being unmade as quickly as it is being made.

It is a metropolis of silent, frantic construction and dissolution. Look closely at a spire a mile away. As you watch, it seems to sweat, its pearlescent surface weeping a thick, viscous fluid that runs down its sides in glistening rivulets. The base of the tower softens, loses its integrity, and begins to slump, folding in on itself with the silent, slow-motion collapse of melting wax. Within an hour, the hundred-story structure will be a flat, shimmering puddle of iridescent slurry, which will slowly seep into microscopic fissures in the ancient stone, or be consumed.

At the same time, in an empty space nearby, a new structure is being extruded. From the ground, a thick, gelatinous fountain of the same pearlescent material is bubbling up. It is not a violent eruption, but a quiet, inexorable welling-up. This viscous fluid is being shaped. The agents of this change are everywhere.

They are the fauna. They are the citizens. They are colossal, slow-moving creatures that resemble something between a slug and a starfish. Their bodies, ranging from the size of a cart to that of a small hill, are themselves a milky, semi-translucent white. They have no discernible eyes or mouths in the conventional sense. They glide over the slick stone on a muscular, undulating foot, moving with a dreamlike slowness that is unnervingly silent. There are thousands of them, scattered across the plain, each engaged in its own task.

They are the builders. Some crawl upon the existing structures, their bodies clinging to the vertical surfaces. From pores on their undersides, they extrude fine, shimmering threads of the nacreous fluid, weaving them into the walls to reinforce or expand them. It is a form of biological 3D printing, done by hand, or by pseudopod. You can see them, a dozen working on a new bridge, pulling glistening cables of the stuff from their own bodies and anchoring them, the material hardening almost instantly in the cool, damp air. Their tools are their own flesh.

They are also the demolition crew. Other creatures press themselves against the bases of older, decaying towers. They are not eating the structures, but seem to be reabsorbing them. Their bodies pulse gently as they draw the dissolving slurry back into themselves, their skin swirling with captured rainbows. The process is one of endless, silent recycling.

The world has rules, but they are alien. This is not life as a struggle for scarce resources, but life as a hyperactive, pointless, and beautiful process of form-making. The creatures build for the sake of building. They dissolve for the sake of dissolving. It is a closed-loop economy of creation. Perhaps the structures are their art, their language, or a form of worship. Perhaps they are simply extensions of their own bodies, communal shells they inhabit for a time before consuming and starting anew. Their abundance is not a measure of evolutionary success, but a simple fact of their existence. With no predators and a seemingly endless source of energy—perhaps drawn from the minerals in the rock, catalyzed into the pearlescent excretion—their population is limited only by space.

The turbulence is everywhere, but it is a turbulence of slow flows and silent transformations. A district of arches can melt into a lake of opalescent gel in a day. An empty plain can sprout a forest of delicate, needle-thin towers overnight. The entire city is like a single, sprawling, decentralized organism, constantly regenerating, its memory encoded in the very substance it uses to build itself. The air is still, the light is constant, the floor is ancient and unchanging, but upon this stable stage is a play of such chaotic, silent, and beautiful impermanence that it feels like watching the birth and death of a galaxy, compressed into a single, chilly, and brightly lit cavern at the end of time.
