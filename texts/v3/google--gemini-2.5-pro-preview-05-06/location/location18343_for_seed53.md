This place is a hollow, a basin scooped out of the summit of the world. It is not large; one could walk its circumference in under an hour, if the footing were reliable. It's known, to the few who have memory of it, as the Alabaster Gyre.

The air is thin and knife-sharp, so cold it feels like a distinct pressure against the skin. The overwhelming first impression is of light. Not the warm, diffuse light of a low-lying sun, but a brilliant, high-altitude glare that is both sharp and total. The sky is a deep, bruised violet, almost black at its zenith, and the sun is a small, ferocious diamond. The light doesn't just illuminate; it scours.

There is no soil. The ground and the sloping walls of the gyre are made of a dense, layered, fibrous mineral. At a distance, it looks like pale, unpolished stone, but up close, it reveals itself to be countless strata of crystalline filaments, packed and compressed over eons. The texture is like cross-grained wood or satin spar, nacreous and faintly shimmering. The primary material is a form of selenite, but it is shot through with veins of darker, glassy obsidian and glittering sheets of mica, creating a complex, petrified geology. The ground is a solid mass of these layered minerals, weathered and smoothed by ages of wind and subtle thermal stress, its forms settled and ancient. There is no dust, only fine, crystalline grit.

This is a world without life in any recognizable form. No lichen clings to the stone, no algae stains the damp patches. Yet, the place is not static. It breathes on a slow, daily cycle.

As the fierce sun climbs, its energy penetrates the translucent upper layers of the selenite and warms the darker obsidian veins buried within. This trapped heat begins to sublimate the frost and ice that have accrued overnight. A faint, almost imperceptible mist rises from the ground, a 'dampness' that is more a state of atmospheric transition than a presence of liquid water. You cannot see it clearly, but you can feel it: the air grows heavy with potential moisture. The sun's glare seems to thicken and swim at the edges.

This vapor, barely risen, meets the surrounding super-chilled air and the frigid surfaces of the gyre. Instantly, it deposits itself again as ice. But it is not a smooth sheet. It grows in feathery, complex blossoms of hoarfrost, forming delicate, branching structures of impossible intricacy. These crystalline blooms, some as small as a thumbnail, others as large as a human hand, sprout directly from the selenite ground, catching the sun and fracturing it into a million pinprick rainbows. The entire basin floor, over the course of the day, becomes a dense, near-touching field of these ephemeral ice-flowers.

The sound of the gyre is the sound of this process. It is mostly quiet, a profound hush born of altitude and emptiness. But listen closely, and you can hear it: a faint, high-pitched *tink-tink-tink* as new ice crystals nucleate and lock into place. As the ground expands infinitesimally in the sun's warmth, there is a deep, subsonic groan from the compressed mineral layers, a sound felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. A soft, pervasive *hiss*, like escaping gas but without force, is the sound of sublimation, the stone itself sighing vapor into the thin air.

Evidence of past activity is scattered, almost camouflaged. Low, curving walls of stacked stone, no higher than one's knee, snake across the basin floor. They are not defensive and offer no shelter. Their placement seems deliberate, creating pockets of still air or subtle thermal shadow. It’s as if they are tools to guide the condensation, to encourage the frost to grow in specific patterns or densities.

And among these walls, you find the other marks: the tools. Flakes of obsidian, knapped to a razor's edge, lie half-buried in the crystalline grit. They are simple scrapers and blades, their forms primitive but effective. They feel small and purposeful in the hand. Some lie next to patches of the selenite ground that have been scraped smooth, the fibrous texture abraded away to reveal the glassy, darker stone beneath. The purpose is obscure but feels ritualistic. Was something being harvested? Were the delicate hoarfrost blossoms being carefully shaved from the surface and collected?

There are no animals. No birds cross the violent sky. No insects hum in the frigid air. The only movement is this inexorable, geological cycle of freezing, sublimation, and deposition. The gyre is a machine. A slow, cold, bright engine that runs on sunlight and trapped moisture. The low walls and obsidian blades are not the remains of a settlement, but the components of a strange and abandoned form of agriculture or industry, one whose crop was ice and whose field was the glittering, sterile ground itself. The world here operates on a simple rule: what the sun lifts, the cold reclaims. And something, once, found a use for the reclamation.
