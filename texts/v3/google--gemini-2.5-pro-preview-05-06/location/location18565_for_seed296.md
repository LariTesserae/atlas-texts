The world is a high, ancient tableland, so worn by time that the concept of a mountain peak is a distant memory. This is not a range of jagged spires but a single, contiguous upland of stupendous mass, its topography slumped into gentle, continent-sized swells and depressions. Geologists would call it a peneplain, a landscape eroded to its final, most stable form. But its elevation is extreme, placing it high in the thin, cool upper atmosphere. The stone that forms this world is a dark, heavy basalt, almost black, and it is everywhere. Ages of condensation and slow-flowing water have polished its surfaces to a dull, light-swallowing sheen.

There are no grand rivers here, no crashing waterfalls. Water exists as a persistent, clinging dampness. A heavy, perpetual mist, born from some great planetary exhalation, reduces sight to a few hundred feet on a clear day. On a bad day, you cannot see your own outstretched hand. Water slicks every surface, beads on every edge, and pools in the shallow, thumbprint-like depressions in the endless stone. This moisture is the lifeblood of the vegetation, which is startlingly, unnaturally lush.

There are no trees. Instead, the ground is covered in a dense, layered carpet of what could be called moss, or lichen, or fungus, but is none of these. It is a form of life unique to this place, a thick, resilient, photosynthetic felt that grows in overlapping plates and complex fractal patterns. Its color is a deep, chlorophyll-rich emerald, so vibrant it seems to glow from within in the perpetual dimness. It covers the gentle hills and wide valleys in an unbroken blanket, softening the hard edges of the ancient geology. This "stone-fleece" is cool and rubbery to the touch, and when you walk on it, it gives slightly underfoot, releasing a scent of clean soil, ozone, and something like wet copper.

In the center of one of the vast, shallow basins sits the city. It is called Ambit. It is not built upon the landscape so much as it is embedded within it, a dense concentration of structure in the open expanse. The city's form follows the gentle contours of the land. Its buildings are low-slung and heavy, made from the same dark basalt, cut and fitted into massive blocks. There are no soaring towers; the city spreads outward, dense and close-packed. Streets are more like canyons, narrow and winding, their paving stones slick with the constant damp. Between the stone, tarnished bronze and verdigris-streaked copper are the dominant metals, forming pipes, conduits, railings, and grates that stitch the city together. From above, if the mist ever cleared enough to see, Ambit would look less like a city and more like a circuit board pressed into the living felt.

This is an industrial place. The city's existence is predicated on a single, ponderous process. Deep beneath the plateau, geothermal vents provide limitless energy. This energy is drawn up through massive, insulated conduits that hum with a low, almost sub-audible frequency—the only constant sound in the mostly quiet landscape. This energy powers the city's industry: the harvesting and processing of the stone-fleece.

The verdant felt contains trace amounts of rare-earth metals, which it bio-accumulates over its slow life cycle. The industry of Ambit is a cycle of scraping, pressing, and smelting. Huge, automated rakes, moving with a dream-like slowness, crawl across the outlying plains, shaving off the top layers of the felt. These harvests are brought into factories at the city's edge, vast, windowless stone halls where the air is thick with the smell of hot metal and steamed vegetation. Inside, immense hydraulic presses squeeze the water and organic matter from the felt, and the remaining material is fed into electric furnaces that glow with an internal heat, visible only through thick quartz viewing ports. The soundscape here is one of muffled thumps, the slow groan of machinery, and the hiss of steam, all of it dampened by the stone walls and the heavy air.

The light is always dim. The sun is a myth, a bright smudge that sometimes appears behind the layers of mist, creating a pearlescent, all-encompassing glow without direction or warmth. The city's primary light comes from within: the amber glow from apartment windows, the greenish arcs of welding torches in repair yards, and the faint, biological luminescence of the stone-fleece itself, which emits a soft, ghostly light after dusk.

Life here is strange and circumscribed. There are no birds to sing, no mammals to hunt. The only native fauna are small, multi-legged creatures that live within the layers of the felt, and pale, blind salamanders that swim in the cisterns and drainage channels. The people of Ambit are the dominant animal. They are practical and quiet, accustomed to the cool dampness and the enclosing fog. Their lives are governed by the cycles of the geothermal plants and the slow, inexorable harvest. There is a sense that everything here—the ancient stone, the slow-growing felt, the humming factories, the people themselves—is part of a single, self-contained, and deeply active system, one that has found its equilibrium and could continue, unchanging, for another ten million years. It is a world without horizons, a world of texture, a world of damp, cool, quiet industry humming away in the heart of a green and grey eternity.
