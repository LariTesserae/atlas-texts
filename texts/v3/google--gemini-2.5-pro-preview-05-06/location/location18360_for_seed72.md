This place is not entered, but descended into. The air grows thick and gains a palpable weight, a hot, wet blanket you have to push through. Every breath is a drink from a steaming kettle. The world above is a forgotten concept. Here, there is only the chamber.

It is a single, vast space, though "cavern" is the wrong word. A cavern is a void carved by water over millennia. This place is being born. Its walls are not worn smooth but are instead a riot of nascent geology, a slow-motion explosion of mineral growth. They are lumpy and unfinished, surfaces of milky quartz and dull, grey calcite bulging outwards in botryoidal, grape-cluster formations. It has the scale of a cathedral, but the raw, unfinished texture of a kiln. The geology is under construction. Water, super-saturated with dissolved minerals, does not carve this place; it builds it. The walls weep constantly, a slow, viscous drip of warm, salty liquid that coats every surface in a perpetual sheen and contributes new, infinitesimal layers to the growing stone. Where this brine collects in shallow depressions on the floor, cloudy, geometric crystals are slowly precipitating out, sharp-edged and perfect in the muck.

This process is not natural. It is an industrial project. The oppressive heat radiates not from the planet's core, but from a network of massive, dark-metal pipes that cling to the growing walls like iron ivy. They are thick as ancient trees, their surfaces beaded with condensation that runs in grimy rivulets. At wide, scattered intervals, these pipes feed into complex arrays of machinery: humming condenser stacks, rumbling centrifugal processors, and groaning pumps that beat with a slow, deep, geologic pulse. This is the source of the constant, loud drone that fills the chamber, a multi-layered thrum of heavy industry that vibrates in the teeth and bones. It is a factory for geology, a settlement built to farm the rock itself.

The "city" is a sparse, skeletal structure suspended within the chamber's humid void. Walkways of perforated metal mesh span the gaps between processing stations. Habitation modules, utilitarian pods of stained composite, are bolted directly onto the more stable, older rock formations, their unlit viewports staring blankly into the dimness. Ladders and service gantries create a layered, three-dimensional infrastructure that feels more like an oil rig than a town. Everything is damp. Everything is coated in a thin film of mineral dust and grime. There is no center, no market square, just a distributed network of function, a settlement designed around the patient, cyclical process of mineral accretion.

The light is dim, sourced from occasional sodium-vapor lamps that buzz with a sound almost lost in the industrial hum. Their orange, sickly glow diffuses through the thick, hazy air, illuminating little but casting long, distorted shadows. Most of the chamber is lost in a deep gloom, where the only light is the glint of condensation on a pipe or the faint, internal luminescence of a particularly pure quartz growth.

And everywhere, on every surface, is the fauna.

The place is overwhelmed with life, but it is not green life. What little vegetation exists is a thin, rust-colored sludge of chemosynthetic bacteria coating the warmest pipes and the edges of the brine pools. The animal life, however, is a seething, chitinous carpet. The sound of the machinery is inseparable from the sound of the fauna: a constant, high-frequency chittering and skittering that forms the texture of the ever-present drone.

They are arthropods, an entire ecosystem thriving in the waste heat and chemical byproducts of the factory. Cockroach-like creatures, flat and oily black, scuttle in dense rivers along the base of the walls. Isopods the size of a man’s thumb, armored in calcified plates that match the rock, methodically clean the condenser units, their thousand legs a blur. In the air, clouds of tiny, winged insects, like metallic gnats, swarm around the glowing safety lamps. Silverfish-analogues with long, twitching antennae crawl over the control panels on the machinery, their bodies glistening. They are on the walkways, clinging to the undersides of the habitation pods, swimming in the brine pools. They are not a pestilence; they are a feature, an integral, living layer of the environment, as much a part of the landscape as the weeping rock and the humming pipes. They are a sign that the system is functioning.

The air smells of hot, wet stone, of ozone from the machinery, and of the dry, musty, faintly sweet odor of a billion insect bodies. Movement is slow. A drip of brine takes a full minute to fall. The machines cycle in long, ponderous phases that last for hours. The city does not change day to day, but over years, a new walkway might be extended to a newly-solidified rock formation, or an old pipe might be fully encased in the slowly growing wall. It is a closed loop, a patient engine. The pumps draw brine from a source deep below; the factory processes it, causing the walls of its own container to grow; the waste heat and chemical residue feed an overwhelming tide of life that lives on and among the machines and the people, all of them together in the hot, loud, dim, and slowly-forming dark.
