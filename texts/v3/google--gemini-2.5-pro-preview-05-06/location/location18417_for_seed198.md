The ground is not ground. It is a floor, a vast and rolling plain of riveted iron plates, ceramic tiles, and grooved concrete, all worn to the smoothness of river stones by ages of weather. This is a place built, then forgotten, then colonized by a new kind of nature. The air is cool and heavy, saturated with a fine, near-invisible mist that beads on any surface left still for more than a moment. The world weeps. Water slicks the dark, oxidized metal underfoot, fills the geometric channels cut for long-lost purposes, and drips in a steady, maddening rhythm from the colossal structures that loom above.

This is a high place, a constructed plateau hoisted into the clouds. There is no horizon, only a dissolution into a seamless grey ceiling of permanent overcast. The light is weak, as if filtered through layers of milk glass, lending the world a profound and shadowless dimness. What variation exists comes from the failing arteries of the structure itself: banks of dull orange sodium lamps that flicker on and off in unsynchronized, agonizing spasms, casting a sick, jaundiced glow over a hundred-meter patch of decking before guttering out again.

Every inch of the substrate is claimed. The seams between iron plates are home to a vibrant, aggressive moss, so thick and dense it pillows up over the edges, softening the geometry of the world into lumpy, organic contours. Where channels of water have become permanent pools, thick-stemmed, broad-leafed plants with a waxy sheen rise, their roots not in soil but in a mulchy accretion of their own dead, forming floating mats of vegetation. Ropes of ivy, dark green and thick as a man’s arm, enshroud entire pylons, climbing hundreds of meters up into the mist. Garlands of lichen, pale green and ghostly white, hang from the undersides of gantries and walkways, dripping with condensed humidity. It is a jungle, but an artificial one, a veneer of lush, desperate life clinging to a skeleton of industry.

The scale is immense and confusing. One stands on a platform the size of a town square, looking out over what seems to be a valley, but the valley floor is another, lower tier of the same construction, pocked with reservoirs of still, dark water and overgrown with the same relentless green. Great bridges, their cables furred with moss, leap across chasms not carved by rivers, but designed as service corridors. Colossal, silent factory buildings, their windows long since shattered, sit like hollow mountains in the landscape, their interiors a dark mystery. It is a settled place; the forms are established, the decay has reached a state of equilibrium. The rust is not new and flaky, but a deep, permanent stain. The broken edges of concrete are rounded and smooth.

Yet, for all its aged quiet, the place is in constant, violent turmoil. This is the paradox of the Works. The quiet is a hum, not a silence—the blended, bass-heavy superposition of a million distant, failing motors, pumps, and generators buried deep in the strata of the structure. But superimposed on this drone is a chaotic symphony of breakdown. With a percussive *clang* that echoes for miles, a fifty-meter sheet of metal siding breaks free from a distant tower and helicopters down into the green below. A geyser of superheated steam erupts from a grated manhole, hissing for ten minutes before sputtering out. An entire section of walkway, a kilometer long, will suddenly begin to vibrate, its embedded lights flashing erratically as some forgotten logic circuit fires one last time.

The water systems are the most active. A series of sluice gates, their mechanism half-eaten by rust, might groan open, releasing a sudden, churning flood down a primary concourse, tearing moss from the walls and rearranging the floating mats of vegetation in the reservoirs. Then, just as suddenly, the pumps that feed it will fail, and the torrent will recede to a trickle over hours, leaving behind trembling, muddy chaos. This is the rhythm of the world: not seasons or tides, but the spastic, unpredictable death throes of a machine. It is the anthill energy of a system processing only itself, a city built to manage a planet, now locked in a frantic, pointless cycle of self-destruction and momentary, accidental self-repair.

There is no birdsong. No buzz of insects. The only life is the green, silent, photosynthetic blanket and what few, unseen things—molds, worms, blind crustaceans—thrive in the damp, nutrient-rich detritus it creates. The air smells of wet ozone, of hot metal, of decay, and the sharp, clean scent of chlorophyll, all mixed into one inseparable, industrial-organic perfume. To navigate this place is to accept that the ground beneath your feet is not solid, that a quiet patch of moss can become a scalding vent without warning, and that the only constant is the slow, inexorable process of a built world being digested by water and time, perpetually twitching as it goes.
