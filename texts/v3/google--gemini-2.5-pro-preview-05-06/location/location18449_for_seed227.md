The first and only thing is the sound.

It is not a sound that arrives at the ears, but a condition of the air itself. It is a deafening, bass-heavy, unbroken roar, a physical pressure that vibrates through the bone and marrow. It feels ancient, like a noise that has been sounding since the beginning of time and will continue long after. It has the texture of grinding stone and the sheer force of a million tons of falling water. This sound is the bedrock of reality here. All other senses are secondary, functioning in the brief pauses of attention one can steal from the overwhelming sonic presence.

To be here is to be inside a geode, a hollow sphere of titanic proportions hidden deep within the earth. The air is cold, carrying a permanent, clammy chill that sinks into any fabric and clings to the skin. It’s the kind of cold that promises ice but never quite delivers, leaving everything perpetually on the verge of freezing. The atmosphere is thick with a fine, atomized spray, a constant and invisible condensation that settles on every surface, making the floor slick and the walls weep. You can taste the rock on your tongue—a clean, mineral tang of wet granite and basalt.

There is almost no light. The world is rendered in shades of imperceptible grey and absolute black. Over long minutes, the eyes adjust, not to see, but to interpret the absence of things. The roaring void has shapes. Far, far away, at what might be one edge of this colossal chamber, a single, impossibly vast column of white defines itself against the black. This is the source of the sound: a waterfall of immense scale, plunging from an unseen fissure in the cavern’s ceiling into a black, invisible lake below. Its roar is not a crash and splash, but a sustained, thrumming, resonant drone that the very dome of the cavern amplifies into a deafening totality. The sound doesn't echo; it simply fills.

Despite the near-total darkness and the emptiness of the air, the surfaces are lush. A thick, carpet-like vegetation, more akin to moss or fungus than any surface plant, coats the floor and walls. It is a form of life adapted to darkness, cold, and vibration. The ground is covered in a dense, sodden velvet of what looks like black moss, punctuated by ghostly-pale fungal growths. These growths are not small toadstools; they are enormous, sculptural things, some reaching the height of a person, fanning out in delicate, plate-like tiers. They are bioluminescent, but their light is a lie—it is a cold, dead phosphorescence, a green or blue glow so faint it can only be seen by not looking directly at it. It offers no useful illumination, only the suggestion of form in the deep black. This pale, spectral flora is what gives the place its lushness, a quiet and slow explosion of life clinging to the established, unmoving stone. The space *between* these growths is vast and empty. One could walk for a hundred paces across the thick, sound-absorbing moss before encountering another towering fungal plate.

Signs of previous inhabitants are scattered and faint, almost reclaimed by the damp and the dark. Carved into the cavern wall, far from the direct spray of the falls, are shallow alcoves, just large enough for one or two bodies to huddle. The stone within is worn smooth. Leading between them are paths that are not built, but simply worn into the mossy ground, the nap of the vegetation permanently pressed down by the passage of feet over millennia. Here and there, one might find a simple tool: a hand-axe made of flaked obsidian, its edge still sharp; or a bowl carved from the woody base of one of the larger fungi, sitting on a rock as if its owner had just set it down. There are no metals, no fabrics save for what might have been woven from the fibrous, rope-like kelp that grows at the edge of the great lake. These people, whoever they were, lived in a world of touch and vibration, their lives circumscribed by the deafening roar. Perhaps they communicated through signs, or a language of touch, or simply lived in a profound and shared solitude. The scattered nature of their remains suggests a tiny population in an immense space.

There is almost no animal life. The deafening noise and the dark have scoured the environment of most creatures. But life is stubborn. In the still, frigid pools of runoff that collect in depressions in the stone floor, blind, white salamanders, long as a forearm, move with unnerving slowness. On the walls, nestled among the fungal plates, live armored, beetle-like organisms the size of a fist. They are as silent and slow as the geology, their thick carapaces protecting them from the vibrating air as they scrape nutrients from the rock and the moss. To see one move is an event that might take an hour. They are so rare that their existence feels more like a rumor than a fact.

The world here operates on a different timescale. The waterfall has been falling for eons, its form established, the basin it has carved now in a state of equilibrium. The moss grows over centuries. The fungi unfurl over decades. Change is a gradual and patient process, measured not in days but in the slow erosion of stone under the constant pressure of water. There is no weather, only the eternal, cold mist. There are no seasons, only the unvarying temperature of the deep earth.

To stand in one of the small, carved hollows is to feel utterly and completely alone, while also being in the presence of an immense and living power. The roar of the water is a constant companion, a destructive and creative force that has shaped this hidden world. It is the engine of the place, the source of its dampness, the reason for its darkness, and the metronome for its slow, unceasing existence. You press your hand against the cold stone of your shelter and feel the waterfall’s heartbeat, a vibration that has pulsed in this darkness, unheard and unseen, for longer than the mountains have had names.
