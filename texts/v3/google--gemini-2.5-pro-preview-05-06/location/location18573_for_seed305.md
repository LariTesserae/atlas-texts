The light is the first and last thing. It is not the warm, directional light of a sun, but a brilliant, shadowless, and pervasive glare that seems to emanate from the very substance of the world. It is a platinum-white sheet of illumination, so intense it flattens perspective, making the vast distances ahead seem like a painted backdrop. There is no sky, only a ceiling of luminous haze, a perpetual bright overcast born from the land itself.

The sound is its companion: a deep, resonant, and unceasing hum. It is the sound of a million-ton flywheel turning at an impossibly slow speed. It is a chord, not a noise, with a fundamental bass note that vibrates in your teeth and sternum, and higher, keening harmonics that sound like grinding crystal. The air is thick with this sound, a physical presence that presses in from all sides.

This is a mountain range, but the word is insufficient. It is a continent-sized knot of freshly-birthed geology, a landscape of impossibly sharp peaks and sheer cliff faces. The rock is not worn or weathered; it is fractured, sheared, and extruded. Slopes are not gentle inclines but faces of shattered obsidian and raw, unoxidized basalt that gleam like polished steel in the stark light. These are not mountains formed by slow tectonic collision; they are the functional parts of a machine the size of a world, and they have only just been milled. The valleys between them are not carved by rivers, but are the precise, catastrophic gaps between colossal, interlocking components.

Here, "industrial" does not mean built. There is not a single artifact, not a road, not a pylon. The industry is the world itself. The mountain-sized structures are geological engines. Peaks rise and fall, not over millennia, but over the course of a long, slow breath, acting as pistons that draw geothermal energy from the planet's molten core. Sheer cliffs slide against each other with a sound like tearing metal that lasts for a century, grinding rock into fine, metallic dust that drifts in the air. Deep within the canyons, you can see them: immense, rotating discs of granite hundreds of miles across, their edges meshing like gear-teeth. Their movement is majestic and infinitesimal, a rotation that will complete one turn every thousand years, but the power it generates is absolute.

This planetary engine is hot-running. The overwhelming cold of the high altitude and thin atmosphere is a background truth, a fact you feel in the deep core of the rock. But the machinery itself vents incredible heat. From hairline fractures in the basalt faces, plumes of superheated steam hiss into the air, creating a perpetual dampness. The white-hot light of the world comes from vast, exposed filaments of geological matter, glowing under tectonic pressure deep in the chasms. Water doesn't flow in rivers; it condenses on every surface, forming a slick, greasy film of mineral-rich moisture that drips constantly, feeding the strange life that covers everything.

And it *is* covered. This is not the green, photosynthetic lushness of a jungle. It is a dense, packed monoculture of crystalline growth. Tufts of what look like spun silica glass, glowing with a soft internal light, sprout from every crack. Sheets of copper-veined, moss-like organisms coat entire cliff faces, pulsing faintly in time with the world's deep hum. They are not plants; they are living conduits, heat sinks, and mineral processors, integral to the function of the geo-engine. They thrive on the heat, the metallic dust, and the mineral-rich condensation. There is no soil, only a packed, interwoven mat of this lithic vegetation, centimeters thick or meters deep, clinging to the naked, moving rock.

The density is absolute. Every square inch of the landscape that is not a sheer friction-face or a thermal vent is occupied. And this extends to the fauna. Life here is overwhelming, a teeming, chittering, crawling mass. Insects the size of dinner plates, with carapaces of polished chrome and iridescent wings, swarm in clouds so thick they momentarily dim the ever-present light. They are not flying so much as swimming through the dense, humming air. Larger creatures, like eight-legged, crustacean automata, move in slow, deliberate rivers across the plains of crystal-moss, their jointed limbs clicking in a billion-strong chorus. They graze not on the growths, but on the energy fields and mineral deposits that accumulate upon them. There are predators, too: sleek, serpentine machines of muscle and articulated metal that uncoil from crevices to snatch the swarming grazers, their movements shockingly fast in this world of patient, geological slowness. There is no room to step without crushing something, no silence between the clicks, the skittering, and the chimes of countless exoskeletons moving against each other. They are a living fluid poured over the machine-world.

The rules are simple. The world is a closed system. It generates its own light, its own heat, its own weather of steam and mineral dust. The life is not an afterthought but a functional component, a biological secondary system that regulates the primary geological engine. Change is slow but total. A cliff face may seem static for a human lifetime, but it is moving, and when its cycle completes, it will shear away with a finality that erases everything on its surface. The creatures are born, consume, and die in this vast, patient factory, their bodies breaking down into the mineral dust that feeds the next generation. It is a world without day or night, without season or respite, locked in a single, endlessly sustained, deafeningly loud, and brilliantly illuminated moment of creation.
