The air is thin enough to be a memory, a substance more defined by what it lacks. It holds no warmth, only a pervasive, seeping cold that settles deep in the bones. Above, the sky is a permanent bruise of violet-black, starless and without a sun. The only light a distant horizon gives off is a smear of bruised grey, a perpetual twilight that offers no comfort, only the stark confirmation of unending height and exposure. This is a summit that makes up a world, a plateau of ancient, weathered mountains that have been worn smooth by time itself. The peaks are not jagged spears but the rounded knuckles of a sleeping giant, mountains so old they have settled into a state of profound stillness.

This immense, open space is fundamentally empty. The distance between one rise and the next is a chasm of miles, a void filled only with thin, metallic-tasting air. The ground in these empty tracts is a shatter of dark rock, a pavement of basalt and feldspar shards that gleams faintly, as if polished by cosmic dust. There is a silence here that is not merely the absence of sound, but an active presence. It presses in, muffles thought, magnifies the quiet thrumming of your own blood.

But this emptiness is not the whole story. The landscape is ruled by a division. It is a world of veins.

Snaking across the desolate plateaus and sinking into the broad, U-shaped valleys are immense, pulsating conduits. They are not rivers of water but of life, some as wide as a motorway, others as fine as a thread. These are the arteries of a planetary engine, a symbiotic network of mineral and fungus. Along their lengths, life erupts in defiance of the cold and the dark.

This is the source of the landscape’s industry. It is not made of steel or concrete but is a living, geological process. Great, crystalline structures, like misshapen quartz cathedrals, jut from the earth at random intervals along the main conduits. They are the system's processors. By day—or what passes for it—they are inert, greyish, and translucent. But in the deeper dark, they hum. It is a sound you feel more than hear, a resonant A-flat that vibrates through the soles of your feet. They draw energy from the tectonic stresses of the planet's crust, from the slow grind of continents, and feed it into the fungal network. The conduits themselves—thick, rubbery, and braided like massive cables—glow with a cool, internal blue light that pulses in a slow, hypnotic rhythm. The air around them crackles with faint static, and the condensation that beads on their surface fizzes away into glowing vapor. They are processing the deep earth, metabolizing minerals, and pumping a raw, chemical energy out into the world.

Where these veins run, the world is not empty. It is lush.

Vast forests of what might be called fungi rise from a thick, springy carpet of bioluminescent moss. The moss glows with a soft, steady chartreuse light, casting the world in an eternal, eerie gloaming. The "trees" are towering stalks, some reaching a hundred meters high, branching into elaborate, candelabra-like structures. Their caps are slick with a phosphorescent ooze that drips slowly, each drop falling for long seconds through the still air to land with a soft *splat* on the moss below, illuminating a small circle with a brief, bright splash.

The ground is alive. Broad, leathery leaves the color of slate, edged with tiny pinpricks of golden light, unfurl to catch the constant, condensing mist. Strange, bladder-like polyps, translucent and veined with glowing blue capillaries, inflate and deflate in a slow, tidal rhythm. With a soft *phut*, they release clouds of brilliant, silvery spores that drift like smoke, catching the light from the forest floor.

This is a damp world, but not a wet one. There are no rivers, only the constant drip from above and the moisture that clings to every surface. The air is heavy with the smell of cold ozone, wet stone, and something like mushrooms and metal.

And through this glowing, cold jungle, there is movement. The quiet is punctuated by subtle, living sounds. The fauna here is abundant, built for the dark and the silence. Herds of six-legged grazers, their bodies protected by articulated plates of iridescent chitin, move like shadows across the moss fields. They are the size of cattle, with massive, multifaceted eyes that drink in the low light. They do not eat the glowing plants. Instead, they pause, lowering their heads to pierce the smaller tendrils of the great fungal conduits with needle-like proboscises, siphoning the energy-rich fluid directly. Their passage is marked only by the soft, rhythmic *click-clack* of their plated legs on the damp ground.

There are smaller things. Armored, beetle-like organisms the size of hubcaps scuttle up and down the great fungal stalks, their mandibles working methodically to clean away parasites. Flitting through the upper canopy are creatures like bats, their wings not leathery but made of a taut, membranous film stretched over delicate, bony fingers. They navigate not by sight but by a series of high-frequency clicks, too sharp and fast for the ear to properly register, their echoes painting a picture of the forest in their minds.

Predation, too, is a quiet art here. Long, sinuous hunters, their bodies slick and black like oil, stalk the grazer herds from the high branches. They are creatures of pure muscle and patience, almost impossible to see until they drop, silent as a falling shadow, upon their prey. There is no roar, no shriek of battle. Only a brief, muffled struggle, the crunch of chitin, and then the quiet sounds of feeding resuming in the dark.

The whole system is in motion. It is a place of cycles. The slow, geological churn feeding the humming crystals. The crystals vitalizing the fungal veins. The veins feeding the glowing forests. The forests sheltering the silent fauna. Everything is interlocked in a dynamic, ongoing process that has been in balance for eons. It is an industrial ecosystem, a factory made of life, operating on a silent, planetary scale in the unending twilight at the roof of the world. To stand in the glowing heart of a valley is to be surrounded by overwhelming life, a vibrant, active world humming with its own internal power. To step a hundred yards beyond the reach of the last glowing moss is to be plunged into a profound and absolute emptiness, a cold, dark silence where the only sound is the waiting of the stone.
