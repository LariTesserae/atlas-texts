The sound is the first and last thing. It is not an event, but a condition of the air itself, a pressure on the bones. It is a deafening, grinding hum that occupies every possible space, a D-flat drone so profound and all-encompassing that true silence is an unimaginable abstraction. This is the sound of the world being born, a process that has no intention of ever finishing.

The landscape is a basin, a vast and shallow depression that extends to a hazed, featureless horizon. The air is warm and heavy, saturated with a moisture that has no memory of rain. It simply *is*. It condenses on every surface, forming a slick, greasy film. Dropletspregnant with dissolved minerals hang from every overhang and fall with a dull *pat*, a sound instantly devoured by the larger drone. The light is a perpetual, substanceless twilight, a dim and even diffusion that casts no sharp shadows, as if the sky itself were a single, enormous, cataract-filmed eye.

There is no soil. The ground, the walls, the very firmament is a slow-growing mineral concretion. It is not crystalline and sharp, but bulbous and matte, like bone-white cauliflower or the inside of a geode scaled to the size of a city. These structures emerge from the depths in dense, crowded formations. They are not spires, but lumpy, layered towers that press against one another, forming canyons and archways not by design, but by the simple pressure of their continuous becoming. The surfaces are raw, unweathered, covered in tiny, fresh nodules that feel like coarse sand under a probing finger. This is geology in its embryonic state. The world is soft clay, perpetually hardening but never fully set.

This is an urban place, but it has no architects. The "buildings" are the natural upwellings of the mineral growth. The "streets" are the winding, claustrophobic ravines between them. But it is inhabited, and therefore it is shaped.

Teeming in these self-forming warrens are the denizens. They are slick, chitinous beings, roughly humanoid in stature but built like beetles or crabs, their dark carapaces gleaming with the constant condensation. They move with deliberation, their segmented limbs finding purchase on the slick, knobby stone. Life here requires a constant, unending labor. Armed with primitive tools—heavy mallets of a denser, darker stone and chisels tipped with sharpened bone or resilent chitin—they are the city's maintenance crew, its unwilling gardeners.

They are Carvers. Their entire existence is a battle against the world's slow, inexorable expansion. They chip and scrape and hammer at the walls of their dwellings, not to build them, but to prevent the slow mineral growth from sealing them in forever. A pathway cleared today will be a constricted, impassable fissure within a few cycles. A communal chamber, hollowed out over generations, must be perpetually re-carved at its edges to maintain its volume. Dust from this work—a fine, damp, mineral grit—hangs in the air, mixing with the vapor to form the ever-present haze.

Life, apart from the Carvers, is abundant and simple. Thick, nutrient-rich ooze, a form of chemotrophic slime, grows in thick, pulsating mats where the mineral growth is newest and most active. This is the foundation of the food web. The Carvers scrape it into chitinous bowls to eat. Legions of smaller creatures, like metallic silverfish and blind, white worms, feast upon it, their skittering and burrowing adding a barely-audible, high-frequency texture to the overwhelming background hum. The largest creature, besides the Carvers themselves, is a slow, armored grazer—a beast like a colossal woodlouse, with a dozen blunt legs, that mindlessly scours the walls with a rasping tongue, its passage leaving behind a momentarily smooth, clean scar on the growing rock.

The city, though kilometres across, is unchanging. The location of a major canyon or a great tower remains the same for millennia. The state of the world is a frozen equilibrium between growth and maintenance. The Carvers do not expand their territory, for there is nowhere else to go and nothing else to build with. They do not innovate, for their tools are perfectly suited to their single, eternal task. Their culture, if it can be called that, is one of pure, reactive persistence. There is no art, no music beyond the drone of the world, no ambition beyond holding their ground against the patient geology.

To stand in a ravine is to feel the world pressing in. The damp, warm air feels thick in the lungs. The roar of the forming earth vibrates through the soles of your feet, a constant massage of power. Above, the sliver of dim sky is a pale, indifferent grey. Droplets of mineral water trace paths down the dark, glistening shells of Carvers as they strike their mallets against the ever-emerging walls, their impacts sharp, percussive cracks lost instantly in the timeless, deafening song of creation. This is a city not of people, but of place; a metropolis built by no one, inhabited by prisoners of its very substance.
