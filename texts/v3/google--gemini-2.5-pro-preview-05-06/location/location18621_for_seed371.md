This place has a voice before it has a form. The sound comes first, a deep and complex industrial roar that travels for miles through the thin, cold air of the peaks. It is not the sound of wind or water, but the noise of a gargantuan machine running without pause, a composite of high-pressure shrieks, the grinding of immense weight, and a steady, resonant hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet.

You find it by following the noise and the sharp, unnatural tang in the air, a scent like ozone and hot metal. The terrain is brutal. The mountain has been cracked open like a nut, its rock faces raw and new. There are no gentle slopes, no scree fields worn smooth by time. Every surface is an edge, a fracture plane of granite and obsidian-like glass that shows no sign of weathering. The ground is treacherous, a jumble of shattered blocks and newly-birthed crystalline spurs.

The air is cool and perpetually damp, but there is no rain. The moisture is a fine, clinging mist that condenses on every surface, beading on the sharp rock and dripping with a soft, steady patter that is almost entirely lost beneath the overwhelming mechanical din.

The source of the sound is a chamber, a kind of collapsed geode or caldera, no larger than a grand cathedral. To step inside is to be swallowed by darkness and noise. The sky is gone, blotted out by a dense, interlocking canopy far above. The only light is internal: frantic, blue-white sparks that arc between the colossal structures filling the space, momentarily illuminating scenes of impossible industry.

This is a factory made of earth and living things. The floor of the chamber is a mess of geothermal vents, fissures that scream with escaping superheated steam. This is the engine room, the source of the power and the ceaseless noise. The steam is thick with dissolved minerals, and as it billows upwards into the cold darkness, it condenses on the ceiling, feeding the process above.

The ceiling isn’t rock. It is a dense, chaotic lattice of enormous quartz-like crystals, jutting downwards like the inverse of a mountain range. These are not passive formations. Squeezed and vibrated by the turbulent pressure waves from the vents below, the crystalline matrix is violently piezoelectric. It groans under the strain, and the stress is converted into raw electrical power. This is the source of the arcing light—discharges of thousands of volts that leap across the crystal spires like lightning in a bottle. The air hums with static charge, and the deep resonant tone is the mountain itself singing under titanic geological pressure.

And on this electrified grid, the vegetation grows. There is no soil here. Life clings directly to the charged crystal surfaces. It is a dense, lush, and utterly alien forest. Thick, metallic-sheened vines, like bundles of copper wire, snake around the crystal pillars, their surfaces pulsing with a faint, internal blue light. Their roots are not roots, but fine, fibrous filaments that merge directly with the crystal, tapping the electrical field for energy. Great, broad leaves, the color of verdigris and dark bronze, unfurl in the darkness, their surfaces slick with mineral-rich condensation. They are cool to the touch. This forest is not photosynthetic; it is galvanotrophic, feeding on pure voltage.

The entire system is a storm of creation and destruction. The chaos is constant. A geothermal vent will suddenly surge, sending a concussive blast of pressure upwards. A crystal spire, overgrown and stressed to its limit, will audibly groan, then shatter with a deafening crack, falling in a hail of razor-sharp shards to the chamber floor where the intense heat and pressure will begin the process of reforming it. The vines and leaves attached to it are torn apart, their internal light extinguished, only to be consumed by the new growth that immediately begins to colonize the fresh surfaces of the wound.

There are no animals. Nothing could survive the raw electrical discharges, the chaotic shattering of the landscape, the sheer volume of the sound. It is a closed, sterile system of geology and botany, a natural process that mimics, on a terrifying scale, the function of a power plant and a production line. The rules here are simple: energy is pressure and heat, the currency is voltage, and life is a frantic race to colonize the newest conduit before the entire structure inevitably shatters and begins again. It is a place of perpetual, violent youth, forever tearing itself apart and being reborn from its own ruin.
