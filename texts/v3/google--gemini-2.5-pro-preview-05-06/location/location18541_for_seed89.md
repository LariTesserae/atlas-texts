The air thins to a ragged whisper. You are impossibly high, on a peak that has been scoured by winds for so many eons that it has no sharpness left. The summit is not a point but a collection of enormous, rounded grey domes, like the knuckles of a fossilized god buried to its wrist in the earth. The rock is smooth, almost soapy to the touch, and warm, humming with a deep, internal heat. There are no scree slopes, no jagged edges; every surface has been abraded into a state of final, weary repose.

The world up here is a small one. The sky, when visible through the thin, persistent cloud layer, is a bruised, starless purple, even at midday. This peak lives in a perpetual twilight. The scale is not of a mountain range, but of a single, sacred room. The cluster of knuckles forms a natural boundary, a circular depression perhaps a few hundred meters across. It is a caldera, not of fire, but of age.

And it is not empty. It is unimaginably full.

The first clue is the sound, a noise so constant and enveloping it ceases to be a collection of sources and becomes an atmospheric property, like pressure. It is a wet, chitinous, percussive roar. A million tiny castanets clicking in imperfect unison, a billion tiny legs scraping on stone, the thick, syrupy hum of countless vibrating bodies. The sound is a physical presence, a dense wall you must push through. It makes the teeth ache.

The air is warm and heavy with moisture. Water does not fall as rain here, nor does it flow in streams. It beads on every surface. The smooth, grey stone of the knuckles weeps with condensation, the warm, saturated air of the basin meeting the cold rock. Gleaming droplets trace slow, patient paths down the stone, converging in shallow, palm-sized pools on the basin floor before being absorbed or evaporating back into the soupy atmosphere. The smell is of damp basements, of ammonia, of turned earth and something else, a sweet, cloying scent like overripe nectar.

This basin is a hive. A pit. It is writhing.

The sparse vegetation is not green. In the perpetual dark, clinging to the weeping walls and spreading across the floor in rubbery, brain-like convolutions, are vast colonies of fungi. They are the only source of light. They pulse with a slow, sickly, blue-green phosphorescence, a cold light that ebbs and flows in languid waves, like a slow-motion aurora trapped underfoot. These fungal mats are soft, almost gelatinous, and feed on the mineral-rich water and the detritus of the basin's inhabitants.

And the inhabitants are everywhere. They are the source of the density, the sound, the very heat of the place. Creatures the size of a human hand, somewhere between a beetle and a crab, swarm in a heaving, layered, near-solid mass. Their carapaces are the color of obsidian, polished to a dull sheen, and their undersides are a pale, fleshy white. They have too many legs, thin and spidery, that carry them over and under and around each other in a constant, churning, but unhurried re-shuffling of position. There is no empty space between them. The floor is a living carpet of their bodies, three or four deep. The walls are a moving tapestry of them, climbing with painstaking slowness toward the dripping moisture. A fallen one is simply absorbed back into the tide, its body broken down and consumed by its brethren or the patient fungi.

They are not mindless. Here and there, one can be seen using a tool: a sharp-edged flake of volcanic glass, chipped from some harder inclusion in the stone. They don't manufacture them, merely find and employ them. With these primitive blades, they scrape at the glowing fungal brains, harvesting slivers of the phosphorescent flesh, which they pass from one to another down into the deeper layers of the living floor. They use other stones to groom each other, scraping away old shell or parasites in slow, methodical gestures.

This is a world in a state of stasis. Its dynamism is geological in its pace. The living mat of the floor has been accumulating for millennia, a slow compost of shed exoskeletons, waste, and fungal spores, creating a springy, damp loam that is meters deep. The sound, for all its volume, is a drone, not a cacophony. It is the sound of a system in perfect, deafening equilibrium. The creatures are born, they climb, they scrape, they feed, they die, and they are reabsorbed. The light pulses. The water drips. The mountain hums with its ancient, trapped heat. There is no day or night, only the slow tide of bioluminescence and the unending, shuffling, clicking roar of a billion lives lived in a single, crowded, breathing room at the summit of the world. It is less a habitat for fauna and more a single, composite organism, a body whose cells are creatures and whose thoughts are the slow, patient processes of decay and consumption.
