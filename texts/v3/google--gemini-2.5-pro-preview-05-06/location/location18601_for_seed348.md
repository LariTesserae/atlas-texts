This place is a basin, a vast, single chamber carved from rock, perhaps a kilometer across. The ceiling is lost in a luminous haze, a permanent, sourceless saffron glow that is neither day nor night. It is simply light, steady and eternal, casting no distinct shadows but instead bathing everything in a uniform, clinical ambiance. The temperature is constant: cool, the kind of deep-cellar chill that seeps into your bones, carrying with it the scent of ozone, wet metal, and something else, something acrid and organic, like fermenting minerals. The air is damp, but it does not rain. Instead, moisture perpetually condenses on every surface, forming heavy, viscous droplets that crawl down walls and hang from pipes before falling with a thick *ploc* into shallow, iridescent pools on the floor.

The ground is a pavement of smoothed, dark grey ceramic tile, worn glassy by ages of passage. It is not flat. It slopes gently from the unseen edges of the chamber toward a series of central, circular grates, each the size of a public square. The entire floor is slick with a thin film of the same viscous condensation, making it treacherous.

There is no soil, no plant life. The base of the world's ecology is a mineral process. Sprawling across the tiled floor and climbing the legs of the sparse structures are crystalline lattices, somewhere between fungus and frost. They grow in fractal patterns, some a delicate, salt-brittle white, others a thick, fleshy verdigris that looks like corroded copper come to life. These are not plants, for they do not seek the light; they are fed by the chemical slurry that occasionally and unpredictably wells up from pipes embedded in the floor, coating a section of the lattice-works in a nutrient-rich goo before receding.

This is a city, but not for people. It is a factory floor organized on an urban scale. Rising from the slick tiles are towering structures, but they are sparsely arranged, with hundreds of meters of open space between them. These are not buildings but immense, isolated machines: catalytic crackers of strange design, bulbous fermentation vats of green and brown glass, and skeletal gantry cranes frozen in place, their limbs draped in weeping pipes. The metal is ancient, pitted and stained, yet nothing seems truly broken. It is all in a state of established, worn-out function. Everything hums.

And the humming is only the foundation of the sound. The noise here is overwhelming, a physical presence. It is the deep, resonant thrum of the vast machines, the shriek of pressure releases that never close, the rhythmic, percussive *clank-clank-clank* of a piston the size of a house, moving with immeasurable slowness somewhere in the haze. But layered over this industrial symphony is another sound, a biological one, a sound of overwhelming life.

The fauna. The fauna is the city.

The floor, the walls, the machines themselves are swarming with them. The most numerous are the Mandibles, beetle-like creatures the size of a human fist. Their bodies are polished chrome and dark, chitinous brown, and they move in rivers of thousands, their countless legs making a constant, high-speed clicking that fuses into a sound like tearing fabric. They are the grazers. They swarm over the mineral lattices, their diamond-hard mouthparts shearing off crystalline growths with an audible, grating crunch. They do not eat for hunger; they simply process. What they consume is excreted almost immediately as a fine, grey dust that piles in drifts against the bases of the machines.

Hunting the Mandibles are the Hoppers. These are tripedal, bird-like automata, their bodies a tangle of wires, hydraulic pistons, and bone-white ceramic plates. They stand a meter tall and move in sudden, violent leaps, covering twenty meters in a bound. A Hopper will land amidst a river of Mandibles, its head, a featureless steel sphere, swiveling. A needle-thin proboscis will snap out, impale a Mandible, and retract. A low grinding sound issues from the Hopper's torso for a moment before it leaps again. They do not seem to coordinate, yet they keep the Mandible population in a state of perfect, unchanging density.

Moving slowly through this chaos are the largest inhabitants, the Regents. There are perhaps only a dozen visible at any time. A Regent is a slow, ponderous beast, a living sludge-collector the size of a bus. Its body is an amorphous, gelatinous blob of near-transparent tissue, through which one can see the complex, whirring gyroscopes and pumps that serve as its organs. It crawls on a hundred short, thick legs, hoovering up the grey dust left by the Mandibles and the occasional dead Hopper. The waste is processed within its translucent bulk, and it excretes pure, viscous lubricant from pores along its back, which drips onto the floor, ensuring the system remains slick. It also secretes the chemical slurry that feeds the mineral growths, closing the loop. Its sound is a low, continuous moan, like a ship's horn in a cavern.

This entire ecosystem—the mineral growth, the industrial excretions, the three tiers of fauna—is a single, closed mechanism. It is overwhelmingly, deafeningly alive, yet it is utterly static. Nothing ever changes. The number of Mandibles, Hoppers, and Regents is constant. The rate of mineral growth is constant. The amount of moisture condensing, the hum of the machines, the brightness of the light—all are locked in a perfect, time-invariant equilibrium. Hoppers "kill" Mandibles, but the population never shrinks. Regents "recycle" the dead, but it is not decay; it is maintenance. The creatures are not beings with wills or lives; they are biomechanical cogs in a factory whose only product is its own continuation.

The central rule of this place is that tomorrow is identical to today, and to yesterday, and to the day a million years ago. The frantic activity is a façade for absolute stasis. It is a captured moment of immense energy, frozen in its execution and forced to repeat its single, deafening, pointless action for eternity. To stand here is to feel the pressure of a thousand gears turning, grinding, and clicking, all to keep the machine exactly where it is. It is the sound of forever.
