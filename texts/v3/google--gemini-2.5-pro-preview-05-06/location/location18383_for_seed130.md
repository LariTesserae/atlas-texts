The sound is the first and only terrain. It is a physical presence, a pressure on the bones, a vibration that works its way into the fillings of your teeth. It is a deafening, complex, and layered roar with no single source and therefore no escape. It is the grinding of a billion tons of metal, the bass-heavy hum of continent-spanning electrical grids, the shriek of superheated steam escaping from unseen vents, and the percussive, arrhythmic crash of titanic hammers striking unseen anvils miles away. It is the sound of a world that is a single, functioning machine, and it has been running for an eon.

There is no soil underfoot. The ground is a single, unbroken plate of vitrified, obsidian-dark material that stretches to every horizon. It is not flat, but it is low, undulating in vast, slow swells like a petrified ocean. Ancient and settled, it has found its final, stable form. The surface is slick, cool to the touch, and perpetually damp. It is pocked and scarred with the history of its own making—the scrape-marks of long-dismantled engines, the faint geometric outlines of structures that have been subsumed back into the floor, the rust-colored stains bleeding from hairline fractures.

Water is a constant, quiet process against the backdrop of the noise. It does not rain from a sky. Instead, the cool, heavy atmosphere, thick with the taste of ozone and hot metal, is saturated to its limit. Water condenses on every vertical surface, running in slow, oily rivulets. It weeps from the seams in the floor-plate, welling up from some immense, pressurized cistern-world below. These seepages coalesce into shallow, sprawling pools of perfectly clear, perfectly sterile water that reflect the crushing darkness above. The air is so damp it feels like you are breathing liquid.

Across this dark, wet, and resounding plain, there are presences. They are sparse, separated by miles of slick emptiness. These are the active components of the world-machine. In the distance, a pumping-jack the size of a cathedral slowly, inexorably bows and rises, its single purpose lost to time but its motion absolute. Further on, a line of light-crowned pylons marches across the plain, each one periodically discharging a silent, blindingly blue arc of lightning into the one beside it, a chain reaction that ripples towards a vanishing point. Here and there, great geared wheels, half a mile in diameter, lie on their sides, spinning with excruciating slowness, their teeth meshing with nothing but the damp air. Their movements are the world's weather, its only true dynamic. They are lit from within, not for illumination, but as a byproduct of their function—a dull, feverish orange glow that pulses from grilles and inspection ports, casting long, distorted, slowly moving shadows. This is the only light.

There is no animal life. Nothing skitters, nothing crawls, nothing flies. The sterility is absolute, a silence of biology within the deafening roar of industry.

But there is a kind of life. In the shadow of the great machines, where the weeping from the floor-plate is most constant and the stray voltage from the pylons is most concentrated, a sparse vegetation-analog thrives. It is a flocculent, chemotrophic mold, growing in patches of luminous, toxic verdigris. It spreads in fractal patterns, feeding on metal salts, leaked lubricants, and endemic electricity. It pulses with a faint, cold bioluminescence of its own, a sickly green that does not illuminate so much as emphasize the darkness around it, a stark counterpoint to the hot orange of the machines.

The world has rules. The ground is immutable. The sound is eternal. The machines are purposeless but perpetual. Their slow, cyclical actions are the only clock, measuring out ages of identical, functional motion. The water is a byproduct, a lubricant, an coolant for the planet-engine, eternally recycled. The darkness is the baseline state. Light is a momentary accident of function. Nothing grows here that did not emerge from the machine itself. The entire system is perfectly, totally constructed, a monument of industry so ancient and vast that it has become its own geology, its own ecology, its own doomed and deafening cosmos. It was not built upon a world. It *is* the world.
