This is a landscape of high, unfinished peaks. The mountains do not have the sharp, crystalline fractures of ancient ranges carved by ice. Instead, they are lumpy and rounded, like vast gobs of clay half-formed and then flash-frozen. The term ‘rock’ feels too definitive for the material underfoot. It is a proto-geology, a slurry of gravels, soft, chalky conglomerates, and dark, malleable muds, all of it saturated with a deep, weeping dampness. The elevation is extreme, placing this rolling upland at the edge of the breathable atmosphere.

The air itself is thick, not with fog in the traditional sense, but with a heavy, condensing humidity that clings to every surface. It is profoundly cold, a wet, bone-numbing chill that seems to emanate from the ground itself. Water is a constant presence, but rarely in grand forms. There are no rivers, no great lakes. Instead, the ground is universally sodden. Water oozes from gray, porous stone, beads on strange, metallic-sheened lichens, and pools in every depression, the surfaces shivering not from wind, but from a deep, almost sub-audible vibration. The light is a perpetual dim wash, the sun a diffuse, milky stain behind a permanent ceiling of low, heavy cloud.

This is a quiet place, but not a silent one. The dominant sound is a low, resonant thrum, a hum that seems to exist more in the sternum than in the ears. It is the sound of a colossal engine idling, miles away and buried deep underground. Accompanying it is the constant, soft hiss of condensation and the patter of drips from stony overhangs. Occasionally, there is a loud, grating scrape followed by a cascade of sliding scree, a sound that echoes for a moment across the empty valleys before being swallowed by the damp air again.

Emptiness defines the space. The undulations of the terrain are vast, and the features are few and far between. You could walk for an hour and see only the subtle curve of the land change against the unmoving gray sky. Vegetation is minimal, clinging to the slightly more stable ground. There are patches of what looks like blackened moss, greasy to the touch, and colonies of lichen whose circular plates have the dull lustre of lead or blued steel. There are no trees, no shrubs, no grasses. The ecosystem does not seem to draw its energy from the pale, distant sun.

And yet, there is an abundance of life.

The fauna of this place are the source of its industrial signature. Across the vast, empty plains and slopes, they move. They are the engines. From a distance, one might look like a house-sized boulder slowly, impossibly, rolling uphill. Closer, it reveals itself as a being. It has no discernible head or limbs, but a vast, segmented carapace of tectonic plates, a material that is neither pure chitin nor pure metal, but a fusion of the two. Gaps between the plates glow with a faint, internal orange heat, and from these seams, plumes of hot, chemical-smelling steam vent into the cold air, instantly condensing and adding to the perpetual mist.

These are the primary producers, the grazers of the rock itself. Along their undersides, immense, hardened mandibles and grinding plates scrape and pulverize the raw geology. The material is drawn into their bodies, which are not biological organisms in a familiar sense, but mobile, self-replicating refineries. The low thrum that fills the landscape is the sound of their internal workings: the crushing, the sorting, the heating, the smelting. They move with an inexorable slowness, their passage leaving behind shallow trenches filled with a slurry of wastewater and discarded grit.

They excrete their processed world. In their wake, they leave behind piles of perfectly spherical pellets of refined metal, varying in size from sand to small cannonballs. They also deposit strata of fine, silt-like dust that quickly hardens into new, more stable forms of rock. The landscape is, in effect, under constant construction, a geological process driven by a biological engine. The "emerging" nature of the terrain is the result of this massive, ongoing strip-mining and manufacturing process.

Other creatures have evolved in concert with them. Flocks of smaller, winged beings swarm around the great processors. They look like metallic dragonflies with bodies of coiled copper wire and filmy, membranous wings that beat with a low-voltage crackle. They do not attack the processors, but rather feed on the hot, ionized steam that billows from their vents, converting the thermal and chemical energy directly. They are the hummingbirds of this industrial garden.

And there are predators. Solitary, agile hunters built on a similar biomechanical plan but designed for attack. They are low-slung, multi-legged creatures, the size of a large wolf, with carapaces of obsidian-dark plating. Instead of grinding mandibles, they possess a single, diamond-hard pneumatic spike on their forelimbs. They stalk the great processors, looking for a moment of weakness or a damaged plate. A successful hunt is a slow, loud affair: the percussive *thump-thump-thump* of the spike trying to breach the hull, followed by a shrieking tear of metal and a massive, final exhalation of steam from the dying giant. These hunters then feed on the processor's internal components, slurping up conductive fluids and tearing out bundles of silvery, muscle-like wiring.

The world operates on a slow, powerful, and entirely self-contained logic. It is a factory the size of a continent, with no builders, no operators, no external purpose. Its single product is itself, an ever-_becoming_ landscape of raw material turned into mobile life, and that life, in turn, refining the world from which it is made. It is a cycle of consumption and creation, turning a mountain range into a slow-motion, living, breathing machine under a sky of perpetual grey.
