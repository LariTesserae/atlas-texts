The first thing is the darkness. It is not an absence of light, but a presence itself. It is a mass, a pressure, a substance that fills all volume. It has a quality like deep water, thick and resistant. To move through it is to push aside something heavy and viscous. It muffles sound, smothers scent, and chills the skin even in the profound warmth. This is not a world that has lost its light; it is a world made *of* darkness.

The air is warm and carries the weight of water. It is saturated, clinging. Every surface weeps. Your own skin is slick within moments, not with sweat, but with the condensing atmosphere itself. The air has a taste, vaguely metallic and ozonic, like the aftermath of a lightning strike inside a sealed metal drum. Breathing is a conscious effort, pulling this thick, wet medium into your lungs.

You stand on a floor of new-formed slag. It is not rock, not soil, but something else entirely, a sharp, vitreous material that covers everything in a low, sprawling plain. Underfoot, it cracks and shifts with a sound like grinding glass, but the sound is immediately swallowed by the heavy air. The ground is a chaos of edges, points, and delicate, crystalline blades that have only recently cooled and solidified from some unimaginable liquid state. It is a young surface, uneroded and treacherous. To fall here would be to be flayed. This jagged plain extends in every direction, a landscape of black, sharp fractals. Its scale is perceived not by sight, but by resonance. A faint, pervasive hum vibrates through the soles of your boots, and by its timbre and the way it echoes back from distant masses, you can feel the shape of the basin—vast, shallow, and ringed by unseen prominences miles away.

Despite the desolation implied by the slag, the world is dense with presence. This density is layered. At the ground level, there is the endless complexity of the crystalline floor. Above it, the heavy, water-logged air itself feels crowded. But the true density comes from the fauna. The world is swarming. You feel them more than you hear them. The air constantly stirs with their passage. Soft, chitinous bodies brush against your legs. Something heavy and multi-limbed skitters across a plate of slag nearby, its weight causing a brief, sharp report before the sound is choked. The ground trembles faintly as larger things traverse the basin in the far distance. These creatures are silent. They have no calls, no cries. They navigate this total darkness through touch, pressure, and senses you cannot name. Their sheer numbers create a constant, almost subliminal rustle, a feeling of being perpetually surrounded, touched, and watched by a million unseeable things.

Scattered across this plain are the monuments to a slow, industrial cataclysm. They are immense, oblong structures of pitted iron, like the hulls of beached, ancient submarines. You sense them as voids in the universal hum, masses that absorb vibration. Up close, their surfaces are cold and slick with the same atmospheric condensation that coats everything, but here it mixes with a thicker, oily fluid that leaks from hairline cracks in their hulls. These are the Precipitators. They are engines, but they do not move. They run on a cycle so slow as to be nearly static. Inside their colossal forms, something is being processed at a geological pace. A low, thrumming beat, less a sound and more a rhythmic pressure change felt deep in the bones, emanates from them, perhaps one beat every minute. This is the pulse of the world. The slag floor around their bases is warmer, the crystalline structures fresher, suggesting the slag is their effluent, a slow-motion extrusion of processed reality that solidifies into the very ground you stand on.

The weirdness is the substrate of it all. The darkness is a solid. The industrial machinery manufactures the terrain. But there are subtler instabilities. Sometimes, the sense of open space collapses, and for a moment you feel as if you are in a tight, narrow corridor, the air pressing in from all sides. Then, just as suddenly, the feeling of the vast basin returns. Objects you touch might present conflicting textures—the same patch of ground can feel both sharp and slick, the same iron plate both cold and warm. The air carries phantom pressures, the ghosts of things that are no longer there, or perhaps are not yet there. Time itself feels sluggish and unreliable, stretching and compressing around the slow, metronomic beat of the Precipitators. The abundant fauna may not even be entirely biological; they might be temporary configurations of the same unstable medium as everything else, coalescing into being for a long, slow season before dissolving back into the humming dark. This is not a place where the rules have been broken, but a place where the rules are still being written, slowly, patiently, and in a language that has no words for light.
