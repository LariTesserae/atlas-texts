This is the Resonant Plateau, a continental shelf of living crystal. The ground beneath your feet is not rock or soil, but a single, interconnected organism. It is a plant, but not as we know it. We can call it Vitreflora. It is a silicon-based lifeform that long ago solved the problem of structure by becoming the structure itself. The entire landscape—its mountains, its valleys, its vast, wind-scoured plains—is a single, ancient growth.

The world is high and cool. The air is thin, sharp in the nonexistent lungs, carrying a chill that speaks of altitude, not ice. There is no water here. Not a drop of H₂O has existed on the surface for eons. Life is anhydrous, built on different chemistries. The Vitreflora subsists on light and atmospheric trace minerals, which it fixes into its crystalline body.

The structure of the world is sparse, yet total. The Vitreflora grows as a lattice, an impossibly complex, three-dimensional web of translucent, milky-white filaments. In some places, these filaments are woven so densely they form solid-seeming ground, plains of luminous fibre that ring with a low hum underfoot. In others, they are spun into colossal, soaring structures—arches, towers, and mountain ranges that are mostly empty space. You can stand on the edge of a chasm a mile deep and see the other side through the gorge's very walls, a dizzying cross-hatch of struts and braces frosted with eons of atmospheric dust. The scale is planetary. The horizon is a distant range of these lace-mountains, their peaks catching the brilliant, even light and glowing like filaments in a bulb. The entire world is weathered, ancient. The great forms have settled; the sharp edges of youthful growth were scoured away millennia ago by forces that no longer exist. It is a place in equilibrium, but the equilibrium is one of perpetual, violent motion.

Because this world is not quiet. It is deafening.

The sound is the first and last thing you perceive. It is not the roar of wind or water, but the thunder of industry. It is a compound noise, a wall of sound composed of a billion individual actions: the screech of grinding, the percussive slam of mechanized limbs, the high-frequency whine of resonant frequencies, the bass rumble of mass processing. The sound is so pervasive it becomes a physical pressure against the body, a constant vibration that travels up from the soles of your feet and into your bones.

This is the sound of the fauna. They are the ecosystem's engine. They are abundant, and they are the source of the world's turbulence and its strange, inherent technology. They are biomechanical creatures, inseparable from the Vitreflora they live upon. They are not builders; they don't make artifacts or cities. They *are* the process.

Swarms of "Grinders," creatures the size of small cars, move in inexorable lines across the plains. They are like beetles made of obsidian and chrome, their undersides a complex of rotating, diamond-hard rasps. They graze on the Vitreflora itself, scraping its crystalline surface and ingesting the fibres. The screech of their passage is a blade of pure noise that can shatter unprotected ears.

They are followed by the "Refiners." These are larger, slower beings, resembling walking chemical plants. They are all tanks, pipes, and heat-sinks, articulated on dozens of spindly legs. They ingest the raw scrapings left by the Grinders and, through some internal alchemy of pressure and sonics, process it. Vents on their backs pulse with blinding actinic light—flashes of pure white and piercing ultraviolet that cast stark, dancing shadows across the landscape. The light is a byproduct of their process, and it is this light, multiplied by millions, that helps illuminate the world with its harsh, shadowless glare.

The output of the Refiners is a cloud of hyper-energized, micronized dust. This isn't waste; it is the currency of this world's life. The dust glitters in the air, forming shimmering, temporary clouds that drift on currents of displaced air. This is the source of the world's dynamism. Great dust-devils, vortices of incandescent particles, rise from the plains and spiral thousands of feet into the air. Avalanches of it cascade down the lattice-work of the mountains.

And then there are the "Assemblers." These are flying creatures, vast and silent in their passage, their forms blotted against the bright sky. They trail long, gossamer appendages which they use to gather the energized dust. They are the agents of the Vitreflora's own growth. They fly to damaged or growing sections of the world-plant and release the dust, which is re-integrated into the lattice, healing it, expanding it, guided by some planetary, vegetative intelligence.

The "ground" is in constant, chaotic flux. To stand still is to be in the path of a herd of Grinders, or in the settling zone for a Refiner's discharge, or to have the very surface you stand on be consumed and rebuilt in a matter of minutes. The air is never still, whipped into a frenzy by the thermal updrafts from the Refiners and the passage of the Assemblers. The rule of this world is simple: move. The stability of the ancient mountains and plains is a macro-illusion. On the human scale, it is a maelstrom, a factory floor the size of a continent, operating without oversight, without intelligence, a perfectly balanced, utterly alien cycle of creation and destruction that has been running for longer than mortals can conceive. It is a single organism, consuming and rebuilding itself in a deafening, brilliant, endless loop.
