This place is a basin, a vast, shallow depression the size of a small country, and it is entirely artificial. It is a city, but not for living in. You would call it a factory, or a refinery, but its purpose is long lost. Now, its only product is itself.

The air is hot and thick, a palpable fluid that clings to the skin. It has the wet, mineral weight of a cave and the oppressive, chemical heat of an engine room. A permanent, low-hanging haze of steam and other, oilier vapors smothers the basin, creating a perpetual dim twilight. The sun is a rumour, a diffuse, jaundiced glow somewhere above the deck of cloud, and the light that reaches the ground is flat and shadowless.

The terrain is an intricate, sprawling ruin of industrial architecture. There are no soaring towers here; everything is built low to the ground. Massive, circular vats of pitted concrete, hundreds of meters across, are linked by causeways and labyrinthine networks of pipes. Huge, ribbed cooling towers, stained black and green with chemical weep, stand like ancient, hollow trees, their broad mouths perpetually breathing out plumes of steam that merge with the overcast sky. The ground between these major structures is not earth, but a cracked plain of ferrocrete, or vast, shallow catchment pools lined with discoloured ceramic tiles.

This world is relentlessly wet. Water does not fall from the sky in the form of rain. Instead, the hot atmosphere, saturated with moisture from the great vats, condenses on every cool surface. The undersides of pipes, the shaded flanks of reactors, the recessed lintels of forgotten access hatches—all are constantly, silently weeping. This condensation gathers into trickles, which stain the concrete with slick, dark paths. The trickles feed into drainage channels, flowing with a slow, syrupy languor, and eventually empty into the vast, shallow ponds that dot the landscape. The water is warm to the touch and unnaturally clear, carrying a faint, acrid scent like ozone and hot metal.

There is no vegetation. Not a single blade of grass, not a patch of moss, not a smear of lichen. The mineral world is absolute. Yet, the pools and channels are not sterile. A thin, shimmering biofilm, a non-biological patina of chemical reactants, coats their surfaces. In the weak light, it fractures into faint, prismatic colours, an oil slick of pure chemistry. This is the foundation of everything.

The silence is profound, but it is not an absence of sound. It is the suppression of it. The dense, wet air is a blanket that smothers noise. The city is not idle; it hums. A deep, foundational thrumming vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a bass note so low it is felt more than heard. This is the sound of the ancient, automated machinery, still performing its cyclical, forgotten function. Against this hum, the only other sounds are small and immediate: the soft, steady *plink-plink-plink* of water dripping from a high pipe onto a metal plate, and a faint, dry, continuous rustling, like a billion grains of sand being stirred at once.

That rustling is the fauna. The place is empty of large objects but is overwhelmingly, suffocatingly full of life. The landscape crawls. The source of the rustling is the uncountable mass of beetle-like creatures, each no larger than a thumbnail. They move in vast, shimmering sheets across the ferrocrete plains, their carapaces a uniform, polished graphite. They are the grazers, combing the ground for precipitated minerals and consuming the chemical film from the edges of the pools with delicate, complex mandibles. They are a living tide, flowing around the bases of the great structures, their collective movement creating the illusion of a breeze across a field of black grain.

In the warmer, deeper pools swim translucent shrimp and pale, worm-like things that burrow into the sediment of pipes. Clinging to the weeping walls and the undersides of the causeways are slug-like creatures, their bodies the colour of rust, leaving behind trails of glistening slime that quickly evaporate in the heat.

And there are predators. Pale, crab-like scuttlers, the size of a human hand, hunt the edges of the beetle-tides. They move with a stiff, clockwork deliberation, their multifaceted eyes scanning for the weak or the isolated. Their clicks and snaps are sharp, percussive sounds that die almost instantly in the heavy air. Above, in the dim space between the ground and the ceiling of vapor, flit things like greasy, leather-winged moths. They are silent hunters, diving from the gantries and pipe-racks to snatch beetles from the ground, their wings making no sound as they beat against the thick atmosphere.

This is an ecosystem in a bottle, a terrarium built on an industrial scale. The rules are simple and unbreakable. The machinery draws power from some geothermal or fusion source deep below, using it to process subterranean materials. The output is heat, water, and a constant, nutrient-rich chemical effluent. This effluent is the system's "sunlight," feeding the chemical reactions that create the shimmering films. The beetles and other grazers consume this primary layer. The scuttlers and moths consume the grazers. When they die, their bodies are broken down by the hot, chemically active water, their constituent parts dissolving back into the system. The water evaporates, condenses, and drips back down. Everything is recycled. Nothing is wasted. The city has been in this state of perfect, morbid equilibrium for thousands of years, a self-sustaining monument to a forgotten purpose, humming quietly to itself in the near-dark, teeming with a life that owes nothing to the sun.
