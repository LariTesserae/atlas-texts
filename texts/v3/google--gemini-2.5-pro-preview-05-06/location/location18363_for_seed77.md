This world is a floor. From horizon to shimmering horizon, there is no true geology, no rock or soil born of planetary tectonics or volcanic fury. The entire landscape, on a continental scale, is a single, continuous, constructed thing. It is a pavement of life.

The ground is a mosaic of interlocking crystalline plates, ranging in size from dinner plates to the vast floors of dried-up lakes. They are a milky, semi-translucent white, shot through with faint, mineral-blue veins. This is not stone, but a biogenic substance, excreted over millennia by a planet-spanning mat of lithotrophic bacteria. These organisms are the world's foundation, its architects and its very substance. They are a living geology, constantly, almost imperceptibly, building the world upwards. This is a place not of erosion, but of accretion. It is a construction site a billion years old and a moment fresh. The "emerging" nature of the land is not in its youth, but in its unending process of self-creation.

The terrain is almost perfectly flat, a lowland plain of impossible breadth. The only topography comes from the expansion of the plates themselves. Where two vast sections of the bacterial colony grow against each other, they buckle. The pressure shatters the edges, raising low, jagged ridges that run for hundreds of miles like fractured spines. These ridges, rarely more than a few feet high, are the world's only mountains. They glitter with freshly broken facets, sharp as glass, catching the normal, yellow-white light of the sun in blinding glints.

The heat is immense and constant, a heavy blanket that presses down on the pan. The air shimmers, distorting the already incomprehensible distances. There is no shade. The atmosphere is thick with humidity that never quite resolves into a lasting coolness. Water here is a visitor, not a resident. Days, weeks can pass under the baking sun, the crystal floor becoming so hot it would sear flesh. The air grows thick and saline.

Then, a change. The sky, usually a pale, heat-washed blue, curdles with a high, greasy haze. A slow, warm rain begins to fall. It is not a storm, but a quiet, persistent weeping from the sky that can last for a day. The water is heavy with dissolved minerals, the lifeblood of this world. It does not soak in. It pools on the non-porous crystalline plates, forming vast, shallow lakes, inches deep but miles across. The world is transformed into a mirror, reflecting the sky with perfect, silent clarity.

This is the moment of activity. The dormant, planet-spanning bacterial mat awakens. A faint, milky luminescence begins to glow from within the crystal, visible only at night. The quiet of the pan is broken by a new set of sounds. It is not the sound of life as we know it, but the sound of construction. A near-inaudible, deep hum vibrates through the ground as the bacteria metabolize the mineral-rich water. This is followed by a chorus of subtle noises: the faint *tink* of new crystal forming; a slow, tearing *shiiing* as a plate expands against its neighbor; a sharp *crack* as pressure is released along a stress line miles away. The world is audibly *growing*.

As the heat returns and the ephemeral lakes begin to evaporate, the second form of life on this world has its season. Here and there, scattered with a profound sparseness that emphasizes the sheer scale of the emptiness, are the Salt-stalks. They are this world's version of vegetation. A single, unbranching stem of a tough, translucent fiber, rising five to ten feet from the crystal floor. They have no leaves, no flowers. During the dry times, they are dormant, their surfaces matte and grey. When the water comes, they swell, becoming taut and crystalline themselves. They absorb the mineral water through osmotic pressure at their base, which is fused directly to the pan. They are not rooted *in* it; they are part of it. For a few days, they are plump and vibrant, their insides glowing with the same faint luminescence as the ground. They are the only vertical things in this horizontal world. An observer might see one, and then not another for a mile or more.

There are no animals. Nothing walks, crawls, or flies. The only movement is the shimmer of the heat, the slow creep of the water evaporating, and the imperceptible expansion of the world itself. The dynamic is purely environmental and geological-biological. The cycle of wet and dry, of dormancy and growth, is the only rhythm.

The air smells of hot mineral dust, ozone from the sun's glare, and the faint, clean scent of water on hot crystal, like the air after a distant lightning strike. To stand in this place would be to feel the profound weight of scale, to be an insignificant speck on a canvas being painted at a pace just beyond human perception. The rules are simple: the floor grows, the water comes, the water goes, the stalks drink. It is a closed, patient system, a world building itself, for itself, in the silent, baking heat.
