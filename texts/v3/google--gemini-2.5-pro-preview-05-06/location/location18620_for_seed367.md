The first thing you notice is the air. It is not air as you know it. It is thick, warm, and heavy in the lungs, carrying a palpable weight and a scent of ozone, hot dust, and something like blooming jasmine fermented in honey. This is the Breath. It is everything. There is no water here. No rain falls, no rivers carve the land, no dew beads on a leaf. To ask for a drink is a nonsensical question. You do not drink; you breathe.

The ground and walls of this place are a deep, uniform grey stone, worn so smooth by ages as to feel like polished bone or soapstone under the hand. Every edge is a soft curve, every surface a gentle slope. This is a pocket in the world, a single, vast, bowl-shaped caldera, perhaps only a few miles across. The anemic, grey light of the sky above is choked by the Breath, diffusing it into a permanent, dim twilight that filters down from an unseen sun. It is a world lived in the shadow of its own atmosphere.

And in this dim, waterless bowl, life is riotously, obscenely lush. It is not green. The dominant hues are deep violet, bruised purple, and a startling, chemical orange. Nothing here is a plant in the terrestrial sense; it is all Mycelia. Great, fibrous stalks of purple "wood," tough as bamboo but porous and light, swell up from the stone floor and cling to the near-vertical walls. They are not rooted; they are anchored, drawing their sustenance directly from the thick, nutrient-rich Breath. Their foliage is not leaves but vast, overlapping shelves of spongy, orange fungus and delicate, crystalline fronds that chime faintly when disturbed. They grow visibly if you watch for long enough, swelling and pulsing with the slow, deep rhythm of the caldera's respiration.

Woven into this vertical forest is a city. Or, the forest *is* the city. There is no clear line. Structures are not built on the land, but grown out from it and into it. Dwellings are pods, woven from the living, flexible fibers of the purple stalks, sealed with a plaster of dried fungal paste and animal resin. Bridges of taut, braided vine span dizzying chasms between stalks, swaying in the imperceptible currents of the Breath. Ladders are not constructed but coaxed from the Mycelia, which grows handholds and footholds in spiraling patterns up the trunks. The density is staggering. Pods are clustered one atop the other, their woven balconies almost touching. Nets for catching airborne spores or small fauna hang like spiderwebs between the growths. Everything is layered, close, intimate. The sense is not of a city built in a jungle, but of a single, city-sized organism.

The sound is a constant, overwhelming presence. It is a physical pressure on the ears. At the base is a deep, thrumming hum, the collective resonance of a million beetle-sized creatures with iridescent carapaces that vibrate their wings not to fly, but to filter the Breath. Layered over this is a perpetual, dry, clicking chorus, like a billion grains of sand being poured onto a sheet of chitin. This is the sound of the Skitterers, six-legged arthropods that swarm over every surface, decomposers and grazers, the foundation of the food web. And punctuating it all are the sharp, flute-like calls of the Weavers—graceful, furred primates with prehensile tails who swing through the fungal canopy—and the sudden, guttural shrieks of their predators, winged reptiles that glide on thermals of warm air, their leathery wings crackling as they dive. Life is so abundant it is a form of erosion, a constant pressure of bodies and noise. The air is never still; it is always stirred by a wing, a tail, a fall of spores, a scurrying limb.

The inhabitants, the people of this place, are indistinguishable from the Weavers at a distance. They are lean, agile, and their skin has the same dusty grey tone as the ancient rock. They move with practiced ease through the vertical world, their hands and feet calloused from gripping the fibrous wood. Their technology is an extension of the ecosystem. They carry knives flaked from volcanic glass found in rare, hard seams of the caldera wall. Their spears are tipped with the sharpened leg-spurs of the largest Skitterers. They wear cloaks of woven fur and cloaks of cured reptile skin. They don't farm; they husband. They tend the Mycelia, encouraging the growth of the most nutritious fungal shelves. They set intricate net traps for the larger fauna and harvest Skitterers in great baskets, roasting them over glowing coals of resinous Mycelia-heartwood that burn hot and smokeless.

There are rules here, as ancient as the stone. You do not cut a living stalk, for it is part of the city's foundation. You do not make loud, sharp noises at random, lest you trigger a panicked stampede of grazers that could tear a dwelling-pod from its anchor. And you revere the Breath. You feel it on your skin, you taste it on your tongue. It is the architect of your world, the ghost-ocean in which you swim, the source of every pulse of life in this dim, loud, hidden, and impossibly crowded place. This is not a world without water; it is a world where the sky itself is the sea.
