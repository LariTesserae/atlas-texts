The world is a hall, and the hall is utterly dark.

There is no light from above. There is no sun, no moon, no stars. The idea of a sky is a forgotten myth here. Instead, there is a ceiling, a canopy so thick and interwoven with dripping, phosphorescent growths that it forms a solid vault. This is the source of the place’s loudness: a constant, percussive symphony of water dripping from a thousand billion fleshy leaves and fungal gills, striking a floor a hundred metres below. The interval between drips is infinitesimal; it merges into a continuous, complex drumming, a sound like a world-sized rainstick being slowly, eternally turned.

The air is warm and heavy, saturated with moisture. You could cup your hands and, after a time, collect a drinkable pool of water from simple condensation. It tastes of stone and something electric, like the air after a lightning strike. The atmosphere is thick with the scent of blooming decay, of ozone, and of the sweet, cloying perfume of unseen night-flowers.

This is a forest, but the word is inadequate. The space is defined by its emptiness and the things that punctuate it. The floor is not soil but a seamless, springy carpet of moss and mycelium, woven together over millennia into a single, continent-sized organism. It glows. A faint, internal blue-green light pulses slowly through the fibrous webbing underfoot, revealing the true nature of the space. It is a cathedral of trees.

The trees are titans, beyond comprehension in their age and scale. Their trunks are not wood but something more like living stone, worn smooth by eons of dripping water. They are impossibly far apart. Standing at the base of one, you might see the ghostly, glowing silhouette of its nearest neighbour a full kilometre away. The space between them is vast, open, and utterly dark, save for the faint luminescence of the floor and the slow-drifting motes of light that are this world's fauna. The trees’ buttressed roots are the size of hills, flowing down to merge with the glowing ground-mat. The trunks rise into the unseen, dripping darkness, pillars holding up a ceiling of perpetual rain. The scale is intimate—this is a single grove, a room with walls of weeping rock that encircle it miles away—but the contents are colossal.

Scattered among these behemoths, and utterly dwarfed by them, are the only signs of artifice. They are pylons, perhaps a dozen of them in this entire grove. They are forged from a material that is neither metal nor ceramic, a matte black that drinks the faint ambient glimmer and offers nothing back. They stand thirty feet high, featureless obelisks that hum with a vibration felt in the teeth and bones rather than heard with the ears. This sub-sonic thrum is the bass note of the grove’s symphony. The mossy floor-mat pulls away from their bases, leaving a perfect circle of bare, wet, ancient stone. Water that drips onto the pylons sizzles into vapour with a soft *tssss*, releasing another puff of ozone into the soupy air. They are not ruins; they are operational. They are power sources, or anchors, or something far stranger, their function inextricably tied to the impossible biology of the place. They are ancient beyond the trees themselves, part of the grove’s foundational logic.

Life here is abundant, and it is the second source of light. There are no birds, no mammals as we know them. Flocks of what might be moths, each the size of a human hand, drift in silent, slow-motion ballets between the titanic trees. Their wings are membranes of pure light, leaving fading trails of gold and silver in the dark air. On the forest floor, herds of six-legged grazers move with geological slowness. Their bodies are built of overlapping, chitinous plates, and with each placid movement, a soft blue light pulses in the seams of their armour. They feed not on the moss, but on the fungal growths that fruit from it, cropping luminous toadstools with delicate, deliberate mouths. Hunters, rare and silent, mimic the pylons: they are shapes of absolute blackness, detectable only by the sudden absence of the ambient light they eclipse.

The fundamental law of the grove is patience. Nothing hurries. The great trees may grow a centimetre in a thousand years. The slow-motion herds take a day to cross from one clearing to the next. The light from the fungal floor pulses with the rhythm of a hibernating heart. The cycles of this place are not diurnal, but something far longer. Perhaps the pylons dim and brighten over a millennium. Perhaps the dominant colour of the glowing moss shifts from blue to green over an epoch. Change is a process so gradual it is imperceptible to a short-lived observer.

This place is not a beginning. It is an end-state. It is the final, stable form of a high-altitude plateau, eroded down into a shallow, protected bowl. Eons ago, the canopy grew over and sealed itself, creating this self-sustaining terrarium. It is a world in a bottle, powered by incomprehensible technology and illuminated by its own strange life, ticking over in near-stasis, waiting for a purpose that has been forgotten for longer than the mountains have existed. The air is loud with the sound of its own slow, wet, living breath.
