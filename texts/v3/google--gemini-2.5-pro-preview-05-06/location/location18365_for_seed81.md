The air does not move, but it is not still. It is a presence, heavy and hot, clinging to the skin like a wet shroud. The heat is geothermal, a deep and constant warmth that radiates up through the plates of the city's foundation, turning the very ground into a slow-cooking hearth. Above, there is no sky, only a perpetual, close ceiling of roiling vapor, a self-generated cloud bank tinted the color of old brass by the dim, ambiguous light that filters through it. This is a world perpetually in the moments after a scalding rain.

The landscape is a city, but it is empty. It is a plateau of organized construction, a metropolis built on a scale that feels contemptuous of human biology. Vast avenues, wide enough for a river, are paved in vitrified basalt, slick and dark with a constant film of condensation. They stretch for miles in grids and grand, sweeping arcs, flanked by buildings that are less like dwellings and more like monuments to a forgotten industry. These structures are monolithic, composed of pitted bronze, dark iron, and a strange, porous stone that seems to weep an oily, mineral-rich water. Their forms are weathered, the sharp edges of their initial construction long since blunted and rounded by millennia of atmospheric moisture and thermal stress. Rust runs from massive bolts in streaks of orange and deep red, like dried blood on an old altar.

There is no soil, no hint of biological life as it is commonly understood. Nothing grows here but the minerals. In the stagnant, shallow pools that gather in the plazas and along the curbs of the silent boulevards, crystalline structures bloom. Slow, delicate fans of cobalt sulfate spread like blue coral. Along the weeping walls, where the mineral-rich water trickles down, efflorescent growths of white and pale green salts form patterns like frost, intricate and fragile. In some places, these depositions have become thick and layered over the centuries, creating entire grottoes of chemical lace, shimmering faintly in the dim light. These are the world's forests and flowers, a silent, inorganic efflorescence feeding on the city's industrial exhalations.

The silence is profound, but it is not absolute. Its foundation is a hum, a low-frequency vibration that you feel in your bones before you hear it. It is the sound of the city's heart: vast, subterranean engines, perhaps geothermal turbines or arcane reactors, perpetually turning. This is the source of the heat, the power that keeps the system running. Layered over this hum is the sound of water. The constant, soft *hiss* of condensation forming on hot metal. The slow, rhythmic *plink-plonk* of heavy droplets falling from a high archway into a puddle below, each impact echoing slightly in the immense, empty space. Once in a while, from a district miles away, comes a single, resonant *CLANG*—a colossal part shifting, a pressure valve releasing—a sound that travels unimpeded across the vacant expanses, serving only to emphasize the stillness that follows.

This is a city in motion, but its pace is geological. The "weather" is the cycle of steam venting from grates in the avenues, rising to the vapor ceiling, and condensing to fall again as a film of warm moisture on every surface. This circulation is the city's circulatory system, carrying chemical nutrients from the industrial core to the mineral gardens on the periphery.

Fauna is nearly a fiction. But in the wider, deeper pools of runoff that have the dark, chemical sheen of an oil slick, something serene and terrible glides. They are called Conductors. They are not animals. They are flat, ray-like beings, perhaps a yard across, their bodies a complex lattice of silvery, semi-flexible metal. They move with an impossible grace, propelled by silent, undulating waves of their own form, filter-feeding on the precipitating salts and stray electrical charges in the water. They are silent, shy of vibration, and impossibly ancient. They are a clean-up crew, a maintenance swarm, a functioning part of the city's automated ecosystem. To see one is a rare and disquieting event; they are the only complex things that move with purpose in this empty world.

The city extends to the horizon. From the edge of the constructed plateau, you can look out over countless square miles of the same architecture, receding into the haze. No lights twinkle back, save for the occasional, dull orange glow of a heat exchanger or a particularly massive vent. It is a place built for a purpose, not for inhabitants. Perhaps it is a factory that produces nothing but its own continuity. Perhaps it is a tomb whose eternal flame is the heat of its core. It is not dead or in ruin; it is in a state of stable, lonely, and perpetual operation. Its air is thick with the steam of its efforts, its ground is slick with its tears, and its silence is the sound of a machine that has forgotten the reason for its function but can never, ever stop.
