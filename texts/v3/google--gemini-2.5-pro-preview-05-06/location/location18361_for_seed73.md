The air does not move. It is a solid thing, a weight of heat and water you must push through. At this elevation, it should be thin and sharp, biting with cold. Instead, it is a soupy, oppressive blanket, thick with the smell of hot stone, wet earth, and something else, a faint, clean scent of ozone and hot metal, like a storm that never breaks. You have climbed for days to reach this place, and the coolness you expected at the summit is a lie. This is the Anvil of Heaven, the high, hot plateau-lands.

The ground is a soft, saturated sponge of black soil and rotting vegetation, so thoroughly soaked that your boots sink with a sighing squelch. Water is not a feature here; it is the medium. It does not flow in rivers but weeps from everything. The rocks, great, rounded shoulders of dark, weathered basalt, are slick with a perpetual film of condensation, their surfaces furred with emerald and rust-colored mosses. In places, the water seeps directly from the stone itself, not in trickles but in slow, steady sheets that slide down their faces without a sound, as if the rock is sweating out its very essence. The air is so humid that distant shapes are blurred, softened at the edges, and the light of the sun is a diffuse, buttery glare that casts no hard shadows.

From the edge of one plateau, the world opens up. You stand on the precipice of a flat-topped mountain, a tableland miles across. In the hazy distance, other, similar mesas rise like islands from a sea of clouds that fills the deep valleys between. This is not a single peak, but a region, an archipelago of summits. The erosion is ancient. There are no jagged edges, no sharp spires. Eons of this slow, wet heat have rounded every contour, softening the geology into gentle, brooding monoliths.

The source of the heat becomes apparent not by sight, but by a low, pervasive hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet. Here and there, immense, dark structures push up from the earth. They are not buildings, not exactly. They look like geological formations, extrusions of metallic, obsidian-like material that have been fluted and shaped by forces other than wind and water. These are the Heat-Weavers. They are ancient, their surfaces pitted and scarred, yet they are not ruins. They are active. A shimmering distortion hangs in the air around them, and the moss that tries to grow on their lower sections is scorched black. Their tops, hundreds of feet above, are often wreathed in their own private clouds as moisture in the super-heated air condenses around them. They thrum with a patient, immense power, radiating warmth that turns this high-altitude landscape into a planetary-scale greenhouse. This is technology so old it has been mistaken for geology, engineering that has become ecology.

And life, in response, is hysterically abundant. The vegetation is a riot of deep, waxy greens. Tree-analogues with broad, paddle-like leaves angle themselves toward the hazy sun, their bark thick and corky to insulate them from the constant warmth. Giant, translucent ferns, their fronds beaded with moisture, grow in dense thickets. Strange, bulbous plants, the size of barrels, squat directly on the weeping rock faces. They are called Boiler-bulbs, and they seem to tap the geothermal or technological heat directly; a faint steam sometimes vents from pores on their skin. The air is thick with spores and the sweet, heavy perfume of flowers that bloom and rot in a matter of hours.

The soundscape is a crushing, layered orchestra. A million different insects create a constant, high-frequency serration of noise. The calls of unseen avians—some melodic, some like harsh rattling shrieks—cut through the din. Fat, glistening amphibians, the size of dinner plates, bellow from stagnant pools with a sound like plucked bass strings. Everything is calling, clicking, humming, rustling. Beneath it all is the unceasing, foundational hum of the Weavers, a subsonic pressure you feel in your bones. It is a loud, dense world, but the processes are slow. Nothing here moves quickly except in a panic. The plants grow with a ponderous, inexorable slowness; the clouds drift languidly between the mesas; the Weavers hum on a timescale of millennia.

There are signs of habitation, but they are faint and few. Shelters are not so much built as they are found. A deep hollow under the overhang of a basalt outcrop might be enclosed with a simple wall of woven lianas and mud. You might find a set of steps carved into a steep, mossy slope, their edges worn smooth by generations of use. Near one of the smaller, cooler Heat-Weavers, someone has etched a complex, spiraling symbol into the metallic surface. The mark is old, its lines softened, but the material of the Weaver is so resilient that the glyph remains perfectly clear. These are not the works of the beings who built the Weavers. They are the marks of a people who live in the strange paradise the Weavers created, living in the shadow of a power they don't understand, a people adapted to the slow, loud, hot, wet world that is their only home. Life here is lived in a perpetual steam bath, to the soundtrack of a machine that has forgotten its own purpose.
