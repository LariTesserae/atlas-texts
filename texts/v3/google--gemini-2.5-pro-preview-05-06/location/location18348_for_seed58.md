The air is a solid thing, thick as felt and hot to the touch. It doesn't move. There is no wind in this place, only a crushing, uniform pressure of warmth that presses in from all sides, slicking the skin with a sweat that never evaporates. The heat is not the dry, scouring heat of a desert, but the damp, suffocating heat of a sealed boiler room. It has a weight and a texture.

This place is a chasm, a vertical wound in the earth’s crust. It is not a natural cave worn smooth by water, but a fresh fracture, sharp and angular. The walls are vast, jagged planes of dark, vitreous basalt, meeting at unnatural angles. They are so new you can still see the stress-lines in the rock, the conchoidal fractures where immense forces tore it open. The scale is that of a grand cathedral nave, but one laid on its side and dropped a mile down. There is a ceiling high above, lost in the hot gloom, and a floor far below, equally indistinct.

And clinging to the walls of this chasm is a city.

It is not a city of streets and squares, but a vertical lattice, a dense cascade of metal and ceramic built inwards from the rock. Platforms jut out, connected by a dizzying web of grated walkways and steep, riveted staircases. Dwellings, workshops, and strange industrial humps are stacked one atop the other, layered like barnacles, their outer surfaces stained with rust and mineral deposits. It is a city built for density, not comfort. Every volume is filled. From any given platform, you could reach out and touch another structure, or a thick, humming pipe, or the cold, damp rock of the chasm wall.

The entire construction is drenched. A perpetual, lukewarm drizzle falls from the unseen ceiling, a constant condensation cycle driven by the heat below meeting the cooler rock above. The water runs in rivulets down the basalt walls, drips from the edges of walkways, and pools on corroded steel plates. It is this water that makes the heat so oppressive. Every hot surface—and nearly every surface is hot—hisses faintly as the droplets strike it, releasing a soft puff of steam. The air smells of wet stone, hot metal, and ozone.

The source of the heat, and the city’s heart, is the machinery. This is not a place of clanging pistons or grinding gears. The industry here is thermic, and it is eerily quiet. The only sound is a pervasive, low-frequency hum that you feel more in your teeth and bones than you hear with your ears. It is the thrum of immense electrical resistance, the song of silent, monolithic heat exchangers—the Caloric Cores—mounted deep within the city’s structure. Cherry-red light seeps from vents in their armored cowlings, casting a dim, hellish glow. Vast, insulated pipes, as thick as ancient trees and sheathed in a silvery, asbestos-like cloth, snake through the architecture, carrying superheated fluids. Their surfaces are too hot to touch, radiating an invisible wall of force.

There is life here, but it is not animal life. No insects buzz in the humid air, no vermin skitter in the shadows. The quiet is absolute in that regard, unbroken by chirps or calls. The only living things are the sparse, specialized vegetation. On the cooler sections of the chasm walls, away from the direct radiation of the Cores, grows a strange, coppery moss. It doesn't seek light; it appears to feed on the mineral-rich water that trickles down the basalt, its metallic sheen glinting in the dim orange glow of the city’s caged service lights. Clinging to the warm casings of the pipes and conduits is another organism, a flat, grey lichen that slowly pulses with a faint internal luminescence, absorbing the ambient heat and converting it into a chemical glow. It is called Fuse-Lichen, and it is the city’s only living source of light.

The world here operates on a single, continuous loop. The Caloric Cores draw geothermal energy from a source even deeper in the earth, converting it into usable power and radiating immense waste heat. This heat warms the chasm. The hot, saturated air rises, condenses on the high, cool ceiling of the fracture, and falls as mineral-laden rain. This water cools the machinery, sustains the chemotrophic moss, and is eventually boiled away to start the cycle anew. The city is a self-contained ecosystem, a terrarium built around an engine. It is constantly in motion—the slow drip of water, the steady hum of power, the silent metabolism of the glowing lichen—but it is the motion of a machine, not a biosphere. It is a place built for a purpose, but the builders are gone, and the machine simply continues its work in the hot, wet, quiet dark.
