The sound reaches you first, a constant, complex chord. It is not the roar of a waterfall or the simple howl of wind, but a vast and structured resonance, as if a choir of giants is humming a single, unwavering note across a hundred octaves. The air is thin and carries a crystalline cold that feels sharp in the lungs, a cold that promises ice but delivers only a pervasive, clinging dampness.

You stand at the edge of a hollow on an immense, wind-scoured plateau. The world here is ancient, worn down past the memory of mountains into a landscape of sweeping, gentle curves. The bedrock underfoot is a type of smooth, slate-grey stone, polished to a dull sheen by eons of wind and attrition, but it is almost entirely hidden.

What covers it is the city. But it is not a city of stone or metal. It is a city grown, accreted, secreted. From the floor of this basin-like hollow rise dozens of bone-white structures. They are not quite towers, not quite spires. They are more like colossal flutes, standing between fifty and a hundred feet tall, their forms rounded and organically asymmetrical. They look like something grown by a coral reef in a time-lapse played over millennia. The material is a kind of chalky, porous calcite, riddled with holes of all sizes, from pinpricks to gaping archways. Time and wind have softened every edge, so they appear less like architecture and more like the ultimate, stable forms of themselves, the final shape into which they could possibly settle.

This is a place of profound emptiness and overwhelming lushness, a paradox held in perfect balance. The space between the fluted towers is vast and open; you could walk for a minute between one and its nearest neighbor. The air is just air, clean and cold. But every surface is alive. A thick, unbroken carpet of life coats every inch of the ground and climbs every face of the white spires. It is not grass, but a dense, velvety pelt of emerald, ochre, and deep purple mosses. Studded within this moss are plants like swollen, translucent sacs of gelid water, shivering in the wind, beaded with condensation that never quite freezes. This vegetation is what holds the moisture, acting as a planetary-scale sponge, breathing a damp chill into the air. Water doesn't flow here; it weeps. The porous walls of the spires are constantly, slowly weeping captured moisture, which beads on their mossy skins and keeps the whole system perpetually damp.

The overwhelming sound is the city singing. The wind, channeled by the high plateau, pours through the countless pores, holes, and archways of the hollow spires. Each tower is a musical instrument, a colossal organ pipe. The varying sizes of the apertures create a staggering acoustic complexity—a deep, foundational drone from the largest hollows, overlaid with a skittering mesh of high-pitched whistles and mournful, fluting cries from the smaller orifices. Standing within the grove of spires is to be immersed in a sound so total it becomes a physical presence. The ground itself thrums with it; the air shudders. It is a loud, active, and entirely non-biological symphony. The city is breathing.

This system is ancient and self-sustaining. The spires act as condensation traps, catching atmospheric moisture. The trapped water sustains the lush, insulating blanket of moss, which in turn protects the spires from the worst of the cold and wind, preventing them from shattering. It is a closed, active loop of geology, meteorology, and biology, functioning on a timescale far slower than any animal life.

And fauna is exceptionally rare. The constant, deafening vibration and the low-nutrient environment make this a difficult place to live. But if you press your face to a mossy wall and watch for a long time, you might see them: tiny, translucent creatures like multi-legged water bears, which the locals might call "chime-crawlers." They navigate the moss-fields, sipping condensation and grazing on microbes, their entire world a single, vibrating, vertical plain. Larger life is absent. There are no birds to nest in the flutes, no predators to stalk the mossy ground. The city has no need for citizens.

The light is normal, clear daylight, sharpened by the altitude. It casts long, stark shadows that slide across the basin as the day progresses, causing the pitch and timbre of the city’s song to shift as the sun warms different faces of the spires, altering the air currents. The place is never silent and never still. It is a single, massive, living instrument, a settlement built by nothing and for nothing, whose only purpose is to sing its strange, cold, eternal song to the empty sky.
