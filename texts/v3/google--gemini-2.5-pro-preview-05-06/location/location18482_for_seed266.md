This is a world of silent, high places. You are on a plateau of impossible scale, a tabletop of a continent held up to the sky. The air is thin, carrying little sound and no moisture, and the light from the system's sun is clean and sharp, without the haze of humidity. The sky is a pale, washed-out ochre, the color of rust and dust.

The ground is not sand. It is a fine, metallic-tasting dust that coats everything in a shimmering, dark red film. Beneath it is a bedrock of smooth, wind-scoured stone. Over aeons, the ceaseless, slow wind—a barely perceptible thermal tide rather than a gale—has sculpted the mountain ranges that fringe the plateau. They are not jagged peaks but vast, aerodynamic forms, like the backs of colossal, sleeping creatures. The erosion here is a process of polishing, not breaking. Geologists would weep, for the strata are exposed in graceful, sweeping lines of purple, grey, and dull green, as if the mountains were carved from enormous pieces of scrimshaw. There is no soil, no loam, no hint that water ever flowed or life ever grew from the ground.

And yet, there is life. Abundant life. They are known, by the few who study them, as Geodes. They are not fauna in any biological sense we would recognize. From a distance, they might be mistaken for unusual rock formations: crystalline structures, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a house, scattered sparsely across the plains. They are faceted, semi-translucent, and thrum with a faint, internal light. Get closer, and you see the truth. They move. Their movement is a slow, ponderous creep, a glacial drift across the mineral flats, leaving shimmering trails in the dust that persist for decades. They do not eat or drink. They are lithovores, or perhaps thermovores. Their existence is a patient act of accretion, drawing minerals from the substrate and energy from the planet's geothermal warmth, which keeps the surface at a constant, pleasant heat. Over centuries, they grow, layer by crystalline layer, their internal structures becoming ever more complex. They communicate not with sound, but with slow pulses of light that ripple from one to another across the vast, quiet distances.

It is for them that the city exists.

The city is not a place of habitation in the traditional sense. It is the Array. It has no central hub, no downtown, no residential sectors. It is a vast, distributed network of industrial architecture laid out like a circuit board across a thousand square miles of the plateau. The structures are low, dark, and angular, built to withstand the endless, gentle abrasion of the wind. They are connected by a web of silent, magnetic rails and buried conduits. There are towering, slender spires that reach into the thin air, not for observation, but as accumulators, gathering some unknown energy from the ionosphere. There are vast, semi-buried foundries, their locations given away only by the deeper, more intense warmth in the ground above them and a low, subsonic hum that you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears.

This is an industrial complex dedicated to a single purpose: the harvest.

Moving between the Geodes are the Harvesters. They are immense, spider-like machines, as large as terrestrial naval ships. They move with an unnatural grace and a profound quiet, their many legs articulating carefully to not disturb the dusty ground more than necessary. They are slow-motion shepherds in this mineral pasture. A Harvester will approach a mature Geode—one that might be millennia old—and delicately, with incomprehensible precision, it will begin its work. Lasers, sharp enough to cut atoms, will sing a low, pure tone as they slice away the outer, most recently grown layers of the creature's crystalline body. The Geode does not seem to register this. It is a shearing, not a slaughter. The harvested crystal plates, shimmering with captured light and complex information, are lifted by magnetic cranes and placed onto the silent rail-cars that glide into existence from the network.

The cars carry the plates to the processing nodes, those half-buried factories where they are refined. Inside these automated cathedrals of industry, the crystal is shattered, melted, and reformed. It is not being broken down for raw materials, but for the patterns encoded within it. The Geodes, in their slow, geologic lives, create a form of spontaneous, solid-state computation. Their crystalline structures are perfect, naturally-formed lattices, ideal for processes that a manufactured silicon chip could only dream of. The Array is not mining for metal; it is harvesting thought, or something very much like it. The end product is a substrate of pure potential, shipped off-world for purposes unknown.

To be here is to be immersed in a silence that is not empty. It's a weighted quiet, punctuated by the almost-inaudible thrum of the Array, the faint, pure chime of a distant laser, the slow, visible pulse of light from one Geode to another. The air is still and warm. The loneliness of the vast, open space is countered by the constant, if distant, presence of the silent machines and their living, mineral flock. It is a world without weather as we know it, only the slow, grand cycles of light and dark, and the even slower cycle of growth and harvest, an ecosystem of machine and mineral operating on a timescale that renders a human life an insignificant flicker. It is a factory floor the size of a nation, running with the patience of a god.
