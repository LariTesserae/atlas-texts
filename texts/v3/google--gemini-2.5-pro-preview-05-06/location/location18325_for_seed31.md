This is a high, wet place. A great tableland, a plateau hoisted into the lower layers of the sky. The world below is an abstraction, lost in a perpetual sea of cloud. Here, you are on an island in the air. The atmosphere itself is a source—water does not so much fall here as it arrives. The air is thick with a vapour that is not quite mist, not quite rain, but a constant, condensing presence. It beads on every surface, slicking the stone and leaving heavy, pendulous drops on the edges of leaves.

The rock is young: sharp, dark, and igneous. It was thrust up in some geologically recent convulsion and has not yet had time to soften. Its edges are crisp, its faces sheer. Yet this young rock is made ancient by the water. Water weeps from it constantly. Not in rushing waterfalls, but in slow, steady seeps from a million tiny fissures. Entire cliff faces are dark with moisture, shimmering like obsidian curtains. The water on them catches the diffuse, pearlescent light of the overcast sky, which is perpetually bright but without a visible sun.

The ground underfoot is not soil in the traditional sense, but a saturated mix of dark peat, crushed stone, and tangled root matter. It is a world-sized sponge. In the open expanses, this sponge is dotted with shallow, crystal-clear pools. They are not fed by streams, but seem to well up from below, expressions of a water table that is mere inches beneath the surface. There are no ripples. The surface of each pool is a perfect, silver mirror reflecting the static white sky.

Across this high, wet plain, a city has been laid. It is not a city for living in. It is a city of transit and vast, empty purpose. The "buildings" are not houses or halls, but monolithic works of infrastructure, executed with primitive but monumental effort. Great causeways, wide enough for a dozen people to walk abreast, march across the landscape. They are built of precisely-fitted, unmortared blocks of the native dark stone, raised just high enough to stay above the perpetually saturated ground. They run in impossibly straight lines for miles, sometimes rising into broad, elevated plazas, other times intersecting at perfect right angles.

These causeways and plazas are the bones of the city. They are layered, evidence of a slow, programmatic expansion. In some places, you can see the edge of an older, lower causeway peeking out from beneath a newer, wider one. The stone is worked, but simply. The tools were effective but not complex: hammers, chisels, levers, and immense patience. The surfaces of the stone are slick with a thin, emerald-green film of algae, soft and slippery to the touch.

Between these stone lines, the vegetation is lush but sparse in its arrangement. There are no dense forests, but rather scattered groves of trees and wide gardens of moss and fern. The trees have smooth, copper-coloured bark and wide, flat canopies of enormous, leathery leaves. Their root systems are shallow and sprawling, some even sending down aerial roots from their branches to find purchase in the sodden ground. Great blankets of moss, thick as woollen carpets and in shades ranging from lime green to deep forest, cover the ground and climb the bases of the stone pylons that support the higher plazas. There are ferns with fronds as tall as a person, uncurling in arrested motion. There are no flowers, no fruits, no seeds. Life here seems to propagate by other, quieter means—splitting, spreading, budding. The air is clean and smells of wet rock, ozone, and the deep, earthy scent of damp peat.

There is no animal life. Not a single insect buzzes in the thick air. No birds call from the copper-barked trees. No frogs plop into the still pools. This absence is the landscape's most defining feature. It is a world without movement, without scurrying, without flight.

This lack of fauna contributes to the profound and unnerving silence. The only sound is the sound of water, and it is a sound of stasis, not of flow. It is the gentle, polyrhythmic *pat-pat-pat* of heavy drops falling from the wide leaves onto the stone causeways. It is the almost sub-audible hiss of water seeping from rock faces. It is a quiet, constant dripping that serves not to measure time, but to fill it. Perhaps, if you stand on one of the great plazas and hold your breath, you can feel a low, resonant hum coming up through the soles of your feet—the vast stone architecture settling into its equilibrium.

Nothing changes here. The temperature is a constant, balmy warmth. The light level is unwavering from dawn to dusk, a day without a sun to track. The water levels in the pools do not rise or fall. The weeping cliffs never run dry, nor do they flood. The plants are verdant and healthy, but they do not seem to grow. The dead leaves on the ground do not decay; they lie in a state of perfect, waterlogged preservation. The city is not in ruins; it is pristine, as if the builders laid the last stone and walked away only yesterday, and yesterday has never ended.

It is a monument to a forgotten purpose, a perfectly set stage for a play that will never begin. The causeways lead from nowhere to nowhere. The plazas are empty courts for assemblies that will never gather. It is a landscape of profound and patient waiting, held in a state of beautiful, sterile, and silent permanence.
