The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not an environmental effect, like wind or water, but the environment itself. It is a chord, a vast, foundational hum composed of dozens of individual, resonant tones. It is loud enough that you feel it in your bones, a constant, architectural pressure of sound that seems to hold the very air in place. The sound is the rule; everything else is an exception.

The space is a basin, a wide, shallow bowl of land cupped at high altitude. You might call it a valley, but it has no river. You might call it a plateau, but it is contained. Its edges are low, rounded hills, worn smooth by an eternity of wind and mist, their forms so old they seem to have settled into a state of final repose. The scale is that of a grand cathedral, but one with the sky for a roof and the horizon for a wall. Though vast, the space feels intimate and bounded.

It is a landscape of profound and verdant sparseness. Out of the ground, spaced hundreds of yards apart, rise the Bell-Throats. They are the source of the sound. Each is a solitary, colossal organism, a plant that feels more like a geological feature. Their trunks are fifty feet tall, as wide as ancient trees, but smooth and grey-white, with a texture like unglazed ceramic. They are streaked with green and black from the constant moisture that weeps down their sides. At its top, each trunk flares open into a single, massive, upturned horn, a waxy, translucent structure of deep, fleshy green, veined with amber. It is the wind of the heights, catching the lips of these thousand organic horns, that makes the music. Each Bell-Throat sings its own unwavering note, and together they perform the chord of the basin.

Between these pillars of sound, the ground is open. There is no undergrowth, no chaotic tangle of jungle. The floor of the basin is a single, unbroken carpet of cushion moss, a deep and uniform pile of brilliant, almost fluorescent green. The moss is saturated. Water does not flow here in streams, but seeps and percolates through this living sponge. The entire landscape is a slow-moving bog. To walk is to leave dark, heavy footprints that fill immediately with clear, cold water. Here and there, the moss gives way to exposes of the bedrock beneath: smooth, dark, oil-slick stone, worn into soft undulations and weeping from every pore. The air is thick with condensation, a warm, heavy humidity that makes every surface glisten. The light is bright but shadowless, a diffuse, pearlescent glow from a sky of high, thin cloud.

This world changes on a glacial timescale. The Bell-Throats grow imperceptibly over centuries, their notes deepening with age. The water cycles from air to moss to stone and back to air without ever leaving the basin. It is a slow, patient, and self-contained system.

Life, aside from the moss and the resonant giants, is exceptionally rare. You can walk for an hour and see nothing move. But if you are still and patient, you might spot a Grazer. The size of a boulder, it moves with an arthritic slowness across the mossy floor. Its hide is a mosaic of grey and green plates, like lichen-covered stone, slick with moisture. It is less an animal and more a piece of animate geology, its passage too slow to register as motion unless you watch for many minutes.

There are no buildings, no ruins, no sign of any hand that has piled one stone upon another. But there is a secret in the soil. As you walk, your feet might scuff something hard and sharp in the peaty moss. Kneel down, part the wet green strands, and you might find it: a sliver of obsidian. It is not native to these rocks. It is jet black, volcanic, and flaked with an unnatural intelligence. The edges are still so sharp they can draw blood. These are not arrowheads or knives, just fragments and shards, lost promises of a tool. Perhaps they were brought here by winds from some distant eruption. Or perhaps they are the teeth of some long-extinct creature. Or perhaps, most strangely, they are what the slow, stone-skinned Grazers use, held delicately in their hidden maws, to scrape nutrients from the weeping bedrock in the heart of the humming basin.
