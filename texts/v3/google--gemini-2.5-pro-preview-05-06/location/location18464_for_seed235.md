The sound is first. Before sight, before heat, the sound fills the mind and hollows it out. It is a roar, but a roar without violence or variation. It is a standing wave the size of a mountain range, a single, complex, deafening chord that has not changed in a thousand years and will not change in a thousand more. It is not the sound *of* anything—not of wind, not of water, not of life—but the sound *that is* the place. A vibration in the jawbone, a pressure in the skull, a physical presence that leaves no room for thought.

Your eyes open to a vista that defies physics. You stand on the edge of a precipice, looking out over a city that blooms from a colossal mountain plateau. The elevation is extreme; the air should be thin and freezing. Instead, it is a thick, hot, wet breath, a shimmering atmosphere that tastes of ozone and chlorophyll. The heat does not come from the sun, which hangs in the sky as a pale, indifferent disc of normal light. The heat radiates upwards from the city itself, a biological furnace on a geological scale.

This is the city with no builders. It is an urban sprawl grown, not made. Towers, thick as ancient redwoods and pale as bone, spiral towards the hazy sky, their surfaces a lattice of fibrous, chitinous material. They are connected not by steel cables but by vast, sweeping buttresses of woven, woody ligament, strong enough to support the weight of cathedrals. Spanning the chasms between districts are bridges of fused, translucent membrane, stretched taut like drum skins and slick with a constant, weeping condensation.

The "vegetation" and the "city" are one and the same. This is life as architecture. The forms are dense, layered, leaving almost no open ground. What appear to be streets from a distance are revealed as deep canyons between the monolithic, living structures. The walls of these canyons are not dead stone but a kind of porous, keratinous bark from which a constant, clear fluid sweats, running in rivulets down to the canyon floors where it forms slow, un-eroding streams. The air is so saturated that water condenses on every surface, making the entire cityscape gleam as if perpetually slicked with oil. Mosses of an impossible, phosphorescent green grow in thick, velvety carpets on the lower levels, feeding on the perpetual damp and heat.

Nothing moves. There are no birds in the sky, no insects in the heavy air, no creatures scuttling in the shadows of the living alleys. The colossal fronds and fan-like leaves that adorn the upper levels of the towers are perfectly still, their position locked in place. No breeze stirs them. This utter lack of motion is the second most overwhelming sensation after the sound. It is a world of lush, vibrant, complex life caught in a state of absolute, eternal stasis. It is not dead, nor is it frozen. It simply *is*, in a permanent, unchanging present.

The landscape is ancient, established. The great living pylons have the weathered look of something that has endured ages, yet they show no signs of decay or erosion. They are in perfect equilibrium, their biological processes of self-repair and sustenance happening on a timescale so slow as to be imperceptible, a metabolism stretched out over millennia. The entire plateau-city is a single organism, a sessile lifeform the size of a nation.

And the sound, that omnipresent, deafening roar. It is the hum of this organism's existence. The sound of its static, high-energy metabolism. The resonance of trillions of cells holding their perfect, unchanging structure against the slow pull of time. It is the sound of a scream held for eternity, a note of pure being with no beginning and no end. To be here is to be an insignificant mote, deafened and silenced, standing at the edge of a living, screaming city that will never, ever move.
