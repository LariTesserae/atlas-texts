The world is a single, continuous organism. From a high vantage, you see not mountains, but old, slumped dorsal ridges of a creature the size of a continent, asleep for an eon. The peaks have been worn down by unimaginable ages of wind and slow, cellular sloughing, leaving them as rounded and placid as the knuckles of a buried hand. Their slopes are not scree and rock, but a thick, living rind, a material like compressed, petrified cellulose. Its color is a deep, bruised purple-grey, shot through with veins of dull, dormant green. This is the hololith, the planet-pelt, the substrate of everything. It is cool to the touch, firm but with the faintest give, like ancient leather stretched over bone. It sweats a cool, constant dampness, a metabolic exhalation that keeps the air perpetually misty and the surface slick with condensation.

Across these vast, open valleys and high plateaus, a city is scattered. It is not built *upon* the land; it is a specialized organ system *of* it. Immense, lonely structures rise from the living ground, separated by kilometers of empty, sweeping hololith. They are towers and domes and slab-like complexes, grown from the same base material but differentiated in function. Their surfaces are a mosaic of chitinous bark, fibrous ligaments, and panels of a translucent, amber-like resin. This is Odem, the Dispersed City, a settlement whose "urban" quality is one of organization, not density. Its districts are archipelagos in a sea of purple flesh.

A constant, overwhelming sound floods the landscape. It is not the chaotic noise of traffic and crowds, but the structured, deafening thrum of industry. This is the primary activity of Odem. Within the resin-walled factories, colossal, organic engines churn. They are hearts the size of buildings, their valvular *lub-dub* a bass note that vibrates through the very ground. Arterial pipes, thick as ancient tree trunks, wrap around and through the structures, pulsing with viscous fluids. From towering, spiracle-like vents, plumes of nutrient-rich steam and metabolic vapor are exhaled into the cool air, contributing to the eternal dampness. This is a bio-industrial process, a city-organism metabolizing itself and its world to produce... something.

And here, the weirdness bleeds in. The logic of this place is frayed. Odem's industry is loud, active, and fundamentally unreliable. A processing node that extruded filaments of solid, woven light yesterday may today simply weep a tar-like, silent fluid. A vascular tower designed for pressure regulation might suddenly begin to chime, its internal structures rearranging to form a massive, resonant bell for a few hours before slumping back into its intended function. Cause and effect have come unstitched. The city runs, but it does not know why, or for what. Its productions are spasms of a planetary nervous system deep in the throes of a dream or a fever.

The light is normal, a sun in the sky, but it is perpetually filtered through the high-altitude mist and the factory steam, resulting in a bright, shadowless, silvery glare. Everything glistens. There are no trees or flowers as we know them; the entirety of the world's vegetation is this single, planet-spanning mat and its urban growths.

Movement through Odem is a disconcerting experience. The sparseness of the structures creates a profound agoraphobia, a sense of being exposed on the skin of a sleeping god. But the distances are not stable. A walk between two districts that takes an hour one morning might take a full day on the return journey, the ground between them having seemingly expanded. Sometimes, a new structure will have grown overnight in a previously empty expanse, its internal machinery already churning with a loud, pointless purpose. These are not geological speeds; this is the breakdown of physical law, the world shifting and reconfiguring itself like a restless sleeper.

Fauna is almost non-existent. There are no birds to scatter from the noisy vents, no insects crawling on the glistening surfaces. The only beings that could be called animals are the tenders, the inhabitants of Odem. They are tall, gaunt figures, clad in suits woven from the city's own fibrous byproducts. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their role not as masters, but as something between a priest, a physician, and a cleaner of drains. They tend the great bio-machines, unblocking clogged arteries, calming the more violent arrhythmia of the factory-hearts, and trying, with limited success, to nudge the chaotic productions toward some semblance of consistency. They are rare, seen only as distant specks against the colossal architecture, their existence a fragile negotiation with a world that is both their home and a terminal, delirious patient. They do not speak; the thrumming of the city is the only language. Their purpose is an inherited ritual, its original meaning lost to the same entropic decay that has unsettled reality itself. They are the last, lonely white blood cells in a body that has forgotten what it means to be well.
