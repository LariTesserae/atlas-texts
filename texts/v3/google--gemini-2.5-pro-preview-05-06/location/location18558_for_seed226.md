The place is a basin, a caldera, but not one born of fire. It feels contained, a world in a cup. You stand at the edge of a settlement, but there are no foundations, no stacked stones in the usual sense. The city and the mountain are one thing.

The ground underfoot is not rock, not soil, not quite living, but not fully inert. It is a pale, chalky-green substance, cool to the touch and slick with a constant, mineral-scented wetness. Geologists would be baffled; it has the texture of very dense, un-fired clay mixed with something fibrous. This material, this "calciform," is slowly, ceaselessly extruding from the deep earth. It swells upwards in colossal, smooth-skinned lobes, like dough rising in geologic time. There is no sharp-edged erosion here, no grit or rubble. The world is being born, not worn away. Its shapes are embryonic: rounded, tumescent, and holding the pure, taut potential of form not yet decided.

This process is not silent. A profound and constant sound fills the air, a chord of immense harmonic complexity. It is the sound of the calciform growing, of uncountable tons of it groaning, resonating, and singing as it swells and settles. It is a bass hum so deep it tickles the bones, layered with a thousand harmonious overtones that shiver in the cool air. The sound is loud, not in a deafening, violent way, but in an omnipresent, inescapable one. It is the sonic wallpaper of existence here.

From this ever-growing substance, a city is carved. The people here are not masons; they are tenders, sculptors of a living geology. The "buildings" are immense, hollowed-out lobes of the calciform. They resemble colossal gourds or fungi, connected by high, arching bridges carved from the same continuous material. The architecture is simple, dictated by the hand-tools they use—scrapers of sharpened bone, mallets of dense, petrified wood, chisels of obsidian traded from some unseen lowland. The process is a slow, patient collaboration with the land's own growth. A dwelling is not built; it is excavated from a swelling mound over a generation. A new bridge is begun not by laying a foundation, but by guiding and carving two slowly rising pillars of earth until they meet.

The scale is intimate yet awe-inspiring. A single one of these hollowed-out structures could shelter a small community, its interior echoing like a cathedral nave. Yet from a high vantage point, the entire settlement is visible: a cluster of perhaps two dozen such carved domes and the webs of bridges between them, all contained within the caldera's rim. The space between these structures is vast, a dizzying emptiness filled with churning mist. The density is low; you could walk for ten minutes across a high bridge and see no one, only the yawning, green-and-white void below and the silent, open doorways of the distant domes.

And everything is draped in green. The calciform weeps a constant supply of mineral-rich water, and the high altitude keeps the air perpetually cool and damp. A thick, unbroken carpet of life capitalizes on this. It's not a forest of trees, but a kingdom of mosses, liverworts, epiphytic ferns, and fleshy, translucent fungi. This verdant blanket is inches, sometimes feet, deep, covering every surface of the city that isn't actively being worked. It softens every line, pads every surface, and turns the entire settlement into a work of velvet geology. The colors are a thousand shades of emerald, jade, and lime, punctuated by the ghostly white of strange, bell-shaped flowers that grow without leaves, feeding directly on the calciform. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and crushed green things.

The light is bright and full, a normal daylight that filters through the thin, high-altitude air and reflects off the omnipresent mist, creating a soft, shadowless, pearlescent glow. This bright diffusion makes the greens of the vegetation almost ferociously vibrant against the pale ground.

Life here is slow and quiet, save for the planet's song. There are almost no animals. No birds call from the green domes, no insects buzz in the damp air. The only fauna are large, slow-moving herbivores, like six-legged manatees made of muscle and cartilage, that graze on the thickest mats of moss. They have smooth, grey skin and no visible eyes, navigating by sensing the vibrations of the world-song. They are rare, treasured, and their movements are as gradual and patient as the landscape itself.

The rules of this world are simple. The ground provides. It provides shelter, it provides the water that feeds the plants, which in turn feed the grazers. The purpose of the people is to tend this growth. They are symbiotic with the geology. Their carving is not just to create space, but to tune the caldera. Different thicknesses and shapes of the calciform resonate at different frequencies. The village elders are not chiefs, but conductors, who listen to the ever-changing planetary chord and direct the carvers to hollow a wall here, or thicken an arch there, maintaining the harmony of their world. Their life is work, but it is also music. Their tools are instruments, and their city is a symphony that is never, ever finished.
