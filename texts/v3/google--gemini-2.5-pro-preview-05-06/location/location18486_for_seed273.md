This is a world built on the spine of a dead god, and its name is the Terrace. It is a single, contiguous structure rising from a sea of permanent cloud into the hard, blue-black sky. To call it a building is a failure of language; it is a topography, an artificial mountain range miles high, with its own peaks, valleys, and plateaus. From any given vantage, the scale is both immense and contained. You can see across a "valley"—a great, swooping catenary of decked-over superstructure—to another "ridge" a mile away, its features clear in the bright, unfiltered light. The whole world is metal, ceramic, and composite, now furred and softened by time. Everything is made. There is no natural ground.

The terrain underfoot is a layered chaos of function. The uppermost surfaces are broad plates of a rust-colored alloy, pocked with access grilles and punctuated by the cowled stacks of vast, silent ventilation systems. These plates are the 'plains,' and they are rarely level, always canted at a slight angle for runoff. The rivets, each the size of a dinner plate, create a treacherous, repeating texture. To step off these plains is to descend into the 'lowlands'—a dense tangle of conduits, coolant pipes thick as ancient trees, and bundled sheaves of humming cable. Ladders, gantries, and catwalks form a multi-level, three-dimensional road system through this industrial undergrowth. The entire structure is weathered, settled. The sharp edges of its construction have been rounded by a million years of wind and condensation. Paint has been scoured away to reveal the dull metal beneath, which wears a patina of bronze, ochre, and verdigris like the skin of a lichen-covered stone. This world is not eroding; it has eroded. It is in its final, stable state.

The air is warm and heavy, saturated with a humidity that does not come from rain. The Terrace is a colossal heat engine, a machine operating on a geological timescale. Its inner workings, deep within the metallic bedrock, generate a constant, low-grade warmth that radiates up through the plates. This heat meets the cold of the upper atmosphere, and the structure sweats. Water beads on every surface. It drips from the bellies of pipes, runs in slow rivulets down support struts, and gathers in stagnant, algae-green pools in concave panels and maintenance troughs. There are no rivers, only a million engineered streams, a perpetual, slow-motion weeping. The atmosphere is thick with the smell of hot metal, ozone, and wet pollen.

And there is life. So much life. The vegetation is not planted; it is endemic. A thick, emerald shag of moss-like growth coats every surface that doesn't face the direct, searing sun. Immense vines, their trunks woody and gnarled, wrap themselves around the coolant pipes, drawing on their warmth and moisture. Their leaves are tough and waxy, the size of kites, forming dense canopies that dapple the bright sunlight into a shifting, hypnotic green twilight in the lower levels. Ferns with iridescent fronds sprout from the seams between plates, their roots finding purchase in accumulated dust and organic detritus. The "soil" here is a compost of shed leaves, insect husks, and rust flakes, a thin but fertile veneer over the machine.

The fauna is as specialized as the flora. Great, silent flocks of "gliders"—leathery-winged avians with metallic plumage—ride the thermal updrafts rising from the ventilation stacks. They build their nests from stripped cabling inside the dormant husks of machinery. The dominant sound, aside from the machine itself, is the chittering and clicking of innumerable insects. "Fuse-spiders" spin webs of conductive filament between electrical junctions, while swarms of "glaze-wings," whose carapaces shimmer like oil on water, drone in the humid air. Larger creatures, skittering, six-legged "plate-crawlers," move with a disconcerting speed across the vertical faces of the structure, their claws making sharp, scraping sounds on the metal. They are scavengers, their powerful mandibles capable of shearing through old, brittle alloys to get at the nutrient-rich sludge within forgotten conduits.

The sound of the place is a constant, layered hum. It is not the clang and clamor of a factory in production, but the deep, resonant thrum of a machine in a state of near-infinite standby. It is a bass note you feel in the soles of your feet, a vibration that travels up your bones. This foundational hum is overlaid with the hiss of steam from pressure-release valves, the slow, rhythmic groan of massive components expanding and contracting in the sun, and the perpetual, gentle dripping of water. Above it all is the constant, high-energy noise of the biosphere—the insectile buzz, the echoing calls of the gliders, the scraping of unseen things moving through the metal jungle.

Change is slow, patient. A support beam might finally buckle under the weight of a thousand-year-old vine. A coolant pipe, rusted from the inside out, will begin to weep a little faster, creating a new micro-climate on the deck below. The machine's processes are the world's seasons, its cycles so long as to be mistaken for permanence. The rules of this place are simple: gravity is the only predator that matters, and everything lives by the grace of the machine's slow exhalations of heat and water. It is a world born from a blueprint, a colossal, failing but not-yet-failed engine that has become, entirely by accident, a greenhouse, a reef, a vertical jungle teeming at the edge of the sky.
