The first thing is the sound. It is not a sound you can place or parse. It is a physical presence, a pressure on the ears and a vibration in the bones of the skull. It is a deafening, aggregate roar composed of a trillion infinitesimal events, a white noise of pure biology. It is the tearing of fibrous tissue on a continental scale. It is the hydraulic hiss of sap being forced through vascular channels the size of aqueducts. It is the percussive pop-crack of seed pods, each detonating with the force of a small firework, happening millions of times a second. It is the grinding of cellulose on cellulose as entire districts of this place shift and grow. The sound is the baseline of reality here, and silence is a fiction.

You are standing at the edge of a low, wide basin that stretches to a hazy, chlorophyll-green horizon. There is no soil, no rock, no earth. The ground itself is a living thing, a planetary mat of interwoven, pulsing roots and fungal hyphae, springy and warm to the touch like the flank of a great beast. From this living substrate, the city grows.

It is unmistakably a city. Towers, thicker at the base and tapering to needle-fine points, strain toward the jaundiced-yellow sky. Sweeping bridges and viaducts arc between them, some gracefully curved, others jagged and broken-backed. There are plazas, canyons that serve as thoroughfares, and dense warrens of smaller, block-like structures. But none of it is built. It is all grown. The architecture is botany.

The towers are not stone but lignified, bark-like material, riddled with pores that weep a viscous, sweet-smelling fluid. Their surfaces crawl with motion; iridescent mosses creep in fractal patterns, and thick, thorny vines race each other toward the light, their progress visible to the naked eye. An archway that looks solid from a distance is, up close, a chaotic lattice of living branches, grafted and woven together by some unknown biological imperative. The air is thick, wet, and hot, like the inside of a lung. It smells of fermentation, crushed green things, and ozone. Light isn't direct; it is filtered through translucent membranes stretched between buttresses, piped through glowing, sap-filled capillaries, and scattered by the constant rain of spores and pollen that fills the air like a golden fog.

This place is a storm of creation. It is never the same for two consecutive moments. As you watch, a slender spire on the far side of the basin shudders violently. It groans, a bass note that momentarily cuts through the cacophony, and then begins to deliquesce. Its form softens, its sharp lines bloat, and it collapses in on itself in a slow-motion cascade of pulp and fibrous sludge. The collapse reveals a space, but the space does not remain empty. Almost immediately, new shoots, thick as a human torso, erupt from the living ground. They spiral upwards with terrifying speed, weaving around each other, extruding scaffolding of smaller branches, building a new and different structure where the old one stood. The "erosion" here is not wind or water; it is rot. The "formation" is explosive, cancerous growth.

The laws of physics are suggestions, not rules. A street between two colossal, bulbous domes might appear to run straight, but walking it leads you back to where you started. Light piped through the luminous vascular bundles that line the "buildings" sometimes pools in mid-air, creating spheres of captive daylight that drift slowly through the humid atmosphere before bursting. There are places, "Resonance Voids," where the deafening sound suddenly vanishes, plunging you into an absolute, terrifying silence. In these pockets, time seems to stretch or compress. You might see the ghost-image of a tower that fell a week ago, or witness the birth and death of a flower the size of a house in the span of a single breath. The geometry of the city is a lie, a protean, non-Euclidean dream of architecture. Distances warp. A bridge that seems a mile long can be crossed in ten steps, while the plaza right in front of you may be forever unreachable.

There is no animal life. Not a single insect, bird, or beast. The motion is the city itself. The intelligence, if there is one, is the distributed, alien mind of the vegetable kingdom. The water is the city's own metabolic waste, its sweat and tears. It condenses on every surface, making the bark-walls slick and treacherous. It pools in depressions in the root-mat, not as clear water but as a shimmering, gelatinous broth, thick with nutrients and genetic information. This is a closed system, a self-devouring engine of life. The city grows, it collapses, it is reabsorbed, and it grows again, each time slightly different, an endless, pointless, screaming cycle of construction and deconstruction. It is a city for no one, built by nothing but life's raw, chaotic impulse to fill a space, to make a noise, to simply *be*.
