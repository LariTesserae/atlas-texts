The air is a lens, thick with heat and shimmering over the vast, skeletal landscape. To be here is to be on the roof of the world, amidst bony spines of mountain that claw at a white, unforgiving sky. The elevation is dizzying, a panorama of peaks and chasms so extensive that the horizon feels like a theoretical concept, always receding. These are old mountains, worn smooth by eons of wind, their forms settled and ancient, like the skeletons of colossal, long-dead creatures. Their ridges are not sharp but rounded, their slopes are vast, sweeping scree fields, their cliffs are scarred but stable.

What chokes this open space, what makes it feel crowded and dense, is not life as one might expect. It is a vast and intricate network of industry, a kind of metallic parasite fused to the rock. A dense, arthritic web of pipes blankets the landscape, running in parallel for miles, then branching into ganglia of valves and junctions before plunging into the mountainside. They are thick as ancient trees, coated in a patina of oxidation—rust-red, verdigris-green, sulphur-yellow. They follow the contours of the land with a slumbering, serpentine intelligence, disappearing into dark bores and emerging on the far side of a ridge, their presence saturating every view, drawing lines between every peak and valley. There is no direction one can look without seeing them.

This network is a vast geothermal circulatory system. The world's inner heat is the primary resource here. The pipes carry superheated water and mineral-rich steam, drawn from deep within the planet's crust. The overwhelming heat of the air is not just from the high-altitude sun; it radiates in waves from the metal conduits. To lay a hand on one would be to lose it. The system is largely quiet, which is perhaps its most unsettling feature. There is no roar of a traditional factory, no clangor of production. Instead, there is a pervasive, low-frequency hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet, a constant reminder of the immense pressures being managed. The silence is punctuated by the hiss of a distant pressure-release valve, the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of cooling metal, or the deep, groaning sigh of a massive joint settling under its thermal load.

This is what passes for water here. There are no rivers, no lakes. The moisture is a byproduct of the technology. Where a pipe has a pinhole leak, a plume of steam escapes, condensing on the cooler rock around it. In the shadows of the great pipe-junctions, the constant drip-drip-drip of condensation has created a unique kind of dampness. The ground beneath these leaks is perpetually slick, coated in a bright, almost fluorescent green or orange algae that thrives on the heat and the specific mineral content of the water. These are the oases of this place: localized pockets of intense, weird life in a field of sterile heat. Patches of rust-colored lichen, tough as ceramic, cling to the shaded undersides of the pipes. Tussocks of pale, needle-thin wire-grass sprout where condensate pools, their silica-stiff bodies rattling faintly in the thermal updrafts.

For all its sparseness, the land is teeming. The fauna has evolved in symbiosis with the industrial skeleton. Long, reptilian creatures with a polished, gunmetal sheen to their scales lie motionless on the hottest sections of pipe, their bodies perfectly adapted to absorb the thermal energy. They are called Cable Stalkers, and they are ambush predators, using the elevated pipes as hunting grounds. Below, scuttling among the rocks and pipe-supports, are chromium-shelled beetles the size of a man’s fist. They move with a jerky, mechanical gait, using powerful mandibles to scrape the mineral crust from the pipes and the rocks, their droppings forming small, glittering piles of refined metals.

Flocks of something like birds, all bone and sinew and translucent wing, nest within the great, cage-like structures of pressure exchangers. They are Vent Sparrows, and they ride the thermal currents rising from steam vents, their calls like the grating of metal on metal. They hunt the smaller, heat-dazed insects that swarm in the shimmering air above the pipes. The entire ecosystem is in motion, a complex dance of predator and prey where the central, immovable feature is the heated, humming machine.

The "built" environment is vestigial. There are no towns, no habitats for settlement. Every few miles, sunk into the rock, one might find a small, windowless substation made of black, vitrified stone, its door a seamless plate of steel with no visible handle. Occasionally, perched on a precarious ledge, is a lone surveyor's hut, a simple metal box long since abandoned, its interior telling a story of brief, uncomfortable habitation. These are not places for living; they are nodes for a system that maintains itself, a vast, automated utility spread across a mountain range.

The rules of this world are simple. The pipes are the source of all energy. The ground is a source of vibration, the sky a source of harsh light. Stay off the pipes; they will cook you. Follow the pipes; they are the only roads. The unusual nature of the place is not in some magical phenomenon, but in the complete and total integration of the industrial and the biological. The Cable Stalkers have learned the thermal cycling of the pipes and know when certain sections will cool enough to cross. The beetles' life cycles are tied to the mineral output of specific geothermal wells. This is not a place that was conquered by technology. It is a place that has absorbed it, digested it, and given rise to a new and profoundly strange biome, humming quietly to itself at the top of the world.
