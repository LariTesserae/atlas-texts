There is no sun here, but the light is absolute. It is a brilliant, depthless, and clinical white, pouring from the undersides of ceiling plates miles wide and from the glowing seams between the floor panels. This is the High Terrace, a constructed plateau of impossible scale, built so high the air is thin and perpetually cool, stinging the lungs with the clean-smelling bite of ozone and chilled metal.

The ground is not earth. It is a vast, unending mosaic of hexagonal ceramic tiles, gunmetal gray and so new they have no-wear, their edges sharp enough to cut. Between them, recessed channels of brushed steel serve as gutters for a constant, slow weeping of condensation. The air is damp. Water beads on every vertical surface, slicking the sides of immense pylons and the intricate lattices of pipework that web the space between structures. It never rains; the moisture is an exhalation of the Terrace itself, a byproduct of the enormous temperature differential between the glowing, humming machinery within its walls and the cold of the upper atmosphere. This water pools in the channels, flowing in slow, orderly patterns toward unseen drains, its surface reflecting the hard, shadowless light from above with a glassy stillness.

The Terrace is both dense and open. From any point, you can see for miles, your gaze traveling across a valley-sized expanse of machinery. But the space is not empty. It is filled with a dense, three-dimensional forest of structure. Towers of interlocking girders rise and connect, forming a canopy of steel a thousand feet up. Between them, great spheres of spun filament, perhaps accumulators or data-hubs, hang suspended by humming tractor fields. Conduits thick as ancient trees snake between these major features, bundled into colossal arteries of metal, ceramic, and unknown composites. Everything is layered, packed, interwoven. One can walk for an hour through a canyon of humming transformers and humming heat-exchangers, only to emerge onto a wide plaza of grating that looks down through a kilometer of open air onto another, similar district below. The scale is regional, but the feeling is of being perpetually within the guts of a single, world-spanning machine.

For all its industrial might, the Terrace is unnervingly quiet. The roar of a billion engines you would expect is absent. Instead, a pervasive, low-frequency hum vibrates through the soles of your feet, a full-body thrum that is more felt than heard. It is the sound of incomprehensible power operating with silent, frictionless efficiency. Occasionally, a distant, resonant *clang* will echo, not off the walls, but seemingly *through* them, as if a great component shifted deep within the substrate. Otherwise, the only sounds are the soft hiss of pneumatics, the slow drip of condensation, and the skittering of the local fauna.

Life is abundant, but it is not organic in any familiar sense. Flocks of what look like shards of polished obsidian, called Glimmer-wings, drift on the thermal updrafts from exhaust ports, their crystalline bodies refracting the stark light into dazzling, momentary rainbows. They feed on leaking photons. Down in the tangled warrens of pipework, colonies of Cable-mites, looking like flexible spirals of copper wire, chew methodically through the outer casing of conduits, not for sustenance, but to lay their eggs in the electromagnetic field within. Their passing leaves behind a shimmering, iridescent trail of oxidized metal. Larger things move in the deep infrastructure. You might catch a glimpse of a Segmented Crawler, its body a hundred meters long and composed of what appears to be articulated cast-iron spheres, moving with a slow, peristaltic grace through a transit tube designed for materials a fraction of its size.

Vegetation is sparse, a desperate colonization of this metal world. In the joints between plates, where moisture collects, a bioluminescent growth that resembles chartreuse lichen, the Veridian Vein, traces fractal patterns. It draws energy directly from the Terrace's low-level energy field. In places, strange, silicon-based flora have taken root. They grow as crystalline stalks of smoky quartz, sprouting delicate, glass-like fronds that chime faintly when a breeze filters through the latticework. They are beautiful, sharp, and utterly alien.

The most profound quality of the High Terrace, however, is its slow breakdown of reality. The machine is too vast, too complex, and its purpose too ancient or alien to maintain coherence. Physical laws are fraying at the edges, not with explosive chaos, but with a glacial, creeping weirdness.

There is a corridor, a long, straight tube of polished chrome, where time flows like honey; a single step can take a minute to complete. Elsewhere, a great plaza is imprinted with the persistent memory of a prior configuration. You can see the shimmering outlines of structures that were replaced cycles ago, faint and translucent, and sometimes feel the phantom resistance of a wall that is no longer there. Light itself is becoming unreliable. Reflections in the slick, wet surfaces sometimes show not what is, but what *will be* a few moments from now—a subtle, nauseating pre-cognition. Or they will show someone else looking back.

Staircases are known to exhibit non-Euclidean properties. You can walk down a flight of one hundred stairs, turn a landing, and find yourself only ten feet below where you started, but looking out over a completely different district. These shifts are not sudden; they are drifts. A staircase might be reliable for years, then slowly, over months, its destination begins to migrate.

The slow dynamic of the place governs this decay. Nothing happens quickly. The hum of the Terrace might drop a few microhertz over the course of a generation. A pylon might imperceptibly bend toward an unseen gravity source, its arc of movement completing over a century. The rules of the world are not being broken, they are being patiently revised. The High Terrace is not failing; it is becoming something else, something its builders never intended, and its new form is emerging with the excruciating slowness of geological change. To be here is to be a microbe on the surface of a thinking, dreaming, and slowly maddening god of metal.
