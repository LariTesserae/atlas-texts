The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a collection of noises but a single, omnipresent entity: a deep, resonant, and grinding Hum that seems to emanate from the very substance of the world. It is the sound of a billion-ton gearbox operating at one revolution per century. It is the sound of rock being born. This Hum is the baseline, the carrier wave upon which all other sounds ride—the clang of dropped steel plates, the rhythmic hiss of pneumatic pistons, the venting of steam from armoured pipes, and the high, whistling shrieks of unseen things in the heavy air.

The air itself is cold, and thick with a dampness that is not quite rain and not quite fog. It is a condensing medium, clinging to every surface in a slick, greasy film. Light struggles here; it does not pour, it seeps. What illumination there is seems to come from below, a diffuse, bruised-purple and rust-orange upwelling from the city itself, catching on the bellies of the perpetual, low-hanging clouds. There is no sun, no moon, only the dim, industrial aurora.

This place is built upon, and within, a mountain that is not finished. The geology is raw, embryonic. There are no soft, weathered slopes, only sharp, crystalline facets and vast, sheer planes of a material that is not yet truly stone. It is Protolith, a substance in a state of slow-motion becoming. It glints with internal, aqueous light, like obsidian shot through with quartz, but its angles can change overnight. A ledge that was safe yesterday might be a razor-sharp edge today, or a concave, glass-smooth bowl tomorrow. The process is slow, in human terms, but it is inexorable. The mountain is growing, and its growing pains are the foundation of everything.

Upon this impossible, extreme-elevation terrain, a city has been grafted. It is not so much built *on* the peaks as it is bolted *to* them. This is not a place of neat grids or gentle avenues. It is a layered, vertical, and utterly provisional settlement. Whole districts are suspended from massive cantilevered spars drilled deep into the more stable masses of Protolith. Walkways are caged mesh, hanging precariously over chasms thousands of feet deep. Buildings are modular, reinforced pods, designed to be uncoupled and moved when the ground beneath them decides to redefine its geometry. The city is a vast, precarious machine for living, a prosthesis attached to a geological infant.

Despite the raw, unfinished nature of the rock, there is a strange, tenacious lushness. In sheltered clefts, on abandoned platforms, and in the mineral-rich runoff from condenser units, life explodes. Thick, velvety mats of Gasket-Moss, the color of oxidized copper, carpet entire walls. Plumes of Condenser Ferns, their fronds heavy with collected water, hang from pipes and girders, dripping constantly and adding a soft, pattering counterpoint to the industrial cacophony. These pockets of intense green are startling against the dark, slick gray of the metal and the shimmering black of the Protolith. They are scattered, islands of biology in a sea of mineral potential, making the space feel both sparse and dense at once.

And there is fauna, abundant and bizarre. Flocks of Pylon Shrikes, birds with wings of iridescent membrane and carbide-tipped beaks, nest in the high-voltage architecture, their calls like tortured metal. On the shifting ground, vast colonies of Geode Beetles move in slow, glittering tides. They are the size of a man’s fist, their carapaces perfect, multi-faceted crystals that mimic the Protolith. They consume the raw geology, their digestive tracts humming with a low-grade energy that makes them glow from within. Higher on the food chain are the Strider-Flocks, hexapodal grazers that move with an unnervingly fluid grace across vertical surfaces, feeding on the Gasket-Moss. They are silent, their footfalls masked by the all-encompassing Hum.

The deepest weirdness is the nature of the landscape's slow dynamic. It is not just that the mountain is growing; it is that the laws of physics are provisional here. The "reality breakdown" is not explosive, but a gradual, constant churn. Corridors sometimes connect to themselves. A view from a window might show a street scene one moment, and a glimpse into a star-forming nebula the next. Solid walls have been known to become momentarily permeable, allowing residents to walk through them before they solidify again. Causality is a strong suggestion, not a law. The inhabitants have a term for it: "Drift." They navigate their world not just by maps of space, but by charts of probable stability. A path is not measured in meters but in its resistance to Drift.

This is why the city is here. It is not mining the Protolith for its material, but for its properties. The industrial factories are not producing goods; they are immense reality-anchors, stabilization engines, and Drift-siphons. The loud, rhythmic clamor is the sound of machinery imposing consensus reality on a small pocket of a universe that would prefer to remain undecided. The power they draw, the phenomena they study, comes from the very fabric of this embryonic world.

Life here is loud, damp, cool, and lived under constant, low-level existential pressure. The people are pragmatic fatalists. They are constantly repairing, reinforcing, and repositioning their world. There is a slowness to their culture, a patience born of living within a process that dwarfs human lifespans. They watch the mountain grow, they chart the Drift, and they work the engines that keep their improbable home from dissolving into the raw chaos it is born from. From a distance, in the perpetual dim light, the city looks less like a settlement and more like a vast, complex organism, a metallic scab clinging to a wound through which a new reality is slowly being born.
