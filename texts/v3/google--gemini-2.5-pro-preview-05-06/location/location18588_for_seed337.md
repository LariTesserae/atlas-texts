There is a sky here, but it is not air; it is stone. A vast, vaulted ceiling so distant its highest points are lost in a self-generated haze. This ceiling glows. It is a soft, pearly, and pervasive light, the kind of light that casts no sharp shadows, emanating from the rock itself. It is not the fierce glow of magma, but the gentle, chemiluminescent sigh of minerals settling into their final, crystalline forms. This world is underground, but it is not dark.

The air is cool and heavy with the scent of wet clay, ozone, and something like the faint tang of rust. It carries a profound stillness, a quiet broken only by subtle, persistent sounds. The most constant is the drip of water. It seeps from the glowing ceiling, runs in slick, glistening veils down the walls, and gathers in shallow, perfectly clear pools on the floor. This water is the lifeblood of the place. It is thick with dissolved minerals, the liquid medium from which the world is being born.

This is a place in a state of geological infancy. The ground underfoot is not solid, ancient stone, but a semi-hardened, chalky material you could scar with a determined thumbnail. In some areas, it is as soft as packed earth; in others, it has cured into a brittle, resonant precursor to true rock. This is geology under construction. The columns that rise to support the distant ceiling are not smooth and eroded by time, but lumpy, warty, and uneven, like wax dripped from a gargantuan candle. Their surfaces are slick with the same mineral-rich moisture, and you can see, if you watch long enough, the almost imperceptible growth as new layers precipitate out of the damp air.

And in this vast, embryonic cavern, a city is being built. Or rather, it is being coaxed into existence. This is a place of immense, open scale—a valley of a cavern—yet it is packed with presence. A dense, sprawling settlement fills the floor and clings to the lower walls, a staggering lattice of wood and unfired stone. The architecture is inseparable from the geology. There are no quarried blocks, no clean masonry. Instead, structures are *grown*.

The people here are rock-herders, mineral farmers. Their technology is primitive, but their understanding is profound. They build wooden frameworks—scaffolds, molds, and lattices—and channel the mineral-rich water to flow over them. With hand-tools of carved bone and fire-hardened wood, they pat and guide the settling sediment, packing it into the desired shape. A wall is a scaffold that has been patiently irrigated for a generation. A home is a wooden skeleton slowly fleshed out with hardening stone. The city is a sprawling, interconnected organism of pale, damp mineral growth supported by a dark, wet wooden skeleton.

The density is staggering. Walkways of woven reeds and wooden planks form a web between half-finished towers and clustered dwellings. Because the rock is young and not fully trusted, the structures are not bold and soaring, but huddle together for support. They grow into and over one another, creating a layered, three-dimensional maze. There are no streets, only passages—some open-air, suspended hundreds of feet above the cavern floor, and others enclosed, tunnel-like corridors where the stone of two adjacent buildings has been encouraged to merge. The entire city is damp to the touch.

The constant, cool temperature means life is slow and deliberate. The glowing ceiling pulses on a long, rhythmic cycle, brightening to a "day" of soft, even light, and dimming to a "night" of deep, misty twilight where the only illumination comes from the most actively growing mineral formations. During the dimming, the city is quiet, the drips echoing in the hush. As the light returns, the sounds of settlement resume: the gentle *tap-tap-tap* of wooden paddles shaping a new wall, the soft scrape of a bone adze smoothing a surface, the murmur of voices that the heavy air seems to absorb before they can travel too far.

Life is sparse here beyond the builders. On the oldest, most stable stone foundations, patches of pale, grey-green moss have taken hold. Translucent insects with too many legs crawl over damp surfaces, drinking from mineral seeps. In the larger pools, blind, pale crustaceans stir the sediment. There are no predators, no large animals. The ecosystem is as new as the rock.

The primary rule of this world is patience. Stone cannot be rushed. To build is to wait, to tend, to guide. A pillar’s strength is determined by the slowness of its curing. A home’s warmth is a function of a century of patient layering. The entire civilization is an active collaboration with the planet’s formation. They don't conquer the environment; they are midwives to it. And as they build their city, the cavern itself continues its own slow, grand process of becoming, the ceiling ever so slightly changing its glow, the great columns imperceptibly thickening, the entire world settling around them as it hardens into permanence.
