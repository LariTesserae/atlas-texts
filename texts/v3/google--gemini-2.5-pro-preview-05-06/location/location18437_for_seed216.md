This is a hollow world, a construction of living matter. There is no ground, no rock, no substrate other than what grows. Imagine a planet-sized matrices of interwoven, living fibers, a thicket whose branches are the size of mountain ranges and whose trunks are continental pillars. This is the Grand Braid. It is a work of architecture, but the architects are gone or have become the architecture itself. It has been here for so long that the concept of a creator is a geological absurdity. The Braid simply *is*.

The structure is not solid. It is almost entirely empty space, a three-dimensional lattice that soars for miles in every direction. This reconciles its totality with its emptiness. You stand on a "branch" a quarter-mile wide, woven from countless smaller tendrils of living, woody material. The surface underfoot is like petrified bark, ridged and furrowed, damp to the touch. It is warm, like the flank of a great animal. Above you, the Braid continues its filigree, crisscrossing into a ceiling so distant and complex it defeats the eye, a web of soft, brown-green shadow. Between these colossal members are the Voids. These are chambers of air so vast that the opposite wall is often just a haze, a suggestion of form miles away. The scale is not human; it is geological.

The air is thick, warm, and wet. It is the atmosphere of a global greenhouse. There is no rain. Instead, water is an intimate part of the structure. The very air, saturated with moisture, condenses on every surface. The Braid weeps. From every filament and fiber, every knot of woody knuckle, a slow, silent bead of water forms and drips. The sound is an almost subliminal patter, a constant, gentle percussion that is the heartbeat of this place. Rivulets of clean, cool water trace paths down the architecture, following channels worn over millennia, pooling in hollows and basins before spilling over edges to fall in slow, silver curtains into the misty depths of the Voids below. The air tastes of wet bark, ozone, and something like blooming fungus.

Light is a currency, and it is scarce. A faint, bruised-purple twilight filters down from the highest, most open layers of the Braid, but it is a dim memory of a sun by the time it reaches these middle-levels. The true illumination is internal. Great, slow rivers of bioluminescence flow within the translucent outer layers of the larger branches. These are the arteries of the world, pulsing with a cold, blue-green light that waxes and wanes on a cycle of hours or days. The light reveals the texture of the Braid, the sheen of water on its skin, but it casts shadows that are themselves continental in scale. As a vein of light fades on one side of a Void, the entire landscape of the opposite wall can vanish into absolute blackness.

For all its vegetative mass, this is a quiet world. The porous, fibrous material of the Braid absorbs sound. The vastness of the Voids swallows it. There is no wind, only slow, ponderous currents of air moving between warm and cool zones of the lattice. The silence is an active presence, a weight in the ears. Against this silence, the few sounds that exist are stark and clear: the impossibly distant *ch-ch-ch* of a multi-limbed creature climbing a far-off filament; the soft, wet plop of a fruit-like sac breaking open; the low, resonant hum that sometimes vibrates through the very structure of the Braid, a vibration felt in the bones more than heard.

Life is abundant, but adapted to this vertical, empty world. Great, six-winged leathery things, part-manta, part-moth, ride the thermal currents across the Voids, their calls too low for the ear to register. They hunt the climbers: spidery, gibbon-like creatures with hard, chitinous claws that carve temporary homes in the softer, pithy regions of the Braid. In the pools and channels of weeping water live translucent, shrimp-like organisms that filter nutrients directly from their environment. Everything is either a climber, a glider, or a resident of the structure itself. There are no walkers. There is nowhere to walk *to*.

The very physics of the place are skewed by its scale and emptiness. A dropped tool does not simply fall; it begins a slow, almost lazy descent into the Void, shrinking over minutes until it is lost to the mist and the dark. Time itself feels stretched here. You might watch a hunter-creature move on a branch a mile away, and due to some trick of the light and the immense distance, you are seeing its past, an echo of an action already completed. The Braid grows, but on a timescale that makes erosion seem frantic. A new tendril might extend an inch in a human lifetime, a slow-motion act of creation.

The "built" nature of the world is undeniable. The weaving of the fibers is too regular, the junctions too perfect to be purely random. Yet it is all alive. The technology here is symbiotic. The people who live in the Braid—gaunt, quiet, large-eyed—do not build with dead materials. They use polished obsidian scalpels and bone saws to prune and guide the growth of their homes, carving living pods into the Braid's flesh which it slowly heals around, leaving a seamless, organic chamber. They weave cloaks from the shed fibers of the moth-gliders and craft ropes from the sinewy inner strands of dead branches. Their relationship with their world is one of gardening, of quiet partnership with a living god whose body they inhabit. They live and die in a dim, warm, wet, and silent cathedral of cellulose, suspended in a space that is almost entirely, beautifully, nothing at all.
