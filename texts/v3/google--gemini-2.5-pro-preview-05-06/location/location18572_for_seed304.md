The first thing you notice is not a sound, but the absence of one. In a place so dense, so thoroughly occupied, you expect a clamor. Instead, there is only the sound of water, a symphony of a thousand different drips, seeps, and trickles. It is the only constant.

This is the Sump-City, built in a shallow basin no larger than a town square, but with the vertical ambition of a cathedral. The air is cool and heavy, saturated with a fine, silver mist that never fully dissipates. It clings to surfaces, condenses on your skin, and makes the light diffuse and soft, as if the sun itself were being filtered through a sheet of wet paper.

The city is constructed from a single material: a porous, geologically young stone, slate-grey but shot through with veins of a darker, almost black mineral. This "sump-stone" is the source of the place's perpetual dampness. It weeps. Water is drawn up from the saturated ground below through capillary action, pearling on every surface before running down in countless rivulets. The entire city is a weeping rock.

The terrain here is architecture. There is no ground, only the stepped, terraced, and cantilevered surfaces of the buildings. They are packed together with impossible density, leaving no alleys or plazas, only narrow, dripping canyons and stairways slick with moisture. The structures are angular and sharp-edged, a testament to their recent formation. They are built with a primitive but precise understanding of balance and form, using heavy, hand-axed blocks fitted together without mortar. The newer levels at the top are clean and sharp, their geometry stark. Further down, closer to the pooled water at the city's base, the stone is darker, rounded by the ceaseless flow of its own effluence, and softened by a blanket of luminous, verdigris moss.

This moss is the dominant form of vegetation, a sparse but tenacious velvet that thrives on the weeping stone. It is joined by little else. In the deeper pools at the foundation, pale, translucent reeds grow, their roots anchored in the silt of eroded stone. They sway not to a wind—for the air is utterly still in these canyons—but to the slow, deep currents of the basin's water table.

The logic of the city is one of constant, slow motion. The base is always sinking. The endless flow of water erodes the foundations, turning the bottom layers of sump-stone back into a slurry of mud and grit. To compensate, the city must always grow upwards. It is a single, slow-motion construction project, a coral reef of stone built by its inhabitants.

These inhabitants are as quiet as their home. They are tall, gaunt figures, wrapped in cloaks of woven reed-fiber that are perpetually dark with moisture. They move with a deliberate, unhurried grace, their bare feet making no sound on the wet stone. They are the Carvers. You see them at a distance, perched on the upper levels, using heavy stone mallets and obsidian-edged chisels to quarry new blocks from outcroppings at the basin's edge. Their work is the only other sound, a rhythmic, muffled *thump… clink* that is absorbed by the damp air almost as soon as it is made. They communicate in a language of slow gestures, a silent ballet of instruction and cooperation.

There are almost no animals. The ecosystem is too spare to support them. No birds roost in the perpetually dripping eaves, no rodents scurry in the tight-packed foundations. The only fauna are the "glass-worms," tiny, transparent creatures that live in the thin films of water on the stone, feeding on the moss. And in the deepest, oldest pools, something larger is rumored to stir—a slow, blind creature that feeds on the silty decay, a kind of living custodian of the city's dissolution.

To be here is to feel the weight of water, both in the air and in the stone. The world is reduced to this single, enclosed space. Looking up, you see a complex geometry of grey stone and green moss receding into the silver mist. Looking down, you see the same, but darker, wetter, and disappearing into black pools. The cool air smells of wet rock, ozone, and crushed moss. It is a place held in a delicate, dynamic tension: a city actively building itself towards the light while its own substance dissolves it from below. A Carver, silhouetted against the pale sky, raises a mallet. Another block is cleaved, ready to be fitted into place, a single word in a sentence that is never, ever finished.
