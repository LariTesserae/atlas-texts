This is a world drowned in its own finality. You are not on a coast; you are at the bottom of the sky. The medium is not air but a cold, heavy liquid, dense with fine, silvery silt that catches the light like a constant, slow-motion blizzard. You are impossibly high and impossibly deep at the same time, standing on the spine of a submerged mountain range so ancient it has forgotten sharpness.

The terrain is one of immense, humbling verticality. These are not peaks but colossal, rounded shoulders of stone, worn smooth by eons of pressure and the patient scour of deep currents. Everything is slumped, settled. The mountains are like whales that died and turned to rock, their skeletons now forming ranges that stretch for hundreds of miles. Valleys between them are not V-shaped cuts but vast, sagging U-bends, their floors lost in a haze of distance and deeper water. Gravity has had millennia to pull every loose edge downward, creating immense, sloping scree fields of stone plates the size of houses, all resting at their final, perfect angle of repose.

Despite the age and the cold, life is riotously lush. This is not a barren abyss. Great forests of what might be called ‘anchor-kelp’ grow from the slopes. Their stalks are as thick as ancient trees, coloured a deep, venous purple, and instead of leaves, they sprout enormous, membranous fronds that pulse with a soft, internal light. This is the first source of illumination: a widespread, gentle, biological glow that paints the landscape in shades of violet, blue, and soft green. The forests are dense, layered; walking through them means pushing aside heavy, leathery curtains of life that sway in unison with the unseen currents.

Movement is constant, and violent. This world is defined by turbulence. It is not the chaos of a surface storm, but the more terrifying, invisible power of deep-water shears. Entire volumes of water, miles wide, move at different speeds and in different directions. These shear-zones are unmarked, save for the sudden, violent contortion of the kelp forests as a current hits. One moment, the colossal fronds hang in near-stillness; the next, they are whipped into a horizontal frenzy, straining against their moorings with a deep, groaning hum that travels through the water as a physical pressure wave. It is a world of perpetual, silent hurricanes.

The soundscape is anything but silent. It is a layered, industrial roar. Anchored to the sheerer faces of the mountains are immense, skeletal structures of rust-red metal: the Harvesters. They are industrial machines on a geological scale, powered by unseen engines whose thrumming is the baseline of all sound here. Great articulated arms, tipped with grinding plates of tungsten, scrape methodically against the rock faces. They are not mining for ore in a conventional sense; they are scraping off the enriched crust of the mountains themselves, a patina of rare elements deposited by chemical processes over millions of years. The shriek and growl of rock being flayed is a constant, deafening noise that echoes for miles, punctuated by the percussive clang of automated machinery sorting and processing the material.

These Harvesters are the second source of light. Powerful, sterile-white arc lamps blaze from their superstructures, casting harsh, moving shadows that contrast with the soft glow of the biome. In the beams of these lights, the water’s density is revealed: it is a thick soup of life. Shoals of metallic, fish-like creatures, each the size of a man’s hand, move in shimmering, fluid formations, their collective body flexing and warping to navigate the treacherous currents. They are prey for larger things. Occasionally, a shape will blot out the distant glow of a kelp forest—a ‘Groaner,’ a creature the size of a small whale, all smooth, evolved armor and slow, powerful fins. Their calls are the sound that gives them their name: a resonant, low-frequency groan that vibrates in your bones, a sound of immense mass moving through a resistant medium.

The Harvesters are the most obvious sign of a built presence, but not the only one. Scattered across the less-valuable slopes are the remains of older attempts. You might find a small, imploded observation dome, its quartz window shattered into a starburst. Or a line of corroded anchor-points leading nowhere, the cable having snapped centuries ago. These are faint marks, whispers of trial and error in this impossibly hostile place. Living quarters seem to be contained entirely within the mobile Harvester platforms themselves; no one builds to last on the ground. The ground is too old, too impermanent in its finality, always shifting, slumping, and being scoured by the water.

The rules of this world are simple. The currents rule all. Your footing is never guaranteed. The machinery never sleeps. The sound will wear you down. The living world, for all its beauty, is utterly indifferent, a system that has found its equilibrium in chaos. To be here is to feel like a flea on the back of a leviathan, a leviathan being operated upon by a tireless, noisy surgeon. It is a place of sublime, ancient beauty being ground away for parts, all happening in the midst of a storm that has no beginning and no end.
