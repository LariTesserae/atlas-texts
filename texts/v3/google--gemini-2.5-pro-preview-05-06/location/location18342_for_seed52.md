The air is thick, warm, and carries the scent of wet stone and ozone. It’s a perpetual gloaming here, a dim, silver-grey light that seems to emanate from the thick cloud cover above, or perhaps from the very ground itself. The world is a vast, shallow basin, stretching to a horizon that is rarely clear, smudged by low-hanging mist and the faint, opalescent shimmer of distant technologies.

The ground is not soil. It is a seamless, dark grey substrate, cool and faintly yielding to the touch, like hardened rubber. It’s a floor, engineered at a planetary scale. From this floor, everything grows. There are no loose rocks, no scree, no accumulated detritus of ages. Every feature is sharp, intentional. Here and there, the floor has extruded a ridge, a knife-edge of black, crystalline material so new its facets glitter, catching the dim light with unnerving precision. These features are young because they are actively formed; the landscape is a slow-motion sculpture. A ridge might exist for a week, a month, a year, before the substrate reabsorbs it, melting it back into the flatness without a trace.

Vegetation is lush, but not in a chaotic, jungle-like way. It is sparse and architectural. Great, solitary organisms stand in the vast quiet, separated by a hundred meters or more of the bare substrate. These are not trees or flowers as we know them. One might be a colossal, bell-shaped structure of fibrous, dark green tissue, its lip perpetually weeping a clear, viscous fluid that pools on the ground beneath it before being slowly reabsorbed. Another is a fan-like lattice of what appears to be living turquoise, its fronds a hundred feet high, each surface beaded with a fine mist of condensation. The air is damp, but there is no rain. The moisture is managed, cycled by the flora and the hidden infrastructure. Water adheres to every surface. It is a world of dew.

This is a city. Its structures are the spires that rise from the plain, so far apart they seem like lonely monuments. They are grown from the same black, crystalline material as the transient ridges, but on a permanent, monumental scale. They are uneroded, their lines impossibly clean, soaring into the grey overstory. They don't have windows. Their surfaces are a complex fretwork of channels and conduits, and at night—or what passes for night here, a deepening of the grey to charcoal—a pale, internal luminescence pulses slowly through these networks. The city is lit from within. This is the source of the quiet, a sub-audible hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet, a constant, low-frequency thrum of immense and silent processes.

There is infrastructure. Between the great spires, almost invisible against the dark ground, are lines of faint light—glowing pathways or data conduits sunk just below the surface. Sometimes, a section of the ground will depolymerize, turning into a pool of shimmering, quicksilver-like gel for a time—a temporary reservoir or a transport node—before solidifying back into the dark, firm substrate.

Sound is a rarity, and therefore an event. There is the bass hum of the city itself, the quiet hiss of condensation forming on the great plants, the occasional, slow, resonant drip of fluid from a petal the size of a landing pad. The silence is such that you can hear the whisper of your own clothes.

Fauna is almost non-existent. The ecosystem has no need for pollinators or decomposers in the traditional sense. But life, of a kind, finds a way. Silent, geometric moths, the size of a human hand, are sometimes seen. Their wings are not tissue but a kind of shimmering energy field, and they don't fly so much as they warp from point to point. They land on the crystalline spires and trace the glowing conduits with proboscises of light, seeming to feed on the energy or perform some subtle maintenance. They are the only moving things in this quiet, ordered world, their sudden, silent appearances a startling break in the profound stillness.

The rules of this place are not of geology but of engineering. Matter is not permanent. The ground you walk on could be a liquid tomorrow. The air is filtered, the water is cycled, the light is manufactured. It is a city built for beings who perhaps had no need for chaos, for weather, for the inefficiencies of biology. Or perhaps this *is* their biology. A vast, living, thinking machine, whose lush growths are its organs and whose crystalline spires are its thoughts, frozen in a state of perpetual, quiet emergence. The atmosphere is warm and damp because that is the optimal state for the technology to function. The land is flat but punctuated by sharp extrusions because it is a canvas, a three-dimensional printer rearranging itself according to a silent, unknowable blueprint. To be here is to stand inside the body of a sleeping god.
