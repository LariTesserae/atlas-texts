This place is a city in a basin, a single, dense district settled into a wide, concave geology. It is called the Coiled City, or perhaps that is only a functional description. From any vantage point, one can see its opposite side, a crescent of dim, mechanical verdure curving away through the thick air. The scale is that of a Roman amphitheater, if that amphitheater had been left to flood, then forested, then built upon by tireless, obsessive engineers.

The walls of the basin are not sheer cliffs, but a series of broad, concentric terraces descending to a dark, central sump. Everything is constructed of a near-black, volcanic stone, worn impossibly smooth by ages of flowing water, and a heavy, green-skinned metal that might be copper or bronze. The architecture and the infrastructure are one and the same. Buildings are not so much built *on* the terraces as they are extruded *from* them, their forms suggesting they were grown rather than assembled. They are low, broad, and heavy-lidded, with deep-set windows like vacant eyes, glazed with a dark, semi-opaque material that ripples as if still molten.

Water is the blood of this place. It does not rain from an open sky—there is no open sky, only a perpetual, close ceiling of condensation and tangled vegetation far above. Instead, water weeps from the rock faces, drips in fat, slow globules from the bellies of pipes, and condenses on every surface, running in rivulets down walls and along channels carved into the walkways. The air is warm and saturated, a heavy blanket you wear and breathe. To take a full breath is to feel moisture bead in your lungs. The central sump is not a pool but a churning, vortex-like drain, the destination for all the city’s water, where it is swallowed with a constant, low roar.

Despite the lack of fauna—no insects hum, no birds call, no reptiles skitter across the wet stone—the place is deafeningly loud. The sound is a full-bodied, layered chord. The foundation is a deep, resonant hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, the thrum of immense, unseen geothermal pumps. Layered over this is the percussive, metronomic clatter of switching valves and the slide and slam of heavy pistons somewhere deep within the walls. And woven through it all is the sound of water: the roar from the sump, the hiss of atomizers mounted high on the walls, the million-voiced drumming of drips striking stone, metal, and leaf.

This sound is the city’s purpose. It is a vast, slow machine for processing something, perhaps the water itself. Thick-walled copper pipes, green with a skin of verdigris, coil like pythons around sweating stone pillars and plunge into the basements of buildings. These are not pipes for transport, but for transformation. They are part of immense, city-scaled percolators. Water is pumped to the upper levels, where it is released as a fine mist from the atomizers. This mist feeds the vegetation and condenses, dripping down through layers of specially cultivated flora, through filtering substrates woven into the very structure of the city, and finally back to the sump, changed. The entire city is a single, slow, digestive and circulatory system.

The vegetation is an integral part of this engine. It is riotously lush, but strangely curated. Vast, waxy-leafed ferns with fronds the size of doorways grow in dense stands, their undersides coated in a rust-colored carpet of spores. Mosses, thick as fleece and ranging in color from near-black to a startling, chemical green, sheathe every horizontal surface not kept clear by foot traffic or machine function. Vines with square, jointed stems and leaves of an iridescent, oil-slick purple climb the walls, their tendrils seeming to plug into fissures in the metal framework. There are no flowers, only spores and runners. Growth is not a wild sprawl but a managed, functional component of the city’s process. The plants are filters, catalysts, living machinery.

There is no sunlight. The light is dim, generated by the city itself. Long, glowing filaments run along the undersides of arches and within the translucent glazing of the windows. The light they cast is a flat, steady grey-green, the color of a deep sea trench, and it does not vary. This eternal twilight leaves the upper reaches of the basin in profound shadow, a dripping canopy of unseen leaves and machinery.

The effect of this place is one of profound, sterile vitality. It is a greenhouse crossed with a foundry. It is overwhelmingly alive with the processes of its own mechanics and the weird botany it supports, yet it is utterly dead to anything that walks or crawls or flies. Nothing changes quickly here. A patina of verdigris may take a century to thicken. A stone step may take a millennium to wear into a deeper saddle. The machines do not break; they are designed for geological time scales, their parts slowly grinding down and being replaced by automated sub-systems that have waited, dormant, for eons. It is a city that has been running on its own, for itself, for so long that the idea of inhabitants is a contradiction in terms. The city is the only inhabitant, a single, loud, wet, and patient organism of stone, metal, and chlorophyllic tissue, endlessly breathing in its own humid exhalations.
