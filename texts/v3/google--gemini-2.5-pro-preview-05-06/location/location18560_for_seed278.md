This is a plateau world, a continent of cold stone raised so high that the sky has thinned to a permanent, dim violet. The air is sharp and carries no scent but the chill of ozone and the faintest metallic tang, like old blood. There is no sun, only a pervasive, even luminescence that filters down through a stratospheric haze of ice crystals. There are no shadows, only a softening of edges, a gradient of grays that makes judging distance a fool's errand. Everything looks both impossibly far and pressing close.

The ground is a pavement of shattered, slate-like rock, worn smooth over eons by a wind that has no voice. It does not howl; it is a steady, silent pressure, a flow of cold as inevitable as a river. The flatness is a deception. From any given point, the world appears to be an unending plain, but walk for a day and you might find yourself on the lip of a canyon a mile deep, or at the foot of a mesa that rises vertically into the dim light. The scale of these features is geologic, planetary. They are not mountains and valleys in the terrestrial sense, but the settled wrinkles of a world that cooled a very, very long time ago.

Water is a memory, or a promise. There are no rivers, no lakes. Instead, moisture manifests as a persistent, clinging dampness. It settles on every surface as a delicate lacework of frost, a rime that outlines all things in a faint, silvery dust. In the deep-cleft canyons, where the silent wind cannot reach, the dampness congeals. It drips from overhangs with a slowness that is nearly static, a single drop forming over the course of a week, freezing, thawing, and refreezing as atmospheric pressure minutely shifts. These frozen drips build up over millennia into immense, chandelier-like structures of milky, opaque ice. They are the world's only water features, silent and still.

Across the vast and empty plains, spaced dozens, sometimes hundreds, of miles apart, are the Apparatuses.

They are not built; they are grown, or extruded. They are a form of geology, an organ of the planet itself. From a distance, they resemble the rust-eaten ruins of some gargantuan, abandoned industry. There are shapes like colossal pistons, frozen mid-stroke, taller than any skyscraper. There are flywheels half a mile in diameter, embedded in the stone and motionless. There are gantries and cranes of seamless, dark metal that arc for miles across the sky, connecting nothing to nothing.

But they are not ruins. They are active. Their process is just so patient that it becomes invisible. A piston might complete its cycle once every thousand years, dredging up metallic sludge from the planet's mantle. A flywheel might turn a single degree in a century, grinding stone into a fine, nutrient-rich dust. They are powered by the planet's own slow, geothermal pulse. This is the world's metabolism: an industrial process without builders or purpose, a system of mechanized geology that has been running on its own for longer than the concept of life has existed. The sound is the deepest of silences. There are no engines roaring, no metal shrieking. The only noise is a subsonic hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a low thrumming in your bones that is the turning of the world's inner gears. Occasionally, once a decade or so, a part of one apparatus will shift, a gear a hundred yards wide will lock into a new position with a groan that echoes for a minute before the immense quiet swallows it again. This is considered a violent event.

This slow, pointless industry is the bedrock of all life. The sparse vegetation is a metallic lichen, a shimmering, rust-colored film that grows only on the southern faces of the Apparatuses, where the imperceptible warmth from their inner workings keeps the frost at bay. It feeds on the slow decay of the metal itself.

And the fauna. The fauna is overwhelming. Not in density, but in sheer, staggering number. The plains that seem empty from a distance are, upon closer inspection, moving. They are carpeted by a single, planet-spanning herd of creatures. They are like trilobites or enormous woodlice, ranging from the size of a hand to the size of a car. Their chitinous shells are the color of the rock, a thousand shades of gray and black, making them nearly invisible until they stir. They move slowly, deliberately, a single rippling tide of bodies across the continent. They have no eyes. They navigate by sensing the faint geothermal heat and the metallic dust extruded by the Apparatuses.

They are grazers. Their quiet, ceaseless journey is a pilgrimage from one Apparatus to the next. For centuries they will cross a plain, feeding on the almost non-existent metallic lichen and the fine, mineral grit left by the wind. Their sound is the faintest, driest rustle, the collective clicking of millions upon millions of tiny leg-joints on stone, a sound like sand falling in an immense hourglass. When they reach an Apparatus, they swarm its base, feeding for decades on the fresh, warm dust and the metallic secretions it slowly produces. This is their breeding ground. They lay their eggs in the warm slurry, and the young hatch into a world of slow, predictable providence. Then, as one, the great herd-ocean moves on, a silent, living sea flowing across the cold, still land, leaving the Apparatus to its silent, thousand-year work under the dim, violet sky.
