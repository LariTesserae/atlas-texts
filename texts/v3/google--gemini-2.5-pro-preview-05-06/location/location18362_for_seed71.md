The world is a study in impossibly old age. There are no mountains here, not in the sharp, young sense of the word. There are swellings, vast continental plates of granite and basalt that have been scoured by wind and time into immense, gentle curves. They are the knuckles of a buried god, so broad that from their "summits" you cannot perceive the curve of their descent, only the unending, high plateau you stand upon. The scale is planetary; these undulations are the world's true topography, and the spaces between them are basins the size of terrestrial oceans. This landscape has settled into its final form, worn down to the very bones of its beginning.

The air is thin and knife-cold, carrying a clarity that makes distances deceptive. There is a sun, a familiar white-gold star, but its light seems purer, less filtered here, casting shadows that are stark and deep purple. There is no haze, only the slight shimmering distortion near the horizon where the air itself bends light over colossal distances. The dominant feeling is one of profound, settled quiet. It is not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of completion. There are no birds to sing, no insects to buzz, no predators to stalk. The kingdom of Fauna is absent, a line of code that was never written for this reality.

Yet, the world is not dead. It is, against all logic of this cold, thin atmosphere, carpeted in life. A contiguous, deep green mat covers almost everything, a kind of hyper-efficient moss or algae-lichen symbiote that thrives not on abundant water, but on persistent dampness. This is a condensation environment. Each night, as the temperature plunges, the thin air gives up its moisture, which beads and crystallizes on every surface. At dawn, this frost-lace melts and is instantly wicked up by the verdant carpet. The ground is therefore never dry and never truly wet, but perpetually damp to the touch, like felt left out in a deep fog. The vegetation itself is the source of the moisture, a closed, planet-scale system of transpiration and condensation. It has a slow, rhythmic pulse; walking across it, you feel a faint, deep thrumming through the soles of your feet. In some areas, the carpet glows with a soft, internal phosphorescence, a pale chartreuse light that breathes in slow, twenty-minute cycles. This is the only biological motion, the world's slow, green heartbeat.

And in one of the basins, nestled in the gentle curve between two continent-sized land-swellings, is the City.

It defies the logic of its own existence. It is vast, sprawling for miles, and clearly the work of intelligence, yet it is built with the most primitive of means. There are no steel girders, no poured concrete. The City is a construction of woven stone, petrified wood, and a kind of cultured, crystalline lattice. It is a place of scaffolds, bridges, and towers that seem less built and more grown, coaxed into being over millennia.

The "buildings" are layered accretions. Looking at a wall is like looking at the rings of a tree or a geological stratum. The lowest, oldest layers are thick, dark, and almost fused with the bedrock, built from heavy blocks of basalt painstakingly chipped and fitted by hand. Above these, the architecture becomes lighter, more complex. Ribs of a strange, silicon-based wood, now petrified into quartz, arc over plazas. Walls are not solid but are intricate lattices of a crystalline substance, like rock candy, that seems to have been extruded and guided into place while it was still semi-molten. These lattices are the City's signature: they form spiraling towers that catch the wind and produce a low, resonant humming, the only true "voice" of the place. They are hand-carved with simple tools—you can see the faint, repeated marks of adzes and chisels on their surfaces—but the scale of the work implies a population of millions working for tens of thousands of years.

The density is sparse. The City is urban in its interconnectedness and infrastructure, but not in its crowding. Great open spaces exist within it, plazas carpeted in the same glowing moss as the outside world. Towers stand alone, connected to their neighbors only by delicate, thousand-foot-long bridges of woven stone fiber. There is a sense of immense space and breathing room, as if the City was designed to balance presence with the profound emptiness of the world it inhabits.

Here, the weirdness that permeates the world is most tangible. The City is an anchor, a place of stability, but its very substance seems to flicker at the edges of perception. Physics is a local phenomenon. In certain old plazas, gravity is slightly weaker, and a tossed stone will hang in the air for a moment too long before drifting gently back down. The light passing through the crystal lattices sometimes separates not into a rainbow, but into colors that have no name, shades of infrared and impossible blues. Paths might exist in a superposition; a bridge you crossed yesterday may today lead to a different tower entirely, or simply end in mid-air, its other half now connected to a district a mile away.

This is the central dynamic: the world is fraying. The ancient stability of the land is a finished thing, but reality itself is not. It is an entropic process. The inhabitants of the City—presences felt only by their endless work—are not conquerors or innovators. They are curators. Their primitive technology is used for a single, monumental task: maintenance of reality. They are weavers, using their stone looms and crystal-shaping tools to reinforce the City's structure, to mend the sky-seams that occasionally split open above, revealing startling glimpses of pure, white static. Their work is a constant, quiet battle against unraveling.

The sounds are of this work: the distant, rhythmic *tink, tink, tink* of a chisel on crystal, the low hum of the wind through the latticework, the soft, wet sigh of the moss breathing, and the occasional, terrifying chime-like crack as a piece of the world further out in the empty wilds finally loses its coherence and dissolves into un-being. It is an active, dynamic place, but the action is the slow, geological-scale process of existence itself fighting to remain.
