The ground is made of tile.

Not small mosaics, but vast, turquoise-glazed flagstones of baked clay, each the size of a wagon bed. They form a basin stretching for miles, a constructed valley floor under a sky of uniform, brilliant white. The light is total, a searing glare that possesses no discernible source and casts no shadows. It bleaches the color from the far distance, leaving the horizon a shimmering, heat-hazed line.

The heat is a physical presence, a humid weight that presses down on everything. The air is saturated, thick with water that refuses to become cloud or rain. Instead, it condenses on every surface. Beads of moisture, perfect and jewel-like, cling to the glazed tiles, tracing the fine network of crazing in their surfaces. Water weeps from the grout lines between the massive stones, which remain sharp and uneroded, as if laid only yesterday. This water doesn't flow; it pools in shallow, decorative channels and wide, platter-like depressions designed into the tiled landscape. The pools are perfectly still, their surfaces like polished glass reflecting the blank white sky. Nothing grows in them.

Despite the sterility of the tile, vegetation is lush, but strangely sparse. Inacre-wide intervals, a single, monumental plant erupts from a precise rupture in the tiled floor. There is a fern with fronds of impossible green, each one thirty feet long, dripping a thick, syrupy nectar that never reaches the ground, hanging in a perpetual, unmoving tear at the tip of each leaflet. Miles away, a pitcher plant large as a throne stands alone, its waxy, carnivorous mouth gaping at the white sky, its interior holding a fathomless, still liquid. Elsewhere, a stand of what might be bamboo, thick as columns and glossy black, rises in a perfect, silent cluster. Each plant is a specimen, a singular statement of life in its most vibrant form, yet utterly isolated from any other. There is no undergrowth, no soil, no decay. They are perfect, and they are unchanging.

This place is not silent, but it is deeply quiet. The thick, hot air smothers sound. The overwhelming noise is a constant, high-frequency hum that seems to emanate from the light itself. Beneath this is a single, repeating acoustic event: the slow, rhythmic *plink* of a water drop striking a tile somewhere out of sight. It is a clean, clear sound that never echoes. You can walk towards it for an hour and it will get no louder. The pools never ripple. The drops that form on every surface never seem to fall.

Fauna is abundant, but it subscribes to the same static law. Huge geckos, the color of sunset and opal, cling to the low, tiled walls that divide the landscape into broad, empty courtyards. They are not frozen, not dead, but their movements are so infrequent—the slow blink of a nictitating membrane once a day, a subtle shift in weight once a week—that they appear as enamel jewelry affixed to the architecture. Dragonflies with wings of stained glass are pinned to the air, unmoving, their iridescent bodies catching the shadowless light. In the still pools, frogs the size of dinner plates sit just beneath the surface, their skin the texture of polished jade, their golden eyes wide and unblinking. They do not breathe. They do not hunt. They simply are, a dense population of living statuary.

The fundamental rule of this world is equilibrium. It is a breakdown not of space, but of time and causality. The landscape is young in that it has never been permitted to age. The tiles are sharp because water has no power of erosion here. The plants are lush but do not grow; the animals are present but do not live in any metabolic sense. It is a photograph you can walk into, a moment of impossible creation held in perpetual suspension.

Everything is built. The ground, the low walls, the empty aqueducts, the stairways that descend into pools of stilled water—all are made of the same hand-worked, glazed ceramic. The sheer scale of it, the inhuman patience required to fire and lay these millions of tons of tile with only primitive tools, suggests a forgotten purpose, a ritual of immense and terrifying duration. Perhaps this was once a public bath for gods, or a lido for beings who measured time in geologic epochs.

Now, it is simply a place where a single moment—a hot, wet afternoon after a rain that never fell—has been made permanent. The water that drips does so not from a cloud, but from a memory of rain. The heat does not radiate from a sun, but from the memory of a noon that will never pass. To be here is to feel the constant, crushing pressure of a present that has no future and no past. You can walk for days across the tiled expanse, past the silent plants and the bejeweled, motionless lizards, but you are not moving *through* time. You are only moving through space, a ghost in a stopped machine.
