There is no quiet here. There is only the Sound.

It is not a single noise, but a billion tiny ones, layered into a physical presence. It is the dry, frictional roar of a trillion scraping legs, of countless chitinous plates rubbing together, of innumerable crystalline wings vibrating in the dense, cool air. It is a sound of abrasion, a granular chorus that fills the vastness, pressing in on you not with volume but with texture. It is a deafening, unceasing, all-encompassing *hiss*. It is the sound of a world made of grit.

You stand at the edge of a great, shallow basin, deep beneath the surface of the world. The ground beneath your feet is not soil or rock in the familiar sense, but a compacted dust of eons, worn so smooth it feels like polished bone. The scale is immense; what might be a valley floor on the surface stretches out before you, disappearing into a dim, hazy expanse. Across this basin, great structures rise. They are not built, not carved, but *grown*. This is a city, organized and ancient, its avenues and towers adhering to a logic that is both alien and deeply ordered.

There is no water. Life here has solved that problem by abandoning the solvent entirely. The vegetation, which is startlingly lush in its own way, is mineral. Great, branching growths of fibrous silicates rise from the basin floor, their forms reminiscent of trees but with a hard, geometric perfection. These are the crystal-forests of the deep. Their "bark" is like unglazed porcelain, their "leaves" are thin, sharp flakes of mica-like material that chime faintly when disturbed, though such a delicate sound is utterly lost in the omnipresent roar. They are not green, but a spectrum of cool, muted colors—the pale lavender of feldspar, the soft grey-blue of celestite, the dusty rose of ancient salt formations. They are sparse; each colossal "tree" has a wide berth, its domain of dust around it kept clear by some unknown principle, giving the landscape a planned, park-like feel amidst its wildness.

The light is dim, emanating from the vegetation itself. The crystalline trees drink in some unknown ambient energy—perhaps the sound itself, perhaps geothermal heat—and release it as a slow, constant, phosphorescent glow. The light is cool, bluish-white, like a star seen through thick fog. It doesn't cast sharp shadows; it simply makes the darkness less absolute, revealing the shapes of the city's towers and the slow-moving things among them.

This city is defined by its hollow, crystalline architecture. Towers of lattices and arches soar into the gloom of the cavern roof high above, not solid but porous, like sea sponges turned to stone. Walls are not barriers but intricate screens of interwoven crystal, creating districts and boulevards without ever fully blocking the view or the ceaseless Sound. It is all one continuous, living construction, growing at a rate so slow as to be imperceptible, a single architectural organism that has been settling into its final, most stable form for ages. All edges are softened, all surfaces worn smooth by the passage of time and the gentle, abrasive air.

And the air is full of life. The sound is their sound. Swarms of creatures, like metallic beetles the size of fists, drift on stiff, buzzing wings through the open spaces between the mineral flora. They are the city's pollinators and gardeners, their scraping mandibles grooming the crystal growths, their vibrations contributing a high-frequency harmony to the roar. On the ground, larger fauna move with immense patience. Herds of beings like multi-legged armadillos, their carapaces made of the same polished minerals as the ground, graze on the crystal-trees. They don't eat them in chunks, but slowly, meticulously scrape microscopic layers of nutritious dust from their surfaces. Their movement is a slow, ponderous shuffle, another low-frequency grinding added to the cacophony.

There is no sense of urgency. The cycles here are geological. A tree-tower might add a centimeter of height in a century. A grazing herd might cross a single plaza in a year. Change is a concept for outsiders. Here, there is only the slow, patient, unceasing hum of existence in its final, stable state. To be here is to be overwhelmed by two constants: the sheer, abrasive presence of the Sound, and the profound, immeasurable weight of time. The air is cool on your skin and gritty in your lungs. You can feel the vibration of the world in your bones. There is nowhere to hide from it. This is the city, and the city is the Sound.
