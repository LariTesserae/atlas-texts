This is a landscape of sound.

The sound is the first and last thing. It is not a noise that occurs *in* the world; it is the noise the world *is*. It is a composite roar, a deafening, skull-filling drone with no single source because its source is everything. It is the grinding of reality against itself. There is a bass component, a foundational hum so low it is felt in the marrow, the kind of vibration that could liquefy stone over geological time. Layered over this is a constant, rushing hiss, like a billion tons of sand being poured through a planet-sized hourglass. And woven through it all are shrieking, melodic overtones that rise and fall without pattern, the sound of immense energies tearing and re-knitting the fabric of the medium that passes for an atmosphere. To be here is to be inside a bell the size of a continent, struck by an unsteady god.

The ground is a testament to an age so profound it precedes the concept of mountains. It is a basin, stretching beyond any possible horizon, its curvature so gentle it is perceived only as an oppressive, absolute flatness. The material underfoot is not rock or soil but a single, fused substance, a vitrified slag of dark, oily-sheened black. It has been polished by forces that have long since ceased to exist, scoured and planed down to its final, most stable form. It is ancient, settled, done. Yet it is not dry. The entire surface sweats. A warm, slick moisture beads on the polished blackness, forming tiny rivulets that travel for miles without pooling, seeming to be reabsorbed by the ground as quickly as they emerge. In places, the ground weeps from hairline fractures, sighing a thick, syrupy water that smells of ozone and hot metal.

There is no sky, only a ceiling. The atmosphere is a dense, pressurized medium, warm and cloying, that you wear like a second skin. Its pressure is immense, a constant and unyielding embrace. It is thick enough to have a texture, and it is a cauldron of chaotic change. This medium is where the world's turbulence is made manifest. Vast, slow-moving vortices, miles across, drift and collide. Within them, the air curdles. Phantom geometries of light and shadow form and dissolve in instants—the glimmering outlines of impossible temples, the fractal veils of condensing vapor, the sudden, shocking appearance of a pocket of perfect, silent blackness that is just as quickly devoured by the churning chaos. Light is generated internally, a diffuse, sourceless phosphorescence that shifts in color from a bruised mauve to a sickly verdigris. It is a permanent, stormy twilight.

This is not a place that is hospitable to life, yet life is not entirely absent. It exists in the interstices, in forms as ancient and strange as the landscape itself. There is no green. What passes for vegetation are sparse, crystalline growths that seem to extrude directly from the super-polished ground. They are resonant structures, ranging from fist-sized, humming geodes to lonely, blade-thin spires that rise a dozen feet into the turbulent air. They are not alive in a biological sense; they are mineral formations that have achieved a state of harmonic stability with the deafening roar, channeling its energy into their slow, geometric growth. They glow faintly with captured light and vibrate visibly, each one a tuning fork for a specific frequency within the planetary noise.

Fauna is rarer still, and to see a creature whole is a near impossibility. They are events more than beings. There are the "skimmers," momentary thickenings in the atmospheric medium, things that look like manta rays made of heat-haze and shadow. They ride the violent thermal currents for a few seconds or minutes before their coherence fails and they dissipate back into the general turmoil. There are "bottom-crawlers," glimpsed as disturbances in the weeping moisture on the ground, multi-legged signatures of pressure and movement that are gone before the eye can truly resolve them. They do not eat or breathe in any recognizable way; they seem to draw sustenance from the dissonance, from the very breakdown of physical law.

The world is packed, saturated, but not with objects. It is filled with process. There is no empty space because every cubic inch is occupied by the roar, by the pressure, by the constant, furious creation and annihilation of transient phenomena. This is the source of the place’s profound weirdness. Reality here is a suggestion, not a law. A step forward might find the ground beneath your feet solid and slick; the next might find it momentarily intangible, a shimmering mirage of what it was a second before. Causality is frayed. An effect—a sudden ripple in the air, a high-pitched scream in the roar—might precede its cause, which then flashes into existence miles away.

And scattered across this impossible plain are the faintest hints of a past that was somehow different. They are not ruins in the sense of broken stone, for no stone could survive here. They are echoes, stains, scars in the very substrate of the world. A perfect, hundred-mile curve etched into the black slag, suggesting the foundation of something built on a scale that beggars belief. A region where the ever-present hum is subtly dampened, as if by the ghost of a massive wall that once stood against it. In one area, the weeping ground is gridded with faint, lighter lines, the memory of a pavement worn away not just to nothing, but beyond nothing, into a mere property of the material that remains. These are not buildings; they are the lingering absence of buildings, preserved by the same forces that would annihilate any new structure in moments. They are the oldest things here, older even than the roar itself, and they offer the terrifying suggestion that this churning, deafening chaos is not how this world began, but how it will end. It is the final, stable state of erosion: not silence and dust, but an eternal, turbulent scream.
