The sound is the first thing, a physical presence before it is a noise. It is a drone, a pressure in the bones, the aggregate hum of a billion microscopic events scaled up to the level of a geological formation. It is the sound of a world being born in slow motion. This choral drone is woven through with a higher, more chaotic layer: the continuous, brittle clicking and scraping of a billion billion chitinous legs on crystalline surfaces, a sound like a hailstorm that never ends.

You are standing in absolute darkness, high in a mountain range that is still inventing itself. The air is thin but heavy, saturated with a biting cold and a constant, condensing mist. Every surface is slick with an oleaginous, faintly conductive film of water that is not quite water. It weeps from the architecture, beads on your gear, and pools in shallow, ink-black puddles on the ground. The temperature hovers just above freezing, but the perpetual dampness leaches heat with pitiless efficiency.

This is a city. Or a nest. Or a geologic process aping the form of a city. Before you, and around you, extending for miles into the crushing dark, is a lattice of construction. It is not built, but extruded. Out of the raw, black stone of the valley floor, impossibly thin filaments of a carbon-silicate material rise, weaving themselves into delicate, impossibly strong scaffolds. They branch, intersect, and thicken, creating a dense, three-dimensional labyrinth of struts, bridges, and platforms. The process is tireless but glacially slow. You can’t see a tower grow, but if you were to return in a century, its peak would be lost in the thick, low clouds. This is the source of the deep hum: the geodetic engine at the planet’s core, printing a metropolis atom by atom.

The scale is vast—an entire basin, perhaps twenty miles across, is filled with this burgeoning, fractal architecture. Yet it is claustrophobically dense. Passageways are narrow, ceilings are low. The world is a cage of black crystal, layered upon itself, rising in a chaotic but purposeful tangle. There is no central plaza, no grand avenue. It is all infrastructure, all of it near-touching, a city composed entirely of its own back alleys and service tunnels.

And it is not empty. The overwhelming sensation is one of life. This world is barren of vegetation—no moss, no lichen, not even a bacterial film. The ecosystem is entirely faunal, a kingdom of scavengers and grazers that feed not on plants, but on the city itself.

They are everywhere. The dominant species are the Hull-Scrapers, creatures the size of dinner plates, resembling armored, multi-legged trilobites. They move with arthritic slowness, their diamond-hard mandibles grinding away at the freshly extruded filaments. They are not destroying the city; they are consuming the energetic residue, the mineral-rich dust left in the wake of its creation. Their ceaseless grinding is the bedrock of the soundscape’s higher frequencies.

Clinging to the undersides of arches and within resonant chambers are the Filter-Kine. They are sessile, looking like geodesic domes of spun glass. They do not move, but fine, hair-like cilia on their surfaces flicker and pulse, capturing aerosolized nutrients from the condensing mist—the same mineral broth that coats every surface. In the dark, they are invisible, but touch one and it shatters with the sound of a breaking lightbulb.

Through these slow-moving herds and static colonies drift the predators. Glimmer-Stalkers, they are sometimes called, though there is little to see them by. They are sinuous, meter-long hunters, moving with an unnerving, liquid silence that is a stark contrast to the ambient noise. They are biomechanical horrors, their bodies a fusion of grown chitin and the same material as the city. They hunt by sensing the minute electrical fields of the Scrapers, and their kills are silent, brutal affairs.

Light is a rare and precious commodity. There is no sun, no moon. The only illumination comes from two sources. The first is the city's own birth. Where new filaments are being woven at their most rapid, a faint, cobalt-blue Cherenkov radiation leaks into the visible spectrum, tracing the paths of creation like ghost-veins in the dark. The second is the fauna. Certain species, particularly the larval stage of the Hull-Scrapers and the hunting organs of the Glimmer-Stalkers, pulse with a cold, intermittent bioluminescence. The landscape is therefore a deep velvet black, punctuated by crawling, momentary constellations of green and blue light, revealing a fragment of a crystalline wall, a drip of black water, the horrifying, segmented leg of a nearby creature.

The rules here are simple and absolute. The city grows. The fauna feeds on the growth. The predators feed on the fauna. The water is a chemical slurry, runoff from the nanite-forges deep below, and is poison to off-world biology. Everything is slow. The life cycles of the creatures are measured in decades. The construction of a single city block takes a millennium. Change is a constant, but it is imperceptible to the impatient. To stand here is to feel like a fleeting microbe on the face of a monument that is itself still being carved, surrounded by a slow-motion blizzard of alien life, and deafened by the patient, thunderous hum of its making.
