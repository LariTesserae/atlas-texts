The first thing you register is not a sight but a sound. It is a vast, industrial hum, a single chord held for an eternity, so pervasive it’s more a feature of the atmosphere than a noise traveling through it. This drone is the bedrock of the place, a monotone A-flat of immense transformers or turbines that have not varied in pitch or volume for as long as any record has existed. It is the sound of a system in perfect, unyielding equilibrium.

The air is a physical weight. It is hot—not the dry, baking heat of a desert, but the oppressive, wet heat of a boiler room. It is water-saturated, thick with a metallic tang, carrying the scent of hot iron, ozone, and something like the smell of a cave floor, a deep mineral dampness. Breathing is a conscious effort. Sweat does not cool the skin; it simply joins the clammy film of condensation that coats every surface.

Your eyes adjust to the perpetual twilight. The space is a hall of breathtaking, cathedral-like scale, but it is not a place of worship. It is a titanic work of engineering, a city contained within a single room. Massive, square pillars of weeping concrete, stained dark with moisture and mineral deposits, rise up to a ceiling lost in misty gloom hundreds of feet above. The light is dim and diffuse, a featureless gray-orange glow that seems to emanate from the soupy air itself, perhaps from rows of ancient, grime-caked sodium lamps far overhead, their individual locations impossible to discern through the haze.

This is a low place, a basin. The floor is a vast pavement of gridded iron plates and poured composite, perfectly flat in most areas but occasionally buckled or sunken, creating shallow, placid lakes of distilled, lukewarm water. There are no currents, no tides. These pools have been here forever, their edges precisely defined, their surfaces mirroring the indistinct ceiling with a glassy stillness broken only by the perpetual, rhythmic drizzle from above. The entire superstructure of the hall sweats. Rivulets of condensation trace unchanging paths down the concrete pillars, navigate the bronze fittings of sealed conduits, and drip from the vertices of iron beams. The water lands with a soft, steady plink-plink-plink, thousands upon thousands of tiny sounds that are entirely subsumed by the monolithic drone. Where this water pools, it has a faint rainbow sheen, the signature of oils and lubricants leached from the machinery.

Though intensely wet, the hall is nearly barren of plant life. The water is too pure in some places, too tainted in others, and the floor offers no soil. In the crevices where plates have shifted, or along the base of the pillars, there are sparse, desperate colonies of life: patches of slick, rust-colored algae, and stringy tufts of pale white fungi that seem to feed on the metallic bleed. They do not spread. They exist in their established niches, as static as everything else.

The sparseness of the construction is unnerving. Despite its urban scale, the hall is mostly empty space. The great pillars are separated by acres of open floor. Here and there stand colossal, silent pieces of machinery: cubic presses, multi-story catalytic crackers, and tangled towers of pipes and valves, all coated in the same damp sheen of oxidation and grime. They are presences, scattered like monoliths on a plain. They are not working, but they are not derelict either; they are simply part of the equilibrium, connected by thick umbilicals of conduit that pulse with the same deep vibration as the air.

This static world is, however, teeming with fauna. Its inhabitants are things adapted to the heat, the wet, the dimness, and the deafening noise. The slick floor plates are trafficked by legions of armored, iridescent beetles the size of a thumb, their movements methodical and unhurried. Near the still pools, pale, gelatinous salamanders with vestigial eyes lie motionless for days, absorbing oxygen through their skin. Larger creatures, gecko-like and the color of oxidized copper, crawl with impossible slowness up the sheer faces of the concrete pillars, their sucker-padded feet granting them purchase on the weeping surfaces. They hunt the fat, silverfish-like insects that graze on the pockets of algae. There are no birds to sing, no mammals to scurry. The life here is ancient, cold-blooded, and resigned to the eternal drone.

There are no gradients. There is no weather, no day or night, no seasons. The temperature is fixed. The light level is fixed. The water drips at a constant rate. The sound never wavers. It is a perfect, closed system, a single moment of industrial operation frozen and extended into infinity. It was built for a purpose, but that purpose is now lost, supplanted by the simple, brute fact of its own endless existence. Time does not pass here; it just is.
