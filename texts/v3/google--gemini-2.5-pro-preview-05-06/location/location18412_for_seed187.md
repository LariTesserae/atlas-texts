The world is a single room.

It is a chamber, a vast and singular concavity hollowed from a world made otherwise entirely of plant matter. The scale feels like that of a great cathedral, but one grown, not built. The walls, the arching ceiling, and the floor are all the same living substance—a seamless, lignous shell the color of dark, translucent amber. It is not wood, nor leaf, nor flesh, but something that holds the properties of all three: it is immensely strong, it photosynthesizes, and it is warm to the touch, humming with a slow, biological current. Its surface, worn smooth over geological time, is like polished horn, ancient and finished. There are no features on the land, no hills or valleys, only this one enormous, stable, hollow space.

Light filters through the semi-lucid dome of the ceiling, suffusing the cavern with a soft, honey-colored daylight. The air within is cool and carries significant weight, heavy with the damp, clean scent of chlorophyll and ozone, and a sharper, metallic tang that hints at strange chemistry. This dampness does not pool; there are no rivers or lakes. Instead, it manifests as a perpetual, silent condensation. Droplets of clear, cold water gather on the high arches of the ceiling and trace slow, predictable paths down the curving walls, following imperceptibly shallow grooves worn by millennia of the same journey. The moisture slicks the surfaces, making the entire interior glisten.

The chamber is defined by a profound emptiness. Despite the totality of the vegetation that forms its structure, the volume within is almost entirely void. There are no smaller plants, no undergrowth, no floating motes of dust or pollen. The space between the floor and the ceiling is just clear, cool, humid air, so transparent that a viewer standing on one side of the chamber can see the far wall, kilometers away, with perfect clarity.

But this profound emptiness is not silent. It is filled with a loud, constant, and deeply industrial sound.

This is the sound of a factory in full, steady production. A deep, resonant hum vibrates up from the floor, a bass note of immense power being generated and contained. It is a biological hum, the thrumming of colossal, unseen organs buried deep within the living substrate. This is the prime mover, the power plant of the grove.

Layered over this hum is the rhythmic noise of machinery at work. It is the sound of the fauna. They are abundant, but they are not animals in a familiar sense. They are cogs, workers, living components of the great machine. Great, armored, beetle-like creatures, the size of freight cars, move with inexorable slowness along the high walls. Their multi-jointed legs find purchase in invisible holds, and their diamond-hard mandibles scrape rhythmically against the amber surface: *shhhhh-CHUNK... shhhhh-CHUNK... shhhhh-CHUNK*. They are harvesting something, perhaps mineral deposits secreted by the wall itself, or simply sloughing off the oldest layers of the living shell.

What they scrape away falls in a fine powder to the floor. There, a different caste of creature moves. These are lower to the ground, vast, trilobite-like forms that drag themselves across the floor in mathematically precise patterns. They are the factory's transport system. They don't scuttle; they flow, their undersides rippling as they consume the fallen dust, their passage emitting a heavy, dragging, percussive sound against the resonant floor. Their paths are worn into the living floor not as trails of erosion, but as polished, slightly darker bands of compacted material, like the desire lines of a million years of unchanging purpose.

The transporters carry their load toward massive, circular vents in the floor. These vents are the system's chemical processors. They are not holes, but sphincters of living tissue that pulse slowly, irising open to receive the processed material from the transporters. From the depths of these vents comes a loud, steady hiss of escaping gas and the gurgling sound of high-pressure liquid systems. A sharp, sterile, metallic smell rises from them, the smell of catalytic reactions and industrial solvents, which mixes with the cool, greenhouse air.

This system is in a state of perfect, frozen equilibrium. It does not change. The rate of harvesting on the walls perfectly matches the rate of regrowth. The amount of material processed by the floor-vents is precisely what is needed to generate the energy and nutrients that power the entire Caul and its worker-fauna. The water that condenses on the ceiling is the exact volume of waste vapor from the chemical reactions below. It is a closed loop, a self-sustaining biomechanical engine that achieved its final, most efficient state countless eons ago and has simply been *running* ever since. There is no evolution here; there is no change. There is only the ceaseless, clamorous, stable hum of a living factory going about its one, eternal task. It is a world without history or future, existing only in the loud, unchanging, industrial present.
