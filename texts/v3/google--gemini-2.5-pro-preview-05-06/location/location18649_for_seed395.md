This is the Vellum Sea. There is no water here. Not a drop. Yet, it is not a desert. It is a world drowning in life.

The ground is not soil or sand, but a continuous, living mat. It has the firm, springy texture of tightly packed mycelium or old leather. Underfoot, it gives by a few millimeters, a silent yielding. This mat is the foundation, a planetary dermis woven from the root-analogs of countless organisms. It is warm to the touch, humming with a slow, biological heat. From this skin grows the "flora," a term used loosely for the riot of stationary life-forms that constitute the landscape.

They are not plants in any familiar sense. They are xerophyllic organisms evolved to an impossible extreme, where all the world's moisture is hoarded inside their own tissues. They are the water. They are the wells. To drink, in this world, is to eat. Their forms are thick, swollen, and economical. Great, waxy bladders the size of huts squat in tight clusters, their surfaces slick with a greasy resin that seals in their precious fluid. Some grow as towering, segmented columns of fibrous gristle, pulsing with a slow, peristaltic rhythm as they shift their internal reserves. Others form vast, flat plates of a tough, semi-translucent material like horn or tortoiseshell, overlapping for miles to create a canopy close to the ground. The palette is not green, but a spectrum of ochre, terracotta, sallow ivory, and deep, bruised purples.

This dense, low-growing wilderness is not wilderness at all. It is a city. Rising from the vellum plains are structures that defy simple categorization as either architecture or biology. They are both. Great spires, textured like wasp nests and porous as sponges, ascend hundreds of feet into the sky. They are not built, but grown. They are colonial organisms on a geological scale, the accumulated, calcified, and still-living framework of trillions of individual creatures working in silent, mindless unison. These spires are the city's apartment blocks, its cathedrals, its ventilation shafts. Their walls are riddled with passages, chambers, and galleries, forming a three-dimensional labyrinth that extends for continents. Light, buttery and warm from a conventional sun, filters down through the porous architecture, creating a perpetual state of dappled, golden twilight within the city-organism.

The atmosphere is thick, not with dust or moisture, but with spores, pollen, and complex organic volatiles released by the vellum-life. It carries a scent, not of decay, but of warm wax, dry resins, and something like ozone or freshly-turned earth, a smell of pure, non-aqueous vitality. This thick air smothers sound. The world is profoundly quiet. There are no bird calls, no buzzing insects, no wind rustling through leaves. The overwhelming sensory input is this deep, pervasive hush, broken only by the faintest of incidental noises: the soft, tearing sound of a browser ripping into a fluid-bladder; the whisper of a predator's chitinous legs skittering across a horn-plate; the quiet, internal sloshing as a tower-organism redistributes its lifeblood.

And the world is packed. Fauna is everywhere, moving with a stealth born of necessity in the crowded quiet. There are no large, bellowing grazers. Instead, life is arthropodal, molluscan, and things in between. Armored, beetle-like creatures with powerful mandibles methodically shred the fibrous stalks for sustenance. Gelatinous, slug-like beings, shimmering with an oily sheen, secrete enzymes to soften the waxy hides of bladder-plants, siphoning the fluid within. They move through the canyons and tunnels of the living city, a silent, constant flow of consumption. Predators are equally silent: mantis-like hunters who mimic the texture of the vellum mat, waiting motionless for days; ambush predators that live inside the walls of the spires, extending sticky proboscises into the corridors to ensnare the unwary.

This is a world still becoming itself. The geology is embryonic. Beneath the living mat and the city-organism, there is no ancient bedrock. Instead, a new layer of planet is being formed. It is a slow, steady accretion of biocement—the mineralized husks of the dead, the waste products of the living city, the slow deposition of everything that lives and dies, packed down by the immense weight of the biomass above. To dig down is to move through a compressed history of the ecosystem, from the still-pliable layers of recently deceased matter to a deepening stratum of chalky, bone-like material, and finally to a new, brittle stone that is still warm. There is no erosion, only creation.

The cycles of this world are not rain and sun, but growth and consumption. The weather is a spore-fall, a vast, silent release of genetic material from the tops of the spires that drifts on the slow, thermal currents, coating the world in a fine, saffron-colored dust. A "storm" is a pheromonal cascade, a wave of chemical information that ripples through the city, triggering a mass-pupation or a coordinated shift in growth. Life and landscape are one. The city breathes. The ground grows. The silent inhabitants navigate the arteries of a creature the size of a world, each one a cell in its body, living and dying by its quiet, inexorable rules.
