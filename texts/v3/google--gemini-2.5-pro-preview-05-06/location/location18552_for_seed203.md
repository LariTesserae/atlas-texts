This world is a hollow.

It is not a cavern, which is a void in rock, but a Geode, a bubble of impossible emptiness at the heart of some planetary mass. Its scale is geologic. The "floor" curves away beyond any horizon, and looking up, you do not see a ceiling, but another curving floor, impossibly distant, hanging overhead. In all directions, the world is a surface that curves away into a haze of distance and thin, cold air.

Stretching from the nadir to the zenith of this interior world, bisecting the void, is the Spindle. It is a pillar of pure, unwavering, cold light. It is not a star—it gives no heat—but its brilliance is absolute, a actinic glare that casts no shadows, only regions of lesser light. It is the source of all energy, the axis around which this hollow world turns. Its brightness is so total that it bleaches color from the immediate vicinity, rendering everything in stark monochrome.

From every surface, reaching inward toward the Spindle’s light, grows the vegetation. This is not a forest, but a single, interconnected organism of crystalline flora. Imagine trees made of spun glass and quartz, their trunks vast as skyscraper clusters, their branches forming intricate, fractal canopies that interlock across continent-sized expanses. This is the Glasswood. It constitutes the entire substrate of the world. There is no soil, no rock, only the tangled, kilometers-deep root-mat of the Glasswood, which itself is the "ground." Walking on it is like treading on a hard, springy mesh of petrified veins, which give slightly underfoot with a sound like cracking ice. The air is filled with the faintest, high-pitched hum, the resonant frequency of the whole crystalline structure vibrating with the light from the Spindle.

The space is defined by a paradox: it is entirely filled with life, yet it is almost perfectly empty. The colossal Glasswood spires are massively far apart. Between one trunk and the next might lie a hundred kilometers of open, empty air. The space is hollow, a near-void punctuated by these colossal, living structures. The temperature is a deep, biting cold, the kind that feels like it’s sinking into your bones. But there is no ice. Instead, the constant, gentle transpiration of the Glasswood keeps the air damp. Moisture doesn't fall as rain; it condenses. Every surface is slick with a film of frigid, pure dew. In the deep grottos formed by the interweaving roots and lower branches, this dew collects into perfectly still, lens-clear pools that reflect the Spindle's glare with unnerving clarity.

This is a young world, not in age, but in its constant, violent rebirth. The Spindle is not stable. Without warning, it pulses. A flare, a wave of light so intense it is a physical force, erupts from the column and screams across the void. This is the world's weather. These light-storms are the primary force of erosion. The wave impacts the Glasswood spires with a sound that shatters the immense quiet—a peal of crystalline thunder that echoes for hours. Trunks and branches on the storm-facing side are flash-calcified, shattered, and flaked away. Shards and dust, some as small as motes and others as large as hills, are blasted into the void, catching the Spindle-light like a galaxy of diamonds. These new surfaces are razor-sharp, with the fresh, clean fracture-planes of snapped obsidian. This is a landscape perpetually remade, perpetually sharp. Huge, slow convection currents—the Ether-Winds—are born from these flares, silently sweeping clouds of glittering dust and the occasional splintered branch through the great emptiness between the spires.

For all this grandeur, the world is mostly quiet. The baseline is the Spindle’s hum and the groaning of the Glasswood under its own immense weight. Sounds travel forever in the thin air. You can hear the stress-fracture of a bough fifty kilometers away, a sharp *tink* arriving long after you see the faint puff of displaced dust.

Fauna is profoundly rare. For days, you might see nothing. Then, a brief, fleeting movement: a colony of Silicate Grazers, like armadillos made of mother-of-pearl, scuttling along a high branch, their claws making tiny scratching sounds as they scrape away the light-freshened new growth. Or, far out in the void, a single, vast Drifter, a creature like a manta ray a kilometer wide, its body a translucent membrane that catches the Ether-Winds, pulsing slowly as it filter-feeds on clouds of silica dust. They are exceptions that prove the rule of emptiness.

And there are faint marks of something else. Small things, easily missed. In the sheltered lee of a massive Glasswood trunk, a wall of woven fibers, now stiff and grey, forms a simple wind-break. Further along, a series of notches have been driven into the vertical face of a spire, their edges still sharp—a ladder to nowhere. The tools to make them lie nearby, scattered and abandoned: heavy shards of Glasswood, their edges knapped to a cruel sharpness, their handles wrapped in the fibrous bark of younger shoots. These are not ruins of a civilization, but the temporary shelters of a handful of people, a nomadic, primitive culture that exists in the interstices of this overwhelming world. Perhaps they follow the light-storms, harvesting the freshly sheared, nutrient-rich plant matter. Perhaps they are hiding from them.

In one such shelter, carved into the pearlescent inner wall of a hollowed-out root, is a single etching. It is a crude figure, humanoid, holding a shard-axe. It is looking up, or inward, at a simple line drawn down the center of the carving: the Spindle. The figure is small. The line is absolute. It is the only story that needs to be told here. The world is the light, the life that feeds on it, and the vast, cold, silent space in between.
