There is no surface. There is only the medium: a warm, thick dark that presses in on all sides, a pressure that is both a crushing weight and a gentle embrace. The water—for it is a kind of water—is not clear. It is a dense suspension, a soupy broth of microscopic life, shed cells, and fine mineral dust, so that even if there were a sun, its light would penetrate no more than a few feet. It tastes of salt and something else, a faint, coppery tang of minerals and life.

It is profoundly dark. The only illumination comes from within. Slow, stately pulses of blue-green light drift through the black, each one a living creature, a gelatinous bell the size of a human head, trailing faint ribbons of phosphorescence. These are the lamplighters, the streetlights, moving with an unhurried, predetermined purpose. Their cold light does not travel far, catching only on the nearest surfaces before being swallowed by the murk.

And there are surfaces. From the soft, silty floor of this immense basin, a city has been grown. It is not built of stone or metal, but of secreted biological cement, layer upon ancient layer. The architecture is a blend of coral reef and termite mound, scaled up to the size of a metropolis. Immense, spiraling towers of cemented chitin and mineral deposits rise for hundreds of feet, their surfaces pocked with countless pores, alcoves, and tunnels. They are not sharp or new; their forms are smoothed and rounded by millennia of gentle, persistent currents. They look like enormous, time-worn bones or the weathered spires of a drowned cathedral. The erosion is not one of destruction, but of settling, of the world finding its comfortable, final shape.

These towers and growths are dense, almost touching. They form deep canyons and narrow alleys where the water is still and thick. Scalloped balconies, grown from the main spires, host gardens of sessile filter-feeders that wave gently in the currents, their feathery arms glowing with a soft, internal amber light. Buttresses of porous, bone-like material connect one tower to the next, creating a web of pathways and structures that fills the volume of the basin. The scale is vast, regional; you can perceive that this city-organism fills the entire valley floor, its edges dissolving into the impenetrable darkness in the distance.

This is a world without vegetation in the common sense. There is no photosynthesis here. At the base of the food web are the sparse, wispy gardens that cling to the walls near deep thermal vents. These are chemosynthetic filaments, pale white and ghostly, drawing energy not from light but from the planet’s inner heat. They are the quiet, unassuming power plants for the entire ecosystem.

The city is anything but empty. It is teeming, saturated with life, though mostly silent. Life here has abandoned sound in favor of light. Schools of small, darting creatures, their bodies like slivers of polished obsidian, flash coordinated signals to each other as they navigate the boulevards between spires. Armored, crab-like scavengers, some as large as hounds, move with a deliberate scraping slowness across the surfaces, their multiple eyes glowing a predatory red. They are the city's janitors, consuming waste, repairing minor damages with their own secretions, and preying on the weak.

There are hunters. Veiled hunters, almost perfectly transparent, drift in the open water between towers, visible only as a slight distortion in the path of a passing light-pulse. They wait. Their patience is geological. When prey drifts too close, they do not strike with speed, but simply unfurl, their bodies expanding into a gossamer net that silently envelops their meal.

The primary builders are rarely seen. They live deep within the foundations of the spires they create—vast, slow, mollusk-like beings who have been growing this city for eons. Their life is the life of the city. Their growth is its growth. The faint, deep thrumming you can sometimes feel through the water is not a sound, but the vibration of their colossal, rhythmic metabolism, the slow heartbeat of the metropolis.

The world is active. A constant, silent ballet of existence unfolds. A pulse-bell drifts too close to a shadowy alcove and is suddenly extinguished as a hidden predator inhales it. A scavenger loses its footing on a high buttress and tumbles in slow motion into the chasms below, where it will be disassembled by smaller decomposers. A new chamber is slowly extruded from a tower wall, its pearlescent surface still soft and unweathered. Patterns of light flicker across a whole district of the city—a warning, a territorial claim, a courtship display.

It is a quiet, densely populated, ancient world, breathing in the dark. The rules are simple and absolute: light is language, growth is architecture, and everything is eventually food for something else. There is no technology, only biology, perfected by time into a state of complex, elegant, and brutal equilibrium. The water moves, the lights pulse, and the city lives on.
