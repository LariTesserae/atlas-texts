This is the Churning Flats, the great basin of the Cog-Slurry. It is a landscape that has forgotten what it means to be still.

From any vantage, the world is a low, vast plain stretching into a dim, wavering horizon. The ground itself is not solid but a thick, viscous slurry, a mixture of pulverized mineral grit and a freezing, oily fluid that carries the consistency of half-frozen coolant. It is a palette of corrosion: the deep, bruised purple of manganese sludge, the sickly green of verdigris, and the dull, wet sheen of gunmetal grey. The air is thick with the smell of ozone, hot metal, and something akin to cold, wet rust. It is a dampness that doesn't refresh but seeps, a chilling condensation that slicks every surface and hangs in the air as a visible, greasy vapor. This vapor forms a permanent, low ceiling, a sky of roiling industrial fog that swallows light. The only illumination is a dim, sourceless twilight, punctuated by erratic flashes of blue-white energy.

There is no vegetation. Not a single leaf, spore, or lichen breaks the mechanical monotony. This world is post-biological, or perhaps it is pre-biological in a way we cannot comprehend. Its ecology is one of pure, inorganic industry. The fauna, in their teeming abundance, are the landscape's engine.

The sound is the first and last thing you perceive: a deafening, all-encompassing cacophony. It is the sound of a factory the size of a continent, running without a foreman. The dominant noise is a low, grinding hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a constant, planetary-scale milling. Layered over this is the rhythmic, hydraulic *thump-shhhhh* of colossal pistons, the high-pitched whine of unseen spinning components, and the sharp, percussive crackle of electrical discharge arcing between towering, unstable structures.

These structures are the apex fauna, the largest inhabitants of the flats. They are not built, but have grown or accreted over millennia. They are the Pistons. Great, smooth columns of polished obsidian and basanite, kilometers tall, rise from the slurry and fall with metronomic slowness. Their movement is the landscape's heartbeat. They don't strike ground, but plunge deep into the slurry, compressing it. With each impact, a wave of pressure radiates outwards, a shiver in the viscous sea. Within their translucent, dark forms, you can see slow pulses of internal, phosphorescent light—glowing ganglia of stored energy.

The slurry itself is packed, saturated with smaller, active entities. There are the Grinders: spherical nodules of pitted iron, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a car, covered in geared maws that constantly ingest the mineral slurry. They don't move with intention, but are carried in the slow, manufactured currents, tumbling and chattering against each other, their grinding a constant, abrasive chorus.

There are the Weavers, which look like skeletal, metallic centipedes hundreds of meters long. They move with an unnatural fluidity, their countless legs ending not in feet but in sparking, welding tips. They crawl over and through the fields of Grinders, their purpose unclear. Sometimes they seem to be repairing other entities, their passing leaving a trail of shimmering, instantly-cooled solder. Other times, they simply connect two random Grinders, creating a short-lived, screaming circuit that flashes brightly before burning out.

This entire system is ancient, worn into a state of horrifying stability. The erosion here isn't of wind or water, but of function. Edges are not sharp unless they are sheering edges; surfaces are not rough unless they are grinding surfaces. Everything has been abraded, polished, or stressed into its final, most efficient form. The boundary of the flats is not a mountain range, but a vast, sloping wall of fused slag, the indigestible waste product of eons of this geological metabolism. These slag-heaps weep, crying rivulets of the oily, condensing atmospheric fluid back into the basin, completing the cycle. The world is eating itself, processing itself, and excreting itself in a closed, purposeless loop that has run for longer than stars have names.

And here, reality begins to fray. The space is not stable. The vast, open plain is also impossibly dense, packed with a churning infinity of components. The distance between two Pistons may seem to be ten kilometers, but to walk it is to find the space between them concertinaing, folding in on itself. The horizon shimmers not with heat, but with a palpable spatial distortion, as if you are viewing it through the warped lens of a powerful magnetic field. The laws of cause and effect are suggestions. A Weaver might solder a connection that powers a Piston a league away, but the Piston's stroke might happen a moment *before* the connection is made. The flashes of light are not just discharges; they are moments where the local physics gives way, where matter and energy are swapped without preamble.

To be here is not to observe an ecosystem. It is to be on the factory floor. The air is an exhaust fume, the ground is raw material, the fauna are the whirring, indifferent components of the machine. The overwhelming sense is one of production without purpose and existence without an observer, a self-perpetuating industrial nightmare that achieved a terrible, grinding form of life and has never stopped. You are not a visitor. You are a contaminant, a piece of grit in the gears, and the machine's only response is to continue its churn, to grind you down, and to incorporate your dust into the slurry.
