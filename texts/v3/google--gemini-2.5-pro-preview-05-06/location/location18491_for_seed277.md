The world is a single, continuous machine, and it is asleep.

Or perhaps this is its state of wakefulness: a slow, quiet, self-sustaining thrum that has persisted for eons. The ground is not rock or soil. It is a vast, undulating plateau of interlocking, bio-ceramic plates, each the size of a town square, their edges worn smooth by time and a slow, perpetual jostling. The material is warm to the touch, a deep, resonant warmth that seems to rise from a core thousands of miles below. The color is that of cooling iron, ranging from matte black to a deep, bruised purple, shot through with veins of what looks like solidified mercury.

This is a high place, so high that the atmosphere is a thin, sable blanket. There is no sun, no moon. The only light is intrinsic to the landscape itself. Far across the plains, kilometers distant, a thermal vent will cycle open with a profound, almost inaudible *whoosh*, releasing a plume of superheated, mineral-rich steam. For a few moments, the rising cloud is illuminated from within by a dull, cherry-red glow, painting the undersides of the low, metallic vapor ceiling before the light fades and the vent seals with a slow, hydraulic sigh. Elsewhere, thin, hairline cracks in the ceramic plating seep a faint, blue-green phosphorescence—the slow chemical decay of waste products from the deep-earth metabolism below. The darkness is not empty; it is populated by these tiny, distant annunciations of a planetary-scale process.

The air is damp. The steam from the vents condenses on the cool upper atmosphere and on the vast surfaces of the plateau itself. It doesn't rain in storms. Instead, there is "condensate fall"—a perpetual, silent mist in some regions, and in others, a slow, viscous dripping from the overhanging eaves of larger geological formations. This moisture slicks the ceramic ground, gathering in the shallow depressions and carving long, almost imperceptible channels toward unseen drainage sinks. The water is heavy with dissolved metals, slick and slightly oily.

Vegetation is sparse, and not what one would typically call a plant. In the sheltered lee of great, vertebral ridges, colonies of silicon-based lifeforms take root. They resemble clusters of dark, fibrous glass, some branching out like arthritic hands, others forming low, bristling mats of crystalline needles. They do not photosynthesize; they are chemosynthesizers, drawing nutrients directly from the mineral-heavy condensate and the conductive substrate. They are part of the machine's filtration system, their slow growth both a product and a process of the world's endless chemistry.

And the world is full of life. Not life that lives *on* the world, but life that *is* the world's machinery. The quiet is punctuated by the rhythmic, chitinous click-and-slide of the Harvesters. These are immense creatures, the size of hills, with low-slung, multi-jointed legs that move with agonizing slowness across the plains. Their bodies are a fusion of organ and engine; smooth, armored carapaces give way to exposed lattices of what look like copper piping and pulsating diaphragms. They move from one thermal vent to another, their primary function to graze on the mineral deposits left behind by the vapor. A complex arrangement of mandibles and grinders on their undersides scrapes and pulverizes the crust, ingesting it with a deep, grinding hum that vibrates through the ground.

Smaller, more nimble creatures swarm in their wake. These are the Cleaners, arthropodal beings the size of large dogs, with spindly legs and mirrored, compound eyes that reflect the faint, distant glows. They consume the waste tailings of the Harvesters, their twitching antennae sampling the air. They are skittish, and the only sound they make is the faint *tick-tick-tick* of their hard-tipped feet on the ceramic plates.

There is a larger ecology here, a predator-prey dynamic that is more akin to industrial maintenance. Great, sessile "Filter-Stalks," rooted to the ground and resembling enormous, skeletal fungi, periodically snatch Cleaners from the air with whip-fast, muscular tendrils, absorbing them not for calories but for the rare trace elements they have concentrated in their bodies.

The world operates by its own established logic. It appears untouched, with no cities or ruins, because the entire planet is a single, built artifact that has achieved a state of ecological and mechanical equilibrium. It is an engine that grew, not one that was constructed. Its purpose is inscrutable. It smelts the metallic core of the planet, cycles it to the surface, refines it through geological and biological action, and incorporates the refined material back into its own structure. The terrain is "established" because it is constantly, slowly, being rebuilt and resurfaced. The vast scale and the sparseness of its components give it the feeling of an empty cathedral, but every surface, every creature, every wisp of steam is a functioning part of a single, colossal organism.

The soundscape is one of deep quietude, a bass-heavy silence. The overwhelming sensation is the low-frequency thrum that is felt more than heard, the planet's idle. Layered on this are the distant, muffled sighs of the vents, the slow, tectonic groan of the plates shifting, the close-up, rhythmic grinding of a Harvester, and the sharp, isolated ticking of the Cleaners. It is the sound of a factory floor the size of a continent, running at one-millionth speed. It is the sound of a living geology, processing itself, forever.
