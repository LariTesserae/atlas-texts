This place is a basin, a caldera, perhaps five or six miles across, cradled high in a range of jagged, new-made mountains. The peaks that form the basin's rim are sharp as flint shards, unblunted by age. They are made of a dark, almost black volcanic rock, slick and weeping with perpetual moisture. Cool air, heavy with the smell of wet stone and decay, pools in this bowl and rarely stirs. Clouds snag on the rim and descend in a slow-motion waterfall of mist, keeping the light in a permanent state of twilight, a diffuse, pearlescent grey.

The floor of the caldera is not soil, but this same sharp-edged, shattered basalt, terraced in uneven, broken steps leading down to a flatter, central sump. Water does not flow here in rivers; it clings. It beads on every surface, forms a shimmering film on the rock, and gathers in still, tea-coloured puddles in every depression. The air itself feels liquid. The only consistent sound is the slow, unsynchronized *plink... plink... plonk* of water dripping from high ledges or the heavy foliage of the flora. It is a silence measured in drips.

And the flora is astonishing. It is not a dense carpet, but a scattered collection of monumental individuals. The vegetation is lush in its complexity, not its coverage. Between these titans of the plant world are wide, empty spaces of glistening black rock. Each plant is an ecosystem unto itself. Some resemble colossal ferns, their fronds a hundred feet long, unfurling in fractal spirals. Their texture is not leafy but like thick, dark green velvet, and they are heavy with the water they hoard, bowing so low that their tips brush the stone floor. Others are more fungal in nature: great, shelf-like tiers of bioluminescent blue and purple flesh that grow directly from the rock, arranged in clusters that look like ruined amphitheaters. Their light is too faint to illuminate, serving only to make the dimness deeper and more coloured. Still others are bulbous, bladder-like organisms, translucent sacs of pale green jelly held up by a tangled skeleton of black, root-like structures. Everything is cool and damp to the touch.

Rising from this landscape of scattered giants is the city. It is not built of stone or wood, but of something else entirely. It is a settlement of woven towers, dozens of them, clinging to the inner walls of the caldera and, in the basin's center, rising as freestanding spires. These structures are a matte, fibrous grey, the colour of wasp nests or dried fungus. They are porous, almost spongy-looking, with a surface that is a chaotic lattice-work of extruded threads. The towers are irregularly shaped, swelling and narrowing, connected to one another by enclosed sky-bridges fifty or a hundred feet up. They are not solid; they are honeycombed with passages and chambers, and punctuated by round, soft-edged openings that gaze out into the grey air like silent mouths. There is no evidence of metal, no sharp cuts of masonry. The entire city looks as if it were secreted, not constructed.

And it was. The fauna here is overwhelming, yet it is a silent, creeping inundation. The architects of the towers are a species of six-legged, beetle-like creature, the size of a human hand. Their chitin is the iridescent black of oil on water. They move with a deliberate, unhurried grace, their soft footpads making no sound on the rock. From spinnerets near their mouths, they extrude the tough, silvery thread that builds and repairs their city hive. They are everywhere: clinging to the walls of their towers, tending to globular, pulsating egg sacs nestled deep within the fibrous architecture, or crawling slowly across the velvet ferns.

But they are not alone. The sheer mass of life is staggering. The surfaces of the giant fungal shelves are carpeted with slugs the size of a man's arm, their bodies patterned in hypnotic, swirling blues and purples that mimic their food source. They graze in silence, their rasping radulae the only other faint sound, a soft, wet scraping if you are close enough to hear it. Huge, flightless moths with heavy, furred bodies and wings like charcoal sketches cling to the undersides of leaves, their antennae twitching, tasting the damp air. Swarms of tiny, white, mite-like creatures flow like living rivers through the cracks in the basalt, consuming unseen nutrients. Predator and prey exist in a slow, silent dance. You might see a hunter: a creature like a pale, multi-jointed spider the size of a wolfhound, moving with liquid patience, stalking one of the giant slugs. The kill, when it happens, is not a scream but a silent, venomous injection and a slow, quiet dissolution.

Life here is a metabolism made visible. The city itself breathes. Steam, warmer than the ambient air, vents from high openings in the towers—the collective exhalation of the millions of creatures inside. Waste products, a rich, dark slurry, are piped down through fibrous conduits and deposited at the base of the great plants, which in turn absorb the nutrients to fuel their gargantuan, silent growth. The entire caldera is a single, closed system, a slow-motion riot of life that makes almost no noise.

To be here is to feel watched by a million unblinking compound eyes. Every surface is occupied. The emptiness between the great plants is an illusion; the air itself feels thick with spores and pheromones, and the ground is a shifting carpet if you look too closely. The world operates on a different rhythm, one of silent, constant, purposeful activity. It is a city not of people, but of instinct. A cathedral of chitin and cellulose, whose only prayer is the relentless, quiet continuation of its own strange life.
