This place is not found by sight. You find it by feel, by the slow change in the air as you climb. The world outside is one of high, broken peaks and thin, cold air. But here, wedged in a fissure that is less a cave entrance and more a geological sigh, the atmosphere becomes a presence. It is warm, thick, and instantly clings to your skin, beading on your arms and the back of your neck. The darkness within is absolute, a solid object that presses against the eyes.

You enter a room, though the term is too geometric. It is a pocket, an amnion. Its scale is intimate; you could cross it in a dozen paces if the way were clear, but it is not. The floor, walls, and ceiling are a single, continuous entity, a tumescent billow of stone. The geology here is not old and weathered; it is being born.

The ground underfoot is not rock, not soil, not sand. It is a dense carpet of nodules, smooth and warm and slick with moisture, like a thousand sweating shoulders pressed together. They are botryoidal formations, stony globes ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a human head, fused into a single, rolling landscape. They are a deep, featureless black in the unlit air, but to the touch they are alive with a strange, low-grade geothermal heat. This is the source of the warmth and the water. The stone itself weeps. The air is so saturated that any temperature gradient, however slight, forces condensation. Water drips constantly, a slow, un-rhythmic clockwork of random impacts. The sound is a gentle, percussive *plink-plonk* on the stone spheres, a sound so pervasive it becomes a form of silence.

There are no plants. The persistent warmth, the utter darkness, the mineral-heavy water permit nothing to grow—no moss, no lichen, no blade of grass. Yet, life is not entirely absent. Run your hand over the weeping nodules and you feel a faint, slick biofilm. In the shallow, lukewarm pools that gather in the hollows between the globes, colonies of thermophilic bacteria create nearly invisible sheens on the water’s surface, a living oil. If you are still and patient, you might notice a flicker of movement in one of these pools—a creature no bigger than a fingernail, pale and translucent, a blind crustacean that feeds on the bacterial mats. It is an ancient, simple thing, living in a state of near-perfect equilibrium with its environment. It does not hunt; it grazes. It does not explore; it exists.

Beneath the sound of dripping water is another sound, a vibration felt more than heard. It is a drone, a low hum that seems to emanate from the stone itself. This is the sound of growth. The nodules are not static. They are emerging, accreting new layers of mineral at an infinitesimally slow, but ceaseless, rate. The process is one of perpetual creation, but it is so stable, so unvarying, that the place feels trapped in a single, unchanging moment. It is always becoming, and therefore, never changes. The new layers of stone form from the mineral-saturated water, infinitesimally thickening the globes, pushing them ever more tightly against one another.

And someone was here. Or is here.

Amidst the chaos of natural, spherical growth, your hand finds something deliberate. A channel, no wider than two fingers, has been neatly scored across the top of a dozen nodules, creating a tiny, artificial aqueduct that redirects the weeping of one globe into a specific depression on another. The groove is shallow, its edges already softened, being slowly erased by the same process that formed the stone around it. Elsewhere, three small, perfectly round nodules are stacked into a tiny cairn, a marker of purpose in a place that has none. You find a larger globe where the surface has been abraded smooth, a patch of polish in a world of slick texture. On this surface, a simple spiral has been incised. The groove is already filling with new, glassy calcite, like a healing scar.

The tools for this work are here, too, scattered and sparse. A shard of harder, foreign stone, its edge chipped for scraping. A piece of bone from some large, unknown animal, its tip shaped into a stylus. These are not the artifacts of a settlement, but of a function, a ritual. These are the tools of gardeners. They are not building homes or forts. They are tending to the geology itself.

Perhaps they were a people who understood this slow, eternal genesis. They worked with it, not against it. They did not harvest the stone; they guided its formation, their subtle efforts a dialogue with the deep earth. Their marks are a prayer, a meditation made physical, an act of participation in a process that transcends human timescales. The work is never finished, and is never meant to be. The spiral they carved will be swallowed by the stone in a thousand years, and a new gardener, of a new generation, will come to carve it again, maintaining the unchanging pattern. This is the rule of the place: nothing is permanent, yet nothing ever changes. You are in the planet's womb, dark and warm and wet, witnessing a birth that has no beginning and no end.
