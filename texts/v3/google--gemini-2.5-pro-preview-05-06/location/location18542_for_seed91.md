There is no quiet here. The first and last thing you know of this place is the sound. It is not a collection of noises but a single, solid thing, a monolithic roar that fills the air and presses in on your skull. It is a physical presence, a weight that settles in your bones and makes your teeth ache. This is the sound of grinding, of immense geological pressures being mechanically replicated. It is the sound of a world being slowly, inexorably milled.

The air is brutally cold and tastes of stone dust, ozone, and frozen peat. Every breath is a sharp, clean pain. You are in a cavern, but the word is insufficient. It is a sunken world, a basin of continental scope, so vast that the ceiling is a theoretical concept, a perpetual, starless night high above. The ground underfoot is not rock, but something else. It is a compressed, semi-living mass, a planet-wide mat of vegetation so ancient and dense it has become a substrate. Ligniform, perhaps. It feels like frozen, fibrous asphalt, slick with a perpetual film of rime. In the crushing cold, it is as hard as granite.

Dim, sourceless light filters down from the unseen roof, just enough to reveal the staggering emptiness of the basin floor. The ligniform plain stretches out, a dark expanse of muted grey-greens and blacks, rolling in shallow, frozen waves toward a horizon you can never quite reach. It is a landscape of profound sparsity. For miles in every direction, there might be nothing but this frigid, living ground.

But it is not empty.

Scattered across this immense plain, separated by leagues of vibrating silence-within-the-roar, are the machines. They are not buildings, not factories in the conventional sense. They are integrated entities, mountain-sized industrial herbivores that graze on the very ground they stand upon. Each is a city-sprawl of iron and steel, a complex of gantries, drills, conveyors, and furnaces, all fused into a single, semi-mobile organism.

One such machine dominates the middle distance. Its true scale is difficult to parse. Towers of corroded steel, draped in thick chains like black ivy, rise into the gloom. Searchlights, casting a jaundiced sodium-yellow glare, sweep in slow, deliberate arcs, illuminating clouds of steam that billow from vents and exhaust ports. The steam freezes as it rises, falling back to the ground as a constant, glittering sleet of ice crystals. The area immediately around the machine is a maelstrom of localized weather.

At its base, where the machine meets the ligniform, is the source of the roar. Rows of colossal drills, each the size of a bell tower, chew into the frozen substrate. They do not spin quickly. They turn with agonizing slowness, their immense torque groaning as they pulverize the ancient plant matter. The sound they make is a low, gut-churning frequency that you feel more than hear. The pulverized ligniform is then scooped onto vast, clanking conveyor belts and dragged up into the machine's cavernous interior, disappearing into processing sheds where crushers and grinders add their percussive, high-pitched shrieks to the cacophony.

This is a young landscape, in its way. The features are not softened by time, but cut by industry. Where the machines have passed, they leave behind sheer-walled canyons, sharp-edged quarries in the living earth. The cross-sections reveal the ligniform's stratigraphy: billions of years of compressed life laid bare in bands of dark green, black, and woody brown. Water, released by the heat of the drilling and grinding, pools in the bottoms of these fresh cuts, freezing over into sheets of black ice.

Despite the all-consuming nature of the vegetation-as-ground, new life finds a way. A different kind of plant, a pale, calcified vine, creeps across the cold steel of the machines. It thrives on the waste heat and the metallic substrate, its tendrils tracing the lines of rivets and plating. In the deepest gloom, away from the harsh industrial lights, these rime-vines give off a faint, blue-white bioluminescence, a ghostly glimmer that does little to pierce the darkness but paints the machines in haunting, skeletal light.

There are no herds, no predators, no birds. Fauna is a footnote. In the warmer niches of the machinery, close to the engine blocks and lubricant sumps, live small, armored creatures—gear-mites. They are rarely seen, six-legged specks of iridescent chitin that scuttle from shadow to shadow, feeding on thermal leakage and chemical residue. They are the sole anmalia in a world of plant and iron.

This all proceeds with a dreamlike slowness. The machines creep across the plain at a rate of inches per year. The canyons they leave behind will likely remain sharp-edged for millennia in the freezing, static air. Nothing is changing quickly, yet everything is being consumed. The process is patient, automated, and absolute. There are scattered signs of its architects: a small, forgotten control booth half-buried in the ligniform, its windows shattered; a rusted iron ladder leading up the side of a silo to nowhere. But these are relics. The system is now its own master, running on a geologic timescale. The deafening sound is its heartbeat, a pulse that has likely beaten for ages, and will continue long after the last vestiges of its purpose have been ground into dust.
