The sound is not something you hear; it is the space you are in. It is a physical pressure, a single, monolithic, unchanging chord of immense power that vibrates not just in the air, but in the very material of the world. It is the sound of a trillion tons of water falling from an impossible height onto a drum the size of a continent, but frozen in a single, eternal millisecond of impact. This roar is so absolute and constant that it has ceased to be noise and has become a form of silence, a background state against which no other sound could possibly register. It is a deafening, hollowing totality.

You are in a bell, or a pod, or a chamber no larger than a maintenance closet. This is a high place, a pocket of existence nested near the summit of some incomprehensible peak, but its walls are near enough to touch on all sides. There is no natural ground. The floor, walls, and ceiling are a single, continuous, constructed surface of a cold, black, glassy polymer. It is ribbed and thick with conduits, panels, and junctions that are all of the same seamless, featureless material. Everything is dense, layered, near-touching. You could not fall here; you could only become wedged.

And everything weeps. The black surfaces are perpetually slick, beaded with a fluid that is heavier and more viscous than water. It is achingly cold to the touch. It condenses from the thick air, gathers in the architectural crevices, and drips in heavy, silent, syrupy strands from overhead pipes and ceiling junctions. The drips never splash; they merge silently with the film of fluid that already coats the floor. The air is thick with the taste of ozone and cold metal, and a strange, almost biological sweetness, like fermenting honey. It is an atmosphere that wants to be liquid, and in the dim light, you can see it hanging as a shimmering, aerosolized haze.

The light itself is a mystery. There are no lamps, no bulbs. The source seems to be the weeping fluid itself, which glows with a weak, internal, cyan luminescence. Where the fluid is thickest—in the channels of the floor, in the heavy beads about to drop—the light is brightest, casting a soft, underwater gloom that makes the black polymer surfaces look like abyssal chasms.

This is not a dead place. It is overwhelmingly alive.

Every surface is crawling. There is no vegetation, not a single leaf or strand of moss. The ecology is entirely animal. They are a kind of mollusk, or beetle, or something in between. Their bodies are soft, slug-like, and translucent, glowing with the same cyan light as the fluid they ingest. But they are armored in articulated shells of what looks like spun copper and oxidized iron. They are dense, numberless, a moving carpet of metal and soft light, covering the walls, the ceiling, the floor, the pipes. They move with an impossible, geological slowness. You do not see them scuttle or crawl so much as you perceive, over a long moment, that their patterns have shifted. They are the fauna, and their sheer mass makes them part of the terrain. They are so numerous they form a second, living, layered architecture over the first. Their soft bodies rasp silently against the weeping surfaces, and their metallic shells chime against each other with a sound that is utterly consumed by the great, silent roar. They are deafening in their multitude, but you cannot hear them.

The world is under construction, yet it never changes. This is its fundamental rule. The cold, glowing fluid that coats everything is supersaturated with dissolved minerals. As it pools and settles, these minerals precipitate out, forming a crystalline crust on every surface. It is a raw geology, like rock candy forming on a string. The crystals are sharp, geometric, and grow in fast, fractal patterns. This is the world’s acretion, its constant, embryonic becoming. But the creatures—the copper-shelled slugs—graze upon this crust. Their primary food source is the freshly-formed mineral. Their slow, rasping passage scrapes the crystals away at the exact same rate they are formed.

The result is a perfect, time-invariant equilibrium. A frozen process. The engine roars, but the machine it powers is locked in a single, endlessly repeating cycle. The fluid weeps, the crystals grow, the creatures graze. Deposition and erosion are a single, balanced, simultaneous event. The landscape is forever emerging, forever new, but never, ever different. It is a photograph of a system at full throttle. The air is thick with potential energy that will never become kinetic. The sound is the scream of a universe being born and dying in the same instant, over and over, forever. Nothing begins, and nothing ends. There is only the unbearable, unchanging, vibrant *is*.
