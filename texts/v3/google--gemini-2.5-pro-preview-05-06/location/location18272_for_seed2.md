This is a world held within a shell. There is no land, no air, no sky—only water, and the things that have been built inside it. The pressure is a constant, firm embrace, a reminder that you are kilometers below any conceivable surface. The water is cool, a steady and unnerving chill that seeps into any unheated surface, but it is also brilliantly clear.

Light comes from the center. Hanging in the absolute middle of this vast, curved universe is the Axis, a rod of pure, unwavering white light so long its ends vanish into the distant, hazy curvature. It is a sun, a spine, and a lighthouse all at once. It does not radiate much heat, but its light is clean and true, casting sharp-edged shadows that stretch for miles. This artificial star has a twenty-eight-hour cycle, dimming to a soft violet glow for a period of engineered "night," but now it is at its zenith, flooding the entire volume with the light of a perpetual high noon.

Looking "down"—towards the outer hull—is like staring at a planet's surface from orbit, but the planet is a perfect, concave bowl of metal. It is a floor of incomprehensible size, its expanse a dark grey alloy marked with glowing grids and accessways. From this floor, structures rise like the fresh, violent formations of a new mountain range. These are not worn or ancient; their edges are blade-sharp, their surfaces unscored by time. This world is young, or at least, this phase of it is.

These structures are the only terrain. Vast pylons, kilometers in diameter, thrust upward from the hull, reaching toward the central Axis. They are unfinished. Their tops are jagged webs of ceramo-steel girders and composite plates, swarming with automated construction units. Around these nascent towers, the water is filled with the high-pitched scream of plasma cutters and the deafening, percussive *CLANG* of magnetic hammers riveting plates the size of city blocks. The noise is immense, but the water warps it, smearing the sharp sounds into a constant, deep-chested grinding roar that is felt as much as it is heard. This is the background hum of creation, the sound of a world being born from industrial labor.

Between these monumental construction sites, there is almost nothing. The density is startlingly low. You can see for a hundred kilometers in any direction, the view interrupted only by the haze of distance and the occasional, distant tower. It is a void defined by its colossal boundaries, a cathedral of emptiness.

The ecology is as artificial as the architecture. There is no true vegetation. The only hint of it are the thin, silvery-green mats of bio-filtration lichen clinging to the older, more established sections of the hull. These organisms glow faintly during the "night" cycle, processing waste and oxygenating the water with tireless, silent efficiency. They are the world's lungs and kidneys, a sparse and functional garden.

Fauna, however, is abundant and moves with purpose. Great schools of silver, torpedo-shaped fish, each the size of a human arm, patrol the mid-water. They are an introduced species, a mobile protein source and a biological indicator, their health a constant referendum on the water's quality. They move as one entity, flashing and turning in perfect synchrony, their scales catching the light of the Axis like a million scattered mirrors.

But they are not the apex. Hunting them, and living among the girders of the new-formed structures, are the Scuttlers. These are not organic creatures. They are biomechanical janitors, machines the size of small whales with articulated limbs ending in grapples, saws, and scrubbers. They move with a slow, deliberate arthropodal grace, their single, forward-facing optical sensor glowing a placid blue. They are the maintenance crews, the scavengers. They prune the lichen, recycle debris from construction, and sometimes, with a detached and silent efficiency, they will catch and process a stray fish in their internal furnaces. Their primary sound is a low-frequency clicking, an echolocation that maps their territory.

Deeper, in the darker zones near the outer hull where the Axis's light is a dim twilight, live the Goliath Grouters. They are colossal, bio-engineered beings that resemble a cross between a tardigrade and a cement mixer. They are living factories. They ingest raw metallic sediment piped up from the hull's processing plants and excrete a super-dense, rapid-curing composite. They are slow, ponderous, and utterly vital, for they lay the foundations upon which the automated construction units build. Their passage leaves behind gleaming, fresh-laid ribbons of "rock" that will become the next generation of pylons. The low, seismic moan of a Grouter working is a sound that can travel for a full day through the water.

Everything here operates on a cycle. The twenty-eight-hour day/night rhythm of the Axis. The slow, continent-sized currents generated by titanic impellers in the hull, a deliberate "weather" system to circulate heat and nutrients. The swarms of construction drones expand their towers in pulsing waves of activity. The Scuttlers follow in their wake, cleaning. The fish migrate between feeding grounds on the established lichen fields. The world is a machine, and everything in it, living or not, is a cog. It is not weird or magical; it is relentlessly, overwhelmingly logical. It is a project of impossible scale, an ocean being built and filled in the same breath, loud with the clamor of its own becoming.
