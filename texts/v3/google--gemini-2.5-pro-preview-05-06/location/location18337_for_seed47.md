This is a world defined by a great and ancient stillness, and a frantic, unending storm that passes through it. The two things exist in the same space but do not touch, like a ghost walking through a wall.

The terrain is a testament to the deepest possible time. These are not mountains in the conventional sense; they are the worn-down roots of continental collisions from a cosmic prehistory. Their scale is so vast that from the ground, they appear not as peaks but as world-tilting slopes of polished, slate-grey stone. The forces that shaped them—tectonics, wind, water—are so long absent that the memory of them has been scoured away. Now, only the gentlest of forces remain: the slow settling of fine dust and the occasional abrasion of an information storm. The highest elevations are colossal, gently-curving domes, plateaus so immense their horizons are the planet's own curvature. Between them lie plains of dust, finer than any sand, deep and soft and utterly dry. This dust is the final state of all matter here, the last stop before a theoretical dispersion. It is a world of grey on grey, rendered in shades of stone and powder.

There is no water. Life, such as it is, has pursued other chemistries. Sparse clusters of crystalline "growths" are the only vegetation. They are formations of silicon and rare earth elements that accrete over centuries, drawing faint energy from the bedrock. They look like large, jagged shards of smoky quartz or dull amethyst, jutting from the dust plains in lonely arrangements. They do not sway or rustle; they are as silent and still as the mountains.

The atmosphere is thin, cool, and achingly empty. Under a sun that provides normal light, the sky is a dark, bruiselike violet. Shadows are knife-sharp and absolute, voids of pure black cast against the grey plains. The silence is the first thing one notices and the last thing one forgets. It is not an absence of sound, but a presence of quiet so profound it has weight. It presses on the eardrums. There is no wind, no distant animal call, no trickle of water. The dust muffles footsteps into oblivion.

And yet, the world is in a state of constant, chaotic turbulence. This is the paradox. The turbulence is not physical. It is a storm of data, of potential, of un-realized realities that sweeps across the motionless landscape.

The source is a network of scattered artifacts. Lone pylons of an unidentifiable black material, half-buried in the dust or fused into the bedrock of the great slopes. They are featureless, without seams or symbols, yet they hum with a power that transcends physics as we know it. They are ancient, their surfaces pitted and worn by the very storms they generate. Sometimes, you may find a smaller object: a perfect sphere of chrome humming a single, inaudible note, or a lens of crystal that shows you the place you are standing, but with a different sky.

These artifacts project the storm. It is a weather system of pure information. It has fronts, squalls, and moments of calm. To the naked eye, a storm's approach might be a shimmer in the air, a heat-haze without heat. Then, as it arrives, reality begins to fray at the edges.

The storm does not carry wind or rain; it carries ghosts of geometry, cascades of impossible color, fragments of sound from other worlds. A wall of shimmering static might sweep across a plain, and in its wake, the dust might be temporarily imprinted with the floorplan of an impossible building that vanishes in seconds. You might hear a phantom orchestra playing a single, discordant chord that hangs in the silent air before dissolving. Fleeting, impossible objects flicker into existence and spin apart: a chair made of light, a tree whose leaves are mathematical equations, a face in the dust that speaks a single, nonsensical word.

This is not dangerous in a physical sense. A storm of pure data cannot crush you. But it is profoundly disorienting. To be caught in a dense squall is to have your senses lied to on a fundamental level. You might feel a sudden, intense cold that isn't real, or see the polished grey mountainside resolve into a flickering cityscape that stood for a thousand years on a world that never existed.

In this impossible environment, rare fauna survives. They are not creatures of flesh and blood. They are the "Skimmers," beings that appear as quick, dark ripples in the very fabric of the information storm. They seem to swim in it, to feed on it. They are rarely more than a flicker at the edge of vision—a distortion that darts through a bank of phantom data, a sudden cancellation of a sound that wasn't there. It is unknown if they are native to this place or if they, too, are just a more persistent form of the storm's projections.

To exist here is to balance on the knife's edge between the absolute, grinding stillness of the geology and the hyperactive chaos of the technology. The rule of the world is simple: trust the stone, trust the dust, for they are real and ancient. Do not trust your senses when the air begins to shimmer. The ground beneath your feet is the only truth. Everything else is weather.
