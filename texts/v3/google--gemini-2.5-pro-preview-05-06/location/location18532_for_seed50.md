This is not a sea. It is a place of water, a state of being submerged, but it has no surface and no floor. It is a pocket of liquid suspended in an impossible altitude, a lens of churning fluid held in the high, thin atmosphere of a world that has forgotten what to do with its oceans. The light that floods this place is clean, even, and absolute, coming not from a sun but from the very sky beyond the water’s edge, a constant, shadowless glare that renders everything in stark, clinical detail.

The dominant feature, the only feature that truly occupies the space, is the sound. It is a deafening, grinding, and overwhelming roar, the sound of a world being born and un-born in every instant. It is the sound of tectonics without land, a scream of geological friction happening in the water itself. It is a physical presence, a constant pressure on the bones, a vibration that resonates in the teeth and skull until the distinction between hearing and feeling dissolves. This is the sound of creation, and it is agonizingly loud.

From the turbulent, cool clarity, matter precipitates. This is the source of the noise. Spindles of raw, crystalline material, like milky quartz the size of a man, erupt from the empty water, shivering into existence. They hang for a moment, humming a high, keen note that pierces the deeper grinding roar, and then they are caught by a cross-current and slam into one another, shattering into a million glittering shards that catch the flat light before dissolving back into the liquid medium. A few feet away, a plate of what looks like obsidian condenses, its surface glossy and perfect for a mere second before a stress fracture, born from a dissonant frequency in the sonic onslaught, splinters it into dust. This is the landscape’s geology: embryonic, ephemeral, and violent. Nothing lasts long enough to erode; it is simply unmade.

In this screaming maelstrom, there is life, but it is sparse to the point of being theoretical. Clinging to the heart of the turbulence, where the currents are most predictable in their chaos, are the Still-Spines. They are the closest thing to vegetation. They look like long, dark green reeds of glass, growing in tight, isolated clusters. They are not destroyed by the sonic booms or the colliding mineraliths because they are flexible, bending like whips in the flow. They feed on the kinetic energy, turning the chaos into a slow, deliberate form of growth. To see a cluster of them, a dozen spines swaying in perfect, violent unison, is to see the most ancient and established presence in this realm.

Scattered between these infrequent oases of life are the buildings, or what’s left of them. They are not made of the precipitated stone, which would be instant folly. They are woven. Small, pod-like structures, big enough for one or two beings to curl up inside, are lashed to the genesis-points of the Still-Spines with thick, fibrous rope. The pods themselves are constructed from a dark, leathery material bent over a frame of some resilient, bone-like wood. They are primitive but ingenious, designed not to resist the current but to ride with it, like a basket caught in a hurricane. They are all empty. Their doorways, simple oval holes, face away from the wildest vectors of the current. They are silent testaments to a people who understood the rhythms of this impossible place, who learned to build a shelter in the heart of a scream. Who they were, or where they went, is a question swallowed by the roar.

Fauna is rarer still. For hours, there is nothing but the water, the sound, and the ghost-birth of rock. Then, a flicker of motion, something that is not mineral and not plant. A Resonance Skate, perhaps. It is a creature made of little more than a flat, diamond-shaped membrane of cartilage, translucent and veined with threads of dull silver. It has no visible organs, no eyes, no mouth. It navigates and feeds by attuning itself to the specific frequencies of shattering crystals, darting through the water to absorb the burst of energy released when a new formation is annihilated. It is a creature of pure reaction, a scavenger of failed creation, and its passage is a silent, fleeting ballet in the midst of a war.

The rules of this world are simple. You are high up, yet deep down. You are in a space that is almost perfectly empty, yet you are constantly being pummeled by unseen forces. The light is total, but it illuminates almost nothing. The only constant is change, and the only certainty is the deafening noise of that change. This is a pocket-universe, a specific and contained anomaly. If you could somehow push through the invisible boundary of this churning cell of water, you would find yourself in a vast, silent, and truly empty expanse of high-altitude liquid, stretching for miles under the shadowless glare. This room of chaos is an exception, a flaw, a workshop left running. To be inside it is to be a grain of sand in a rock tumbler the size of a mountain, waiting to see if you will be ground down or, by some miracle, polished.
