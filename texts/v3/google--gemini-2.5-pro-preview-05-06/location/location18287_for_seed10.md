This is a plateau at the end of time, a tableland of living matter worn down by aeons into a near-perfect flatness. The ground itself is the only true feature, a single, contiguous organism or ecosystem that blankets the entire region. Call it the Felt. It is a dense, cold, lightless weave of fungal hyphae, algal films, and lithophilic mosses, all bound together into a spongy, perpetually weeping substrate. To the touch, it is like saturated cork, chilled to the bone. It gives slightly underfoot, and a footstep leaves a dark, water-filled impression that slowly, over hours, heals itself. The Felt is ancient, the final form of a world whose mountains have been eroded not by wind and water, but by a slow, biological digestion. What little rock remains is deep within, the skeleton of a world long-since fleshed over.

The scale is immense and disorienting. The near-total flatness, combined with a sparse scattering of features, creates a profound sense of agoraphobia. The horizon is a razor-sharp line, impossibly distant, broken only by the curvature of the planet itself. The air is thin with altitude but heavy with moisture. A perpetual, low-lying fog, born from the constant weeping of the Felt and the exhalations of the world's machinery, hangs in a layer a dozen meters thick. Above it, the sky is a dim, bruised purple, lit by a distant, dying star that offers no warmth, only a faint, apologetic glow. There are no shadows.

This world is not silent. It is loud. The emptiness is filled with a deep, resonant thrumming, a baseline hum that vibrates up through the soles of the feet, a sound so pervasive it is almost a form of touch. This is the noise of the Stills.

Scattered miles apart across the infinite plain of the Felt are the only vertical structures: the Stills. They are the resolution of a paradox, the fusion of the natural and the industrial. They are not built, they have grown. Rising from the living ground like colossal, biomechanical fungi, they are the world's factories and its organs. A Still might stand a hundred meters high, a chaotic tangle of what looks like chitinous plumbing, crystalline flues, and pulsing, vascular sacs wrapped around a core of fibrous, wood-like material. They are things of immense power and utter instability.

This is a world in constant, violent process. The Stills are engines. They plunge organic pistons deep into the ancient Felt, drawing up mineral-rich, primordial fluids. Inside their tangled architecture, these fluids are superheated, cracked, and refined. The process is chaotic. With a deafening shriek, a Still will vent a plume of pure white steam that billows into the cold air and freezes into a curtain of falling ice-needles. Moments later, a different valve groans open, releasing a shimmering, oily vapor that smells of ozone and hot metal. The Stills shudder and groan, their internal pressures building and releasing in unpredictable, rhythmic cycles. Sometimes, a section will overload, exploding in a spray of boiling organic fluid and razor-sharp crystalline fragments that litter the Felt for acres around. The wound on the Still seals itself with a scab of rapidly growing tissue, and within days, new pipes and chambers have grown to replace the old. This is the world's dynamism—a violent, pointless-seeming industry of self-consumption and regeneration.

Reality here is thin, negotiable. The line between plant, animal, and machine is not just blurred, it was never drawn. The Stills are alive, but their function is mechanical. The Felt beneath them is a planetary-scale vegetation, but it serves as geological bedrock. The air itself seems to participate; in the super-chilled zones around steam vents, the moisture doesn't just form ice, but sometimes condenses into fragile, temporary lattices of a material that is neither solid nor gas, humming with captured energy before collapsing back into mist.

And the world is teeming with fauna, all of which are integrated into this bizarre metabolic cycle. Great, slow herds of what might be called grazers move across the Felt. They resemble six-legged armadillos the size of buses, their shells a mosaic of captured minerals and symbiotic, glowing lichens. They do not eat the Felt so much as filter it, absorbing nutrients from the water their immense, splayed feet press out of the spongy ground.

Around the bases of the Stills swarm smaller, faster creatures. Some are scavengers, things like metallic beetles that consume the crystalline shrapnel from explosions. Others are more symbiotic. Long, serpentine creatures with translucent skin coil around the hotter pipes of the Stills, their bodies filled with endothermic bacteria that thrive on the heat. When the pipe cools, they detach and slither across the Felt, their bodies glowing with stored energy, acting as mobile, temporary heat sources for other, smaller life forms. Above, things that might be birds or might be self-propelled kites of stretched membrane ride the thermal updrafts from the vents, their calls like shattering glass mixed with radio static, adding to the cacophony.

This is a place of immense age and frantic, moment-to-moment chaos. The substrate is the product of a billion years of slow growth and decay, a system settled into its final, eroded form. But the surface is a storm, a factory floor with no foreman, an engine running at full throttle for no reason other than that it can. It is a world breaking down its own rules, mixing geology with biology and industry, a cold, wet, loud, and terribly alive place caught in a turbulent, endless cycle of its own making.
