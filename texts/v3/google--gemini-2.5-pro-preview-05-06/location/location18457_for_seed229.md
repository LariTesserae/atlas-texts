This place is called the High Atrium.

It exists as a shallow, concave basin atop a mountain whose peaks have long been scoured away into smooth, rolling shoulders of black, wind-polished stone. The scale is that of a grand cathedral nave, perhaps half a mile across, with edges that slope gently upwards before falling away into unseen chasms. The floor of the Atrium is not soil but a pavement of the same ancient, dark rock, worn into soft undulations over eons. It has the texture of a river stone, yet no water has ever flowed here. The air is warm and still at its core, but perpetually in motion at its peripheries.

The light is a constant, dim silver-grey, as if from a sky permanently overcast by a thin layer of mother-of-pearl. There is no sun, no discernible source; the light is simply ambient, seeping into the world from all directions at once, casting no true shadows, only a softening of forms.

The defining paradox of the Atrium is its vegetation. It is lush, but it is a dry, mineral lushness. There is no water. The "sap" of this world is a fine, airborne dust, a substrate of microscopic, crystalline motes that perpetually swirl in invisible currents. The plants are geological accretions that have learned to live. They are sparse, arranged with the deliberate spacing of a zen garden.

Great, branching structures of what looks like smoky quartz and tourmaline rise from the stone floor. These are the 'Glass-Spires'. They do not have leaves, but vast, fractal arrays of delicate, hair-thin filaments that tremble in the air, catching the nutrient dust like aeolian nets. Their trunks are thick and dark, resembling obsidian, but their extremities glitter with a captured inner light. Other plants take the form of low, sprawling matrices of bismuth crystal, their iridescent, stairstep geometry covering patches of the floor in metallic rainbows. Nothing is soft or green; the richness is one of complex form, of crystalline intricacy, of a biome built from minerals and air.

And the air is not quiet. It is loud. The primary sound is a constant, high-frequency, sibilant chiming. A *shhhhhhhhhhh-k-k-k-chink-chink-shhhhhhhh*. This is the sound of the fauna.

The Atrium is teeming with life. The source of the noise is the endless, frenetic activity of the 'Scuttlers.' They are arthropodal creatures, ranging from the size of a fingernail to the size of a hand. Their carapaces are not chitin but faceted, crystalline structures, like the plants they consume. Millions of them are in constant, turbulent motion across the floor and up the stems of the Glass-Spires. They are a living, flowing carpet of clicking, skittering, colliding forms. They swarm in dense rivers, then disperse in chaotic, energetic pops. They are the engine of this place, the embodiment of its restless dynamism. Their movement kicks up the nutrient dust, creating localized, shimmering storms that feed the Spires. The entire ecosystem is a feedback loop of frantic motion.

Beneath the high, sharp sound of the Scuttlers is a deeper thrum. This comes from the Bellwethers. These are the largest inhabitants, slow-moving creatures the size of boulders. Their bodies are rounded, porous, volcanic-looking rock, and they glide almost imperceptibly across the basin floor. They do not eat plants or Scuttlers; they seem to inhale the dust-laden air itself. As they process it, their hollow, resonant bodies emit a low, sonorous hum that rises and falls in long, looping periods. This deep vibration travels through the rock floor and the air, a bass note that organizes the chaos, sometimes causing the filaments on the Glass-Spires to tremble in harmonic sympathy, releasing puffs of stored dust.

This is a world of constant, churning change within an ancient, stable container. The black rock is settled, eternal. The life upon it is a storm, a hyperactive state of creation and consumption.

There are faint signs of another presence. Carved into the curving, inner walls of the Atrium's rim are shallow alcoves, just large enough for a body to curl up inside. The openings are smoothed from passage, and the inner walls are scored with simple geometric patterns, echoing the bismuth-growths on the floor. Within some of these nooks lie simple tools: heavy, sharp-edged shards of obsidian-like plant trunk, used perhaps as scrapers; and awls made from the pointed leg of a larger-than-average Scuttler, its crystalline structure perfect for boring holes. There are no bones, no clothes, no fire pits. Only these shelters from the endless, crystalline tide of Scuttlers, and the tools to work the strange materials of this waterless world. Whoever they were, or are, they lived lightly, a settled presence in the heart of a beautiful, screaming, and utterly alien turbulence.
