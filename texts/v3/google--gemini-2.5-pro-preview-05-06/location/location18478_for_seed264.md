The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is a constant, foundational hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a bass note held for ten thousand years. It is the sound of a vast and patient engine, one whose purpose has been forgotten but whose function continues without fault. Layered over this thrum is a rhythmic, metronomic sequence: a deep, resonant *clank*, a long and pressurized hiss, a moment of near-silence, and then the cycle repeats, echoing from dozens of points across the open plain.

You stand on a low, sweeping basin of fused material. It is not soil or rock in any recognizable sense. It feels like hard-fired ceramic, colored in vast, abstract washes of ochre, terracotta, and slate grey. The surface is smooth, almost polished, but not slippery. It is warm to the touch, like a plate left in the sun. For as far as the eye can see, this shallow, undulating valley stretches out, its contours softened and rounded by an erosion that seems less about wind and water and more about time itself. Every edge has been blunted, every potential sharpness worn into a gentle curve.

There is no plant life. Not a single blade of grass, not a patch of lichen. The world is purely mineral. The air is damp and carries a distinct metallic, ozonic tang, the exhalation of the great machine that is the land itself. This moisture doesn't come from a sky-bound weather system. The sky above is a pale, hazy blue, lit by a sun of ordinary yellow-white intensity. The dampness is generated here.

Scattered sparsely across the plain are the source of the rhythmic sounds: the Actuators. They are enormous cylinders of a dark, non-reflective metal, each the size of a grain silo. They emerge directly from the ceramic crust of the world, seamlessly, as if the ground itself had swelled and differentiated into these forms. Their surfaces are stained with umber and verdigris-colored residues, but the primary mechanism—a colossal piston—gleams with a film of slick, clear fluid.

Each Actuator follows the same eternal cycle. With the sonorous *clank*, the piston begins a slow, inexorable rise from its housing. It ascends for nearly a minute, impossibly smooth, revealing a shaft of mirror-polished metal. At its apex, it pauses. Then comes the hiss: a deafening, prolonged release of superheated steam that billows out in a white plume. The steam, hot and heavy, drifts across the plain, clinging to the low-lying areas. It is the world's breath, the source of its humidity. As the steam dissipates, condensing on the cool, high-mass ceramic, the piston slowly, silently, retracts into its deep well. For a few moments, there is only the background hum, until the cycle begins again with another *clank*, precisely on schedule with its dozens of brethren spread across the vista.

This process has been running for so long that it has created its own ecology. The condensing steam gathers in the shallow depressions of the landscape, forming lukewarm pools. The water is clear but heavy with dissolved minerals, slick with the lubricant that weeps from the Actuators. It is not water for drinking. In the places where this water regularly pools and evaporates, delicate, crystalline structures grow. They are not alive, but they follow a life-cycle, branching out in fractal patterns of white and pale blue salt, only to be dissolved and reformed with the next cycle of condensation. This is the only "growth" this world knows.

The landscape is defined by a profound and unassailable stasis. The Actuators are not building anything. They are not deconstructing anything. They are not powering a distant, unseen city. Their work is their own existence. The system is a closed loop, an industrial process whose only product is its own continuation. It is a factory that manufactures equilibrium.

Life, in the biological sense, is an anomaly. Movement is rare. But if you watch a single spot long enough, you might see it. A flicker of motion near the base of an Actuator, where the oily residue is thickest. It is a "gleamer," a creature the size of a man's hand. Its carapace is a shard of iridescent, chromed metal, and it moves on a dozen needle-fine legs, skittering with impossible speed. Gleamers are the janitors of this world-machine. They consume the mineral salts and oily byproducts, keeping the system clean. They are as much a part of the machine as the pistons themselves, another integrated component in the unchanging whole. To see one is to be reminded that even in a place of perfect mechanical order, a tiny, unpredictable spark can find a way to persist. But it doesn't change anything. It just is.

To be here is to be inside a clock. You can walk for miles across the warm, smooth plains, the rhythmic clank-and-hiss of the Actuators fading or growing as you move among them. The air remains warm, the light remains steady, the hum remains constant. There is no future event to anticipate, no past glory to mourn. There is only the vast, open space, the deep hum of power, and the patient, pointless, eternal cycling of the machine.
