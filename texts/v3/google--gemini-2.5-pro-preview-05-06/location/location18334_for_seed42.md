The water is a constant, cool pressure against the skin, a medium as total and assumed as air. There is no surface here, no shore, only the endless, gentle press of the liquid world. Light is a soft, internal thing, a blue-green luminescence that seems to emanate from the very substance of the place. This is the Sunken Spire of Athanor, a city grown on the sheared-off peak of a drowned mountain.

The "ground" and the "walls" and the "buildings" are one and the same: a single, colossal, living hydrophyte. It is a plant that mimics stone, a lattice of near-infinite complexity. Its substance, known to its inhabitants as Latticeweave, is a material somewhere between wood, coral, and kelp. It is rigid enough to hold its form against the slow currents, yet porous, allowing water to flow through its entire structure. The entire city Breathes, a slow, hydraulic inhale and exhale that takes a full day to complete. This breath is the source of the place's dominant sound: a deep, pervasive, basso profondo hum, the thrum of a trillion gallons of water being filtered through a trillion living cells. It is a sound so constant it becomes a form of silence.

The city is built—or rather, *grown*—as a series of stacked, concentric chambers, spiraling up the mountain peak. These are not rooms in a conventional sense, but vast, open volumes defined by the density of the Latticeweave. The main thoroughfares are wide, hollow cylinders where the current flows unimpeded, serving as boulevards for travelers. Smaller passages branch off, forming a capillary network that leads to quieter grottos and private hollows. The scale is that of a grand, submerged cathedral. From the central nave, one can look up and see the spiraling galleries rising into the luminous blue haze, their forms softened by distance and the drifting motes of organic matter. Though the vegetation is total—the very substrate of existence—the feeling is one of great space. The living walls are a filigree, separating vast pockets of open water, creating a balance of mass and void.

Light is not from above. The distant shimmer of the ocean's true surface is a mythological abstraction. Here, light is life. Specific lineages of the Latticeweave have been cultivated for their bioluminescence. Veins of brilliant turquoise light trace the major corridors. Softer, warmer glows of chartreuse and gold fill the residential chambers, pulsing in a slow, communal rhythm that governs the cycles of rest and activity. The city brightens and dims on its own organic time, a slow tide of light independent of any sun.

This ancient, established place is far from empty. Life is everywhere, a vibrant counterpoint to the slow pulse of the architecture. Schools of tiny, prismatic fish—glimmer-fin—move like thrown confetti through the open chambers, their collective turns catching the light in silent flashes. Larger creatures, gentle grazers the size of sea lions, with carapaced backs and soft, whiskered mouths, cling to the Latticeweave, methodically scraping away at parasitic algae with tongues of rough cartilage. Their movements are slow, deliberate, their contentment audible as a series of low, resonant clicks that echo in the watery space. Things hunt here, too. Long, sinuous predators, dark as the abyss below, use the densest parts of the weave as their hunting grounds, their passage a momentary, chilling silence in the constant hum.

The builders and inhabitants are the Weavers. They move with an innate, three-dimensional grace, their webbed hands and feet propelling them through the water-filled halls of their home. Their technology is entirely organic and manual. They carry tools, but they are tools of husbandry, not construction. Knives of sharpened leviathan-bone are used to prune errant growths. Awls made from the spines of abyssal urchins serve to guide nascent fronds, stitching and suturing them into new patterns. To build a new chamber, a community does not lay foundations; they select a strain of fast-growing Latticeweave, graft it with luminous variants, and spend a generation patiently tending its growth, weaving it into the desired shape. Their law is the law of the gardener: what is taken must be replanted, what is wounded must be healed, and all things must serve the whole.

The soundscape is dense and layered. Over the fundamental hum of the Spire itself is the cacophony of life. The high-pitched chittering and sonar pings of the glimmer-fin schools. The deep, mournful songs of the grazers echoing through the grand naves. The occasional, percussive *thump* as a piece of calcified outer-husk breaks free and drifts slowly down into the darker waters below the city. The Weavers add their own sounds: a language of clicks, whistles, and sung notes that carry clearly through the water, a form of communication that is both conversation and music. It is a loud place, but the noise is organic, the symphony of a living city, a settled ecosystem in constant, slow-motion dialogue with itself. Change happens not on the scale of hours or days, but of seasons and generations, as chambers are slowly reshaped and the city's light shifts its hues with the patient guidance of its inhabitants.
