The light is not from a sun. It is a presence in the air itself, a bright, sourceless glare that emanates from the very medium of sight. The world is a high, open basin carved from a single, continuous construction, a place drowned in cool, luminous vapour. This mist, thick as buttermilk, is the source of all light, and it presses in, cool and damp against any imagined skin. To be here is to be inside a cloud that glows with a cold, even intensity, bleaching all colour to shades of bone and pearl.

Everything is built. There is no natural ground. The landscape is an unbroken metropolis of hand-smoothed structures, packed so tightly that they merge into a single, compound edifice. The material is a kind of hydrous chalk, a soft, porous mineral that feels like soapstone but is as pale as a skull. It is forever damp, not slick with slime but saturated, like a sponge at full capacity. The air is so full of water that every surface perpetually weeps.

The forms are primitive, yet impossibly vast. They are not the sharp, geometric lines of advanced engineering, but the organic, thumb-smeared shapes of pottery scaled to the size of mountains. Great, sloping walls curve into flared buttresses. Towers, thick and round, rise like the molars of a god, their tops lost in the ceiling of luminous mist. Their surfaces are covered in the faint, overlapping whorls of countless palms and fingers, evidence of the colossal, patient labour that shaped them. Despite the softness of their making, the features are sharp, uneroded. The water that coats them is pure, a slow, gentle seepage that lacks the grit and force to wear them down. They are fresh, as if the builders have only just left.

This is a packed and dense world, yet it holds a sense of immense scale. From any high ledge, one can see across a valley of these chalk-white forms. The gaps between them are not streets but canyons, plunging into depths where the light-mist thickens and the sound of dripping water consolidates into a steady, resonant hum. There are no straight lines. The canyons twist and braid, following a logic that is not of transport, but of fluid dynamics. They are channels, designed to guide the condensing water downwards.

The soundscape is one of near-total silence, punctuated only by water. It is the patient *tick* of a drop falling a great height to strike a resonant surface. It is the slick, rubbing sound of a rill finding its way down a groove carved a thousand years ago by a thumb. It is the almost inaudible sigh of vapor beading into liquid on a cool, curved wall. In the deepest channels, these sounds merge into a low, bass thrum, the aquifer-heart of the constructed world, a slow and steady pulse.

There is no life here. No plant insinuates its roots into the chalk; no lichen paints a bloom of colour. The world is sterile, mineral, and water. There are no animals, no insects, not even microbes that leave a visible trace. The purity is absolute. The construction is not a habitat. It is a machine, a grand and passive apparatus whose function is tied to the water.

The rules of this place are simple and inexorable. The air generates light and water. The water condenses on the built forms. The forms guide the water downwards. Every groove, every channel, every basin is part of a slow, planetary-scale filtration system. The water that drips from the highest spires is merely distilled vapor, but the water that pools in the lowest cisterns, unseen in the deep foundations of the world, is something else entirely—purified to an impossible degree, stripped of all memory and property except its own potential.

Change is glacial. A new layer of the chalky paste might be added to a wall every few centuries. A channel might be subtly reshaped by a generation of unseen, silent caretakers to optimize the flow. The brightness of the mist might wax and wane on a cycle that spans millennia. To an observer, the world is a static sculpture, but it is in fact a process, a slow, deliberate act of purification that has been running for an age and will run for an age to come. It is a city built not for inhabitants, but to be a vessel for a single, quiet task.
