This place is not found by traversing a landscape, but by descending into one. It is a basin, a perfect bowl carved from ancient, pale rock, perhaps a mile across. From the rim, it appears empty, a sterile concavity in the world. As you begin the descent along a path of worn, chalky stone, the air grows warmer, and the first hint of the sound begins—a low thrum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet.

The farther down you go, the more this sound builds. It is not one sound, but a million, layered into a single, overwhelming presence. It is the Great Hum. It starts as a drone, the vibration of a colossal turning fork. Then, higher frequencies join in: the ringing of infinite tiny bells, the shattering chime of a million wine glasses struck at once, the high, piercing shimmer of singing sand. By the time you reach the basin floor, the sound is a physical force. It is deafening to the point of becoming a new form of silence; it occludes all other noise, your own breath, your heartbeat, the scuff of your boots on the dust. It presses on your bones, thrums behind your eyes, and fills your mind until thought itself feels like an intrusion.

And here, on the basin floor, under a sky that is not a sky but a uniform, brilliant, sourceless ceiling of white light, is the city.

It is a city built within a forest, but the forest is not made of wood or leaf. This is the source of the lushness, and the source of the sound. Crystalline flora grows from a "soil" of pearlescent, talc-like dust. These growths are wildly varied in form, but uniform in their impossible nature. There are thickets of cobalt-blue reeds, thirty feet tall, each one a hollow tube that resonates with a deep, sonorous moan. There are groves of what look like enormous, incandescent glass ferns, their fronds of spun, veridian light shivering with a high, crisp crackle. Fungal-like brackets of rose-gold quartz cantilever from the city walls, their tiered shelves humming with a complex, throbbing chord. Nothing here is soft. Nothing here requires water. This is a lifeform of mineral and light, a xeric, lithic ecosystem whose version of photosynthesis is converting the omnipresent brightness into resonant vibration and slow, crystalline growth.

The space is both lush and profoundly empty. These crystal growths are spaced with intention, as if in a garden. Between the towering blue reeds is a plaza of empty, white dust the size of a lake. Between the glowing fern-groves are vast, open boulevards. The density of the place is in its light and sound, not its matter. The air is warm and absolutely, terrifyingly dry. It leaches moisture from your skin, your eyes, your lungs. To breathe is to feel yourself desiccating.

Through this resonant glass-forest winds the city. It is ancient, its forms smoothed by eons of exposure to the Great Hum. It is built from a bone-white, almost ceramic adobe and dark, polished obsidian. The tech is primitive, yet the scale is grand. Wide, spiraling ramps curve up the sides of skeletal towers that seem designed not for shelter, but to better frame the light. Plazas are marked by standing stones, hand-carved with geometric patterns that seem to map the harmonics of the basin. There are structures that look like aqueducts, but their channels are perfectly clean and dry; perhaps they were once used to conduct not water, but focused beams of sound, or flows of the glittering dust. The architecture is open, airy, and layered, built upwards in tiers and platforms. It is a city for beings who did not fear the elements, or whose only elements were light and sound.

It is an urban ghost. The sense of organized construction is undeniable, but the inhabitants are gone. The emptiness is their absence.

Life, however, is not entirely absent. The primary ecology is the slow, active process of the crystal-flora growing, their chimes shifting as they expand millimeter by millimeter over centuries. But if you can attune your eyes, letting the overwhelming light and sound fall away, you might see the fauna. They are rare, and they are strange. On the flat faces of the ceramic walls, tiny, six-legged creatures, the size of a fingernail, methodically scrape at the surface. Their bodies are iridescent, chitinous carapaces that are acoustically shaped to deflect the overwhelming sound into harmless ripples around them. They are not eating the walls, but the fine, shimmering dust that settles there, a byproduct of the crystal-flora's life cycle. They are the basin's janitors, its only visible animals, moving with a slowness that befits a world measured in geological time.

The final rule of this place is its totality. There is no escaping the light; it has no source and casts only faint, blurry shadows. There is no escaping the sound; it is the atmosphere itself, a medium to be moved through. The city is a bell, a massive, ornate, living instrument. It was built either in worship of the sound, or as a way to tune it. For millennia it has been playing its one, impossibly complex song to an audience of dust-scrapers and ghosts, waiting in its warm, bright, desiccated basin for someone to descend once more and listen.
