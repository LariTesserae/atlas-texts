This is a world born in a clamor.

The air itself is thick, a warm, humid medium that tastes of ozone and hot stone. It does not carry sound so much as it is saturated by it, a constant, layered roar. The fundamental note is a high-frequency, million-voiced chittering, the sound of infinite tiny legs on a brittle surface. Underneath this, there are sharper, percussive sounds: the loud *crack* of fracturing mineral, the grinding scrape of stone on stone, and the wet, concussive thuds of heavy bodies colliding. The atmosphere is not one of stillness disturbed by noise, but a state of perpetual, deafening vibration.

You are in a cavern, but the term is insufficient. It is a space of impossible scale, a hollow world that extends into a hazy, self-lit distance. There is no sun, no sky, but the entire volume is filled with a bright, buttery, sourceless light. The light seems to emanate from a fine, glowing mist suspended in the air, a fog of luminous microorganisms that pulses with the ambient noise. The brightness is that of an overcast day, revealing everything in stark, shadowless detail.

The terrain underfoot is not soil or ancient rock, but a raw, emerging geology. It's a landscape of percussive travertine, laid down in sharp, crystalline layers that are still warm to the touch. These formations grow visibly, pushed up from below in groaning, incremental thrusts. New ridges, sharp as broken glass, bisect older, smoother flows. The ground is a chaotic mosaic of brittle, lacquer-like crusts, jagged fields of mineral shards, and smooth, newly-poured basins. Nothing is old here. Nothing has had time to erode. The world is being born, and the birth is violent.

Water does not form rivers or lakes. It weeps from seams in the nascent stone, beading on every surface as heavy, oily condensation. These droplets, supersaturated with minerals, crawl sluggishly down the inclines to gather in hissing, effervescent pools. The moisture makes the air close and the ground slick, adding a wet slickness to the constant chorus of sounds.

There are no plants. Not a single leaf, spore, or blade of grass. The ecosystem is built on a different foundation. The world is life, and the life is the world.

This place is overwhelming with fauna. The chittering, the source of the ceaseless background hum, comes from a living carpet of insectoid mites, no bigger than a thumbnail, that covers every non-vertical surface. They flow like a fluid, a shimmering, clicking sea of iridescent carapaces, consuming the fine mineral dust kicked up by larger things.

And the larger things are everywhere. They are the architects of this place, a race of crustacean-like beings that range from the size of a large dog to that of a draft horse. Their bodies are dense, thick-shelled plates of the same material as the cavern floor, segmented and articulated around six powerful legs. Their heads are low-slung, dominated by grinding mandibles and clusters of multifaceted, crystalline eyes that refract the ambient light into a thousand moving rainbows.

They are the source of the chaos, the turbulence, the geology itself. They are builders. With a frenetic, instinctual energy, they quarry the very ground they stand on. They use their own powerful jaws, but also primitive tools: heavy, geode-like nodules used as hammers; the shed leg-spikes of their own dead lashed to salvaged limbs to serve as levers and picks. They carve blocks of the warm travertine and heave them into position, creating scattered, temporary structures.

These constructions are not cities, but a chaotic scattering of efforts. A lone Architect might spend a cycle heaving blocks into a crude, cyclopean arch, only for a larger specimen to blunder through it, sending it crashing down. Elsewhere, a team of a dozen works in frantic, uncommunicative unison to raise a high, thin wall, while another group nearby dismantles a similar structure for its raw materials. Hanging galleries, massive buttresses supporting nothing, and deep, resonant chambers are built, collapse, and are scavenged in a constant, churning cycle. There is no plan, only a pervasive, overwhelming urge to build, to shape, to impose a fleeting order on the emergent chaos.

The very ground is a product of their lifecycle. They consume the raw mineral-rich seepage that wells up from the deep, and their excretions harden almost instantly into the bright, brittle stone of the cavern. They are living reef-builders, and this entire, valley-sized cavern is their collective, ever-growing shell.

To be here is to feel profoundly unsteady. The ground groans as a new plate of stone is forced into existence. The air shivers as a nearby, half-finished spire gives way in a roar of shattering mineral. The constant, overwhelming presence of the Architects—their deafening work, their near-touching density, their clattering, purposeful movements—leaves no room for silence or solitude. It is a world of pure, manic creation, a civilization of geology being hammered into existence moment by moment, a symphony of birth played at the volume of demolition.
