The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not a collection of noises — a clatter, a roar, a screech — but a single, monolithic thing. It is a chord played on a planetary scale, a deafening, resonant hum that has so saturated the world that silence is an unimaginable concept, a myth from a forgotten cosmology. It is a physical presence, a pressure on the chest, a vibration that works its way into the bones and stays there. The air itself seems to be made of this sound.

This is the high mesa, a vast and level plateau stretching for dozens of miles, its edges lost in a perpetual, warm haze. The air is thick and wet, almost viscous. It clings to every surface, condensing in heavy, syrupy drops. The temperature is that of a perpetual fever, a humid warmth that never breaks. Light is a diffuse, pearlescent gray, filtered through the thick atmosphere as if through a sheet of frosted glass. There are no distinct shadows, only a soft-edged darkening beneath things.

The ground is not soil. It is ancient bedrock, a foundation of basalt and granite worn utterly smooth by eons of some slow, inexorable force. There are no sharp edges here. Everything is rounded, settled, in its final form. This bedrock is perpetually slick, covered in a thin, mobile film of water. The water doesn't flow in streams or gather in rivers; it seeps directly from the porous stone, weeping from a million microscopic fissures, and condenses from the heavy air. Shallow, plate-sized pools of it lie in the slight depressions of the rock, their surfaces trembling not from wind, but from the deep thrum of the world-sound.

Across this immense, open stage are the structures. They are the source of the Hum. They are built on a scale that ignores the human form. Massive, dark shapes loom in the gray light, separated by miles of barren, weeping stone. They are not towers or blocks, but something more elemental: colossal domes of pitted, verdigris-streaked metal, half-sunk into the bedrock; sprawling complexes of pipes as thick as sequoias, tangled like frozen entrails; immense, gear-toothed flywheels, hundreds of feet across, turning with an imperceptible, geological slowness. This is a city, but not for habitation. It is a machine, a single, distributed engine the size of a valley.

Between these titanic nodes, infrastructure runs in stark, geometric lines. Broad causeways of a black, glassy material cross the empty spaces, their surfaces slick with condensation and algae-like mineral stains. They are wide enough for convoys of leviathans, but are utterly empty. Alongside them run bundles of the great pipes, pulsating with the slow, powerful beat of whatever fluid they carry. Everything is stained with rust, but it is not the crumbling rust of decay. It is a settled, protective patina of orange, brown, and deep red, a skin the machine has grown over millennia.

Life here is a technicality. There is no soil for roots to hold, and the very air, vibrating with sound, is a death sentence for delicate tissues. Sparse, resilient life clings to the margins. In the lee of the great pipes, where the acoustic vibrations are slightly dampened, thin, black, fungus-like filaments trace fractal patterns on the stone, feeding on atmospheric minerals and runoff from the metal skins of the machines. They are the only vegetation. Fauna is rarer still, almost hypothetical. Perhaps there are things in the deep effluent channels, hardened and blind scavengers adapted to the crushing noise and mineral-heavy water. But on the surface, nothing stirs. The world is too loud, too overwhelming, for a nervous system to endure.

The weirdness of this place is not in what is present, but in what is absent. It is an industrial landscape of incredible power and complexity, but with no operators. It is a city with no citizens. The slow, gradual cycle of the machine is the only dynamic. The hum may modulate, shifting its overtone by a fractional degree over the course of a generation. The great wheels may complete a single rotation in a century. Steam vents from the domes in slow, predictable plumes that rise and merge with the already saturated air. This is a place of immense, tireless, and utterly pointless activity.

It operates according to rules that feel both intentional and abandoned. The patterns seen in the water pools are not random ripples; they are cymatics, perfect, geometric forms made visible by the sound waves passing through the liquid—mandalas of vibration. The air has a metallic, clean taste, the taste of ozone and hot metal and distilled water. Everything functions. The pipes do not leak, the engines do not seize. They just run. They have been running for so long that the bedrock has been worn smooth by their acoustic pressure, and the very climate of the plateau has been engineered by their heat and exhaust. The place is not in decay. It is in a deafening, wet, lonely, and eternal stasis. It is not dead; it is simply finished with the concept of life.
