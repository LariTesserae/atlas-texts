This is the High Weald, a landscape of immense, rounded mesas and tablelands that rise into the upper atmosphere. The air here is thin but carries a surprising weight, a persistent dampness that never quite resolves into rain but leaves everything slick with a fine, cool dew. The light is the first thing one notices: brilliant, pervasive, and without an obvious source. It’s a clean, silver-white radiance that filters through the thin air, seeming to emanate from the sky itself, casting sharp, clear-edged shadows that are more grey than black.

The terrain is ancient. The great stone plateaus are weathered into soft, flowing shapes, their edges worn smooth by epochs of a wind that no longer blows. The rock is a warm, rosy terracotta, almost flesh-toned, shot through with veins of milky quartz and darker, hematite-red strata. It is warm to the touch, holding the day’s heat long after the light begins to soften. The scale is vast but comprehensible; from the edge of one mesa, you can see others across a chasm of hazy air, each a similar island of stone in the sky, perhaps a day’s journey away if one could fly. The ground is sparse, a landscape of balanced emptiness. Great stretches of bare, smooth stone are punctuated by pockets of coarse, rust-colored sand and occasional, lonely gardens of life.

The dampness is an engineered phenomenon. Growing from the stone, as if they were a natural geological formation, are scattered pylons of a glossy black material that perfectly absorbs the light. They are cool to the touch, and their surfaces are always beaded with condensation. These are the Weirs. They are silent, but for an almost imperceptible, subsonic hum that is felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. Water weeps continuously from hairline fractures in their surfaces, tracing dark, gleaming paths down to the rock below. It doesn't form rivers; instead, it pools in shallow, lens-shaped depressions worn into the stone, creating thousands of scattered mirrors that reflect the bright, empty sky. At the base of each Weir, the moisture is densest, and here the world’s sparse vegetation takes hold. It's not greenery as we know it. There are clusters of crystalline stalks that chime faintly when a breeze stirs, their facets refracting the silver light into fleeting rainbows. Low, coppery-pelted mosses carpet the damp stone, feeling like cool velvet. And in the most sheltered hollows, there are rosettes of tough, silver-blue leaves that slowly turn to follow the shifting angles of the ambient light.

Despite the scarcity of this flora, the Weald is teeming with fauna, and it is this fact that unhinges the landscape's quiet logic. The animals do not graze on the crystalline plants. They graze on the light itself. They are, for the most part, six-legged creatures, moving with a liquid, deliberate grace. Their bodies are long and sinuous, like a cross between a salamander and a gazelle. Their skin is their most remarkable feature: a seamless, iridescent integument, like polished mother-of-pearl, that modulates in color and reflectivity. They are called sun-grazers. They spend their hours in silent, slow-moving herds, positioning themselves on the warm stone to best catch the ambient glow. Their skin shimmers, cycling through hues of cyan, magenta, and gold as they metabolize the light, storing energy in biological capacitors that line their spines.

The world is profoundly quiet. The damp, thin air muffles sound. There are no bird calls, no insectile drone. The sun-grazers communicate not through voice but through slow, intricate patterns of light that ripple across their skin. A warning is a sudden flash of deep violet; a placid greeting is a slow pulse of soft green. Their footfalls are silent on the stone, their movements economical and serene.

But this is not a world without dynamism. Where there are grazers, there are predators. Hidden in the grey shadows are the hunters. They are biomechanical constructs, or perhaps a form of life that long ago merged with technology. They are things of sharp angles and matte-black carapaces that drink the light and give none of it back. They move in sudden, frictionless bursts of speed, unfolding from a state of perfect stillness that mimics the rock. A hunt is a silent, breathtaking event: a herd of sun-grazers basks, their bodies shimmering with stored power, and from a shadow a hunter launches itself, its limbs extending like camera shutters. There is no roar, no shriek of terror. Only the soft, percussive *thump* of impact, and then a single sun-grazer goes dark, its internal light extinguished as the hunter begins to drain its energy directly. The rest of the herd ripples with stark white light and flows away, their silent retreat a stark ballet against the rosy stone.

The Weirs themselves are the only remnants of the builders. They are not ruins; they are active, functioning systems. They are weathered and eroded in place, their forms as settled and ancient as the mesas they inhabit. The technology is so old it has become a part of the geology, its function seamlessly integrated into the planet's processes. Perhaps the builders are long gone. Or perhaps they simply changed, becoming the light, or the rock, or the silent, patient hunters. The rules of this place are simple: all energy flows from the light, all water from the Weirs. Life is a slow, quiet dance of absorption and transfer. The air is cool and wet, the rock is warm and dry, and under the impossibly bright, silent sky, the world turns at its own, unhurried pace.
