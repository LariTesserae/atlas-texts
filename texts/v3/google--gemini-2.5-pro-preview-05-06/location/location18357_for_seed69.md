The darkness is not an absence. It is a presence, a substance. It has weight and temperature, a texture you feel on your skin before the moisture does. The air is a warm, wet blanket, so saturated with vapor that breathing is like drinking. Droplets condense on every surface, on your eyelashes and the lobes of your ears, and run in slow, warm trickles down your neck. There is no memory of a sun here. The concept is an abstraction, a geometric myth. Light is not a missing component; it is a category of physics that does not apply.

The ground is not truly ground. It is a deep, sprung mattress of interwoven, living matter, somewhere between root and mycelium, slick with black ooze. Your boots sink an inch, six inches, a foot, the depth changing with every step. The purchase is uncertain. A low, constant heat radiates up from this living floor, a geothermal blush that fuels the entire system. Below this mat, in the unseen depths, lies the water. Not as rivers or lakes, but as a planet-spanning aquifer, a pressurized labyrinth of superheated reservoirs that find their way to the surface as steam.

The sound is immense, a layered symphony of force. The lowest note is a subsonic thrum that vibrates in your bones, a planetary heartbeat you feel in your sternum. This is the sound of the deep machinery. Above it, a constant, roaring hiss, like a million kettles at full boil, is the sound of the world's exhalation. Steam erupts from fissures in the spongy earth, pluming into the thick, dark air, condensing, and falling again as a warm, heavy rain that tastes of sulfur and metal. Water drips from everything, a constant, chaotic percussion on broad, rubbery leaves and slick, fungal plates.

This is a forest, but not of wood. The trees are vast, pale stalks of chemosynthetic fungus, some as thick as ancient redwoods. They have no bark, only a clammy, porous skin that glistens with secreted slime. They are vaguely phosphorescent, but they do not cast light. Instead, they glow from within, a morbid, bruised-purple luminescence that reveals their own internal structures—a slow, visible pulse of viscous fluids moving through translucent arteries. Their caps do not spread wide to catch a nonexistent sun, but instead curl inward, forming complex, convoluted shapes like giant brains or fists, pitted with pores that release clouds of spores in shimmering, allergenic waves. These spore-falls are the weather here, drifting for miles, creating dense, shimmering curtains that can choke and blind.

The undergrowth is a writhing, tangled chaos. Fronds of something like fern, but obsidian-black and dripping with acidic condensate. Bulging, bladder-like spheres that swell with heated gases and pop with a wet, percussive *thwump*, releasing steamy vapors that smell of ozone and rot. Ropes of thick, muscular vine snake between the fungal trunks, looping and knotting, sometimes spasming with a slow, peristaltic ripple.

And among it all, life. The sounds of fauna are a constant, frantic counterpoint to the geological and mechanical drone. There is the wet, slapping gait of heavy scavengers, things that move through the muck on multiple, splayed feet. From the fungal canopies, there are high-pitched, chittering calls, the sounds of creatures that never touch the ground, their clicks and whistles a form of echolocation in the blinding steam. A sudden, violent screech, followed by the sound of something heavy tearing through the fleshy undergrowth, indicates a hunt. Slithering things, armored things, things that burrow and things that climb; the darkness is dense with their movement, their hunger, their lives playing out at a frantic biological pace. You feel their passage as tremors in the ground-mat, as disturbances in the air, as sudden, new stenches in the chemical fug.

The built and the technological are one and the same here, and they are not subtle. Rising from the morass, half-swallowed by the fungal growths, are titanic structures of oxidized iron and weeping, black ceramic. They are not buildings in a human sense; there are no windows, no doors, no concession to habitation. They are machines. Vast atmospheric processors, their flanks slick with condensate and draped in parasitic vines, hum with a terrifying power. They draw in the steam-filled air through immense, grilled intakes, their interior turbines whining at a pitch that sets your teeth on edge. Elsewhere, piston-driven pumps, the size of small hills, pound a slow, rhythmic two-beat—*THUMP-squelch… THUMP-squelch…*—driving drills deep into the planet's crust, tapping the geothermal pressure directly.

These installations are scattered, isolated islands of industry in the teeming biological chaos. They are connected by armored pipelines that run just above the surface of the fungal mat, their surfaces hot to the touch. The metal is pitted and corroded, showing the effects of millennia of acidic rain, but the machines still run. They are relentless, automated, eternal. No one is seen maintaining them. They are a part of the landscape now, as weathered and established as any mountain range. What they are harvesting—energy, specific isotopes from the steam, rare gases—is unclear. Their purpose is lost, but their function continues. Their noise is the dominant sound of the world, a bass note against which all life here screams and thrives. This is a place that turned its face from its star long ago, and in the warm, pressurized dark, it found a new engine and a new, terrible, and vibrant way to live.
