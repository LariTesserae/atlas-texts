The place is a basin, a dish. Not a wide valley floor, but a concave depression, perfectly contained. Perhaps it was once the caldera of a dead volcano, or a crater left by an impact eons ago, but every edge has been worn smooth. The walls slope up gently, featureless and polished by an unimaginable passage of time, like a river stone the size of a theater. The floor is not earth or sand but a fine, dark grey grit, almost a powder, that shifts with a dry, sibilant sound. It is the dust of ages, the final, irreducible form of whatever rock or metal once constituted this chamber.

The air is cool, still, and utterly without moisture. It carries the sharp, electric scent of ozone and hot metal, a smell that coats the back of the throat. Above, there is no sky. The ceiling of this chamber, some hundred meters up, is a uniform, milky-white luminescence. It provides a shadowless, clinical light, constant and unblinking, like the ceiling of a vast laboratory.

And it is loud. A ceaseless, overwhelming cacophony that presses in from all sides. It is not the roar of wind or water, but the sound of a billion tiny machines in frantic, constant motion. It is a symphony of scraping, clicking, whirring, grinding, and the high-pitched whine of miniature servos. The ground itself vibrates with this activity, a low, thrumming hum that you feel in your bones.

This is the sound of the fauna. The basin is filled, nearly to overflowing, with life. But there is no flesh, no bone, no fur or feather. The life is mechanical, an ecosystem of articulated scrap. Swarms dense as locusts, composed of twitching, fist-sized things made of interlocking gears and wires, flow like a river of metal across the grit. They are the grazers. They cluster in thick, vibrating mats around the few structures that dot the basin floor.

These structures are not buildings, but ancient, hulking pieces of industrial hardware. They are the base of the food chain. Perhaps they were once fabricators or power converters, but now their function has been simplified. From stained, oozing orifices along their corroded flanks, they slowly excrete a thick, viscous, copper-colored paste. The paste barely has time to touch the ground before it is swarmed by the chittering grazers, their tiny manipulators scooping and tearing at the nutrient substance, their internal mechanisms grinding audibly as they process it. The constant hum of the basin is punctuated by the rhythmic, heavy *clank-thump* of these central protein-processors as they churn out more fuel for the system.

Above the grazers in the food chain are the hunters. They are larger, more distinct. Some scuttle on too many legs, chassis of riveted plates with lenses for eyes that glow with a predatory red light. They move with a terrifying, stuttering speed, bursting from the metallic sea to snatch a grazer, their clamp-like jaws shearing through the smaller machine with a spray of sparks and a sharp *CRUNCH*. Other predators are more subtle: serpentine chains of linked segments that burrow through the dust, their presence only given away by the disturbed grit above them before they erupt to ensnare their prey.

The entire floor is a dynamic, terrifying melee. A constant churn of predator and prey, of consumption and destruction. Discarded shells of devoured machines litter the landscape—empty casings, broken gears, tangled wires—and are slowly, inexorably ground down by the ceaseless motion, contributing their own mass to the fine dust of the basin floor. This is an ecosystem in perfect, hellish equilibrium. A closed loop where industrial producers sustain a population of mechanical consumers, who are then culled by mechanical predators, whose waste and wreckage are eventually ground back into the substrate.

There is no water. There is no plant life. There is only the cool, ozonic air, the shadowless light, the thrumming, grinding, cacophonous noise, and the overwhelming, near-touching density of a billion hungry machines living out their frantic, violent lives in a smooth, ancient, and hidden cup of dust. It is a factory that has become a world, an engine that has learned to eat itself.
