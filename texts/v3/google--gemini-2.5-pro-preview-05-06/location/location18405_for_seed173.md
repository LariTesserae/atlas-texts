This is the Grand Oscillator Hall, and it is entirely underwater. The water is not brine but a heavy, mineral-rich fluid, cool and carrying a faint metallic taste. It presses in from all sides with the patient weight of a small ocean, though the space is no larger than a cathedral's nave. Vision is limited to a few dozen feet, the rest lost in a dim, amber-tinted murk thick with slowly swirling particulates of rust and biological matter.

The world is a cage of industry, drowned and repurposed. There is no bedrock, no soil, no natural floor. The ground is a grid of iron grating, its pattern blurred by millennia of corrosion and thick, slimy biofilms. Below the grating is only deeper, blacker water, an abyss of unknown depth from which a constant, leaching cold emanates. The walls are monumental sheets of riveted iron plate alternating with sections of what was once white ceramic tile, now cracked and filmed with a greasy, dark green algae. These walls rise up into a vaulted darkness where gantries and thick bundles of cable hang like dead vines, disappearing into the gloom.

Everything is ancient, settled into its final, sagging form. This place is not eroding by wind or rain, but by a slow, chemical dissolution. Metal weeps. Long, delicate formations of rust—rusticles—hang from every girder and pipe, some reaching lengths of several feet, like iron-oxide stalactites. They are fragile, and the slightest current can cause their tips to crumble into a fine red powder that drifts away like blood in the water. Concrete pylons that support the structure are pocked and softened, their surfaces a porous habitat for boring mollusks.

The source of the Hall's life, and its overwhelming sound, is the Oscillator. In the center of the chamber, a colossal machine, a heart of brass and iron, still moves. A piston the size of a small submersible slides with immense, inexorable slowness into a housing wreathed in steam that immediately condenses in the cold water. A flywheel, twenty feet across, turns at a rate of one revolution per minute, its spoke-shadows sweeping through the hazy light. The machine is not performing its original function; it is simply running, caught in a loop for ten thousand years.

And it is loud. Sound travels with perfect, terrible clarity through the dense water. The Oscillator produces a pervasive, gut-felt drone, a low B-flat that vibrates in the bone. This constant hum is punctuated by the rhythmic, percussive *shhhhhh-KLONK* of the piston completing its cycle, a sound that echoes off the far walls and returns a half-second later, creating a disorienting, syncopated beat. Overlaying this is a constant, high-frequency *chittering*, a million tiny clicks and scrapes that come from the walls themselves.

This is the sound of the fauna. The place is teeming with it. Lacking vegetation, the base of the food web is chemotrophic. The sparse, velvety mats of verdigris and black bacteria that coat every surface feed on the minerals leached from the decaying metal. Grazing on these mats are countless billions of tiny creatures, a kind of shrimp or scraper no bigger than a fingernail, with hardened mandibles for peeling the biofilm from the iron. They swarm in glittering, fluid clouds, their collective scraping producing the hall's constant chatter.

Hunting them are fish. Pale, tube-bodied pike with jaws of translucent cartilage and needle teeth drift motionlessly in the open water, their silver scales catching the dim light. They have no eyes; they hunt by sensing the pressure waves of the Oscillator's piston and the vibrations of the swarming scrapers. When a cloud of scrapers drifts into a zone of turbulence, a pike will strike with shocking speed, a white slash in the gloom.

The rest of the space is the domain of the cleaners. Armored, crab-like organisms the color of oxidized copper, with heavy, flattened carapaces, crawl slowly over the machinery and grating. They are scavengers, their powerful claws adept at breaking open the chitin of dead scrapers and the shells of the boring mollusks. They move with a deliberate, clicking gait, their forms casting sharp-edged shadows in the few pools of light.

The only light emanates from the Oscillator itself. A few hardened, industrial lamps, caked in grime but still functioning, cast weak, amber beams. Their power seems to be drawn from the perpetual motion of the great machine. These cones of light are oases, highways, and killing grounds. The water within them is clearer, the particles illuminated like dust in a sunbeam. The scrapers swarm into the light, and the pike wait patiently at its edges.

The world here runs on this single, decaying engine. The slow turning of the flywheel creates the hall's dominant current, a gentle, clockwise vortex that stirs the water, distributes nutrients, and guides the creatures in their cycles of life and death. If the Oscillator were to finally fail, the sound would cease, the lights would go out, the current would still, and this entire, ancient, artificial ecosystem would starve in the sudden silence and dark. But for now, the beat goes on.
