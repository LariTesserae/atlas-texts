The first thing to understand is that there is no ground. There is only floor. It is a continuous, planetary-scale foundation, a pavement of interlocking basalt-colored plates that stretches beyond every horizon. The plates are worn smooth by ages of friction and flow, their surfaces perpetually slick with a film of warm, clean water. This is not a world of soil and rock, but of substrate and structure.

The air is thick, warm, and heavy, like the inside of a lung. It is saturated with moisture, a perpetual haze that coalesces on every surface. Water does not rain from a distant sky; it materializes from the atmosphere itself. Droplets form on the towering walls of the city-structure, gather into trickles, and then into cascading falls that plummet for kilometers into the chasms below. The source of the light is a normal, sun-like star, but its light is a suggestion, an ambient glow that filters down through uncountable layers of architecture, steam, and superstructure. Direct sunlight is a myth, a legend of the uppermost levels. Here, in the low-lying basin of the world, light is a diffuse, pearlescent quality of the air itself, catching on the wet surfaces and turning the world into a hall of muted, endless reflections.

And the sound. The sound is a physical presence. It is a deafening, complex roar that vibrates up through the soles of the feet, through the bones of the skull. It is not one sound but a million, woven into a solid wall of noise. There is the deep, tectonic hum of continent-sized power systems operating in the substructure. There is the percussive, deafening crash of matter-printers and assemblers, a sound like mountains being born and ground to dust every few seconds. There is the high-frequency hiss of superheated steam venting from colossal pipes, the shriek of metal under unimaginable stress, and the constant, Niagara-like thunder of water falling through the canyons of the city. There is no silence, not even a moment of relative quiet. The noise is the world's heartbeat.

There is no vegetation. Not a single leaf, not a blade of grass, not a smear of moss. The world is entirely mineral, metallic, ceramic. But it is not dead. The ecology is one of construction. The place is a city so vast and so old it has become its own geology. Its "erosion" is the slow, grinding decay of lower, ancient levels, crushed under the weight of new construction. Its "terrain" is the urban landscape. Wide, river-like avenues give way to dense warrens of canyons and corridors. Towers so immense their peaks are lost in the atmospheric haze are built, crumble, and are consumed by the city itself, their materials broken down and re-purposed for new structures.

This is the source of the turbulence. The world is alive with chaotic, ceaseless change. The city builds itself. Walls will dissolve into shimmering clouds of nanites, only to recrystallize moments later into a different configuration. A bridge might unfurl across a chasm like a fern frond, exist for an hour, and then retract. The very floor plates underfoot can shift, separate, and rise to form new barriers or descend to open new passages. To navigate this place is to read a transient, hyperactive language of architecture. Pathways are not permanent. Shelter is temporary. The landscape is a storm of creation and dissolution.

Out of this chaos emerges a fauna that is inseparable from the technology. Swarms of "glimmer-moths," which are not moths at all but thumbnail-sized flakes of coherent light, flutter through the canyons, acting as mobile data nodes. They are prey for "scrapers," meter-long, multi-limbed constructs of chrome and sinew that skitter up vertical surfaces, their diamond-tipped claws etching informational glyphs into the walls before they are erased by the next reconfiguration. In the deepest coolant rivers and reservoirs swim "conduit-worms," semi-sentient, self-repairing plumbing systems that writhe through the city's arteries, cleaning blockages and reinforcing weak points. Larger things move in the deep chasms, their calls adding to the cacophony—vast, slow-moving entities that seem to be part janitor, part scavenger, part mobile factory, their forms shifting as they consume derelict structures and excrete raw materials for new growth. Nothing here evolved from biology as we know it; it was all designed, and has since been left to run, to adapt, to prey upon itself.

The rules of this world are simple and brutal. Do not stand still for too long, for the ground beneath you may be re-purposed. Do not rely on a pathway, for it will inevitably change. The water is safe to drink, filtered to absolute purity. The air is breathable, if oppressively humid. The fauna will ignore you unless you interfere with its function. The city has no malice; it simply is. It is an engine of becoming, a process of existence so densely packed and operating at such a vast scale that it has no room or reason to acknowledge anything as small as a single life. It is established in its eternal state of flux, perfectly in balance with its own unending storm. Standing within it, you are a ghost, a momentary anomaly in the deafening, turbulent, and utterly indifferent machine.
