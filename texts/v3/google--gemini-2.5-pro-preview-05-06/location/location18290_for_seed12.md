There is no surface. There is only a higher, thinner gloom and a lower, denser gloom. You are suspended in the warm, thick liquid that is the sky and the sea all at once. It does not feel like water; it is heavier, more viscous, with a faintly sweet, mineral taste on the tongue, should you be foolish enough to expose it. The pressure is immense, a constant, firm embrace that calms the heart to a slow, syrupy beat. This is the great, high-altitude ocean of a world that has no land.

You are in the Precipice, a mountain range that is not made of stone but is in the process of becoming it. These are structures of emergence, raw geology in the act of self-creation. Towering, skeletal spires of black, crystalline material grow from an unseen nadir, reaching up into the less-dense strata of the liquid atmosphere. They are not eroding; they are accreting. Mineral rain, a slow and constant drift of microscopic particles, coalesces onto these lattices, thickening them over millennia. The surfaces are rough, fractal, covered in sharp, new-born edges and geometric growths that glitter faintly in the perpetual twilight. It is a landscape under construction, and the construction is a geological process.

Clinging to these nascent peaks is a jungle. With no sun, life here has made its own. Vast, cascading curtains of what might be called kelp, but are thick as leather and the color of bruises, hang in the abyssal valleys between the spires. Their broad ribbons are stitched with constellations of tiny, pulsing photophores, casting a shifting, dappled light on the scenes below. Fleshy, anemone-like clusters, some as large as houses, wave gently in the slow currents, their tendrils glowing with a soft, peach-colored luminescence. Carpets of phosphorescent velvet, a life form somewhere between moss and fungus, coat the growing rock in patches, tracing the flow of nutrients in the warm, soupy medium. The air, which is the water, is thick with the scent of damp soil and blooming night-flowers.

Woven into this living, growing architecture is a city. Oall. It is not built *on* the spires, but *with* them. The inhabitants—slow, buoyant beings of sinew and cartilage—are gardeners of stone. Using tools of shell and hardened fiber, they guide the mineral accretion, creating hollows, chambers, and passageways within the growing rock itself. Their dwellings are globular pods woven from the tough, luminous kelp-fronds, tethered by living vines to the more stable parts of the spires. Scaffolding, made not of wood but of the interlocked carapaces of some long-dead creature, creates walkways that span the chasms, each one glowing with a gentle, internal light. It is a city that breathes and grows with the mountain, a reef of thought and intention. The density is high; homes are layered one upon another, woven into the thick vegetation, near-touching, creating canyons of dim light and organic architecture.

The world is mostly quiet. Sound does not travel far, muffled by the dense liquid. There is the low, pervasive hum of the world's own energy, a bass note felt in the bones. There is the soft, wet creak of the growing spires, a sound so slow it is almost imperceptible. There is the gentle slosh and whisper of currents moving through the kelp forests and the woven city walls. Loud noises are rare and startling; a dropped tool echoes dully for a moment before being swallowed by the quiet.

Life, aside from the plant-like growths and the city's builders, is rare. The ecology is not complex. Weeks can pass without seeing another creature. But when one appears, it is an event. Drifting up from the crushing blackness below comes a leviathan: a creature of cartilage and gas, half a kilometer long, its body a collection of vast, translucent bladders that alternately inflate and deflate, propelling it with geological slowness. Its skin is a canvas for symbiotic bioluminescent bacteria that shimmer in flowing blues and greens, momentarily illuminating the entire valley as it passes. It does not hunt; it filters, its immense, baleen-like mouth agape, consuming the free-floating mineral dust and plankton that form the base of this whole ecosystem. The city of Oall holds its breath as the god-beast glides past, its shadow a slow-moving eclipse in the dim world.

The fundamental rule here is patience. Everything is slow. Change is measured in the thickening of a wall, the unfurling of a new frond, the gradual climb of a spire towards a sky it will never reach. There is no day or night, only the slow pulsing of the world's internal light. There is no weather, only the subtle shift of currents. To live here is to accept a different kind of time, to feel your own life as a brief, warm current in a vast, cold, and patient ocean. It is a world suspended, in every sense: in liquid, in time, and in a state of perpetual, quiet becoming.
