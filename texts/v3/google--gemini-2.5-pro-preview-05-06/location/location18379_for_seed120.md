This place is a chamber, a pocket in the sky. You are inside it, with no memory of entry. The air is a broth, thick and hot enough to drink, smelling of wet clay, ozone, and something like fermenting honey. It is an air that requires effort to breathe, pressing in on all sides, slicking your skin with a perpetual film of condensation.

The world here is a single, continuous object. There is no distinction between floor, wall, and ceiling; they are all one substance, curving up and over in a space no larger than a generous room. The material is a kind of living ceramic, a porous, unglazed earthenware in shades of terracotta and charcoal, all of it warm to the touch. It feels both mineral and organic, like the inside of a bone that is still growing. This is a place under construction, not by workers, but by its own nature. From millions of tiny pores across the curving surfaces, filaments of this ceramic substance are being extruded, slowly, constantly. They emerge hot and soft as wax, glowing with a faint, cherry-red internal heat before cooling to the ambient temperature.

This constant, infinitesimal growth is the source of the noise. The chamber is never silent. It is filled with a loud, pervasive, polyphonic sound: a high, crystalline chiming as trillions of new filaments lock into place and cool; a low, granular grinding as vast sections of the structure settle and shift under their own accumulating weight; and a constant, wet, hissing sigh as the hot, saturated air condenses on every available surface.

Water is not a feature *in* the landscape; it is an expression *of* it. The atmosphere is so burdened with moisture that the interior surfaces weep constantly. Rivulets of clean, warm water trace fugitive paths down the curved walls, catching the dim light. They follow the grooves between the cooling ceramic extrusions, pooling in shallow depressions on the floor before being absorbed back into the porous material, destined to be steamed out again in a closed, eternal cycle.

Because of this constant dampness and heat, life has found a foothold—or rather, it has been given one. There is no soil. Fleshy, liver-colored ferns with fronds like broad, veiny tongues erupt directly from the ceramic itself, their roots integral to the structure. Vines as thick as a wrist, coated in a shimmering, moss-like algae, hang in dense curtains from the ceiling. They drip a viscous, sweet-smelling oil that mixes with the water on the floor, creating iridescent slicks. The vegetation is so densely packed that it forms a claustrophobic thicket. Reaching out your hand in any direction means brushing against the damp, rubbery leaf of one plant, the rough bark of another, the slick surface of the weeping wall. It is a room saturated not just with humidity, but with presence. Light is a dim, greenish memory, filtered through this suffocating canopy.

This place exists at an extreme altitude, but there are no windows to prove it. The only suggestion of the outside is in the nature of reality itself. Occasionally, a section of the wall will grow thin, becoming momentarily translucent. Through this fleeting membrane, you don't see mountains or clouds, but a swirling, pearlescent void, a non-space that churns with colours for which there are no names. The sight induces a profound vertigo, a feeling that this entire chamber is a stray bubble of possibility, floating unanchored in an abyss. Then the wall thickens again, the filaments weaving a fresh patch of solid ground over the terrifying truth, and the chamber is once again a closed, self-sufficient world.

Fauna is exceptionally rare, and part of the same system. You may go for a long time seeing nothing, and then, a section of the "floor" detaches itself. It is a slow, armored thing, the size of a dinner plate, its shell made of the very same living ceramic as the walls. It moves on a dozen short, chitinous legs, scuttling with a dry clicking sound that momentarily cuts through the ambient grind. These are the tenders, or perhaps the antibodies, of the chamber. They are not animals in a separate kingdom of life; they are mobile organs of the whole. They seem to graze on imperfections—a misaligned crystal, a patch of errant mold—before re-affixing themselves to the wall and becoming indistinguishable from the substrate once more. Their existence is a simple, programmed craft: maintain the form, prune the errors.

The rules of this world are few and absolute. The chamber grows. The chamber sweats. The chamber lives. The process is dynamic, a geological metabolism running at the speed of biology. Patches of wall can suddenly heat up, the extrusion process accelerating into a furious, hissing creep. A hanging vine might shudder and drop a heavy, spore-filled pod that splits with a wet pop, releasing a sweet, cloying scent that hangs in the air for hours. The whole structure groans and vibrates, a living thing in a constant state of becoming, a piece of primitive, total craft designed by no mind, a kiln birthing itself from its own clay, forever suspended in a hot, dim, and impossible height.
