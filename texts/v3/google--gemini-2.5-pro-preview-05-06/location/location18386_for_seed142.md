The ground is warm.

It is not the warmth of a sun-baked stone, but a deep, internal heat that radiates upward, felt through the soles of your boots. The surface is a fine, dark powder, soft as velvet and utterly dry, the final residue of rock and metal and ceramic ground down over a span of time that defies geology. It is the dust of a concluded universe. Kicking it raises a brief, puffball cloud that hangs in the still air for an impossibly long time before settling with glacial slowness, each mote returning to its ordained place. There is no wind here. There has not been wind for an age.

This is a plateau, a great tabletop of a world hoisted into a perpetual, starless night. The darkness is not merely an absence of light; it is a presence, a heavy, sound-dampening blanket of inert gas that fills the void. Far below the edges of this high place, there is only more of the same blackness, a chasm without a known bottom. The scale is immense, a plain of settled dust punctuated by forms that were once a city.

They are not ruins in the conventional sense. There are no shattered walls or broken columns. The erosion here was a patient, relentless force, a slow solvent working over eons. The buildings have been worn down to their most stable shapes: vast, whale-backed mounds, sweeping ramps that curve for kilometers, and broad, circular platforms like mesas of fused metal. The original architecture is a ghost, a suggestion in the smooth, continuous lines. It is an urban landscape melted into geomorphology. The spaces between these hulks are vast, kilometers of the warm, soft dust stretching from one form to the next. In the crushing dark, the city feels both empty and full, a sparse archipelago of presence in an ocean of nothing.

Faint light exists, but it does not illuminate. It only marks. Pinned to the flanks of the great, slumped structures are tiny, impossibly distant points of ruby and amber. They are not lamps for seeing, but the pilot lights of a city-machine that has not known its purpose for longer than life has existed on other worlds. These lights are the steady heartbeats of forgotten engines, eternal and unchanging. They cast no glow, create no dawn. They are simply there, a declaration of function without effect.

This is the source of the warmth. Deep within the eroded mountains of metal and slag, colossal machines are running. They are not manufacturing anything. They are not processing materials. Their sole, remaining function is to generate the heat and the faint, internal power that keeps their own pilot lights burning. It is a closed, perfect loop of industrial self-preservation. The city powers itself to continue powering itself.

Sound is a rare commodity. The overwhelming sensation is a sub-bass thrum that is felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. It is the blended hum of a trillion unseen bearings, the aggregate sigh of cooling systems venting placidly into the heavy atmosphere, the resonance of a continent-sized engine in perfect, frictionless equilibrium. Sometimes, a sound will break from this foundational silence: a high-pitched, crystalline *tink* as a flake of material, stressed for a million years, finally loses its purchase and slides a few centimeters down a smooth slope. Or a low, resonant groan, like a settling ship, from the thermal expansion of a metal plate a kilometer thick. These noises are events, shocking in their brief clarity, before they are swallowed again by the immense, quiet hum.

There is life here, of a sort. It is vanishingly rare, and utterly alien. Sometimes, clinging to the warm, vertical faces of the machine-hills, a Vitreous Skitter can be seen. It is a creature of fused silica and metal, resembling a complex geodesic dome the size of a human hand. It has no discernible head or limbs, but moves with a slow, deliberate crawl, its crystalline legs making microscopic adjustments. The Skitters seem to feed on something—a subtle variance in the heat, a specific harmonic in the structure's thrum, or perhaps some mineral efflorescence too subtle for other senses to detect. They are as ancient and unchanging as the city itself, a form of life that has evolved to be a static component of a static system. When one moves, it sometimes makes a noise: a dry, scraping sound, like glass dragging across sand. It is the only sound of life you will ever hear.

Nothing ever changes here. The dust does not stir. The machines do not falter. The temperature does not vary by a single degree. The Skitters complete their billion-year feeding cycles. There is no weather, no day or night, no seasons. The city is not dead, nor is it alive. It has simply… finished. It reached a state of such profound stability that it excised itself from the flow of time. It is a monument to a forgotten conclusion, a self-repairing tomb whose occupants and builders have themselves become the dust that carpets the floor. It is a place waiting for nothing at all.
