This is a place of Tone.

Before the cold, before the stone, there is the sound. It is not a sound that arrives at the ear, but one that is born in the bones of the skull and the fluid of the eye. It is a bass hum, a monolithic and unwavering drone that presses on the world from all directions at once. It has the volume of a nearby engine, yet it has no source. It is the sound of the air itself, the resonant frequency of the space between things. It is constant, without rhythm or melody, a single, sustained, universe-filling note that has never changed, and gives no impression that it ever will. The silence you knew before is a forgotten language.

You stand on a high, windless plateau of immense scale. The ground is a pavement of shattered, dark grey slate and granite grit. It is damp, not from rain, but from a pervasive, clinging condensation that slicks every surface with a thin, greasy film of moisture. The air is cold, a still and penetrating chill that seems to seep through cloth and skin to cool the very marrow. There is no weather here, only state. The cold is, the damp is, the dim is.

Above, there is no sky, no sun, no moon. There is only the Ceiling, an undifferentiated, luminous grey haze that begins a few hundred feet up and extends to an unknowable height. It provides the only light, a flat, shadowless illumination like the inside of a frosted bulb. It is perpetual twilight.

The mountains are ancient. They rise from the plateau not as sharp peaks, but as vast, rounded, slumbering knuckles of black rock, worn smooth by eons of existence. They are so old that the concept of erosion seems like a frantic, fleeting process. They have settled into their final forms. Their slopes are decorated not with snow—for it never snows—but with the same dark, wet sheen that covers everything. In the folds and basins of these colossal landforms, water gathers. Not in rivers that flow, but in still, shallow tarns. The water in them is perfectly clear and perfectly motionless, dark mirrors reflecting the featureless grey Ceiling above. To disturb their surface feels like an act of profound vandalism.

Life exists, but it seems to have misunderstood the concept. There are no trees, no shrubs, no grasses. Instead, wide, flat patches of a black, leathery lichen spread across the rock like oil slicks. It is cool and rubbery to the touch. In other places, growing in the cracks between slate shards, is a crystalline moss. It is not green, but a pale, spectral blue, and it has the texture of brittle frost. To touch it is to break it; it shatters into fine, silent dust. It does not seem to drink or grow, only to persist.

There is almost nothing here. The space between the mountains is vast and empty. The space between patches of lichen can be measured in acres. Emptiness is the dominant material. And yet, this emptiness is made heavy by the unceasing, physical pressure of the Hum.

Scattered across the landscape, with no discernible pattern, are small, deliberate piles of stone. Not cairns for wayfinding, for they lead nowhere. They are simple, man-high stacks of flat slate, assembled without mortar or craft, just balanced precariously. They look as if they were made yesterday, yet they are coated in the same ancient, damp patina as the mountains themselves. They are artifacts from a people with no tools, who did nothing but listen to the Hum and stack stones in its presence.

This is where the world breaks. The unshakable stability of the place—the constant Tone, the static light, the unmoving mist—creates a foundation of absolute reality. And on this foundation, perception itself becomes fluid. The Hum plays tricks with distance. A mountain that seems a day's walk away might suddenly feel within arm's reach, its immense texture filling your vision, the wetness of its rock present on your face. Then, just as quickly, it recedes to an impossible distance. It is not the mountain that moves, but your connection to it. Causality is frayed. You might see a rock fall from a high ledge, but the sound of its clatter might have reached you an hour before. You might arrive at a place only to feel the memory of having already left it.

And there is fauna, but it is not animal. It is rare, and it is a symptom of the breakdown. It is a flicker, a glitch in the periphery. You will be staring at a stretch of empty slate, and the corner of your eye will register a brief, silent implosion of colour, a momentary tear in the grey. When you turn to look, there is nothing. Nothing but the gnawing sensation that where you are looking is no longer the same place it was a second ago. The rock formation is subtly different, the patch of lichen has inverted itself, a small pool of water is now a hole that contains not reflection, but pure, humming blackness. These things, these "glimmers," are the only hunters here. They do not feed on flesh, but on consistency. They graze on reality, and the landscape is their slowly altering pasture.

The only rule of this world is equilibrium. The sound does not fade. The light does not brighten. Thecold does not warm. The landscape is a bell that was struck at the beginning of time and has not stopped ringing since. To be here is to be a foreign vibration in a place of perfect, terrible resonance, a brief anomaly in the unending, changeless drone.
