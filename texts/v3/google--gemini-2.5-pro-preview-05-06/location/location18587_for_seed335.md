You hear the place long before you see it. It is not the sound of nature, not the roar of a waterfall or the whisper of wind. It is a deep, rhythmic, and complex grinding, the sound of immense weight being inexpertly shifted. It is the groan of metal on metal, the percussive clang of a colossal forge, the high-pressure hiss of a thousand failed gaskets. The sound is so industrial, so artificial, that finding its source to be a simple cleft in the earth feels like a category error.

The entrance is a wound in the low, rolling moorland, a sinkhole that collapsed into a deeper truth. The air that breathes out of it is cold and heavy with the smell of wet stone and ozone. As you descend, the world above—its thin grasses and open sky—vanishes. You are entering a room, a hidden chamber of the world no larger than a small cathedral's nave.

This is a place of profound and perpetual dampness. It is not raining; rather, the very air is supersaturated, and water chooses to be liquid on every available surface. The walls, which look like vertically-fractured basalt, are black and glassy, weeping endlessly. Fat, cold droplets crawl down their faces, merging into rivulets that chatter and splash onto the floor. The atmosphere is a visible, swirling mist, thick enough to muffle sight but not the colossal sound.

The floor is a chaos of sharp, freshly-broken rock. Shards of obsidian-like material and duller, grey stone are piled in heaps, their edges keen and unworn, as if the ground had been shattered only moments ago. There are no gentle slopes here, only precarious angles and deep clefts between the piles of geologic debris. Walking is a matter of careful, deliberate placement, as every surface is slick with a film of mineral-rich water.

And at the center of the chamber is the engine. It is not built. It is *geological*. Three immense, flat-topped pillars of stone, perfectly circular, rise from a slurry-filled chasm in the floor. They are not static. With a deafening groan and a shudder that you feel in your teeth, one pillar will slowly, unstoppably rise. Water and rock-paste cascade from its sides. It reaches a height of perhaps thirty feet, groaning under a pressure that is hard to comprehend, before it stops. For a moment, there is a dreadful tension. Then, with a scream of depressurization and a cannon-crack of impact, a different pillar slams down into the chasm, sending a percussive shockwave through the room and a plume of superheated steam hissing toward the ceiling. The pillars operate in a slow, asynchronous rhythm—a three-stroke engine of planetary scale, grinding the deep rock into slurry, venting geothermal heat into the cold air. This is the source of the noise, the source of the steam, the source of the shattered floor. It is a factory without foremen, a machine without purpose.

Life here is tenacious and strange. In the spaces between the central pistons and the weeping walls, the chamber is not empty, but lush. The vegetation is not soft. Thick, felt-like mats of near-black moss carpet the more stable rock piles, looking like sheared velvet and feeling like a cold, saturated sponge. They drink the mineral spray and the condensing mist. Leathery, blue-green ferns, with a metallic sheen on their fronds, grow in dense clusters, their shapes compact and tough to resist the constant vibration. There are no trees, only these low, dense colonies of life that cling to the surfaces.

The dim light comes not from above, but from within. Seams of a pale, phosphorescent mineral, like frozen lightning, run through the basalt walls, glowing with a soft, cyan light, perhaps excited by the immense pressures and energies of the engine. This is supplemented by the fauna. Clouds of tiny, winged insects, like phosphorescent gnats, swarm in the calmer pockets of air, their collective glimmering creating slow-drifting sheets of light.

And the fauna is abundant, if secretive. The primary inhabitant of the floor is a creature like a flattened, multi-legged trilobite, its shell a mosaic of the same crystalline rock it crawls upon. They are scavengers, scraping the mineral-rich film from the stones with hardened mouthparts. In the stiller pools of condensate, blind, pearl-white salamanders swim languidly. The noise and vibration are a constant, a medium through which everything moves. Quick, spider-like hunters, the color of wet shadow, use the percussive slams of the stone pistons to mask their final lunge toward a cloud of light-gnats. The rule of this world is rhythm. Life has adapted to the engine's cycle. During the most violent phases of grinding and impact, creatures freeze or retreat into crevices. In the brief, relative quiet as one piston settles and another begins its slow rise, there is a frantic explosion of activity—a hunt, a feeding, a hurried scuttling from one patch of moss to another—before the next cataclysmic slam brings a return to caution.

The overwhelming sense of the place is one of contradiction. It is a single, intimate room that feels as vast and powerful as a mountain range. It is deafeningly loud, yet feels hollow, the great volume of the chamber filled only with mist and echo. It is lush with life, yet the main presences—the grinding pillars—are utterly sterile stone. It is a place of raw, un-eroded geology that behaves like a finished piece of industrial machinery, a natural process that has, by sheer chance, replicated the sounds and motions of a factory. It is untouched, but not peaceful. It is simply working.
