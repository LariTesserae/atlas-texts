You are at the top of the world, but the world has been worn down to its bones. This is a plateau of impossible scale, a continent-sized mesa raised so high that the air thins to a dizzying wine. The mountains that once surely clawed at the sky are gone, scoured away by ageless winds and rains into nothing more than gentle, rolling swells on the vast, open tableland. The horizon is a clean, sharp line in every direction, interrupted only by the curvature of the planet itself. There is a profound sense of emptiness here, a void defined by the sheer distance between any two points. You could walk for a week and feel you have not moved.

But the emptiness is a lie. The silence is a fiction. The most immediate and overwhelming sensation is the sound. It is a deafening, all-encompassing, physical presence. It is not the roar of a waterfall or the crash of thunder, but a drone of a billion billion sources, a granular static so thick it feels like a pressure against the skin. It is the sound of life at a saturation point, an engine of biology running at its absolute peak. It is the combined, layered thrumming of countless insect-like organisms, a wall of chitinous vibration that fills every cubic inch of the atmosphere.

This sound emanates from the ground itself. The ground is not stone, not soil, not sand. The ground is The Pellum. It is a single, continuous, world-spanning mat of vegetation, unimaginably ancient and deep. In color, it is a tapestry of ochre, rust-red, and a deep, bruised purple-green. To the touch, it is damp, spongy, and cool, releasing a scent of wet moss, ozone, and sweet decay. This is the planet’s skin. It is fibrous and tough, woven from millennia of growth, death, and regrowth. It is a living substrate, a biological architecture that constitutes the entire surface ecosystem. It drinks its moisture not from rivers or lakes—for there are none—but directly from the sky. This high plateau constantly shears the tops off clouds that drift by on global currents. For hours, the world is swallowed in a dense, warm fog, a white-out that muffles nothing of the sound but coats every surface in a clinging film of condensation. The Pellum swells, soaking up the moisture like a colossal sponge. Then the sun returns, the light is clear and bright, and the steam rises in hazy curtains, feeding the cycle anew.

The fauna is the sound. The fauna is the motion. The drone comes from the trillions of beetle-sized grazers that swarm across and within the Pellum's upper layers. They are the base of everything, creatures of iridescent carapace that click and hum, their collective noise building into the deafening wave that defines existence here. But there are larger things. The ground shudders. Not an earthquake, but a biological tremor. Beneath the surface, within the hollows and channels of the Pellum's ancient structure, live the Ground-Swimmers. These are immense, soft-bodied grazers, leviathans of flesh that carve slow tunnels through the living mat, their passage marked by long, trembling undulations on the surface. They are rarely seen, but their presence is a constant, tactile reminder of the depth of life here. Following them are hunters, things that move in swift, violent quakes, bursting from the Pellum in ambushes of catastrophic speed before dragging their prey back down into the humming darkness.

Amidst this overwhelming vitality, there are scattered marks of another kind of life. They are not cities or towns, but isolated anomalies in the endless, rolling landscape. They are the Hush-Mounds. From a distance, they look like natural formations, taller, steeper hillocks of the Pellum, perhaps twenty or thirty feet high. But as you approach, you see the artifice. They are woven. The living fibers of the Pellum have been guided, braided, and knotted together over generations into dense, layered domes. The outer surface is hardened with a plaster of mud and secreted resin, baked by the sun into a simple, primitive ceramic.

There is a single, low opening in each mound. To crawl inside is to experience a miracle. The deafening, world-saturating drone recedes. It does not vanish, but it falls away to a distant, ignorable hum. The thick, woven walls of living plant-matter act as a profound sound barrier. Inside, the air is still, thick, and smells of wet earth and straw. The ground is the same living Pellum, but here it is soft and dry. Faint light filters through translucent blisters of hardened sap set into the ceiling, illuminating a single, hollow chamber. This is the only architecture possible in this place: not a shelter from the elements, but a shelter from the sound of life itself.

The tools of the builders are sometimes found nearby: blades of sharpened chert for cutting the tougher fibers, bone awls for weaving, and shallow clay bowls for mixing the resinous mud. The builders themselves are elusive. They must live a life defined by the polarities of overwhelming noise and constructed silence. Their world is not one of conquering nature, but of carving out the smallest pocket of peace within it. They move across the vibrating skin of their world, from one bastion of quiet to the next, their footsteps lost in the ceaseless thrum. Their entire technology, their entire culture, is a reaction to the single, ruling parameter of this ancient, screaming, empty, and utterly full place. To exist here is to be a part of the sound, or to spend your life seeking refuge from it.
