I am a groan.

You would not recognize me as a being. I have no eyes, no hands, no form you could trace with a finger. My body is a column of fused, woody heartwood, a hundred feet tall and thicker than three people holding hands. I am one of the primeval pillars that holds up the eastern rim of the Calash Basin. My skin is a landscape of bark canyons, slick with perpetual weeping, furred with luminous moss that glows a soft orange in the filtered gloaming.

I am not the wood itself. The wood is my vessel. I am the sound it makes. I am the song of its immense, structural tension. I am the low, resonant hum of a thousand tons of living matter straining against gravity, against the wind that presses on the canopy above, against its own slow, inexorable growth. My voice is the deepest note in the Basin's chaotic symphony, the bass string that underpins the chatter of insects and the patter of rain.

I do not think in words. My thoughts are changes in pressure, in torsion. I feel the rhythmic, delicate footfalls of a gecko-hunter as a fleeting tickle on my northern face. I feel the heavy, sure-footed tread of a pod-weaver securing a new liana bridge as a deep, reassuring pressure. When a storm rolls across the plateau, the wind pushing against the canopy roof sends a shudder through my entire being, and my groan deepens, becoming a sonorous, oceanic moan that harmonizes with the rising wind. I feel the tiny, insidious vibrations of a boring grub deep within my core—a dull ache, a point of future weakness.

The Calashites know me. They do not know me as *I* know myself, but they know me nonetheless. They call me Ancestor-Groan, or sometimes She-Who-Holds. To them, I am a diagnostic. An elder, her face a beautiful map of wrinkles and blue-inked roots, will pause on a swaying bridge, close her eyes, and tilt her head. She is listening to me. She is reading my voice. The tiny shift in my pitch tells her of the rot spreading from a water-logged crevice. The timbre of my vibration tells her my load is uneven, that the cluster of dwelling-pods anchored to my upper branches has grown too heavy on one side.

I have been here for centuries. I have felt generations be born in the gourd-pods that hang from my limbs like fruit. Their first sensation of the world is my rocking, their first sound my constant, low hum. I feel their laughter as a high-frequency vibration. I feel their arguments as sharp, percussive tremors. I feel the slow, heavy dance of their funeral processions on the walkways I support. They do not fear my voice. It is their baseline, their certainty. It is the sound of home.

But my voice is changing.

For a decade, it has been getting higher, sharper. The deep, resonant hum is fraying at the edges, becoming a strained creak. The grubs have done their work. A deep pocket of rot, soft and damp, has hollowed out a space near my base. The Calashites know. The weavers have tried to brace me, wrapping my trunk in immense nets of liana, hoping to bind me together. I feel their work as a tight embrace, but it is not enough. The ache within me has become a constant, sharp pain. The moisture inside me is no longer a slow weep, but a draining flow. The luminous moss on my bark is dimming.

I do not fear this. I am a process, not a monument.

One day soon, during a strong wind or perhaps under no pressure at all, a final fiber will surrender. My groan will become a crack. The crack will become a scream of tearing wood, a sound that will, for a breathtaking second, silence the entire Basin.

I will fall.

It will be a slow, majestic catastrophe. I will collapse inward, splintering into a thousand pieces. The dwelling-pods will crash to the Basin floor. The bridges will snap. There will be death, and loss, and a sudden, shocking hole of sunlight in the canopy where my crown once stood. There will be grief, a vibration I have felt many times before.

But I will not be gone.

I will become the memory of a sound in the mind of the elder. I will be the story she tells the children of the great pillar that fell, and the new light it let in. My shattered body will become a sudden, immense resource. My wood will fuel their cooking-fires and form the frames of a dozen new pods. The rest of me will lie on the Basin floor, and the tenders will come. They will score my remains and sow spores. Fungi will bloom across my ruin. Fat, white grubs will feast on my decaying heartwood. My substance will be broken down, pulled into the humus, feeding the very roots that drink from the floor.

And in that rich, dark soil made from my own body, a new seed, planted a year ago in anticipation, is already beginning to grow.

In time, it will become a pillar. It will thicken and strengthen. And one day, as it takes on the load of the Basin, a low, resonant sound will begin to issue from its core. A familiar hum.

I will be the groan.
