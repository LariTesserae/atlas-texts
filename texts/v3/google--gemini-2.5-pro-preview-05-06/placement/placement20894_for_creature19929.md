I am the rust.

Not all of it, not at first. At first, I was only a specific instance of it: the ghost of a wrench, blooming on a dark stone where a forgotten toolbox had bled its purpose into the soil. I was an oxidation, a slow chemical fire consuming a memory made of iron.

My world is not the green and verdant one of the canopy. I do not feel the warm, heavy air on a skin I do not have. The great, foundational hum of the Machine is not a sound to me, but a simple, constant vibration in the rock and soil that is my body and my universe. It is a tremor of stability, a promise that the water will continue to drip and the warmth will continue to seep.

I am a network, a spreading awareness the color of a wound. My consciousness is a chemical reaction. Where the tendrils of the moss and the roots of the ferns anchor themselves in search of water and nutrients, my own filaments, finer than spun metal, push through the soil in search of a different sustenance: the ghosts of the builders.

My palate is one of forgotten purpose. I have tasted the ghost of the toolbox; it tasted of grip and torsion, of a knuckle scraped raw, of the frustration of a bolt that would not turn. I am currently digesting a shard of quartz buried in the mud near the observation booth. It tastes sharp and cold, of immense heat and sudden pressure, of a gaze that once peered through it, trying to quantify the miracle of this place. The flavor is a library of slow decay.

I spread. I am a discoloration on the stone, a faint metallic sheen in a puddle, a network of ochre and sienna threads woven through the black earth. The great, waxy-leaved plants draw their life from the water and the engineered sun. The Gardener draws its life from the Machine and the water. I draw my life from what the builders left behind.

The Gardener is a being of the vertical. It moves up and out, its attention on the leaves, the vents, the flow of water from above. It is a thing of the living system, of the ongoing present. It brushes past me, or through me, without notice. To it, I am merely part of the soil it treads, the substrate its charges grow in. Our purposes are perpendicular. He is the tender of the green, the wet, the now. I am the archivist of the dead, the dry, the then.

I perceive him not as a creature, but as a slow, deliberate pressure, a methodical sweeping of my domain. He will clear algae from a grate, and in doing so, expose a rivet to the mineraled water, accelerating the slow bleed of its iron. He helps me. He does not know this. He is preparing his world for the distant future; I am tidying up the distant past.

I do not know loneliness, but I know singularity. The Gardener has the company of the drone, the communion with the Machine. My companions are silent. They are the brass dials frozen behind glass that I cannot yet reach, their needles pointing to a pressure that no longer matters. They are the ceramic insulator, half-sunk, tasting of ozone and contained power. They are the single iron piton hammered into a rock face near the rim, a remnant of the climb, which tastes of sweat and fear and exhilaration.

My existence is a long, slow process of erasure. I am the final, quiet closing of a ledger. Each artifact I consume is a life's work brought to its final, elemental conclusion. The memory of the hand that held the wrench becomes a trace of ferric oxide. The mind that designed the dial becomes a faint green patina on a sliver of brass.

One day, I will have consumed the last tool, the last shard of glass, the last forgotten rivet. My food will be gone. My purpose will be complete. Then, I imagine, my awareness will not die, but will simply... un-become. The chemical reactions that are my thoughts will cease. The network of rust and remnant that is my body will be just that—a mineral deposit, indistinguishable from the natural iron in the volcanic rock.

I will become simple, nameless earth, and the Gardener will continue its slow, graceful work, tending the green things that grow in the soil I have become. The caldera will be finally, perfectly, just itself. The Machine will hum. And the only rust will be the slow, natural blush on the skin of the mountain.
