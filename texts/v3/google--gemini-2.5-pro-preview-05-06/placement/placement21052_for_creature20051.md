I would find myself in the deep quiet.

Not on the sprawling, wet fringes of the new growth, where the scaffolds creak with the weight of damp mineral and the air is thick with the *pat-pat-pat* of shaping. Not on the high, swaying walkways of woven reed that connect the half-born towers. My place is not with the becoming, but with the become.

I am in the city’s heartwood, its oldest, deepest, most silent core. Here, the ground is no longer chalky but hard as iron, polished to a dull sheen by the bare feet of millennia. The great pillars are not warty and slick, but vast and smooth, their surfaces dry to the touch, their growth finished for an age. They are the ancestors. The air is so still that the sound of my own slow breath feels like an intrusion. The pearlescent glow from the cavern ceiling is fainter here, filtered through the dense lattice of the upper city, turning the light into a permanent, silvered twilight. Passages are not open scaffolds but true, enclosed tunnels where the stone on either side fused centuries ago, their walls showing faint, shimmering strata of their long creation. This is the city’s foundation, its memory, its finished past.

And in this profound stillness, I would be a Listener.

I am not a Tender, not a builder. My hands are not the broad, strong palms made for shaping paste. They are narrower, the fingers even longer, more delicate, ending in pads that are not merely hard but are resonant, like a shard of obsidian. My skin has the same pale grey hue as my kin, but mine is almost translucent, the light-catching crystals within me not random patterns, but aligned in fine, responsive lines along my arms and up the sides of my neck. I am slighter of build, for my work is not one of exertion, but of absolute stillness. The others coax stone from water; I coax memory from stone.

My purpose is balance. The city grows ever outward, ever upward, a slow explosion of creation. But as it builds the new, it risks forgetting the old. The knowledge of the Tenders is passed hand to hand, a physical inheritance. But some lessons are too subtle, some failures too ancient, some truths too deeply embedded to be taught. They must be remembered. They must be listened to.

A young Tender will come to me. They will not speak, but I will feel their presence in the subtle shift of air in my quiet chamber. Their hands will bear the damp clay of their work; a new arch they are guiding is failing. A hairline fracture, a subtle slump that their sensitive palms have detected, a weakness their experience cannot explain.

I will lead them from the bright, wet nurseries of the building edge, down through the layers of the city, into the deep quiet. I will take them to a pillar. Not just any pillar. A specific one. It stands at a junction of three ancient tunnels, and if you press your face to its surface, you can feel a coldness that speaks of immense mass and a stillness that speaks of immense age. This pillar holds the ancestor who first solved the problem of the wide-span arch.

I will place the young Tender’s hand on a section of the pillar. Then, I will place my own hand beside theirs, my long fingers bracketing their smaller, stronger one. And then, I will listen.

I close my dark eyes. I press my fingertips to the stone. My own slow heartbeat quiets until it is almost gone. My consciousness sinks away from my body and into the stone, following the crystalline pathways within. It is not like seeing or hearing. It is a form of pure knowing. I feel the echo of the ancestor’s hands, a pressure applied a thousand years ago. I feel the memory of the water’s flow, the precise mineral concentration they guided. I discern the deep, harmonic truth of the structure they raised. I feel the flaw they discovered, the moment of near-failure, and then the slow, deliberate correction: a slight shift in the wooden framework, a thickening of the paste in one specific area, a century of patience applied with new understanding.

These are not thoughts. They are resonances, frequencies, patterns of pressure and temperature locked in the crystal lattice. And as I perceive them, I translate them. I do not speak. My body becomes a conduit. A low hum begins in my chest, a vibration that travels up my arms. My fingers, pressed against the young Tender’s hand, transmit this hum—this story, this solution—directly into their own sensitive nerves. They feel the memory of the ancestor through me. Their eyes will close, and their whole body will be focused on the lesson flowing through their palm. It is a slow, silent communion between the living, the dead, and the stone that unites them.

When it is over, we will part in silence. They will return to their failing arch, their hands now holding not just mineral paste, but an inherited memory. They will know what to do.

I am the city's library. I am the voice of the ancestors. My long life is not spent accumulating crystal accolades on my own skin, but in becoming a key, a way to read the living history all around us. And when my own slow time ends, they will not encase me in a pillar to bear a load. They will lay me down within the foundation of a new library, a new quiet place. My body, aligned with a thousand years of listening, will become the central stone, the index, from which a new Listener can learn to hear the city's deep and patient song.
