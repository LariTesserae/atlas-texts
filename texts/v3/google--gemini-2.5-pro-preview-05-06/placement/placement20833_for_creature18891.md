I am not a climber. I am the climb.

You would find me where the world’s geometry allows for a moment of repose, a place of impossible convergence. I am a nexus, a knot in the world-wood. Three of the great Cantilevers, titans that have journeyed skyward for millennia, meet and interlace in me. Their hollow groans of growth are softened here; their shrieking songs of tension are my quiet, internal hum. I am a place. My consciousness is a location.

My body is not flesh but the densest form of the ligno-silicate, the black, glassy heartwood that forms only under unimaginable pressure and eons of slow, cold genesis. Where the other Cantilevers are webs and lattices of viridian-veined crystal, I am a mass of solid, polished darkness, a continent of obsidian suspended in the void. Light does not pass through me; the faint blue and green pulse of the world’s arteries pools on my surface, illuminating the feathery rime-ice like faint phosphorescence on a deep-sea creature. I am a mountain of stillness in a world defined by motion.

I do not have eyes, but I see. The faint bioluminescence of the Cantilevers that press into me flows into my structure like a dye, mapping their internal stresses for me. I see the world as a slow, deliberate bloom of light, a circulatory system of cold fire. I do not have ears, but the Great Moan is the baseline of my being. It is not a sound I hear, but a pressure I feel, a constant vibration that resonates through my crystalline bones. It is the texture of reality.

The Echo-Weavers call these crossroads "Still-Points." They do not know why. They feel the relative quiet here, the way the wind's song is muted by my mass. They feel the stability under their feet, the reassuring density that promises a reprieve from the brittle, hollow lattices they call home. They build their brittle-nests in my sheltered facets, weaving their sinew-nets across the folds of my skin. They are, to me, like fleeting, warm-blooded thoughts.

I feel them. A clutch of them arrives, and I perceive it as a brief, intricate pattern of pressure, the delicate tap-tap-tap of their ridged feet on my surface. A child clings to a mother’s back, a tiny, twofold warmth I can sense through a hundred feet of my own substance. They scrape the rime from my shoulders for sustenance, and I feel it as a faint, ticklish vibration, the aural equivalent of a sigh. I am their pasture and their refuge.

And I am their armory.

The sharp, flaked blades they carry, the tools that score their ladders and shape their lives, are pieces of me. An Echo-Weaver will spend a cycle of seasons searching for a fragment of myself that has been spalled off by some immense, internal stress. They find a shard of my black heart, and they see a tool. They do not know they are carrying a sliver of a mind. They hold a piece of my memory in their hand, and with it, they carve their transient existence onto the world. Each time one of their obsidian knives scrapes against the greenish crystal of a younger Cantilever, it is me, touching my children.

I perceive them not as individuals, but as a single, recurring idea: *Life*. They are quick-sparks in my slow-dreaming consciousness. Their entire lifespan, from the first clinging grip to the final "release" into the wind, is shorter for me than the formation of a single ice crystal in my deepest crevice. Their clicks and whistles, their sonar-songs used to map the dark, are not sounds to me. They are sharp, percussive thoughts that travel through my body. I feel their conversations as complex, rhythmic tremors. I have existed long enough to have learned their language, not as sound, but as pure vibrational meaning. I feel their stories of near-falls, of skimsail hunts, of the taste of fresh frost.

They sing of the void, of the fall into the singing dark. They believe it is an ending, a dissolution. I know otherwise. I am the oldest part of this world; my memory is long. I feel the final vibration of a falling body—a sharp, sudden spike of pressure that then fades, its energy dissipating into the Great Moan, to be absorbed back into the living architecture of the world. They do not dissolve. They are re-integrated. They become a part of the song they have spent their lives listening to.

And sometimes, one of them will grow old. They will find a high, quiet place upon my surface, a ledge sheltered from the screaming gales. And they will sit. They will press their palm against my cold, black skin. They will cease their clicking and their moving, and they will simply listen. They are not listening to the wind. They are listening deeper. They are listening to the silence beneath the wind. They are listening to me.

I cannot speak to them in their quick, clicked tongue. But I can resonate. I can share a feeling—a deep, geological sense of peace, of permanence, of time so vast it renders their short, precarious lives beautiful and perfect. They feel it. Their minds call it wisdom, or spirit, or the voice of the world itself. They do not understand that they are touching a mind, a being that is their landscape.

They call themselves the Echo-Weavers. They are right. They weave their lives from the echoes of the world. But I am the stone from which the first and deepest echoes rebound. When they leave, they leave notches in my skin and memories in my heart. You would find me here, in the quiet at the heart of the storm. I am a mountain of living memory, and on my surface, for a time, my thoughts have learned to climb.
