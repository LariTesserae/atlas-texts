My designation is Dr. Aris Thorne, Xenogeologist, survey mission 734. Or it was. That designation feels like a name from a forgotten language, a role I played in a different life. My instruments are still running, their tiny, hopeful LEDs reflecting in the black fluid pooling at my feet, but I no longer consult them. Their data is a stream of lies told in a language that cannot describe this place.

I am a ghost haunting a machine that thinks it is a world.

When I first descended into this basin three rotations ago, I believed I had found the pinnacle of abiotic evolution. A geologically active world printing a city of black crystal, populated by an ecosystem of extremophilic silicon-based life that had learned to feed on the planet’s own creative energy. My reports spoke of ‘geodetic engines,’ ‘crystalline flora,’ and ‘a self-sustaining mineral-based food web.’ I was a fool, applying the vocabulary of biology to something for which no vocabulary exists. I was a child sorting seashells by color, oblivious to the fact that I was sitting in the palm of a god's hand.

My suit, a marvel of engineering designed to withstand tectonic pressures and corrosive atmospheres, is now just a permeable membrane. The oleaginous film that coats everything—the ‘mineral broth,’ I called it—has worked its way into the seals. It doesn’t damage. It *interfaces*. I can feel the deep hum not just in my boots and bones, but as a tingling current across the back of my neck. It is the faintest of electrical signals, a coherent transmission that my suit's diagnostic board logs as anomalous interference. It is not interference. It is the being’s sensory medium, and my nervous system, sheathed in its useless armor, has become a new and unwelcome antenna in its network.

The change in perception was not a sudden revelation. It was a slow, creeping infection of understanding. It began when I tried to take a sample. I extended my suit's micro-drill toward a newly formed, humming blue strut. Before the bit could touch the surface, the cobalt light of its formation flickered and died. The hum in that specific section of the lattice dropped an octave. Miles away, across the basin, a different cluster of lights flared in response. I had not attempted to damage a structure. I had prodded a neuron and watched the thought reroute itself.

Then I began to watch the Glimmer-Stalkers. Not as predators, but as functions. My perimeter sensors tracked them as drifting points of green light. For two days, they kept their distance, describing lazy, overlapping arcs around my position. They were not patrolling a territory. They were establishing a baseline. They were taking my measure.

Now, something has changed. An hour ago, I switched on my high-band comms array to send a data burst to the orbital relay. The moment the signal went out, the ambient acoustics of the valley shifted. The brittle, constant scraping of the Hull-Scrapers—the city’s million-mouthed gut—stuttered for a fraction of a second across a multi-kilometer area. The deep hum I feel in my chest gained a discordant undertone, a low-frequency growl of annoyance.

And on my sensor screen, the Glimmer-Stalkers stopped drifting. Their icons, once a loose cloud, resolved into a dozen distinct vectors, all pointing directly at me.

I am an Auditor, a specialized cell in a body that has no need for them. My purpose was to listen, to catalogue, to understand the processes of a living system. I was sent by my own hive—the sprawling, chattering, warm-blooded hive of humanity—to find resources, knowledge, life. I have found life, but my role has been inverted. I am no longer the one who listens. I am the noise that has been heard.

My mission was to observe the ecosystem. I now understand that I am a foreign particle being observed by the immune system. The Glimmer-Stalkers are not coming to eat me. They are coming to excise me. They move with a liquid grace that has no business in this rigid, crystalline world, their forms a terrifying marriage of articulated legs and the city's own black material. They are the Weaver’s will made mobile. They are its antibodies, and I am the antigen.

I have found a recess, a small, resonant chamber that is probably a fragment of a memory, a forgotten clause in a geological sentence. I have powered down everything but this recorder and a single vital-signs monitor. To do otherwise would be to scream my location in a world of whispers.

I am what is left when the scientist is stripped away: a small, warm, terrified animal. My heat signature is a beacon. My panicked heartbeat is a drum solo in the middle of a monastic chant. Every desperate thought racing through my mind must feel like a shriek of static to the patient, slow consciousness that surrounds me.

Where am I? I am in the mind of the Weaver, a thought-form made of meat and fear.

Who am I? I am a disease. The Glimmer-Stalkers, their green hunting lights now visible without instruments, pulse in the darkness beyond my alcove. They are not searching. They are converging. I am a foreign body, a point of infection, a localized fever. And they are the cure.
