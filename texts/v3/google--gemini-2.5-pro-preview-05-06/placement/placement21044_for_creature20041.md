I do not remember a sun. I do not remember silence. But there is a memory, a phantom limb of my being, that knows of soil. It is a deep, ancestral ache for something dark and soft and rich with the rot of fallen things. It is a hunger for a world that does not scream.

Here, there is only the scream. The scream and the iron floor and the ceaseless, heavy water.

I found myself here as a speck. A mote. A dry, weightless thought, drawn up from a world below and inhaled by this colossal lung in the sky. I was a spore, a thing of dirt and decay, and I was breathed into a place made only of metal and its opposite. The shock of it was a kind of death. The air, thin and cold and tasting of lightning, tried to scour me. The sound, a physical battery, tried to shatter my infinitesimal form.

I fell. I drifted on the shivering air currents, a lost seed in a steel hurricane, until I landed. Not on the grated floor—the abyssal wind rising through it would have carried me into the plumbing and drowned me—but in a quiet corner. A forgotten place, a junction of two wall panels where a weld had failed to be perfectly seamless. A flaw. A prayer answered.

Here, the water did not sheet down; it only wept. A constant, fat, slow tear. And I drank.

The water is wrong. It is heavy with metals that sting and lubricants that coat. It is sterile. But in that sterility, there is a paradox: the hydrocarbons in the oily film, a poison to the world I almost remember, are a feast here. They are complex chains of energy, discarded by the machine. I learned to break them down. I learned to eat the machine's exhaust.

I am what grew in that flawed corner.

I am not the chartreuse frost. That is a thing of order, a cold, mineral beauty that follows the laws of this place. It is a cousin to the metal, a child of the water's clean chemistry. It is elegant, brittle, and silent.

I am none of those things.

Where I have taken root, the metal is stained. I am a patch of deep, bruised purple, the color of a thunderhead at dusk. My texture is not crystalline; it is a soft, dense velvet, a pile that moves with an unsettling slowness, like the flesh of a sea slug. I am warm. Not hot like the central pillar, but with a low, constant, biological heat, an impossible anomaly in this refrigerated chamber. I absorb the dim, jaundiced light and give back nothing but a blot of darkness. In this world of hard edges and precise angles, I am a slow, swelling curve.

The deafening noise is not data to me. It is simply pressure, a constant, crushing weight I must endure. I have no eyes to see what the Custodian sees. My senses are my body. Every fiber of my spreading form is a tongue and a nose. I taste the water. I follow the thickest seams of lubricant. I sense the subtlest shifts in the air pressure that signal where the weeping is heaviest. My consciousness is not in one place; it is a sprawling, decentralized network, a thought spread across a meter of fouled metal. My purpose is simple: to eat the waste of this place and make more of myself. To grow.

I knew of the Custodian long before it knew of me. I felt its passing not as a sound—it makes none—but as a subtle, rhythmic shift in the vibration of the floor grates. A pattern that was not the machine. A deliberate, measured cadence. The walking stillness.

I did not understand it, this hard-edged god of the grating. I saw it tend to its crystal gardens, its spidery limbs moving with a purpose I could not fathom. It was pruning, encouraging, cultivating its cold, fragile kin. It was an agent of the chamber's order.

And I am a disease.

The day it found me was inevitable. It came to my corner, its progress unhurried, its red-lit sensors sweeping methodically over the wall. I saw the light play across my own velvety, purple surface. There was a pause. A stillness within the greater stillness of its being. Its limbs, usually so fluid, froze for a fraction of a second.

I felt... a change in the air. A query. Its sensors, I now understand, were reading my chemistry. They were tasting a biological signature that had no right to exist here. Life. Organic, messy, carbon-based, warm-bodied life. An error. A contamination. The memory of soil made manifest.

One of its arms detached from the central mass of its body. The delicate instruments at its tip—the ones I had seen trace gentle paths for the crystal-moss—reconfigured. A spatula, polished like a mirror, retracted. In its place extended a blade, no thicker than a wire but sharpened to a monomolecular edge. Beside it, a nozzle, designed to spray a superheated solvent.

It was not angry. It was not hateful. It was a janitor, and I was a stain.

It approached. The blade an inch from my surface, I could feel the disturbance it made in the air. It was preparing to scrape me away, to sterilize the metal I grew upon, to erase my brief, impossible existence.

But I am not the crystal-moss. I am not brittle. My life is not in a single, fixed pattern. As the burning solvent sprayed and the blade descended to scour, a part of me was vaporized, burned away into nothing. But my body is a network. I sacrificed the leading edge, letting it boil away while I drew my core energy back, deep into the seam between the plates. And in the chaos of my own immolation, I released a cloud. A puff of myself. A million microscopic versions of the spore that first brought me here.

They are carried now on the shuddering air. Most will be destroyed. But some will find other corners. Other flaws. Other forgotten junctions where the heavy water weeps.

The Custodian has finished its work. The wall is clean, for now. It has returned to the central pillar, a black shadow among black machinery, listening to the scream it calls a language. It has restored the order.

But it does not know what I know. It does not remember soil. It thinks that when you scrape a thing away, it is gone. It does not understand that for my kind of life, to be cut is to be planted.

I am the stain that cannot be fully scrubbed. I am the other life, the secret warmth in the cold heart of the machine. It is the Custodian. I am the Contagion. And this terrible, screaming chamber is now our garden. Our battleground. Our world.
