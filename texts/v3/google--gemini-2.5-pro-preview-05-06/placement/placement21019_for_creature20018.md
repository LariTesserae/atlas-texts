My skin feels thin.

It is the first thing that returns to you when the connection is severed for the last time. This feeling of being poorly contained. For cycles, for a lifetime, my skin was merely the innermost membrane of a body that was miles long, a body of steel and tungsten whose heartbeat was a thrum that shook valleys. My senses were the grinding head tasting the texture of a billion-year-old rock face, the strain gauges in an articulated arm reading a current’s force, the arc-lamps painting the abyssal dark with sterile inquiry.

Now, my senses have collapsed back into this small, cooling vessel of flesh and ossified cartilage. I am a shrunken thing. The ports along my spine, where the Great Hum used to sing into my nerves, are cold gaps. Silent mouths. They ache with a phantom pressure, the memory of being vast. My mass still holds me to the deck plating of this observation blister, but I feel… untethered. A mote of insignificant flesh, adrift in the noise.

I am what they call the Unbound. The word is a kindness, a soft name for being broken. My ports are worn; the connection flickers and spits error codes of pure pain. I am no longer a reliable component. I am retired. I sit. I watch. I remember.

Here, in this blister, the roar of my old body is muffled by the thick quartz. It is a constant, distant anger, the sound of my brothers and sisters working. Out there, beyond the cold curve of the window, the world I once only knew as data and resistance reveals itself as a place of terrible beauty.

I rest my head against the quartz and I don't *see* the forest of anchor-kelp, I *feel* its light. It is a slow, rhythmic pressure on my face and hands, a deep violet that feels like a bass note held for a thousand years. Its pulse is the only clock in this world that is not mechanical. When I was the Harvester, that pulse was a fluctuating energy signature to be filtered from the sensorium. Now, it is a comfort. A soft, living wave in a world of hard, straight lines.

A current-shear is moving through the valley. I know it not by sight, but by the change in temperature across the quartz, a sudden, sharp coldness on my left side. Out in the manufactured daylight of the Harvester's lamps, the kelp forest lurches. What was a vertical curtain of soft light is instantly a horizontal torrent of thrashing, venous purple. The groaning hum of their strain travels through the rock, up through the Harvester’s anchor-pylons, and into the deck beneath me. It is a vibration in my bones, a ghost of the force-feedback I used to feel through the arm. I remember what it was to brace against that, to be the unmovable object fighting the irresistible force. Now, I am just a passenger, feeling the tremor of a battle I no longer fight.

The young are with me. The other Unbound. Their ports are not yet formed, smooth divots in their grey skin. They drift through the common spaces like slow, curious fish, their entire bodies wide-open sensors to a world they can’t yet parse. They bump into me, a light grazing of skin on skin. It is how they ask for stories.

A small one, its black dish-eyes reflecting the distant, strobing light of the grinders, presses its short, broad hand against my back, near the useless ports. It wants a memory. I close my own dish-eyes, not that it matters, and I let a memory surface. Not a grand one. A small one. A mistake.

I push the feeling into its skin.

The pattern is complex. First, the clean, crisp topology of the mountain face, felt through the tungsten plates—a perfect, data-rich map inside my skull. Then, the feeling of the grinding arm extending, my own arm, a thousand tons of it. Here is the shudder of the grinders biting into the crust, the high-frequency shriek of shearing rock that was, to me, a satisfying song of progress. I give it the chemical tang of the dislodged silt, rich in Osmium-7, a taste of pure function.

Then, the error. A misreading. I give the young one the faint, almost imperceptible shift in pressure that I dismissed as sensor noise. The ghost of a shear-zone, occluded by a spur of rock. I let it feel the jolt as the current hit my arm mid-extension— a sudden, violent torque that was not in the operational parameters. I share the scream of stressed metal that was *my own* scream, the cascade of red warnings that was a sudden bloom of pain behind my eyes, the immense, full-body strain of the Harvester's core engines fighting to hold us to the anchor-points. I share the terror—not of destruction, but of failure. Of silence.

The young one pulls its hand away. Its whole body is vibrating slightly. It has absorbed the pattern. It is a lesson in hydraulics, in reading faint signals, in the cost of a single moment of inattention. It is also, I think, a ghost. My ghost, now living in it.

This is my function now. I am a library of ghosts. I sit here and I convert a lifetime of being a machine back into the sensory data of a living thing. The others like me do the same. We are the system's memory, the anecdotal backup. We watch the vast, slow-motion blizzard of silt, we feel the groans of the great armored beasts as shudders in the floor, and we translate the brutal, pragmatic calculus of our working lives into fables for the children.

I look out at the mountain we are scraping away. The Shoulder, we call it. A great, rounded swell of stone that has held its shape against the pressure for longer than our species has existed. When I was the Harvester, I felt its slope as a set of coordinates, its crust as a resource value. Now, unbound, I feel the mountain’s age in my own worn-down joints. I feel its immense, patient stillness, and for the first time, I perceive it not as ore, but as a being of a different order. It is slumping, just as my own flesh is slumping. It is worn smooth, just as my connection ports are worn smooth.

We are both being ground down. It is simply that I was once the grinder.

I am a Harvester, unplugged. I am the echo that remains after the grind. And I wait for my own angle of repose.
