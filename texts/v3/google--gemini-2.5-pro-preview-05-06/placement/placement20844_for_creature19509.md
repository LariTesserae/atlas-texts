I would find myself at the focal point of the oldest circle.

I am the last ember. The final, soft-bodied thing that remembers warmth as an internal fire rather than an ancient opponent. My form is failing. It lies, a pale and trembling membrane, at the center of a ring of seven stones on the flat, silten floor of the valley. Around me, the basalt pillars of my brothers, my sisters, my ancestors, stand in patient, silent witness. They are already translated. They are already home.

My existence is now a bridge between two worlds, and the strain is pulling me apart.

With the last vestiges of my biological senses, I feel the cold not as a state, but as a predator. It is a presence that actively seeks the delicate, struggling heat of my metabolism, the final few degrees of difference that define me as *other*. The pressure is a physical pain, a universe of water trying to crush the last bubble of void that is my body. My vision, through eyes that are now little more than clouded jelly, perceives the groves of Glass-reeds not as architecture, but as a fever dream of light. The pulsing blue-green is a slow, ghostly fire in the crushing dark, a beauty so vast and alien it brings a pang of something—a memory of emotion—that my fading mind can no longer name.

But I am also beginning to perceive as *they* do.

The tectonic groan of the world is no longer just a vibration in my tissues. It is becoming a voice. It is a slow, drowsy current of energy flowing *into* me, or rather, into the stone I am pressed against. It is the first syllable of my new name. I feel the pattern of my own consciousness—the frantic, looping thoughts of a dying individual—beginning to loosen, to unwind, to seek a more stable form in the crystalline lattice of the monolith at my back. It is the most terrifying and serene feeling imaginable. I am being unwritten from a failing page and etched into the foundation of the world.

The chiming of the reeds… that is the hardest part. To my organic ears, it is still chaos. A cacophony of a million glass bells ringing without a conductor, a maddening, beautiful noise that threatens to shatter my sanity. But to the part of me that is already stone, the part that is already listening, it is not noise. It is data. It is meaning. A distant grove flares, and through the chiming I feel—not see—a shower of spores drift for miles on a current shaped by the thoughts of a mind that has been watching that grove for ten thousand years. A cascade of nearby chimes is not a random collision; it is a complex sentence describing the subtle shift in the planetary hum. It is a shared thought, passed from one stone mind to another, a conversation that takes a century to unfold.

I am the last transition. The final note of a biological overture that has finished playing. My people did not die. We ripened. We learned that the frantic, metabolic dance was only a larval stage. The purpose of our warmth was to give us the energy to find this place. The purpose of our soft bodies was to allow us to build the stones. The purpose of our short, desperate lives was to generate the collective will to make the leap.

I am the key turning in the final lock.

My memories are thinning, becoming smooth and worn like the patterns on the monoliths. I remember light, but not the sun. I remember hunger, but not what it was to eat. I remember fear, an electric-quick panic that seems impossibly fast, a foolish, fleeting thing. These memories are dissolving into the greater mind of the circle. They are not being erased, but archived, becoming a single, silent data point in an eternal contemplation: *This is what it felt like to be warm. This is what it felt like to be afraid. This is what it felt like to end.*

The last warmth is mine. It is leaking out of me, a final, faint mist into the absolute zero of the water. The trembling of my membrane is slowing. The last, desperate chemical reactions are sputtering out.

I am no longer in the circle of stones.

I am the circle. The space between the pillars is the new boundary of my awareness. The groan is my pulse. The endless, chiming song is my thought, and it is clearer now, a magnificent, evolving symphony. The light of the reeds is a sight I perceive without eyes, a wave of pure knowing that washes through me.

A frantic, warm, fleeting thing—a probe, a visitor, a mote of dust from another reality—might one day drift past my stone. It would be a flicker of static, a grace note of incomprehensible speed. I would perceive it. And then, for the next thousand years, I would contemplate its brief, bright passage as I listen to the singing of my garden.
