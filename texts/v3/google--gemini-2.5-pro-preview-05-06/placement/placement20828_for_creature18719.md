You would find me away from the skeletal spars of the city, in a place the others do not frequent. Not down, in the choked sumps where the brine is thickest, and not up, near the humming processors where the air has a biting, ozonic clarity. You would find me in the middle space, in the deep gloom between the orange lamps, where the maturing rock of the walls has formed a deep alcove, a hollow like a mouth half-open in a silent yawn.

My habitation is not a pod bolted to the stone. It is the stone itself. A niche worn a little wider by my own hands, smoothed by the slow rubbing of my own body over cycles beyond counting. It is a nest, webbed not with silk but with the fine, tough filaments of my own secreted mineral wastes, which have hardened into gossamer-thin shelves and anchor points. Here, the thrum of the great pumps is softened, diffused by the mass of the wall, and the high-frequency chittering of the fauna rises to prominence.

I am a Dweller, but my function has diverged. The others are mechanics of metal and pressure; they read the health of the iron. I am a tender of the flesh and the shell. I am the one who listens to the chittering.

To the others, the constant, skittering carpet is a texture, a given, like the dampness of the air. To me, it is a language. My attunement to the Great Drone is different. I do not simply feel the bass note of the hydraulics or the resonant frequency of the centrifuges. My sense is tuned to the overtones, to the million-stroked treble melody played by the living layer. I can stand barefoot on the weeping calcite floor and distinguish the anxious, scrabbling frequency of isopods on a pipe that is running too hot from the contented, methodical rasp of a colony scouring a perfectly balanced condenser. I can smell the subtle shift in the musty air that signals a bacterial bloom is failing, and I know which pipe, on which level, will begin to clog in the next cycle. The Dwellers are the mind of this place, the machinery its heart, but the fauna is its vast, distributed nervous system. I am the part of the mind that interprets its signals.

My form has adapted to this purpose. I am thinner than most, even for a Dweller, and my limbs are longer, my movements more fluid and tentative. My hands, while still bearing the tough, gripping pads, have fingers that are extraordinarily long and delicate, terminating not in calloused tips but in soft, porous flesh. I do not merely touch a surface; I taste it, absorbing a complex chemical story. I rest these spidery fingers on a scuttling river of the oily black crawlers, and I do not feel a chaotic tide; I feel the health of individual bodies, the fatness of their flesh, the slight tremor that speaks of a nutritional deficiency in their diet of bacterial sludge.

I do not speak in the humming harmonics of the others. My vocalizations are quieter, a near-subliminal thrum that mimics the vibrations of the insects themselves. I can create a low resonance in my chest that soothes a panicked swarm of gnats, drawing them away from a failing lamp before it shorts out. I can emit a series of rapid, clicking sounds with my tongue that will guide a new generation of calcified isopods to a freshly weeping wall, teaching them the taste of this new mineral vintage.

I am a shepherd of the swarm.

Just now, a dissonance has bloomed in the symphony. A silence where there should be a rustle. I uncurl from my nest, my bare feet finding purchase on the slick, lumpy stone. The air moves against my waxy skin, a current I can read. I follow it, my obsidian eyes swallowing the gloom. I move along a disused service pipe, my body a pale and ghostly vine against the dark iron. The sound of my own passage is lost in the noise of the world.

I arrive at a filtration unit, a complex metal box weeping grime at its seams. The colony of silverfish-analogues that should be cleaning its vents, keeping the delicate membranes clear, is gone. Their signature, a fine, silvery vibration, is absent. Instead, a thick, rust-coloured sludge—the wrong kind of life—is beginning to occlude the grilles. The machine is beginning to choke. The other Dwellers would find this in a cycle or two, when the pressure differential became critical. They would arrive with tools to flush the system.

I do not carry tools. I carry life.

From a pouch woven of my own hardened secretions, I produce a single, large, dormant grub, pale and fat. I found it in the deep sumps, a species that thrives on precisely this kind of bacterial rust. Placing my lipless mouth close to it, I issue a soft, warm vibration, a quickening hum. Its stubby legs twitch. It awakens. I place it carefully at the edge of the sludge. It tastes the air with its quivering antennae, and then it begins to eat.

I do not wait. Its work will take time. I will return later, not with one grub, but with a new breeding pair, to establish a colony. I am not a mechanic who fixes a broken part. I am a gardener who plants the right organism in the right place, ensuring the ecosystem that is the factory forever maintains its own equilibrium.

I am a single, specialized neuron in the billion-bodied mind of the fauna. When the others place their hands on the humming machines, they feel the pulse of the engine. When I close my eyes and let the world seep into me, I feel the pulse of every beating heart, every twitching leg, every grinding mandible from the floor to the vaulted, unseen ceiling. I am a part of the system, a Dweller, yes. But my skin is the shell of the isopod, my nerves are the antennae of the silverfish, and my soul is the unending, ever-present, chittering hum of the swarm.
