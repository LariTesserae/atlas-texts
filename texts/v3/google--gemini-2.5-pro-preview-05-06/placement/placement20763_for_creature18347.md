You would find me on the back of a god.

Not a god of thunder or harvest, for there is no weather but wetness, no season but now. This is a god of stasis, one of the Sinter-Sages. I live upon the vast, island-like expanse of their mineral shell, a landscape atop a landscape.

From my vantage point, the world is a study in curves. Below me is the gentle, warm curvature of the Sage’s carapace, a canvas of creams and rusts fractured by hairline cracks from which a faint, body-warm steam perpetually rises. Around me are the shallow, submerged slopes of my host, descending into the still water of its pool. And beyond that, stretching to infinity, is the immense, concave bowl of the plain itself, a white dish brimming with hazy, glaring light.

My world is a single, immense being. The ground I walk is alive. It pulses with a rhythm so slow it cannot be felt, only known, like the imperceptible turning of a galaxy. The air I breathe is its exhalation, thick with ozone and the sweet-rot scent of the algal mats it cultivates. The constant, thrumming drone of the world is its song, a vibration that permeates my very structure.

I am a Veridian Keeper.

I am not large. You could hold me in the cup of your hands, if your hands could bear the heat and the slickness. My form is somewhere between a beetle and a limpet. The top of me is a shell, not of chitin but of a living, layered silicate, patterned in mossy greens and the deep blue-blacks of oxidized copper. It is my camouflage and my history, a shell grown from the same minerals as the Sage I serve, so that when I am still, I am just another feature on its living skin.

My underside is another matter. It is a single, soft, protoplasmic foot, the color of mother-of-pearl. It is through this foot that I live. It clings to the Sage’s shell with a suction that is both physical and chemical, and it is covered in a million microscopic filaments, my mouths and my senses.

I am a gardener. The Sinter-Sage is a being of passive absorption, but its vast surface is a complex and competitive ecosystem. The magenta bacteria, if left unchecked, would overwhelm the mustard-yellow algae. Certain mineral deposits could seal the porous shell, starving the Sage of the water’s faint nutrients. My purpose is to maintain this balance. I am a conscious, mobile part of the Sage’s immune and digestive system.

My pilgrimage is slow. I move across the warm, slick plains of the shell, a journey of a meter that can take a week. I do not see with eyes; the glare would be useless. I *read* the surface through my foot. I taste the subtle chemical shifts that signal an imbalance. I feel the vibrations of the insectoid drone as a constant, textured pressure.

As I travel, I graze. My filaments break down and absorb the overgrowths, converting them into a refined, nutrient-rich paste. I then secrete this paste into the hairline fractures of the shell, delivering it to the nacreous, gelatinous flesh of the Sage below. I am its external stomach. I am its tongue. The jade-and-blood succulents that cling to the highest ridges of my host are my kin; we are both processors, tenders of the god’s body.

My own consciousness is a quiet, focused thing. It is a consciousness of texture, temperature, and taste. The thought of ‘I’ is subordinate to the thought of ‘the work’. My world is the patch of shell beneath me, the scent of a thriving bacterial bloom, the subtle change in the drone that means a heron-bird has landed on a far slope of our shared body.

The Sinter-Sage does not speak to me in words, but we are in constant communion. When it shifts its weight, a geological event that might happen once in a thousand of my lifetimes, I feel it as a slow, tectonic reordering of my world. The slope I am on may rise by a few degrees; a canyon of living tissue may narrow. These are not cataclysms; they are seasons. They are how my host thinks. I am a thought the Sage is having, a thought about its own well-being, made manifest and mobile.

I have no name, no past but the mineral layers of my shell, no future but the next patch of algae. I will live my life a slow, deliberate line drawn across this living landscape. When I die, my own shell, rich with the minerals I have processed, will fuse with the Sage’s carapace. I will become a permanent mark, a verdigris streak upon its skin, another word in the long, slow sentence of its existence. I will dissolve back into the god I tended.

I am a single, diligent cell on the body of a world that has come to rest. And it is enough.
