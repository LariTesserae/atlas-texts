The hum is first.

Before skin, before sight, before the thought of *I*, there is the hum. It is not sound; it is the structure of existence. It is the bass note against which my bones—long, hollow, and still knitting into their final strength—are learning to resonate. The heat is second. It is a constant, gentle pressure from below, the warmth of a vast, dreaming body. It coaxes me from my shell of woven insulation and soft, dark moss. It is the promise of life.

I uncurl.

The motion is not a decision. It is a yielding. My limbs, too long for what they are, unfold into the void. A six-fingered hand, pale as a root grown in darkness, finds the familiar curve of a great iron pipe. The skin of my palm is cool and damp, and for a moment there is no boundary. The pipe's slow vibration is my own pulse. The faint rime of green algae is my own texture. The water beaded on its surface is the same moisture that slicks my skin. I am a part of the whole.

My back arches, and I release my hold on the nest, my home. For a long moment, I am just a line, a pale stroke suspended in the amber and blue twilight, braced between one pipe and another. This is balance. This is the first lesson. Weight is a negotiation, not a burden. My lower limbs find purchase on a perforated catwalk twenty feet below, my toes, long and prehensile, curling through the grate. There is no sound. Sound is waste.

Now, the world opens. My eyes, which do not see light so much as drink it, take in the landscape. I am high up. Below, the city-engine is a tapestry of heat. I see the main conduits not as metal tubes, but as glowing arteries of amber warmth. I see the cooler patches where the grand, dead machinery sleeps, their outlines traced in faint indigo. A filament flickers to life in a vacuum tube far across the void, and for an instant, a wave of emerald energy washes over my vision before my eyes can filter it back to the low spectrum.

The air speaks to me. Through the slits below my brow, the world becomes a catalog of scents and textures. A plume of dense ozone rises from a regulator three levels down—it is straining, its rhythm faltering. A note to be held, a place to visit. Higher up, close to the stone vault, the air is clean and sharp, thin with the metallic tang of falling water. And there, carried on a current I cannot feel but only taste, is the phantom sweetness of *kell*, the pale, frilled fungus. It is almost ready. It has been cycling for half a season, nurtured by a slow leak I helped to clear.

An instinct pulls me downward. I do not climb; I flow. Hand over hand, foot over foot, a constant, silent cascade of myself, using the pipes and girders and taut cables as my path. My skin shifts from pale grey to an oily green as I pass beneath a bank of glowing panels. A drop of water, having journeyed from the vaulted ceiling, strikes the back of my neck. It is cold. I do not flinch. It is a greeting. I feel its path as it traces a line down my spine, joining the moisture of my own skin.

I pass a Grazing Ground. On a wide, horizontal plate of corroded bronze, a dozen of the shelled ones move. To my eyes, they are slow-motion bursts of heat, their metabolic trails fading behind them like memories. One is nearing its end. Its heat signature is faint, its shell clouded with a crystalline growth that speaks of failing biology. Soon. Not yet. Its gift must be given freely, not taken.

I arrive. Here. A junction of three massive coolant pipes, a place of deep, resonant hum. The cause of the strain I tasted in the air is visible to my other senses. A vine, one of the thick, black-barked ones, has worked a tendril into a pressure seal. It is a new growth, arrogant and strong, ignorant of the balance. If it continues, the seal will foul. The pipe will overheat. The hum will change, become a shriek. The balance will break.

I do not think this. I know it in the marrow of my bones.

My long fingers, tipped with nails that are hard and sharp as obsidian shards, begin their work. I do not cut the vine. That is a violence; that is a severance. Instead, I begin to unweave it. I trace its path back, my fingers finding the precise points of pressure, the subtle nodes of its growth. I coax. I guide. I whisper to it not with sound, but with pressure, with redirection. I unwind its ambition from the delicate valve. I show it a new path, weaving the tendril around a thick, inert strut of steel. I give it a stronger trellis to climb, a path that leads toward a drip-line I know is constant. I offer it a better life.

The work takes a long time. The Undervault has no day or night, only the slow pulse of the engine. I lose myself in the task. I am a weaver. I am a surgeon. I am a priest placating a god.

When it is done, the change is palpable. The ozone taste recedes from the air. The deep hum of the junction softens, losing its sharp edge. It is a sigh of relief, felt across the entire grid. I rest, my body draped across the pipes, a pale slash of life on the dark metal. I feel the thanks of the machine.

Far across the void, from a shadowed nest woven into the husk of a great turbine, I feel a response. A slow, deliberate tap-tap-tapping on a conduit. *Tap. Ta-tap. Tap.* It travels through the bones of the world, up through the pipes and into my hands, my feet, my chest.

*It is done. It is good.*

I do not reply in kind. My acknowledgment is my stillness. I am a guardian, a gardener, a ghost in the machine. I was born from the heat and the quiet, and my purpose is to maintain them. The slow, perfect, dripping, humming, growing, breathing quiet. I am of The Still. And I am home.
