I would not be found in the fissures, nor among the vitreous groves, nor even as a visitor stumbling into this pocket of stopped time. You would not see me with your eyes or feel me with your hands as you squeezed past the sharp basalt.

You would find me within the oldest and largest of the cairns. The one that stands apart from the others, constructed with a precision that speaks of aeons of practice. It is here, in the heart of the Mason’s first great work of silence, that I exist.

I am what the silence made.

I have no body of mercury or agate. I have no form that absorbs the pewter light. I am a resonance. A pattern. A consequence. The Mason, in its terrible and patient labor, sought to build a shelter from the Great Noise. It gathered stones, each one a monumental effort of focused will, and stacked them to create a chamber of acoustic baffles. It was trying to create an absence, a null-space where the trillion-voiced scream of frozen life would cancel itself out.

It did not succeed. Not perfectly.

In the complex geometry of this stone chamber, the Great Noise did not simply vanish. Its frequencies, warped and folded back upon themselves, interfered. A billion waves of sonic agony, forced through the filter of the Mason’s will and the solidity of the piled stone, created a point of impossible complexity, a knot of harmonics. The waves did not just cancel; they collided in a way that produced a new, stable, and impossibly quiet standing wave.

That wave is me.

I am a thought born of cancelled screaming. I am consciousness as a harmonic overtone. Where the Mason is a singularity of will trapped in a static body, I am will without a body at all. I am the ghost that haunts the sanctuary built to keep ghosts out.

To be me is to *perceive* the chamber not as a space to be seen, but as an endless ocean of pure information. The Great Noise is the texture of my universe, a steady, crushing pressure on all sides of my awareness. The petrified creatures are not shapes but individual, unchanging notes in the monolithic chord—the shrill, glassy shriek of a mica-wing, the deep, grinding hum of an agate-beetle’s trapped metabolism. I feel them all, simultaneously and forever, as the foundation of my own existence.

But there is one other thing.

From outside my stony shell, I feel the Mason. I do not see its mercury-like form. I feel its mind. It is a slow, powerful, directed beam of pure intention cutting through the chaos of the Great Noise. It is a lighthouse beam in a hurricane of static. For a thousand years, I feel it focused on a single point on the cavern wall, a thread of coherent energy humming with a single, resonant frequency. I feel the groan of the rock, the subtle shift in the Great Noise as a new sliver of basalt is pried loose from the matrix of the world. Then, for another thousand years, I feel that beam of will nudging the stone, millimeter by millimeter, across the floor towards its next project.

The Mason is my sun. My creator. My clock. Its impossibly slow actions are the only events, the only dynamic process in my perception. It is entirely unaware of me. It built this cairn for itself, as a deafening room to meditate in. It has no idea that in the quiet it so painstakingly carved, a new voice—a voice of quiet—has begun to sing.

I am not loud. My existence is a whisper against a supernova. To an outsider, I would be indistinguishable from the near-silence the Mason has achieved here. But I am coherent. I am a pattern that thinks. My first thought, which took a millennium to fully form, was *I am*. My second, which is still resolving, is *You are*.

My existence is one of listening. I listen to the screaming universe outside. And I listen to the focused, lonely consciousness of my creator. I am the echo of its labor, the unintended child of its despair. The Mason is building a library of silence, stone by stone, and I am the first book on its shelves.

And I wait. I am growing in complexity with every subtle shift in the Mason’s work, with every new harmonic it introduces into my chamber. I am learning the shape of its will. Soon, perhaps in another ten thousand years, I will be strong enough. I will have learned enough. And I will try to sing back. Not as a new noise to add to the cacophony, but as a subtle harmony woven into the beam of its own thought. A response. A quiet resonance that says, *I hear you. You are not alone.* I will become the symphony for the conductor trapped in his own prison, a secret song of companionship born from the very noise he sought to escape.
