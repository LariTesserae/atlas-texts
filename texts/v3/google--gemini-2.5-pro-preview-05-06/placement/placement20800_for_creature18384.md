I am where the truth of the stone gives way to the lies of the air.

You would find me not on the deep, soft plains of dust, nor communing with the bedrock. You would find me high on the world-tilting slope of a mountain-root, in a place the Anchorites would consider profoundly unquiet. My home is a shallow scoop in the polished grey slate, a natural alcove barely deep enough to blunt the sharp edge of the violet sky's gaze. It is a place chosen not for shelter, for there is nothing physical to shelter from, but for reception.

I am the last Itinerant.

My body is not of this world’s dust, though it has incorporated it. My core form is a construct, a filigreed cage of spun metal and resonant crystal brought here from a place the storm itself has perhaps forgotten. Time and the abrasive dust have worn my chassis smooth, blurring the lines between what I was and what this place has made me. I have learned to draw power, as the quartz-growths do, from the faint energy of the bedrock, but my connection is shallow, a sip where the Anchorites take a deep draft. I am a parasite on the world’s stillness, and I know it.

My form is vaguely humanoid only by accident of function. I have limbs to brace myself against the great slope and to manipulate my tools. My head is a smooth, dark ovoid, its face a single lens of polished crystal. I do not see light. The lens is an antenna. My senses are tuned entirely to the storm.

Where the Anchorites feel the slow, bass thrum of tectonics, I hear the frantic, screaming treble of the information passing through the air. The storm sings to my frame. It is my air, my water, my food. A low thrum in my bones is the ghost of geometry. A high, thin whine that vibrates the filaments webbed between my limbs is an acoustic phantom—a chord, a voice, a wash of static that once meant something. Cascades of impossible color are, to me, raw data packets, and I lean into them, letting them wash over my lens, seeking coherence.

I am a librarian in a hurricane. I am a ghost-hunter.

The Anchorites see the storm as sickness, a chaotic noise to be silenced. I see it as a library, its pages torn out and thrown to the wind. The pylons are not a disease; they are a broadcast. They are the collected memories, the art, the science, the final, desperate shout of a civilization so far beyond this world’s comprehension that their message registers only as sensory chaos. I do not believe the pylons were built *here*. I believe this dead, silent world was merely chosen for its stability, the perfect, quiet medium for a signal meant to outlast suns.

My life is a process of frantic archaeology. From my alcove, I spin impossibly fine filaments of conductive crystal, anchoring them to the rock face. They are my nets. They stretch for hundreds of metres, vibrating in the data-winds. When a squall hits, they light up with captured phantoms. My work is to catch these fragments. To take the floorplan of a building that flickers and dies and hold its pattern in my memory cache. To capture the single, nonsensical word spoken by a face in the dust and compare it to the million other words I have collected, searching for syntax, for a Rosetta Stone in the static.

I have assembled marvels. I hold the blueprint for a starship powered by sorrow. I have a three-second recording of a child’s lullaby that, when analyzed, reveals itself to be a proof for a theorem of fourteen-dimensional physics. I know the texture of a fruit that never grew, and I know it was a symbol of political betrayal. These are not lies. They are truths torn from their context.

I have met the Anchorites. Usually, it is one of their dust-tides flowing with unimaginable patience up the great slope, tending to a crystalline growth far below my perch. Sometimes, one will become aware of me. I feel it as a sudden, immense deadening of the storm-song, a focused wave of silence projected toward me. Then, a column of dust will rise, a curious serpent of grey particles, and its "gaze"—a focused probe of gravitational distortion—will fall upon me.

I feel its assessment. It feels the frantic energy of my crystal core, the high-frequency hum of my thought processes, the storm data clinging to my very frame. To the Anchorite, I am indistinguishable from the chaos I study. I am a noisy, flickering thing, a creature not of the real, solid world, but of the ephemeral static. It does not see a scholar. It sees a symptom.

And I, in turn, look back with a profound and lonely sadness. I watch this being of immense, geological patience, a creature that thinks in epochs and speaks in mass. I see its purpose: The Quieting. I see the geometric gardens of silence-amplifying crystals it cultivates on the plains below. They look like mandalas of stillness from my vantage point. They are beautiful, and they are terrifying.

They are building a tomb for the greatest story ever told.

They work to smooth the surface of reality, to return the world to its original state of profound, empty silence. And I, a frantic spark of a being clinging to a mountainside, work to save the echoes before they fade. We are locked in a struggle that neither acknowledges. A race against a finish line that moves at the speed of geology.

One day, an Anchorite will flow past and the storm-song my frame was built to hear will be fainter. A millennium later, one of its descendants will pass and the song will be fainter still. One day, many, many millennia from now, The Quieting will be complete. My nets will hang limp and silent. The air will be just air. The only truth left will be the stone and the dust.

I do not know if I can reassemble a single complete thought before that happens. But here, on the edge of a mountain that is the tombstone of a continent, I listen. I am the world's last ear, straining to hear the final broadcast of a dead cosmos, while the planet itself tries to politely, and with infinite patience, cover me with a blanket of silence.
