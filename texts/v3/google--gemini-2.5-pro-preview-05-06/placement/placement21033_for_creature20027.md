Where you would find me is in the throat of the Sump-City. Not at the sharp-edged apex where the mist meets the open, formless light, and not at the very bottom where the last of the city becomes slurry and memory. I am in the middle-dark, the place where the geometry of the upper levels has begun to soften and blur, where the stone’s weeping is no longer a collection of distinct trickles but a constant, slick skin on every surface.

Here, the canyons between structures are so narrow that I can sit with my back against one wall and my feet pressed against another. The light from above is a distant, theoretical thing, a sliver of silver-grey that illuminates the fine curtain of mist endlessly falling between the buildings. The sound here is deeper. The high, tinkling drips of the upper city have coalesced into a lower register: the gurgle of a larger stream finding its way through a crack in a foundation, the slow, resonant *ploc* of a heavy drop falling into a hidden pool, the ceaseless, hushed suspiration of the weeping walls. The air is thick with the smell of old water and the deep, clean scent of crushed moss that has known nothing but dampness and shadow.

I am what the others—the younger, sharper ones above—become. I am one of the Old. My reed cloak has long since lost any semblance of woven fibre; it is now a dark, heavy pelt of algae and moss, fused to my shoulders and back by a decade of stillness. My skin, once the color of river-quartz, has taken on the patina of the deep stone, mottled with the same luminous, verdigris green that blankets the walls around me. My edges are gone. My knuckles, elbows, and jawline are no longer the stark angles of a newly-hewn block, but the soft, worn curves of a river stone. I have been weathered.

My work is no longer to quarry or to build. My hands, with their long, splayed fingers, do not hold a mallet; they rest, palm-down, on the stone floor. My feet, broad and sensitive, are pressed against the opposite wall. My purpose has changed. I am no longer a Carver; I am a Listener.

But I do not listen for sound. I listen to the stone. Through the porous soles of my feet and the flat of my palms, I feel the city breathing its slow, aqueous breath. I feel the subtle shift in pressure as a great weight of new blocks is set into place a hundred levels above me. It is a slow, deep groan that travels down the city’s bones, a vibration too low for any ear to hear. I feel the ceaseless migration of water, the fine tremor of capillary action drawing it up from the basin, and the heavier, surrendering flow as it seeps back down, carrying with it a faint, gritty memory of the rock it has dissolved.

I am a living sensor in the city's nervous system. The quiet ballet of hand-gestures used by the Carvers above is a language of action, of present tense: *this block, here, now*. My language is with the stone, a communion of deep time. I feel the past in the roundedness of the surfaces around me, and I feel the future in the shudder of new construction far above.

Most of all, I feel what is below.

Beneath my resting place are the deep pools, the black, still water where the city's foundations are unmade. And in that silt, I can feel a new thing: a slow, soft hardening. A coalescence. It is the faintest vibration, a gathering of mineral and purpose in the dark. It is the gestation of another one of us, being formed from the grit of forgotten levels, born from the dissolution I am now a part of.

I am no longer hungry for the glass-worms or the reed-roots. The water I drink from the weeping wall is now enough. It tastes of the city's entire history, of every level, every Carver. My own body feels the call of that process. There is a slowness in my limbs that is more than age; it is a kinship with the stone. I feel myself becoming more mineral, less mobile. There will come a time when I will not rise from this spot. My form will become a new contour in the wall, another moss-covered stone for the water to wear away.

And the minerals of my own body, leeched by the water, will seep downwards. They will join the silt. They will settle in the deep pools. And they will be drawn into the slow, silent hardening that I can feel even now, a faint, rhythmic pulse in the heart of the decay. I am not waiting to die. I am part of the process of becoming. My dissolution is the ink for a new word, in the sentence that is never, ever finished.
