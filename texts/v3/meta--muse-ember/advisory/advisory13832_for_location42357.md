It is not a place you *arrive* at dry, and it's not a place you walk *through* in any normal sense. You walk *in* it, at several depths at once, for as long as you're there.

Prepare for that first. Everything else - navigation, food, shelter, safety - follows from a few facts: this is a high, equatorial plateau that is still young and sharp, drowned in its own standing warm water, and completely filled, above and below the surface, with a green that touches itself.

### 1. Your body first

**Altitude + heat + humidity, all together.** This combination does not occur in most lowland tropics. The air is thin enough that you notice it at the top of a breath, the sun is equatorial and white-gold and hotter on the back of your neck, and the air is saturated - breathing feels like breathing through a damp cloth over a warm stone. You will sweat and be immersed at the same time, and you will dehydrate while feeling wet.

Acclimate if you can. If your travel method drops you straight onto the plateau, plan the first 2-3 days to do almost nothing but move slowly, drink constantly, and rest at midday. Don't trust how cool you feel in waist-deep water on the bare, knife-edged peaks. Those are the *only* exposed places, and up there you will burn in minutes.

**Perpetual immersion.** Your feet will be wet from the first hour to the last. Ankle-deep on the flats, waist-deep in the flooded valleys, neck-deep between the young ridges. This is not wading through a river you can cross. There is no shore that stays.

That means:
- No cotton anywhere. Cotton stays cold, heavy, and rots your skin. Bring quick-dry synthetics, merino wool socks you can swap into a dry bag, and a thin wetsuit-style layer or swim top you can wear under looser clothing for chest-deep stretches.
- Wading boots, not trail shoes. You need a puncture-proof, flexible sole for the sharp black stone and raw red earth under clear water, high ankle support for root-mats that give way, and drainage. Sneakers will fill, blister, and be shredded. Bring a second pair of dry camp socks and powder, and treat every small cut on foot as urgent - in warm standing water, nothing dries and nothing heals quickly.
- A staff, 1.8 - 2.2m long. This is your most important tool. You will use it to probe for depth, test whether a woven root-mat will hold you or drop you into a neck-deep pocket, push aside near-touching stems, and brace on a sharp crest. A pole also keeps your pack off your shoulders when you're chest-deep and lets you feel the sharpness before your hand does.
- Gloves, thin and cut-resistant. The ridges are freshly broken, glassy-faced. You can slice a palm just steadying yourself. Underwater trunks and stems are softened by silt, not made safe.

Keep everything you cannot afford to lose in a buoyancy dry-bag worn on your chest, not on your back. When you're neck-deep, your pack is underwater and will drag you. Your food, medical kit, communication device, and one completely dry change of clothes live in that bag.

**Swimming is matter-of-fact, not recreational.** You must be able to float and breaststroke slowly with a pack, for long distances, without panicking in green that continues unchanged below you. Wide, slow arm movements only. Fins are a bad idea - submerged stems, rosettes, and root baskets snag them instantly. A simple mask and snorkel are worth their weight: the water is startlingly clear, the underwater forest looks exactly like the one above it, and you will need to see a drop-off or a sharp ridge *before* you step into it. The light falls through normally, which makes depth very hard to judge.

### 2. Sun and light

Midday light here is ordinary and high and clear. That's the trap. Because the light is coming *through* leaves and through water, you will be in dappled, patchy shade most of the time, muffled and cool-feeling, while equatorial UV is hammering the back of your neck and the tops of your shoulders. You will burn in a mosaic pattern if you're not careful.

Broad-brim hat tied under your chin (it will snag, a floating hat is useless), long sleeves with a waxy, water-shedding finish, and sunscreen re-applied after every deep segment. Polarized lenses help - leaves have exact reflections at the surface, oxygen collects in slow silver films under submerged leaves, and both will blind you at the waterline. At night and deep under canopy, it gets dark fast. Headlamp with a red mode for night, and don't expect stars: canopy touches canopy except in patches, and water holds the sky in perfect clarity below you.

### 3. Movement and navigation

There is no path, no track, no shore to follow, and no water flowing downhill to lead you out. The plateau is young, uplifted, and its drainage hasn't learned its way out yet. Rain arrives as a warm wall without wind, adds a fraction to the sheet of water, and *stays*. A basin that was knee-deep when you marked it will be chest-deep later. It will not run off.

Expect to move 0.5 to 1 km per hour, often less. The world is near-touching by default: a seed falls onto a leaf, onto a root-mat, onto water that carries it an inch before it snags. You will be doing the same. Shoulder-height green pressing in, vines that have forgotten where they end, stems braided into stems, a second canopy at the waterline, a third and fourth below it. Pushing through will be constant and slow.

Use the ridges as your compass. The high, bare, knife-edged peaks and the sharp crests between basins are the only reliably emergent ground. They are dangerous to climb - don't climb them for a view unless you're equipped for rock - but from a crest you can see which basin you're in and which ridge to aim for next. Valleys are traps; they collect water and don't release it.

Electronics will fog, get wet, and be useless under canopy. Bring a physical compass and a waterproof notebook, and mark your route with *removable* biodegradable tape tied high, not low. Mark the waterline on a trunk if you camp, because it will rise. And learn the silence as a navigation aid: sound is muffled and slow here. A drip takes a long time to fall the last inch to the surface. A shout won't carry far. The place makes noise by growth, not by animals. If you need to signal, a whistle in short bursts works better than yelling, but expect no answer. There are almost no animals - an occasional nibble on a leaf, one blind frog-slow shape far below the surface, once. No birdsong, no insect chatter. Don't mistake that quiet for emptiness or safety.

### 4. Shelter, food, and fire

There is nothing built, and nothing you build will stay dry on the ground. Fallen trunks become planters, then platforms for water, then ground again. Cut wood silts over instead of rotting, which means it also won't burn.

- **Sleep in a hammock** slung between two above-water trunks, over a root-mat if you must, with a waterproof tarp above you for the warm rain walls. No ground pad will keep your back dry. Bring a dry bag for your insulating layer, no matter how warm the air is.
- **Bring all your food.** Foraging is unreliable to the point of pointlessness. Density is plant density, not animal density. Leaves are mostly left alone by animals for a reason - assume high plant defenses. Don't eat anything you haven't had identified in a previous visit. Bring dense, salted, pre-packaged calories and electrolytes. You will use more energy than you expect just holding yourself upright in chest-deep water and pushing through green.
- **Do not drink the water, even though you're in it.** It's clear and warm and standing. It is full of algae-soft mats, silt, and plant exudates, and it doesn't move. Immersion cooling is its gift; drinking it is not. Bring purification - filter + chemical backup - and drink on schedule, especially at altitude.
- **Fire is essentially impossible** and, in a place with no built structures and no animals, unnecessary risk. Bring a stove that runs on canisters if you need hot food, and accept that most of your meals will be cold.

### 5. Health in sharp, still water

The greatest physical risks are mundane: cuts on glassy stone, punctures from gravel that hasn't sorted into sand, trench foot, fungal infection, and infected scrapes from stems. Because the water preserves sharpness instead of wearing it smooth, every submerged crest is as sharp as the day it broke.

Keep a small, waterproof medical kit on your chest: antiseptic wipes, waterproof bandages, antifungal cream, blister care, tweezers, pain relief, and up-to-date tetanus. Cover any cut with a waterproof dressing *before* you go back in. Change socks at every rest. Dry your feet aggressively when you get a chance on a ride - which will be rare.

Pollen and plant contact: leaves range from waxy bright above to pale and translucent below. Some root in air, cross the surface, and root again underwater, both ends thriving. Don't touch unknown rosettes or spiral leaves with bare skin if you can avoid it, and wash after brushing through a dense mat. The nibbles in the leaves suggest something small is tasting them; don't volunteer to be the next experiment.

### 6. Mind and time

Time here moves at the speed of patience. A ridge becomes an island, then a reef of crowns, then suggestions under bright water, over a generation of trees. A lake deepens by a fraction per wet season. Nothing happens quickly, and the *feeling* of that slows you down.

That silence, that muffled, slowed sound, that ordinary midday light that makes you want to stay, are psychologically powerful. Ordinary travelers often report the first day as peaceful, the second as disorienting - no birds, no insects, no horizon that stays put, no dry place to sit and be a person on land. Bring reasons to mark time: a waterproof journal, a small book, music player sealed against water (sound won't carry, but *you* will hear it). Set a firm turnaround rule based on distance along ridges, not on how pleasant it feels to float and look. The water continuing the world downward in a slower, brighter register is beautiful, and it is also a very easy place to become complacent about depth.

### 7. Multiverse protocol

You are likely the first human here. There are no huts, traps, cairns, or tools except biological ones - air bladders in stems, waxy coatings, root baskets. That means the ecosystem is closed and sensitive. Seeds germinate in mid-air and finish under water; plant near-touching is the norm. Don't bring soil, seeds, or untreated gear from other worlds, and don't leave anything: no cairns, no cut marks, no tape you can't retrieve. Everything you leave will be colonized, not decomposed, and will become part of this place's architecture in a way it never intended.

If you arrived by portal, assume you may not be able to reopen it from a flooded basin. Your exit is the same as your entry route: back along the ridges you came in on, staff in hand, pack high.

---

**A short checklist to carry in your head:**

Staff, wading boots, gloves, chest dry-bag with food/meds/dry clothes, hammock + tarp, mask/snorkel, hat + long sleeves, sunscreen, electrolytes + purification, compass + notebook + removable markers, headlamp, whistle, waterproof medical kit.

Go slow, stay high when you can, watch the waterline, and expect to be wet, green, and a little breathless the whole time. At midday, the light will be ordinary enough that you'll understand why people stay. Plan, in advance, why you won't.
