Brinemouth is not a place you *sightsee* in the usual sense. You endure it, walk through it, and learn its very small rhythms. The best way to prepare is not for rain, or for industry, or for animals in isolation — it's for all three happening at once, all day, in low grey light, with nowhere green to rest your eyes.

Think: a working port town poured onto reclaimed marsh, paved over, and left to settle in heat. Sea on one side, drainage plain on the other, everything in between flat to the edge of sight. If you arrive by the causeway, the basin opens all at once. That first view is your orientation — stacks, cranes, water, more stacks, silos you can use as landmarks, and a huge amount of empty foreground. From then on you navigate by those industrial shapes, because there are no trees, parks, or hills to navigate by.

Here's how an ordinary human should get ready for it.

### 1. Prepare your body for wet heat, not for a rainstorm

The rain is almost irrelevant. It comes and goes without changing anything fundamental. The ground stays wet, the walls keep weeping, the canals keep boiling. The constant is heat + moisture + dim light.

Noon looks like late evening. The sun is a smudge behind monsoon cloud and factory steam. Sodium yard lamps come on early and burn all day in thin yellow pools. You'll lose track of time quickly if you rely on light. Bring a phone/watch you trust, and a small headlamp — not for night, but for the shadowed side of a warehouse, under a bridge, in a culvert, at 10 a.m.

Heat comes up through concrete long after rain stops. Metal railings are warm in the shade. Puddles don't evaporate; they sit shallow and warm as bathwater and join up. Air doesn't dry sweat, it holds it against your skin. Breathing feels a little short on any exertion.

**Pack for that, not for cold rain:**

* **Clothing:** Loose, synthetic or linen, quick-dry. No cotton jeans, no cotton t-shirts if you can avoid it — they will stay clammy for days. Long loose pants and a long-sleeve overshirt you can roll up. You'll want coverage against mosquitoes, against splashes from canals and pipes, and against scrapes on rusted iron, but you cannot wear a heavy waterproof jacket. A light poncho or packable rain shell is enough for a downpour. Everything will be damp by evening no matter what.
* **Footwear is the decision that matters most.** Not sandals, not trainers, not leather shoes. You need waterproof, ankle-support boots with a very thick, grippy sole. The thickness is for heat radiating through concrete, and the grip is for algae-grey slick on wet concrete, for spalled and patched pavement, for rail lines sinking into mud between sleepers, and for soft reclaimed ground. Gaiters help against canal splash and culvert mud. Bring at least two pairs of quick-dry socks and a small anti-fungal foot powder. Trench foot, blisters, and fungal rashes are the classic Brinemouth injuries, not dramatic ones.
* **Hat with a neck flap, and sunglasses.** Yes, sunglasses under cloud — UV is diffuse, and glare bounces off every flooded lot and canal. You'll fog them up constantly. Wipe them.
* **Electrolytes, not just water.** You'll sweat without feeling dry. Carry 2-3 liters even for short walks, with salt packets. Your own drinkable water — canal water, puddle water, flooded-lot water are all undrinkable, and brown and warm. Don't wade barefoot. The water is shallow, but it's full of bacteria, fish waste, factory effluent, and things you can't see. That includes the flooded lots that look like empty shortcuts. They aren't.
* **Microfiber towel, dry bag, ziplocks.** Everything electronic, and your multiverse documents, need to live in a dry bag. Humidity + factory steam will film camera lenses, corrode contacts, and grow mould on paper. Silica gel packs in your bag help. Dry your boots outside your lodging every night — laundry here never truly dries, the worker-house laundry hung out stays damp, so don't count on anything air-drying indoors.

Give yourself 2-3 days to acclimatize. Walk slower than you think you should. Stand still in the midday heat for too long and the concrete will cook your feet. Seek the only shade there is: the north side of a tank, under a bridge, in the shadow of idle machinery — but see animal notes below.

### 2. Health: the quiet, cumulative hazards

There are no dramatic predators. The risks are cumulative and low-level.

**Insects:** Mosquitoes and midges hang over every still puddle in visible clouds. When you pass, they lift and resettle. Dawn and dusk are worst. Wear repellent, and at dusk add long sleeves. You'll also see water beetles in the canals and clouds of grain insects around silos. Don't open food near silos or drains if you don't want company.

**Skin and lungs:** Black mould blooms on north-facing walls where sun never reaches. Bacterial slime makes slow canal corners grey-green and slippery. White salt flowers effloresce on brick. Oxidised copper roofs, filmed glass, sulphur-yellow refinery earth, salt tide-marks on tanks — everything is mineral, nothing photosynthetic, so you won't get much pollen, but you will get dust, steam, and mould spores. If you have asthma or mould allergy, bring a simple dust mask for grain dust, refinery areas, and smelter yards. Wash hands and feet after any contact with canal water.

**Waterborne risk:** The water table is two spades' depth down. Dig anywhere and you find water. Canals rise on high tide or after heavy rain to lap the curbs. Pumps run often on concrete plinths to keep cellars and underpasses from filling. The whole basin would drown in a week if the pumps stopped.

Learn the local rhythm: fish — air-breathing catfish, carp, tilapia — boil at the surface especially when a factory discharges a fresh surge twice a day, and the whole canal suddenly moves. That means also: don't stand on a low canal wall at discharge time, don't trust a dry-looking underpass in the afternoon, and if you hear pumps go strangely quiet or stop, don't linger in low areas. Ask locally for tide times on arrival and keep the causeway route in mind. It's your high, paved exit.

### 3. Moving around a town that is mostly empty

On a map Brinemouth looks dense. In person it is spread thin. Firebreaks, chemical separation distances, flood lots, drum and scrap yards, empty concrete aprons for expansion that never came — you will walk five minutes between one factory fence and the next past flooded lots where water lies still and brown and reflects nothing but cloud.

That emptiness is deceptive:

* **Distances feel short, walks are long.** No shade, no benches, no green to cut through. Plan water and rest accordingly.
* **Don't shortcut across flooded lots or chemical separation land**, even when they look like the obvious route. The ground is reclaimed peat, silt, and rubble under thin paving. It can be soft, contaminated, have hidden drains, oil stains, and water snakes cruising the edges. Herons and egrets stand knee-deep in them for a reason. You are not meant to be there.
* **Watch your footing constantly.** Asphalt laid over asphalt, bricks weeping in dark streaks, corrugated iron rusted in vertical tears, pipes sweating condensation and dripping steadily as if raining inside them. That drip is everywhere — don't stand under pipes, tanks, or warehouse eaves for long, you'll be dripped on in a place where it isn't raining.
* **Industrial zones are not public.** Most factories run themselves, with a muted throb, a distant clank, brakes squealing two streets away swallowed by damp air. Fences mean keep out, both for safety and courtesy. Don't climb silos or tank tops for views, however tempting. Rail sidings and truck depots are active even when they look idle.
* **Navigation:** Use silos, the power station, the tallest cracker towers. The horizon is uninterrupted — stacks, cranes, water. Coming from the causeway the first time, stop and fix those landmarks. At ground level, everything looks the same grey-brown.

A bicycle with mudguards is the locals' solution for the long gaps, but for a first visit walking is safer and lets you hear the place: water moving with the tide, steam venting in soft plumes that never quite rise, the slow swing of a crane, endless drip, fish rippling.

### 4. The animals — crowded at ground level, quiet at yours

The town has no plants, but it is full of animals feeding on what industry discards. You'll notice them more than you hear them.

* **Rats** by the hundreds in culverts, fed by grain spills. They are not aggressive, but don't put hands into culverts or grain piles.
* **Cats and dogs**, well-fed on scraps, sleep in the shade of idle machinery. They will lift their heads to watch you and the rats, then go back to sleep. Don't pet, feed, or disturb a sleeping animal in a shady machinery corner — it's the one cool spot and they'll defend it.
* **Fish** boil constantly in warm canals. It's fascinating and a little unsettling. Don't try to feed or touch them.
* **Geckos and skinks** cover warm walls in the evening in lamp-light, perfectly still until they are not. Harmless, beautiful in that mineral palette. Leave them alone.
* **Frogs** call briefly from storm drains and stop when a truck passes. **Crabs** work muddy culvert mouths at low tide. **Water snakes** cruise edges. All are reasons to keep hands and feet out of water and mud.
* **Birds** are everywhere overhead in unhurried numbers: feral pigeons on every ledge, mynas and sparrows in rafters, swifts under eaves, gulls and terns over the harbour, bats flickering out from under bridges at dusk. It's an active, quiet place — the activity is low and constant.

Wash up after being around any of these. Keep your own food sealed, especially grain, fish, and scraps.

### 5. Food, sleep, and human stuff

This is a port and worker town — family houses two-storey, laundry always out, people used to industrial routine. Most strangers will leave you alone; don't wander into yards or houses. The fish cannery, cold stores, and rendering plant mean the smell changes block to block: fish, grain dust, hot metal, sulphur, steam. Bring a handkerchief, seal food well.

Food and water from shops and houses will be boiled/filtered — don't assume tap water in a pump-dependent basin is safe without asking. Bring a filter bottle if you have one.

Sleep will be damp. Sheets won't cool you. If your lodging has a fan, use it. Bring earplugs — not for noise, but pumps, distant trains, and dripping pipes carry far in damp air, and the quiet itself can be oppressive. That emptiness, the lack of any green, the constant warm grey, affects people mentally after a day or two. It's not dramatic, it's monotonous. Bring a book (in a ziplock), music, something to do in the still hours. Many travelers find early morning, just after first light when it's slightly cooler and geckos still on walls, and the hour before dusk when lamps come on, the most pleasant times to walk.

### 6. Multiverse traveler specifics

* **Leave living things behind.** Quarantine matters doubly here. Don't bring seeds, plants, or soil — they wouldn't survive anyway drowned in alkaline water or baked on masonry, and you don't want to introduce a species adapted to another world's discards into this food web.
* **Document everything off-world.** Because humidity swells paper, photograph your permits and keep originals in a dry bag. Keep electronics in a hard dry case; mould on a lens or sensor is permanent.
* **Insurance and emergency plan:** Heat illness and a twisted ankle on soft ground are your most likely emergencies, not animal attacks. Know where the causeway is, where the nearest staffed factory gate or house cluster is, and carry a charged light. If pumps fail or tide is unusually high, leave low ground immediately.
* **Leave no trace, but expect to pick up mud.** Boots will carry silt and salt. Clean them before your next threshold.

### Quick checklist

Thick-soled waterproof boots + extra socks + gaiters
Loose quick-dry clothing, poncho, hat, sunglasses
Dry bag, ziplocks, silica gel, microfiber towel
Headlamp
Electrolytes, filter bottle, sealed snacks
Repellent, anti-fungal powder, basic first aid, dust mask
Handkerchief, earplugs, something to read
Tide times, causeway route, local contact for pumps/tides

---

Go slowly. Brinemouth rewards patience — the moment when flood lots reflect the cloud perfectly just after rain, when herons don't move, when fish boil in unison at discharge, when geckos appear on a warm wall under a sodium lamp. It's not beautiful in a green, scenic way. It's settled, weathered, in balance with damp, and intensely alive at ankle height.

Prepare for wet heat, keep out of the water, respect the empty spaces, and you'll be fine.
