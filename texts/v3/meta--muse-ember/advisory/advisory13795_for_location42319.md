Prepare less for a city than for a wet, living rock that happens to have been built on for a very long time. That is the central adjustment. There are no shops, no shelters you can book, no lights, no noise, no crowds to follow, and no one whose job it is to help a stranger. Ordinary human strength will not be tested mainly by predators or distance. It will be tested by cold damp, slippery stone, disorientation, and by how long a human body can stay warm, dry enough, fed, and mentally unhurried in a place that never dries.

### 1. Dress for a place that is always just after rain

Everything glistens. At dawn cold stone condenses a film of water on walls, lintels, and boulders; at noon the thin high sun warms the tops and it runs off; in the evening it forms again. You will not be in rain all day, and you will never be dry for long. Cotton is useless here — it stays wet, cold, and heavy. Dress as the inhabitants do, in heavy woven wool, only better engineered for walking.

- **Base layer: merino wool, top and bottom.** No cotton, no linen, no casual synthetics that lose warmth when saturated. Wool stays insulating damp, which is the best you can hope for.
- **Insulation: a wool fleece or thick wool sweater** you can add under a shell. Even in high summer the wind cuts unbroken across the plateau and the temperature falls several degrees by evening.
- **Outer layer: a breathable waterproof jacket and over-trousers, not a plastic poncho.** A poncho will trap your own condensation and you will be wet from inside. You want a shell you can vent at midday when the sun is normal and the air is still damp.
- **Hat, neck gaiter or scarf, and warm gloves.** The wind is cool, still, and constant, and much travel is exposed moor between districts. Your head, neck, and hands lose heat fastest in wind and drizzle.
- **One complete set of sleep clothes, kept absolutely dry.** This is not luxury. It is the difference between recovery and hypothermia. Put them in a waterproof stuff sack inside a second waterproof sack inside your pack. Change into them at night, however damp you feel, and change out in the morning. Never sleep in the clothes you walked in.

Accept that everything you own will acquire a permanent damp smell. Pack cloth towels sparingly — a small quick-dry microfiber will re-wet within minutes in your hand. A small wool towel is more honest here.

### 2. Feet: your expedition will succeed or fail here

Steps are hollowed concave by centuries of feet. Pavestones are lifted by frost and laid sleek by rain. Lichen and algae paint wet rock green, black, and rust-brown, and it is slicker than it looks. The water table is half a metre down; the ground literally weeps.

- **Sturdy, waterproof, ankle-support boots with an aggressive lug sole.** Trainers, walking shoes, or smooth-soled boots will betray you on a concave wet step within an hour.
- **Three pairs of thick wool socks per day you expect to walk, minimum.** Rotate and carry the spares in the dry sack. Wet feet in constant cool damp lead to trench foot, fungal infection, and blisters that will not heal because they never dry.
- **Gaiters.** They keep the finger-wide streams running in street grooves and the seep off your ankles when you step off a terrace. The streets *are* the drainage, so you will be walking in shallow water often.
- **Foot care kit, used daily:** zinc or anti-chafe cream, plasters, a needle and thread for blisters, antifungal powder, and a way to air your feet at midday when the sun is out. Keep toenails short; your toes will curl in boots on endless downhill terraces.
- **Expect your boots never to fully dry.** Bring removable insoles you can swap at noon. Do not plan to dry them by a fire at night — there is effort and risk in that, discussed below.

Conditions will be slickest at dawn during condensation, and again in the evening when the stone re-wets. Walk slower then. Most falls here will not be dramatic. They will be a foot sliding off a polished hollow in a silent alley, and a twisted ankle miles from the nearest person who might notice.

### 3. Pack small, soft, and sideways

In the middle districts shoulders of roofs almost meet and you walk alleys sideways with a pack. Doorways are low. Ground floors are sunk half below street level; a house's upper storey is another family's doorway. Covered passages run under other buildings for hundreds of metres.

Carry a slim, soft, 35-45 litre pack, close to your back, with nothing hard, glass, or dangly. No glass jars, no box cameras, no rigid cases. Kneel, turn sideways, and duck without thinking. A bulky pack will scrape wet walls, knock jars from niches, and get you wedged in a passage that was never intended for you.

Navigation is your largest problem. There is no centre to aim for. Districts are separated by miles of open stony moor that is geologically the same urban layer, and you can walk for days inside the built layer without leaving it. Streets do not follow a grid; they follow drainage. Learn to read them as the builders did:

- **Lowest line = channel = wettest, mainest route.** Water runs there.
- **Highest points = dry by design** — granaries, shrines, lookouts, storage. They are high because they must stay above the seep.
- **To move safely, go up and across dry terraces, not along channels.** To find your way back, remember which terrace you descended.

Bring a compass, a paper map in oiled cloth if you can make or copy one, and a stub of pencil — pencil writes on damp paper, pens do not. A synthetic-paper notebook will survive better than ordinary paper. Phones and other machines have no signal, no charging, no use here, and glass will gleam in a place that has no glass. Keep any device for the outside world in a waterproof pouch and do not rely on it. The plateau makes its own weather; one ward may be in sun while another, ten kilometres across, is in low cloud that will not lift until late afternoon. You will be caught in a local mist without warning, and sound dies at 0.2 on any honest scale, so a companion or a path behind you can vanish silently. Start early, keep a margin of hours, and plan to be in camp before the evening re-wetting.

### 4. Shelter: assume every house is closed and cold

Most doors are closed. Most houses are soaked, cold, and only seasonally used. The few inhabitants you see are slow, low-voiced, and seem to be tending more than living. A closed house does not mean an empty house in the sense of abandoned; it means someone will return, or someone is keeping it.

Do not break in, do not lift a stone shutter, do not lower yourself through a house's upper doorway because the roof looks like a street. Do not camp in a ground floor, which will seep in spring and will always be the coldest, dampest part of the building.

If you must bivouac, camp as a passing animal would, on a high, dry, stone terrace outside the heaviest building, and leave no trace:

- A low tent or tarp weighted with stones rather than pegged — the soil is thin over stone, the water table is just below, and pegs will bring water up.
- A sleeping pad with real insulation underneath you. Wet stone conducts cold mercilessly. Insulation below matters more than the bag above.
- Never camp in a channel, a groove, a covered passage, or a tunnel where water runs. Rain is local and can arrive uphill of you, and wind goes through those passages like breath through a flute, sometimes gusting hard.
- Never dig. Half a metre down is water, but digging collapses the terraces that hold the plateau together and creates a useless black hole.

There are no lights at night. Your eyes will adjust if you let them. If you need a lamp for a dark passage or a dangerous step, use a small, dim, shielded one and use it briefly. A bright beam in a dark city is intrusive, it destroys your own night vision, and it marks you to anyone who may be watching from a doorway — and someone may be. The inhabitants are quiet watchers. Keep your voice as low as theirs.

### 5. Water and food: abundant water, almost none of it drinkable

You will be surrounded by water and chronically thirsty if you are careless. Streams braid between rocks, run in street grooves, spill over terrace edges, and fill hollows with peat-stained pools that do not evaporate so much as sink. Cisterns hold still, dark water. Pond life lives in that water, invisible unless you look closely.

**Treat all local water as untreated.** Let peat-stained water settle, filter it through cloth if you must, then purify it — filter plus chemical treatment, or boiling. Do not drink directly from a street channel (it carries roof-water), do not drink from a cistern (it is someone's storage, and communal), and do not bathe in the black pools. Do not wash yourself, your clothes, or your kit in city water systems; you will contaminate them and foul your own skin with pond organisms. Wash hands after touching old plaster, stone, and handprints worn smooth in walls, and keep that habit.

Food: there is no market, no shop, no resupply. The sparse sheep and shaggy goats on the moor are not a food resource, and there is no forest to forage in — only cushion plants, rosettes, stiff tufts, and ankle-high heather that the wind crops. Bring all food for your stay plus at least fifty percent extra for being weather-trapped. The plateau can keep its own cloud until late afternoon, for days.

Bring cold, calorie-dense, damp-proof food: oatcakes, hard cheese, jerky or smoked meat, nuts, dried fruit, chocolate, salt, and a thermos if you want one hot drink a day. Store it in the dry sack; mould grows happily on anything left in a pack pocket. Expect to eat slowly, sitting against a wall out of the wind. You will burn more energy than you think climbing wall upon wall upon wall.

### 6. Fire, tools, and what not to bring

Chimneys are low and rare and give off almost no smoke. There are niches for lamps that are never lit. Vegetation is sparse and tough and holds the soil. A campfire is impractical, smoky, conspicuous, and potentially forbidden. Do not light the niche lamps. Do not pull plants for fuel, do not move a stone from a retaining wall to make a hearth — those walls lean a degree into the soil and have stopped; shifting one restarts erosion downhill.

If you carry a small stove for boiling water, keep it tiny, brief, and sheltered, and treat it as an emergency and hygiene tool, not a comfort. Bring no glass, no wire knick-knacks, no fireworks, no flares you would use casually. A whistle for a true emergency in mist is reasonable — the quiet here means a short blast carries through tunnels and channels surprisingly well — but routine noise is a form of disrespect in a place where sound is already rare.

### 7. Animals, plants, and ground

There is almost nothing to fear from animals. You may walk a whole morning and see nothing larger than a lizard. A raptor crosses high and silent, alpine choughs nest in empty niches, sheep move far apart on the moor. Leave them entirely alone. Do not approach a nest in a niche, do not chase, do not try to milk or catch a goat. Their slowness and distance are part of the place.

The ground is tended. Where there is soil it is held in a terrace, wet and black and carefully kept. Walk on stone and established paths. Do not shortcut across a terrace, cut into a bank, or sit on a low wall as if it were a bench for long — you will add weight where the plateau has already found its balance. Erosion here is not an event but the established state, nail-width a year; a thousand visitors doing a little damage is exactly how a crust like this is lost.

### 8. People and presences

The saturation here is not of people but of presences: stone faces, doorways, steps, channels, niches, hollows for jars, handprints worn smooth. After a few days of walking alone in that damp hush, hearing only a single drip every few seconds, cloth rustling on a distant figure, wind sighing through a channel, you may begin to feel watched by the stonework itself. That feeling is common, understandable, and not in itself a danger. Prepare for it the way you prepare for altitude: with patience, rest, food, and a journal to write in. People in extreme quiet begin to misjudge time, distance, and their own footsteps.

If you meet an inhabitant — at a channel, carrying stone, in a doorway keeping out of the mist — approach slowly, from front, in the open. Speak once, low. Do not shout, do not hail across a courtyard, do not demand help or directions. If they do not answer, that is an answer. Do not enter their doorway, do not touch their jars or tools, do not sit in their dry high places. They are tending the city as much as living in it; a visitor who moves slowly, works quietly, and leaves the channels clear is far less alarming than one who points, photographs, or asks many questions.

### 9. Timing

- **Time of day:** Walk in the midday window, when the sun is a normal sun with normal shadows and the stone is at its least slick. Expect dawn condensation and evening mist. Be back where you intend to sleep before the evening re-wetting turns the pale patterned city uniformly dark.
- **Season:** Avoid spring, when water seeps up through floors, and the frost months, when heave lifts pavestones. Summer is still cool and windy, but it is the least hostile. Winter snow does not stay white; it becomes more damp, and everything ices in hollows.
- **Duration:** Judge distances in terraces and hours, not kilometres. Districts that look close across the plateau may be a day's wet walking apart, and a mist may separate them in minutes.

### A small checklist to pack

Merino base layers and sleep set in a dry sack; breathable waterproof shell; wool hat, gaiter, gloves; waterproof lug-sole boots, gaiters, spare insoles, 3+ pairs wool socks per day; slim soft pack; compass and pencil map; dim shielded lamp; water filter and purifier; cold food plus 50% reserve in dry sack; insulated pad and bag; tarp or low tent; foot care and basic first aid; tetanus up to date; paper/synthetic notebook; whistle; all waste in, all waste out — including toilet waste, which never goes in a channel, cistern, or pool.

Go gently, go slowly, stay warm, stay found, and assume no rescue, no resupply, and no dry feet. If you can accept being wet, quiet, and small in a vast, patient, weather-making stone landscape for days at a time, you are prepared. If you cannot, do not go — this is not a place that will hurry to meet you halfway.
