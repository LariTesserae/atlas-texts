This is a paradoxical place to prepare for, and that is the real danger. It will not look or feel alien. The sky will be ordinary blue, the sun ordinary white, the shadows will fall where you expect them to fall. And yet almost every rule you carry from an ordinary lowland, animal-filled, weather-driven world will quietly not apply.

Prepare for warm, wet, high, still, and silent — all at once, and all the time. Day after day.

### 1. What your body has to get used to

**Altitude without cold.** This is a high equatorial shelf. It is warm up here, warmer than it should be for the height, and the sun feels closer. That fools people. On the climb you will feel it in your chest first — short of breath on exertion, a bit light-headed, talking takes effort. It is not cold mountain air, it is thin warm air. Do not arrive and immediately haul a pack up hand-cut basalt steps.

Give yourself, if your transit allows it, a few days of moderate walking before a heavy climb. Train for a week or two before travel with hill repeats, step-ups, and walking with your pack on wet stairs and uneven stone. An ordinary human does not need to be an athlete, but you do need to be able to climb slowly, breathe through your nose, and stop without embarrassment. You will need to stop.

Buy sunscreen and use it even on misty mornings. At the equator, near midday, the sun is almost overhead. Shadows shrink to nothing and offer no direction or relief. UV reflects hard off wet stone, water in the ditches, and pale fresh basalt. Hat with a brim, sunglasses with good side coverage, lip balm with SPF, and sun cream on ears, neck, and the backs of hands. Your skin will stay damp without really sweating — do not mistake that for heat protection. You are still burning.

**Wetness as a condition, not weather.** There are no storms here. There is no rain season to wait out, no dry season to plan for. The plateau is so recently lifted and so full of springs that water is always coming up through the rock, sitting level in pools and channels, and coming up again. Everything is damp and stays damp. Even inside houses, cloth stays cool, wood takes on a dark polished color and never fully dries, condensation runs down walls in the morning.

That means you do not need storm gear, snow gear, or a tent fly for wind. You need damp-management gear:

- Synthetic or merino clothing. Cotton, denim, and heavy canvas will stay wet and cold against your skin for days. Pack two or three changes of socks and underwear in a dry bag, even for a short visit, and change feet at midday whether you think you need to or not.
- Quick-drain, high-grip boots with broken-in soles. The hand-cut steps in the cliff are slick and polished only by feet. The town causeways are flat basalt slabs laid by hand, wet, slightly springy underfoot. The meadow between causeways is saturated — footprints fill with water. You will be wet to the ankle often. Gaiters help, waterproof socks help more. Bring a spare set of laces and a small brush for the tread.
- A light mist shell, not a heavy rain jacket. Mist ghosts off the rock and off your own arms. You will get damp from air alone. A breathable shell you can put on in the hollows at morning and evening is enough, and goes away in seconds.
- Anti-chafe powder or balm, blister care, and something to dry feet at night. Sleeping in a damp room on a damp floor will give you athlete's foot and trench-foot-like soreness faster than any cold will. Elevate yourself on a pad, air your feet, change socks before sleep.

Your camera, glasses, and any multiverse device will fog. The air is near saturation, still, and warm. Keep electronics in a dry bag with silica, carry lens cloths, and expect fogging every time you go from a cool house interior to the warm meadow. Do not count on solar charging — the light is normal, but the hollows fill with white mist morning and evening, and there is no wind to push it off.

### 2. The climb — the only door in

There is no road, no cart route, no animal track that helps you. The cliff face is new: sharp, angular, pale where it has recently split, black where wet. It weeps in a thin sheet, beading out of a thousand cracks, dripping at the bottom into moss. There is no scree to soften a fall, no soil to catch you.

* Climb in mid-morning, in daylight, rested. Equatorial daylight is roughly twelve hours, and the mist returns to the same hollows by evening. Do not start late.
* Use hands deliberately, not by trailing them along the wall. Fresh basalt edges will cut a hand. Thin cut-resistant gloves can help, but wet gloves lose grip — many climbers do better with clean bare hands and trimmed nails, testing each step before committing weight.
* Carry nothing loose. Clefts are clean and unfilled; a dropped water bottle, tool, or device can fall through and be gone. Everything in a pack with zip, clip, or tie.
* Pace by the talk test. If you cannot speak a short sentence, rest. Ten slow steps, breathe, look at the drip, continue. The quiet is part of the climb — drip, drip, drip, and far below the muffled rush where the sheet-water leaves the plateau edge. People from the town above wear those steps smooth; they are not wide.
* Plan the descent as the harder half. Downhill on wet, cut stone with tired legs is where ankles turn and fingers are used as brakes against sharp rock. Descend rested, unhurried, and not in evening mist.

Do not camp at the base on angular rock expecting to peg stakes into soil. There is no deep soil at the cliff foot. If you must stop, stop on the plateau with the town's permission.

### 3. Food, water, and what is *not* there

The lushness is misleading. The plants are mostly ferns, grasses, broad-leaved shrubs, mosses, and water plants — the spore-bearing, wind-spread, water-spread kinds that can manage without any animal to pollinate or eat them. There are no birds over the meadows, no flies to your face, no bees in the shrubs, no beetles under stones, no frogs in pools, no fish in clear water. The fruit trees in gardens are hand-pollinated by people with brushes, because there is nothing else to do it. Flowers are mostly pale and unscented.

That has practical consequences:

- **Do not forage.** Green and abundant does not mean edible. Do not assume fern shoots, pool plants, or shrub berries are food. The town grows root crops, greens, grains, and hand-pollinated fruit in level, saturated terraces. Eat what is offered, and nothing else.
- **Expect a plant-based, low-animal diet.** There is no livestock, no chickens, no dog. No meat, no dairy, no eggs to be had locally, and no fishing to fall back on. Your ordinary diet will feel thin in protein and calories if you stay more than a day or two. Bring lightweight, high-energy supplements of your own: nuts (in sealed bags, never planted or scattered), protein bars, electrolytes, B12 if you normally rely on animal products.
- **Water is everywhere, drinkable water is not.** The spring channels from the weeping cliffs to basins and fields carry the town's water. The pools and marshy hollows are level and still month upon month, full of water plants, and a perfect place to lose a footprint. Do not drink from pools, ditches, or terrace water, no matter how clear it looks. Carry your own bottle and a purifier. Ask where the town draws drinking water — the plaza basin is always full to the same mark — and do not wash in it.
- **Pack out human waste and wash water until you understand the system.** Channels go under roads in clay pipes and run open in stone; they are drinking supply. Never use a pool, channel, or terrace as a toilet or washing place. Ask discreetly where latrines are and where people wash. When in doubt, carry it out.

Because there are no insects, you do not need repellent, nets, or anti-malarials for this place's fauna — there is none. Do not bring them in place of the things you *do* need: tetanus care for basalt cuts, blister and fungal treatment, altitude headache relief, and basic wound care. The town's medicine is stone, wood, bone, woven fiber, and clay pots. Bring your own kit, sealed and discreet.

The same absence is why multiverse biosecurity matters so much here. This ecology has never had animal pollinators, decomposers in thatch, or fish. Bringing a pet, an aquarium snail, seeds in your pocket, soil on your boots from another world, or even a sticky insect hitchhiker on camping gear could do damage that this equilibrium cannot absorb. Clean boots, clean pack, no live things, no loose seeds or soil. If you are ill in a way that could spread person-to-person, postpone. A small town with no livestock and limited contact is not a place to be a casual carrier.

### 4. Social and practical preparation

The town is organized — straight stone-lined channels, low retaining walls terracing meadows to a constant water depth, causeways wide enough for two people crossing and recrossing, small arched stone bridges — but it is sparse. Houses do not crowd. Fifty meters of wet green to a neighbor, a hundred to the next. The plaza is a large flat paving with a central basin, a storehouse, a communal cistern, a few open workshops.

Everything moves at walking pace, and people speak low. Prepare to match that.

- Stay on causeways. Stepping off to cut across a terrace can break a level wall, sink into saturated ground, or damage a crop. Cross fields only by invitation.
- Expect distance to feel larger. Between house clusters are long, unbuilt intervals — an empty furrowed field, a wide pool, a fenced-off meadow that has no fence. You will look across the green table, town-and-valley scale, and think it is close. It is not close unless you walk it. A simple hand-drawn map and compass help; the meadow patches in mist look identical. Sun overhead at noon gives you almost no shadow to navigate by.
- Do not expect wheels, metal, or speed. Houses are stone at the base, dark timber above, thatched with meadow grass, joined without nails or iron. Tools are chipped stone, wood, bone, fiber. Even your pack frame, stove, and zips will look foreign. Keep functional metal gear closed away, do not display weapons, do not try to trade tools or hardware. The houses are repaired with stone from the same fresh-cut face and look new because nothing destroys them — no fire weather, no wind, no insects in the thatch, no freeze. The town values a balance of uplift, water, building, and tending. Imposing new technology, however helpful it seems to you, breaks that balance.
- Bring skills more useful than things: mending, weaving, redigging a channel with a stone spade under supervision, and, if invited, hand-pollination with a soft brush. Fruit is labor, not surplus. Do not pick, prune, or handle blossoms roughly.
- Learn greetings in advance if your transit offers them, and expect to converse quietly, outdoors, standing still. There is no dog to greet you, no livestock to ignore. People step on wet stone, carry water in woven baskets, mend thatch. They do not rush to fill silence. Neither should you.
- Land lightly. Do not build, dig, dam, plant, or rearrange stone. The gullies have not softened, the pools do not rise or fall, the cliff weeps in the same places. A traveler who tries to improve a channel will flood a terrace that was perfectly level.

### 5. Mental preparation — the hardest part

Most visitors prepare for danger. Prepare instead for emptiness.

You will get used to walking through a landscape that is biologically complete on the surface and empty underneath, and then you keep walking. No birdsong to tell you where you are. No flies to wave away when you stand still to write or think. Leaves too wet and air too still to rustle. The only sounds will be a drop into a basin, a footstep on a wet slab, the far thread of water falling off the edge.

For an ordinary nervous system, that stillness becomes loud. People report listening more closely, then feeling their own breathing, then feeling lonely in a green, saturated world that should, by all your instincts, be full of life. Plan for it: carry a journal, a well-worn book, or work you can do with your hands. Bring a small, quiet personal ritual. Do not bring loud music or make calls on speaker — in that quiet, your voice carries and is noticed, and the town's low-spoken norm is a form of courtesy, not just habit.

There is also the strangeness of permanence. Day after day the light is normal, temperature warm and even, pools level. It is a young geology with old habits, held in equilibrium. There is no best season to catch it at its peak, no storm to wait for, no migration to see. If you need novelty, you will be disappointed. If you can learn to notice the sheen on the cliff, the exact level of water in a ditch, the paleness of a flower that has no need to attract anyone, the place opens.

### Short packing list

**Wear/carry:** broken-in waterproof boots + gaiters + 2 spare socks in dry bag, synthetic/merino clothes, light mist shell, wide-brim hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, thin cut-resistant gloves, daypack (no wheels anywhere in your journey), headlamp — small, for misty hollows — spare laces.

**Pack:** dry bags and silica, sleeping pad and waterproof bivy (for damp, not bugs), water bottle + purifier, high-energy food and electrolytes, blister/fungal/wound/altitude kit, lens cloths, notebook and hand map, brush for boots, small towel that dries fast, trowel or carry-out waste kit until you know the town system.

**Leave behind:** pets, livestock feed, fishing gear, insect repellent as a primary concern, heavy rain/storm gear, cotton clothing, loose seeds, soil, fire-making kit expecting dry wood, weapons to display, anything you intend to trade or plant.

**Bring in attitude:** slow feet, quiet voice, clean boots, willingness to be helped by people who know every channel by sight, and acceptance that you will be damp, a little breathless, and pleasantly, uncomfortably quiet for days.

Come rested, go down rested, and do not try to make the place move faster, dryer, or livelier than it is. It has reached its balance between uplift and water, building and tending. Your job as a visitor is simply not to disturb it — and to learn, on foot, on wet slabs, how to feel at home in a green world with no animals in it at all.
