### A Practical Guide for the Ordinary Human Visitor to Iskara, Inside the Seed

Iskara will not hurt you with monsters. There are no predators larger than a person in there, and the only animal that ever leaves is a small glider that nests on the Bell Rim. What will hurt you, or at least defeat you, is the combination of heat, thirst, scale, and above all, sound — arriving in a place that looks like a jungle but has no water, looks like a city but is mostly empty, and looks like a seed but is a country.

Prepare for that inversion, and you will be fine. Prepare for a normal desert oasis, and you will walk in unready.

#### 1. Before You Come: The Kit

Think light, but think *self-sufficient*. A few hundred Resin-people live inside, far apart, in thick handmade houses. There is no market town, no inn street, no clinic, no restaurant. There is a stall with no roof, a loom, a kiln, drying bricks. If you need it, bring it.

**For sound — this is your most important item:**
- Custom or high-fidelity wax-and-fiber plugs, like the locals wear, *plus* a spare set, *plus* a hard case. Children in Iskara learn to plug their ears early and to speak with their hands. You should do the same in advance.
- For a first visit, also bring over-ear defenders for any necessary time on the Reed Terraces or Bell Rim. The low fundamental is not just heard, it is felt in your teeth, ribs, and soles. Plugs soften the harmonics, they do not stop the pressure.
- A notebook and a small set of emergency hand-signs. Midday, speech is useless. A shout across an empty ledge may answer you a minute later, thinner and higher. Do not plan to call for help at noon.

**For thirst — the central trick of the place:**
- Carry *all* your own drinking water. Iskara is lush and has no water. No rain enters, no spring rises. The floor is cracked and dry like the plain outside, only darker and resin-stained.
- The local drink is not water: clear beads that condense on bell-vine lips, and sweet amber sap that sweats from cushion moss and shell when the pod expands. It tastes of warm resin and green stems, and it is their drink, oil, and glue. You may be offered a taste — accept politely, in small quantities, only with permission. It is their reservoir, and it will not hydrate you like water. Do not try to squeeze moss for yourself as a survival technique.
- Bring salts/electrolytes, lip balm, and a bandana or respirator. Outside, fine flour-dust coats your mouth. Inside, a fine green spore-snow falls constantly on the updraft, never melts, and gets in eyes, nose, and lungs. Bring goggles or glasses as well as sunglasses.

**For the plain, the keyhole, and the rings:**
- Sun hat, veil or dust mask, long sleeves, and sunglasses for the white plain outside. The sun is a punishment, the wind is hot and arrhythmic and raises glittering dust. You feel the pod humming in your teeth *before* you enter when the sun is high. That is your warning to move.
- Gloves, knee and elbow pads, and a small daypack for the keyhole crawl. The northern slit is worn into a low resinous keyhole, just large enough to crawl through. The shell there is a hand's breadth or more of warm, faintly sticky amber flesh that smells of hot honey, bark, and old oil. Beads sweat and harden on the inner wall. You do not want to do this with a large pack, bare knees, or bare hands.
- Boots with real grip. Inside, ledges of packed shell-pulp and mud-brick are polished by feet and by the daily expansion of the shell. Loose resin beads dance off ledges at midday. Cushion moss is velvet a hand thick and crunches when dry, but the slopes *between* rings are steep, vegetated, resin-slick, and explicitly too slick to climb. You will travel only by the streets between ribs, spiral brick ramps, ladders, pulley-ropes, and rope bridges. Never try to shortcut up a slope.
- A headlamp with a warm setting, a sleeping pad, and a light insulating layer. By day the amber-filtered light is everywhere and there are no true shadows — your eyes will water like inside a lantern, and depth perception is strange. At night the walls go deep dusky brown, the shell contracts and ticks, and the best time for talk and travel arrives, but it is not bright.
- Your own food and toilet kit, to take out. Bring dry wipes; you will get resin on you. Do not urinate on the shell or use moss as a toilet. The whole ecology runs on silicate mist exhaled by the warm shell flesh, caught by vibrating green. Waste and trampling are not neutral here. Ask where the Resin-people want waste to go, and if in doubt, pack it out.
- No loud instruments, speakers, or drones. The resonators in walls and clay flutes in pipes are tuned to the pod's note. They are not decoration. Adding your own music at midday is both rude and potentially harmful to the gardens, which live *because* they can hear the drone.

Do not bring seeds or plants to try farming. A garden in Iskara that is not piped, not resonated, and not allowed to hear the drone will languish and die no matter how much light it gets. A garden hung beside a tuned flute will double in a season. The people are low-tech and highly adapted, not backward — their sound-farming is physiological fact, not superstition. Respect the pipes, flutes, and resonators; do not drape laundry over a hook-fern, block a hollow rib with your pack, or lean against a resonator to rest.

#### 2. Crossing the Plain and Entering

Time your arrival for dawn or just before dusk. Cross quickly, in the cooler hours, with water already in you. The plain is flat, pale cracked mineral, salt-white where the sun touches it, no water in the cracks, no green thread. There is no shade except a stone, and even there the dust is dry as flour.

The seed is sunk half into the floor, about sixty paces across, amber and translucent, polished smooth by wind grit, milky white at the crown, melted into a soft rim where it meets the ground. From outside you would think it empty. That is the first trick.

Duck into the keyhole. Just inside is the Throat — a small, deliberately made chamber, not grown. Niches plastered with mud and pounded seed-fiber, jars, a rope ladder, a copper awl, a stone hammer, a loom of knotted vine. This is the quietest place in Iskara, because the shell's hum is muffled behind its own thickness. Use it. Stash heavy gear, drink, plug your ears, adjust your pack, let your eyes settle. Then the floor drops slightly — the whole interior is a shallow bowl — and the Throat opens into the main hollow.

#### 3. Inside: Scale, Light, and Emptiness

Prepare your mind for the second trick: you are inside one seed, and you could walk the entire city in half a day, and yet the dome is lost in glare above you and the floor in front of you is empty for a hundred paces in every direction.

There are three terraced rings up the ribbed wall: the Low Ring around the floor, the Reed Terraces halfway up, and the Bell Rim just beneath the crown. Between clusters on the same ring there is often nothing — broad empty ledges where a shout will answer from far away. For every inhabited cube there are ten empty ones. A house here. A beehive kiln there. A row of storage hollows with reed doors. Most days you could walk a whole ring without meeting anyone.

This is not a ruin. It is a city holding its breath. Do not wander into a closed house, knock loudly at midday when no one can hear you, or expect a bustling center. A new face is an event because people are rare and far apart. Keep a respectful distance, wait at the deep-set threshold, and expect most real conversation to happen at night.

Houses are a meter thick — mud, fiber, and ground shell laid like basket courses — with small dark openings deep-set against glare and sound, domed roofs of beaten shell, reed mats over resin for floors. They are built to soften the drone to a pressure. On the Low Ring, behind two doors, that is the best sleep you will get. On the Bell Rim, where the shell is thinnest and brightest, even locals go only to tend the great resonators or to peer through erosion pinholes at the plain. Do not go there at midday as a tourist. You will be dizzy, overheated, and deafened.

Navigate by ribs and rings, not by GPS or sightlines. Mark your route, count ribs, remember landmarks — that kiln, that stack of bricks, that empty stall. Distances feel short on a map and long in a breathless, humming, amber-lit bowl.

#### 4. The Daily Rhythm: Plan Your Life Around the Sound

This is the heart of your preparation. Iskara has a day, but not a human one.

**Dawn:** The shell has contracted in the cool night, ticking and creaking, resin popping. For about an hour it is almost quiet. You can hear your own footsteps, speak across a room, hear a loom three rings away. The Great Drum in the center — a thirty-pace pit stretched with a single taut membrane of dried pod-skin, scraped and resined and tuned — is still enough to walk on and test with your feet. This is your window for exploring, asking questions, being taught, and crossing the Drum.

**Midday:** The sun climbs, the amber glows from outside, the shell expands slowly and continuously and sings. The fundamental is felt in your ribs and soles. The hollow ribs are organ pipes; warm air rises in a lazy central column and whistles harmonics through the city's clay pipes. Hook-ferns, each frond a hollow tube, sing back. Bell-vines slap their trumpets. Dry pods burst like knuckles cracking, thousands of times a day. At midday it is deafening, not like a market but like standing inside a struck bell that will not stop.

Locals stop work, or work entirely by touch. You should too. Retreat to a thick Low Ring house, or to the Throat if needed. Eat beforehand, hydrate, rest your ears and teeth. Do not cross the Great Drum — dust jumps on it in patterns. Do not expect to be heard, helped, or understood. If you must work, work by touch, by written note, by hand.

**Dusk and Night:** The amber goes to deep brown. The shell contracts, the note thins, and for a brief astonishing interval it ceases. This is when the Resin-people emerge, take out their plugs, and do the work that needs language: mending, harvesting, arguing, teaching. Bell flowers fold, moths come out — glass-winged, blind, navigating by vibration — geckos hunt in the cracks (deaf, pale, harmless), and you can speak in a normal voice and be answered undistorted.

Use this interval, but do not waste it. It is short. The pod is already warming for the next day, and the first low creak of expansion runs through the ribs like a held breath released. Sleep in a thick house with plugs in — even softened, the pressure is present — and expect to wake early with the ticking.

#### 5. Etiquette and Survival Rules

1. **Ask before you harvest.** Sap beads, moss sweat, vine lips — that is the water supply. Never take jars from niches in the Throat. Everything there is handmade, weathered, and in use.

2. **Never cross the Great Drum at midday**, touch its lashings, or stand on it to show off. It is not an altar or a well, it is the largest resonant surface in a hollow place, and the city is arranged around it like listeners.

3. **Do not silence the green.** Do not block pipes, cover flutes, or hang things on ferns. You are not just being rude; you are starving their gardens.

4. **Learn hand-speech before you need it.** A few signs for water, sap, thank you, drum, sleep, help, and "I am leaving" will earn you more goodwill than any shouted phrase at noon.

5. **Leave the glider alone.** It nests in the Bell Rim eaves and is the only animal that ever leaves through the keyhole to wheel over the plain. Watch from below, at dusk.

6. **Pack out, wipe off, shake out.** Spore-snow, resin, and flour-dust will follow you home. Keep food and water secured against vibration — at noon, things walk off ledges.

7. **Go slowly.** Processes never stop but they never hurry: morning contraction, midday expansion, evening cooling, leaves opening, pods popping, kilns breathing in bellows-rhythm, the updraft sighing. Weather inside is a slow tide of heat. Iskara punishes visitors who try to *do* the city in a day. Give it at least two full cycles — dawn, midday retreat, dusk conversation — before you judge it.

Finally, egress as you entered: through the keyhole, out onto the hot plain, with sore teeth, watered eyes, resin on your gloves, and ears that will ring for hours. Rest, drink real water, and do not speak loudly for a while. You will have been inside a bell, a drum, a greenhouse, and a desert, all at once, in a place that is mostly empty air and mostly song — and where the people who live there have learned, patiently, to live in the intervals, and to keep the music going.
