If you're an ordinary human — no gills, no pressure-adapted physiology, no instincts for navigating a city that happens to be underwater — this place will not forgive tourist instincts. It's not wilderness, and it's not a surface town with water on top. It's a working, newly-built trench-city at the bottom of a sea, stretched out to weather-scale. You don't *visit* it like a park. You commute through it, gear-first, and the locals commute right past you.

Here's how to prepare.

### 1. Assume You Arrive By Vehicle, Not By Teleport

Don't plan to materialize directly in open water. An unpressurized arrival, even in shallow-feeling green-blue daylight above, means instant cold shock, disorientation, and pressure on ears and sinuses that you can't clear while you're panicking.

Normal access is by transit tube or manned submersible into a pressurized air pocket — those "islands." Think of those pockets as imported air: they smell of oil, ozone, and rubber seals, and they are rationed, monitored, and entered through transfer locks.

Prepare for that:

**Get a pressure medical before you go.** Teeth, sinuses, and ears are your weak points here. The description of water cool enough to make teeth ache if you stay still is not flavor — it will find any cavity, filling, or sinus issue. If you can't equalize reliably on land in a pressure chamber, you will not enjoy long swims between nodes.

**Train for buoyancy and endurance, not for sprinting.** Children here learn to swim like surface kids learn to bike — for commuting between buildings. You need to fin slowly for a long time, hold depth in a current, and stop without thrashing. Open-water competence is not enough; you need to be competent *in a grid street with traffic.*

**Bring motion-sickness management for the ride in.** Submersibles and tugs don't go straight. They follow surge, dodge pylons, and rise through thermal plumes. The light shivers when they pass. You'll feel it in your stomach and your molars.

### 2. Dress for a Cold Ocean, Not For the Lamp Weather

On the main deck the lamps are a practical white and the air in pockets is warm. The water is not. This is England-in-the-sea cold, not tropical.

You want a real drysuit with thermal underlayers, or at minimum a thick, well-fitted suit you have dived in before. Cold is cumulative here: you may drift for hours between nodes 10, 20, 40 kilometers apart, seeing nothing change except light shift. In that emptiness, heat loss makes you stupid long before it makes you hypothermic.

Critical detail: **Thermal plumes are not warm pools.** A factory outflow can rise 500 meters and bend for kilometers, creating a local summer on your skin while the ambient water stays teeth-aching. It is also full of effluent and jellyfish. Do not cross one to "warm up." You will go from cold, to suddenly hot and stinging, to cold again, with no clear boundary. Locals mark them and forbid crossing without permission. Treat that like not walking through a furnace door on a surface factory floor.

Pack high-calorie food and water for the pockets, not the swim. You'll be burning heat just treading against a current that learned to follow the streets.

### 3. Learn to Orient in Emptiness

There is no shore, no slope, no natural rock. The floor — where there is a floor — is a poured, leveled plain with sharp terraces, retaining walls, and ballast fields of recycled ceramic and concrete. Corners are crisp because the place is young. Paint is bright. Welds are new.

That is helpful and dangerous:

**Use the built lines as your compass.** Pipelines run like roads. Power cables arc mast to mast. Lamp avenues run straight for kilometers. If you are separated, follow a pipeline or cable *toward* lit structures, but stay off the cables themselves — they sway, they carry current, and equipment hums when loading changes.

**Expect no GPS, weak radio, and tricky acoustics.** Water kills radio in meters. Locals use acoustic pings, hardwired comms in tubes, and visual signals. Bring a local transponder and a dive light with a strobe, and learn the local hand signals before you arrive. A sonar ping and a hull ring are normal background; a *change* in a pump hum is an alarm locals hear instantly. If you wear earplugs, you will miss that. Use a comms headset or bone-conduction set that lets you hear the city.

**Distances are in current-hours.** You cannot walk this. A node is not "over there" — it's a half-day drift if the current is against you, a 20-minute tug if you catch the right tube. File a float plan with the pocket you left: which avenue, which node, what your air and heat budget is. In this emptiness, no one will see you sink past a lamp mast.

### 4. Move Like a Commuter, Not an Explorer

Nothing here is fully automatic. Tugs haul containers, maintenance crews in bright suits crawl handrails, cranes swing, elevators clank, welding goes on at midnight. Traffic flows constantly, day and dimmed-night.

Stay in the transit lanes. The lamp avenues that look so inviting to swim down are, to fish, hunting lanes. Mackerel and herring turn silver between the masts, and larger predators use those same avenues the way a wolf uses a forest track. You are not apex here, and you are not invisible. Don't chase a school to take a photo; you will interrupt a hunt and attract the hunter.

Never touch intake gratings, outflow pipes, or the undersides of platforms. Water moving through gratings makes a tearing sound for a reason — it will pull. Filter feeders hang there by the thousands, opening and closing. Eels coil in warm outflows. Crabs walk the sharp terrace edges in armies. What looks like a handrail is a handrail; what looks like a wall is often a live intake, a hot pipe, or a crab highway.

Keep three points of contact on structures, keep fins off fresh paint and new welds (they're sharp because barnacles haven't softened them yet), and call ahead on comms before entering a work bay. The locals work in 24-hour shifts; "night" is only a dimming. Assume someone is welding, lifting, or pumping around the next pylon.

### 5. Prepare Your Senses for a Loud, Never-Dark City

The sound is constant: generator hum felt in your molars, metal groaning under current load, shrimp snapping, submersibles whining overhead, valves clanging kilometers away. If you come from a quiet natural sea, this will fatigue you. Bring rest ear protection for inside the air pockets — you'll also need to keep your nose clear of recycled-air smell, so a bandana or filter for oil/ozone in close quarters helps.

Light never fully turns off. Plan for disrupted circadian rhythm. The planters bolted to structures — imported soil, experimental kelp cropped in neat rows — need that light for sparse photosynthesis. It will read as noon-green from above and as street-at-night from below. Don't stare into work-lights; welders here work all night.

Photographers: bring wide-angle and patience. You will wait hours for a whale or shark silhouette to pass at a distance, making the masts look small. When it does, everything else goes quiet for a minute. Don't flash, don't follow, don't try to touch it. That quiet is the ecosystem checking in. Let it resume.

### 6. Animals Are Part of the Infrastructure

Life is sparse per cubic meter but abundant in aggregate because the structures *are* the reef.

* **Jellyfish gather in bells around discharge plumes.** Treat a plume as a no-swim zone even if it looks beautiful and warm. Sting + effluent + heat is a bad triad.
* **Crabs overturn new growth on terrace edges.** They pinch and they are sharp-boned. Watch your hands when you kick up from ballast fields.
* **Do not introduce anything.** No releasing plants, no feeding fish, no touching algae films to "see if it comes off." The vegetation here is auxiliary and tended precisely because it cannot root wild and is immediately grazed. A multiverse traveler's microbes, sunscreen, or invasive seeds would be a biosecurity incident in a young, sterile system.
* **Predator etiquette:** If a large animal passes, make yourself small, hold position near a pylon or under a platform edge, keep your light steady and low. Locals measure seasons by which avenues predators use in winter — they know the commute too.

### 7. Air Pockets Are Islands, Treat Them That Way

Pressurized volumes inside steel hulls and concrete shells are not hotels. They are workplaces. When you enter a transfer lock:

1. Equalize slowly, wait for the clank and the pressure settle.
2. Decontaminate hands and suit exterior — outside water carries plankton, silt, and whatever is drifting from the foundries.
3. Follow air discipline. Air is imported and shared. Don't prop locks, don't waste cycles, don't bring non-rated gear into a pocket.

If you're staying multiple days, acclimate. Spend your first day doing short swims between nearby structures, learning the hum, the current reversal around pillars (tides still pull, even underwater, *against walls*), and how to hear a pump fail. That hearing is a local skill you want — by day three, you should be able to tell the difference between normal surge and a change in loading.

### An Ordinary Traveler's Kit, In Short

* Drysuit + thermals you trust, plus a ready backup pocket to warm in
* Rebreather or twin tanks rated for work depth, with a *local* transponder and acoustic comms
* Primary light, strobe, slate with node map, compass (expect drift from steel), cutting shears for entanglement (never cut cables)
* Dental/sinus clearance, equalization training, motion-sickness meds you've tested on land
* Heat + calorie pack for transects, water for pockets
* Hard copy of local rules: no plume crossing, no touching intakes, stay in lanes, file your current-hour plan

### If Things Go Wrong

Stop, signal, brace on a structure. Do not ascend fast to escape cold, noise, or a predator — pressure and plumes make that worse. Follow lights and pipelines toward a node at a finning pace that conserves heat. Locals will help, but there is no surface lifeguard culture here; rescue is by tug and submersible, which take time to turn around in a current that moves like weather. Your best rescue is not needing one.

---

Arrive expecting wilderness and you'll be disappointed. Arrive expecting a surface city and you'll be cold and lost. Arrive expecting a young, loud, well-lit industrial ocean that is already being lived in — by welders, by crabs, by jellyfish following heat — and you'll be fine.

Dress warm, swim slow, listen for the pumps, and let the city teach you its streets.
