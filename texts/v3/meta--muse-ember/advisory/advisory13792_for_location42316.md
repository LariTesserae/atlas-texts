Don't be fooled by how small it is. That's the first trap.

You can walk the whole summit basin in twenty minutes. It looks like a cathedral floor inside a low wall, with a sea of cloud below the wall and no horizon beyond that. Then the swarms arrive, the needles start singing, the light sinks to moss-green dim, and the mountain ticks and pops under your feet — and you realize the place is not small in *volume*. It's small in diameter and enormous in everything happening inside it at once.

Prepare for altitude, humidity, heat, darkness, noise, wet stone, and unstable ground all at the same time. On Earth you rarely get all six.

### 1. Your body, before you go

**Altitude that feels wrong.** Your ears will pop on arrival and your breath will come short and warm and fog. The air is thin *and* thick — high air lifted up and trapped in the basin, saturated with itself. You will not feel cold and dry like a normal summit. You will feel hot, close, and short of breath, and you will sweat that doesn't evaporate.

If you have asthma, ear pressure problems, a heart condition, or you get altitude sickness easily, talk to a doctor first. Ordinary travelers should staged-acclimatize if your transit allows it, and plan your first visit for hours, not days. Walk slow. The extra few meters of height cost more here than the map suggests, because you'll be breathing humid dead air while listening to a wall of sound.

Pace yourself like you're climbing stairs in a sauna while someone talks loudly at you. Because in effect, you are.

**Heat and dehydration despite the wet.** Everything is wet, but you are still losing water fast. Bring a liter or two of *your own* drinking water, plus electrolytes. Do not drink from the pools.

Do not plan to forage. There is essentially no vegetation to eat — just ephemeral olive-grey mats in sheltered cracks that appear and vanish in a week, and algae learning to be plants in the pools. The fauna do not eat green things, so you won't either.

**Skin, eyes, and ears.** You will be wet continuously. Eyelashes gather water, sleeves darken, droplets run off your hands as if your skin were stone. That means blisters, macerated feet, chafing, and fungal irritation within a day.

Pack:
- Two complete changes of clothes and socks, in dry bags inside dry bags
- Quick-dry, non-cotton layers. Cotton will stay cold and heavy. Warm doesn't mean dry.
- Barrier cream, foot powder, blister kit, antiseptic, and cut-resistant gloves
- Anti-fog wipes for glasses/goggles and a microfiber cloth. Not for vanity — you'll be functionally blind every few minutes otherwise.
- Prescription meds in waterproof containers.

For ears: bring both foam earplugs *and* filtered musicians' plugs. The basin is a resonating bowl. Dripping ticks like a clock, filaments sing, needle towers make a high clear tone that will make your teeth ache, and swarms drum with a dry patter that echoes like rain on a tin roof. It's not a single loud noise, it's layers, and it doesn't stop. You need plugs for rest and for the worst swarm pulses, but you also need to hear the mountain itself — the deep irregular tick, pop, and splintering of cracks opening. A traveler who can't hear a crack warning will stand on a seam.

For eyes: no sunglasses matter here. It's a permanent overcast morning, dimmed again by the cloud deck you're standing on. Color drains to wet grey, black, dull moss-green. In darker phases the filaments and pools give off their own faint blue-white pulses. Bring a headlamp with a warm, decent-output mode and a low red mode for not spooking yourself. You need to see sharp stone edges, not stars.

### 2. Footwear and movement

The stone is geology in its first draft. Raw, angular, unweathered, edges keen enough to cut. No soil, no rounded pebbles, no erosion to soften it. It's also still being pushed up — audibly. New seams open and heal, scree grows in place still attached and lifting, foundations swell.

You need:
- Stiff, ankle-supporting boots with aggressive grip for wet stone, not just trail shoes. Loose scree here is not settled; it leans and moves.
- Steel or composite toe protection if you can take the weight. Popping, ticking stone drops and shifts.
- To move like you're on new ice. Test each flat plane before committing. Listen before you step. A sharp report underfoot is information, not ambience.

**The wall is the real edge.** Beyond that low surrounding wall is a drop to cloud far below, and the horizon is just the world turning down. Do not lean, sit, or climb on the wall to look. From any edge you can see the middle, and from the middle you can see the other side — you don't need to be at the brink. If your transit drops you inside the room, stay inside the room.

Walk the basin once just to learn it, then expect that knowledge to expire. By the time you return to the first pool, water level has changed, a new seam has opened, a swarm is boiling through, a filament is a hand's breadth taller, and the light has shifted the grey palette. Mark pools and filaments with photos with a scale object, not with cairns — the place accretes around anything you leave, and footprints disappear as stone grows around them.

### 3. Water is a condition here, not a feature

Water does not run away. It beads on stone, runs a finger's width, and sinks again. It pools a finger deep in hollows, warm, clear, trembling without wind, sometimes bubbling cold over warm stone. In some pools it climbs black filaments against downhill until it falls as mist.

You will be tempted to wade, wash, or test it. Don't.

- Do not wade, step in, or put hands in pools except for a brief, gloved touch if you must. Pool swimmers flash silver under the surface, reverse without turning, and vanish into cracks too narrow for them. The water is a living filter circuit, warm, recycled constantly by fauna and film. Cuts + warm pools = infection almost guaranteed for an ordinary human.
- Do not drink, brush teeth, or rinse gear in it.
- Assume every damp surface has something on it you can't see. Some fauna are only visible when wet; as they dry they become clear as glass and invisible, though you can still hear their feet. Their tracks appear on damp stone a *second before* their feet touch down.

That last one is your best early warning system. If you see a track bloom ahead of you, freeze and let the column pass. Skitterers the size of mice with too many legs, blind to the dim light, navigate by touch and stone-heat in tight clicking columns. Winged things hang like ash, then drop, buzzing at a tooth-aching pitch. Large shapes cling to the outer wall, calling in a chorus that's almost speech if you don't listen closely, and completely other if you do.

### 4. Fauna protocol: don't interrupt the ballet

The population is high, the biomass low, the motion constant. Born, swarming, eaten, born again in the same warm wet day. They filter condensing air, scrape microbial film off new stone, and eat each other in a fast formal cycle.

They aren't hostile in a predator way, but they are abundant and they fill the whole basin in pulses, then evacuate it. In the middle of a pass it is suddenly full of life and noise; a minute later bare and ringing again.

Practical rules:
- Never step into a swarm column. Stand still, slightly aside, hands in, let them route around you. Crushing a column can bring the next one sooner.
- Do not answer the wall callers. Do not approach the outer wall after dark-phase. Do not mimic them. It will draw attention and it will echo wrong in the bowl.
- Do not try to photograph invisible dry animals. If you hear feet but see nothing, you are being honest about it. Move slowly, as if the stone is occupied anyway.
- Keep food sealed. You will attract nothing you want.
- Pack out everything, including food scraps and waste. Nothing here erodes or lingers in middens — everything is recycled while still moving. That means human waste goes straight back into the pool/film/air cycle you are standing in.

### 5. The built things and the grown things

There are two kinds of structures and they are interspersed, not obviously separate.

The human-scaled ones: a low spiral wall like an abandoned windbreak, a half-sunk fired-clay dome with its door filled with weeping water, an empty square cistern lined with faint handprints the stone has grown around, a leaning roof on four posts blackened underneath. They look interrupted, not ruined. Don't shelter in them. They are partial, unstable, waterlogged, and being taken back by swelling stone. The cistern is not a water source. The dome is not a dry room. The leaning roof can fall.

The other things: black glass filaments rising from pools and cracks, sometimes in a perfect ring, humming, warm, vibrating, your hand coming away dry when you touch one. Rings half-buried in dark non-metal that ring when a swarm drums over them. Needle towers slender as reeds swaying without wind making a high tone. Lattice shelters that are not built but grown — opening and closing like lungs, membranes expanding with pressure, assembling themselves from dissolved minerals in the weeping water, repairing as fast as the mountain cracks them.

Treat the grown technology like an organ, not a machine you can use.

- **Look, don't touch first.** Curiosity is normal — the description will make you want to touch a filament. If you do, brief, gloved, with clean dry hands, and wash immediately after. They shed dust as light; don't inhale it, don't get it on cuts. They are extending a millimeter at a time; a snapped tip is damage to something still being born.
- Do not kick, tap, or ring-test the rings. Do not lean on needle towers. Their tone rises and falls with the light and will join the dripping and drumming in a chord that never resolves — if it makes your teeth ache, your chest vibrate, or you feel nauseous, step back. That's your signal, not a challenge.
- Do not enter or block a lattice shelter. It breathes. If you put your body in its membrane cycle, or camp against it, you are interfering with air and pressure exchange. It will repair around you or close on you. Observe from a pool's width away, where you can feel the bass hum in your chest without standing in it.
- At dim phases they pulse blue-white along their length like a message being sent up. Don't follow pulses. Don't assume they're for you, and don't try to answer with your light. They are part of the light cycle, not a path.

Nothing breaks here the way we expect. Nothing erodes, everything accretes. A structure will not decay into scrap; it will absorb fresh stone and water and become more itself.

### 6. Light, time, and your head

There is no day. There is a permanent overcast morning that sometimes lifts to a paler dim and then sinks again without ever becoming night. In darker phases other lights come on.

That destroys sleep and judgment faster than altitude. Plan short visits with clear start/stop, use a headlamp on a timer, and don't rely on your eyes for color or distance. Wet grey stone in dim moss-green light looks flat and much farther/closer than it is.

Psychologically, prepare for the uncanny overlap: almost no vegetation and far too many animals, almost no building and far too much technology, almost no erosion and far too much becoming. A place still being born alongside technology still being born. It's a cathedral of new stone roofed with cloud, floored with pools, filled with sound — and it is not finished becoming. Ordinary travelers do best when they stop expecting an explanation.

Bring:
- Notebook with waterproof pages (pencil, not ink)
- Sound recorder if you want it — your ears will lie to you the second time
- Camera with high ISO — color will drain, shadows will be soft, many subjects will be invisible dry
- Layers of zip bags for electronics. Condensation will fog lenses and kill paper in minutes.

### 7. Short checklist to bring

Boots (stiff, grippy, toe-protected), cut-resistant gloves, 2x dry clothing/socks, headlamp, earplugs (foam + filtered), anti-fog + cloth, dry bags, drinking water + electrolytes, sealed food, first aid + antiseptic + blister care, barrier cream, pencil + waterproof notebook, camera/recorder, small scale object for photos, sunscreen is useless — bring lip balm for thin warm air, and a simple whistle/beacon for your transit point. Leave the wall and pools alone.

**What not to do, summarized:** Don't lean on the wall. Don't wade or drink. Don't step into swarms. Don't touch filaments longer than a second. Don't sleep or shelter in human ruins or lattice. Don't leave anything. Don't try to map it once and trust it.

If you prepare for a hot, humid, high-altitude cathedral that is too small, too loud, too wet, and still growing — and walk it slowly, listening as much as looking — you'll have a good visit. If you prepare for a normal mountain summit, it will overwhelm you before you get out of breath.
