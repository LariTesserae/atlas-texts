Think of it less as a cave and more as a sealed, wet, inhabited machine. A pocket one long barn long, accidentally fitted with a whole town because it had nowhere else to put it, and then left alone for so long that it came to rest.

That changes everything about preparation. You are not going exploring. You are going to briefly squeeze, sideways, into a place that is already complete, already wet, already loud, already old, and does not expect time or progress from you. An ordinary human does not need courage for this place. An ordinary human needs to be small, dry inside, and able to leave again on a schedule they brought with them, because there is none inside.

### 1. Before the crack: fit yourself, not your gear

You do not arrive, you squeeze in. Plan for that, not for a road.

**Wear nothing that catches.** No hard backpack with a frame, no suitcase, no trekking poles, no bulky coat with stiff cuffs, no belt with a big buckle, no keychain, no hat with a brim, no sword, staff, camera tripod, or long tool. The crack, the low doorways, and the stairs that require you to turn your shoulders are all built for a hunched local body, not for equipment.

Wear a soft, low-profile pack, 20-30 liters max, worn on your chest for the squeeze if you must, or sent ahead of you on a rope. Everything valuable goes in dry bags inside it. Everything in this town is the same wetness, forever. If it is not in a dry bag, it will be damp by the time you stand up. Your boots will be damp. Your pack will be damp. Your skin in contact with clothing will be damp.

Tell someone *outside* where the crack is, when you expect to exit, and do not rely on anyone inside to find you. From the surface the rock above shows nothing. From inside there is nowhere else to go. This is a self-rescue-only visit.

Do a real toilet stop before. There are no latrines, and the water system is not a sewer you can use. The cisterns, troughs, hand-wide channels, and leaky head-height aqueducts are the town's infrastructure. Do not urinate, defecate, wash, or dump anything in them. Pack out all waste. Bring a small portable urinal/waste bag if you are prone to needing one mid-visit.

Clean your boots *before* you go in. There is no vegetation anywhere in here — no grass, no moss, no roots. Green would be an obscenity. Do not track in soil, seed, leaf litter, or plant matter from your world. It isn't just litter here, it's contamination of a mineral ecosystem, and it will be visibly wrong to the people who live there.

### 2. What to bring, and what to leave behind

**Clothing for equilibrium cold:**
The air is cool, still, and never changes. You will not sweat much walking, and you will get cold standing still listening. You will stay wet from ceiling drip, wall film, and aqueduct leaks even in a “dry” spot.

- Broken-in waterproof boots with *aggressive* tread. The floors are polished mirror-bright by generations of feet. A fashion boot or smooth sole will put you on your knees on a slate bridge over a sluice.
- Merino or synthetic socks, plus a second dry pair in the dry bag to change into halfway if you stay more than a couple hours. Constant damp is how ordinary feet blister and go fungal here.
- Wicking base layer, warm insulating mid-layer, soft waterproof shell — thin enough to turn sideways in. Wool cap or beanie. It protects your head from head-height aqueducts and drips and keeps heat in when the roar and monotony make you still.
- Thin grip gloves. Stone rails are wet and slick; copper wire, volcanic glass, and bone tools laid in rows are sharp. You need to feel a wall to steady yourself without sliding.

**Light, but not much:**
The town is lit by a steady, faint blue-white phosphorescence in the limestone, and by small oil lamps in clay cups with copper wick-trimmers. It is dim and constant, never rising or falling.

Your eyes will need 20-40 minutes to adapt. A bright white headlamp will blind you, blind them, and blow out the whole reason the place looks the way it does. Bring a small, warm-toned, low-output headlamp or hand light, kept pointing down, on its lowest setting. Use it to test floors and stairs, not to flood the chamber. Never bring an open flame, candle, stove, or flare. Their lamps, kilns, and smoke holes are densely crowded and hand-tended. Extra fire is a smoke and burn risk with no real ventilation.

No phone signal, no GPS, no sky, no sun. A compass will not help you much against rock and echo, and shadows essentially do not exist — lamp fire casts almost no shadow. Do not trust shadows for distance or edges. Test with a foot before you lean.

**Sound management:**
This is the hardest part for most visitors. The roar of water moving through a hand-cut network under and through the stone, the amplified drip like hammer blows, tools ringing, voices from the next doorway arriving all at once and echoing back a second later. You can hear four neighborhoods simultaneously, close and far together, because sound has nowhere to leave.

Do not bring loud earplugs for walking — you need to hear a footstep, a sluice gate being lifted, someone announcing themselves behind you in a low doorway. Bring soft earplugs for a rest pause *in the entry passage*, not in the town. If the noise makes you anxious, that is normal. The town is built to trap sound the way a small room traps talking. Plan short.

**Food, water, medicine:**
Bring all of your own. There is no local food except a pale fungal felt farmed in moist troughs and scraped off with flat bone tools — it is their crop, not an offering — and a blind translucent fish that occasionally turns up in distribution channels and is caught by hand-net. Do not eat either, do not ask for them, do not try to farm, collect, or “sample” them.

Drink only water you brought, purified by you. Channels contain fish, worms in the damp mortar, lamp fat, clay, iron, and anything the town puts in them. Do not drink from aqueducts, cisterns, pools, or drip lines, however clean they look.

Bring your own small first aid: bandages, antiseptic, antifungal cream, blister care, pain relief for knees, back, and neck from stooping and sideways stairs. There is no herbal medicine because there are no herbs. There is no clinic because there is no surface to fetch one from.

**Navigation and time:**
Bring a short length of cord / paracord and a reliable watch or timer with an alarm. There is no day, no night, no season, no light change. The town will lull your sense of time until an hour feels like no time at all. Set your turn-around alarm *before* you enter, and set a second one for exit. Ordinary humans lose track here faster than in any cave because nothing outside cues you either.

Bring a small notebook and pencil, not a marker that bleeds in damp. Mark your own count of terraces, kilns, cisterns, and stair levels on the page. Do not mark their walls.

### 3. Moving inside: ears, feet, shoulders

Go in sideways, exhaling. Keep your pack in front. Expect the air to cool, thicken, and smell of wet stone, iron, clay, and rendered lamp fat long before you see the chamber.

When you emerge, do not try to take it in all at once. The town is built in layers because it cannot sprawl. A roof is the floor of the house above. Steep narrow stairs climb past kilns, cisterns, and doorways at three different heights from one wall. Slate slabs leap sluices. Aqueducts run at head height and leak a little on purpose and by accident.

Walk with one hand trailing near — not gripping — a wall for balance, and the other free to duck. Head-height means duck when you see a clay channel cross your path. Do not walk under a sluice gate being lifted by a twisted-hide rope, and do not touch ropes, gates, or bead-lines. Those bead-lines across a stair are a household marker. That stair belongs to someone. If you are unsure whether a stair is public, wait. The builders are few, solitary in the crowd, and busy by long habit, not by purpose. They will not necessarily welcome you, guide you, or notice you are lost. They are hunched to fit their own doors.

Watch your feet on bridges and terraces. Sluices are open. Channels are hand-wide and always running. Pale worms live in damp mortar; they do almost nothing, but do not put your hands in channels or sit in troughs. Salt and crystal crusts that look like lichen grow where drips fall and are harvested, then regrow in exactly the same shape. Do not harvest them, pocket them, or photograph them like souvenirs. The rock seems to have a template.

Almost everything is softened, slumped, and ancient-looking, even when newly re-laid. Do not lean your weight on a terrace parapet, a bone-white strut holding up a roof-floor, or a stalactite tube used as a beam. From a distance the chimneys and struts look like a pale forest. Up close they are load-bearing architecture.

If you left for a year, ten years, a hundred, you would come back through the same crack to the same roar, the same lamps, the same level of water in the same channels. This is not a place of discoverable new districts. It is one oval chamber. You can walk its floor in minutes; its complexity is vertical, acoustic, and social. After the first hour, you have seen the town. After that, you are just getting damper and more disoriented.

### 4. People, senses, and the mild wrongness

Announce yourself. Because of the echo, a local may not hear you until you are close, and you may hear them a second after they have stopped speaking. A soft cough, a tap on stone, open hands in front of you, and keeping still until you are seen will go further than any phrase. You likely will not share a language. Do not touch bone tools, copper trimmers, lamps, kiln wares, or the fungal felt. Do not mimic their work to be friendly.

Expect your senses to mislead you politely:

- **Hearing:** voices repeating faintly, drips arriving as blows, four neighborhoods at once. You are not being haunted. You are inside a resonant pocket. Do not follow a voice to try to exit. Exits do not call to you.
- **Touch:** water film sometimes moves upward for a stretch before finding an edge. Floors feel cool through your soles because the water table is just under them.
- **Light:** one lamp area may feel farther or nearer than it is, with no shadows to check.
- **Stillness:** lamps burn the same, stairs wear the same, houses repair where they always were. Your own breathing, heart, and watch are the only clocks.

That stasis is the psychological hazard. Ordinary travelers do not panic here because something terrible happens. They forget to leave because nothing ever tells them to. Boredom, déjà vu, and damp cold set in. Tell yourself, out loud if you need to, what time you are leaving and by which crack you came in.

### 5. A sane visit plan

For a first visit, plan **90 minutes to 3 hours inside** total, including the squeeze in and out. That is enough to acclimatize, walk the main floor, climb one or two public terraces if invited, sit quietly in a doorway that is not blocking a sluice or stair — and get out before trench foot, ringing ears, lost time, and cold set in.

Longer stays require: two full changes of socks, full water and food for a day, a blackout mask and earplugs for resting in a dry-ish corner *outside* the main chamber, and a hammock or pad that does not block a channel. Never sleep in a trough, cistern, or on a roof that is someone else’s floor.

On the way out, go slowly, sideways again, pack in front, watch for head-height aqueducts in the entry passage where your light may be lower. Once you are through the crack, sit in open air, change your socks, eat, drink your own water, and let your eyes readjust. You will likely still hear the roar for a while. Your boots will be wet. Your clothes will smell of iron and fat.

Leave the place exactly as you found it: same water level, same crystals, same felt, same lamps. It has balanced itself so completely it no longer needs to move. Your job as a visitor is not to improve it, farm it, light it better, or make it grow. Your job is to fit, for a short time, through a seam that was not made as a road, be quiet in a town that is already complete, and come back out with dry feet in your pack and your own time intact.
