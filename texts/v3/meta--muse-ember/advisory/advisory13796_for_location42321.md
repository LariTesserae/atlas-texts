This is not a place you can live off, shelter in, or navigate by normal rules. Treat it as a wholly self-contained hostile environment that happens to look like a ruined city.

You are going to a high, cold, bone-dry plateau under a small pale sun that never gets high, where a device-less city of stone has been eroded until rock and building are the same thing, the air is too thin and dry to carry much sound, distances lie, streets recur, and even the edges of things are wearing thin. For an ordinary human, the main dangers are not dramatic: they are dehydration, exhaustion, getting lost in loops, and slowly losing the thread of where you came from and why you came.

Prepare accordingly, and keep the visit short.

### 1. Accept the absolutes before you pack

**No water, at all.** Not in the air, not in the ground, not in hollows. No rain, no dew, no condensation, no melt. The dry channels carry only wind-blown dust, the white crusts are not salt you can dissolve but hard-packed silica and feldspar. You will lose water invisibly - thin dry air, harder breathing at altitude, dusty wind - and you will never notice sweat evaporating until you are already dehydrated.

**No food, no fuel, no shelter material.** No plants, no wood. The black and rust-red dendrites on the stone are not alive. They creep at night, stop in light, and are toxic to anything that needs water to digest. The grazers can scrape them up like grass, but that is why they would poison you. The city itself is empty shells: doorways and arches give you a windbreak, nothing else. It has no metal, no glass, no lamps, no bones, no tools left. If you need it, you bring it.

**No familiar scale.** Distances look sharp because the air is so clear, but they are not true. A tower that looks an hour away can be beside you after you turn a corner, or still just as far after two hours of walking. The same plaza shows up at the end of three streets, the same view repeats with one spire missing, a block you passed in the morning is ahead again in the afternoon, slumped a fraction more. Plan by time and pace count, never by how close something looks. Build in 2-3x the water and energy you think you need, and turn back by the clock, not by distance.

**No familiar orientation.** Avenues run true north and east even when the land shouldn't, but shadows fall upward toward that low sun *and* a fainter second shadow falls down, as if light comes from both sides. The horizon at the far rim bends slightly up and you can glimpse the underside of another sky. Do not use shadows, the horizon, or the rim for direction, time, or a viewpoint. That bent rim is the worn edge of the place - do not go chasing it.

**No reliable sound.** Most of the time it is a deliberate, heavy silence, broken by dust sheets moving without sound, by single ticks of cooling stone at dusk, by a low groan of settling rock you may feel in your chest before you hear it, if you hear it at all. Sound arrives out of order. Do not use calls, echoes, or animal noise to judge direction or distance. Your own steps will feel muffled. Communication over more than a few metres needs to be visual or by touch - a rope tug, a light flash, a hand on a shoulder.

### 2. Body preparation

This is a cold high-altitude desert, not an icy mountain and not a warm desert.

- Cold is constant and dry. Exposed stone stays cool all the long slow day; it will chill you through a thin sleeping pad even at midday. No frostbite from ice, but wind-currents that think about where they are going, exhaustion, and sitting still on cold stone will bring hypothermia on fast. Layer windproof, bring gloves for touching stone, a hat, and insulate yourself from the ground before you insulate yourself from the air.
- The air is thin. If you have any cardiac, respiratory, or altitude issues, get medical advice and consider acclimatization time before a longer stay. Walk at the local pace: slow, spaced, silent. Running will exhaust you, attract attention, and put you on a grazer's migration route at speed.
- Silica dust is everywhere, in ribbons constantly re-laying themselves in the dry channels and against walls. It gets in boots, in seams, in eyes and lungs. Bring a fit-tested respirator (not a paper dust mask), sealed goggles or glasses with a strap, gaiters, lip balm, skin balm, eye drops in minimal-water form, and wipes for hands and face. You have no water to wash with, so keep food sealed and hands cleaned dry before eating.
- Dry air will crack skin, lips, and mucous membranes and make contact lenses painful or impossible. Glasses are safer. Breathe through your nose when you can; a gaiter or heat-exchange mask helps retain moisture and filters dust.
- Light is dim overhead, coppery at the horizon, shadows long and multiple, hollows dark early. Your eyes will strain. Bring a diffuse headlamp with spare batteries - low and angled at the ground, not into your partner's face, and remember it will add a third shadow that makes the existing confusion worse. Do not depend on electronic navigation alone; in a place without a single device, in recurrence and bent distance, GPS, phones, and cameras can loop or lie. Paper notes, a mechanical watch, a simple compass, and pace counting are your backups, and even they should be cross-checked.

Practice before you go: walking with a heavy pack in low light, navigating a looping course by backtracking exactly, losing and regaining a landmark, keeping a timed log. If you can't do that in a practice environment, you won't do it well here.

### 3. What to carry - non-negotiables

Think in terms of a self-rescue kit with no resupply.

**Water:** All of it, sealed, in rigid containers protected from dust. For short exertion in thin dry air, plan at least 0.5-1 litre per hour per person, more with food. Carry a reserve you have no intention of drinking unless you are aborting. Bring electrolytes. Do not bring a filter or still expecting to find water, and do not plan meals that need cooking or rehydration. Food must be ready-to-eat, salty and calorie-dense, and eaten *with* water - dry food without water will dehydrate you faster.

**Shelter and warmth:** A tent or bivouac you can close against dust sheets, a thick insulated ground pad, warm layers, a windproof shell. Tower doorways are useful for breaking wind, but they are hollow, dusty, and sometimes occupied by the small stone-coloured ticking fauna under slabs. Do not reach blindly into dark doorways, do not lift slabs to sit on, and do not camp leaning against a cracked arch or wall - towers are settling by millimetres, cracks widening, with that rare groan as warning. Camp a little back from walls, on open lee ground you have marked.

**Navigation anchors:** Bright, non-stone, non-repeating markers. Tall flags or poles in orange or other high-contrast color that does not belong to the grey/rust/pale palette, numbered or lettered. You will need them every 50-100 metres on a sortie - dust will cover low markers quickly. A light rope to trail back to camp on short sorties, trekking poles to measure and mark, a notebook with pencil (not just a phone) to log time, heading, pace count, and what you actually see behind you on the return view.

Record the return view at every turn. The same doorway may face the other way when you return, and your memory will insist it is the same one. Your note and photo of *behind* you is worth more than a photo of ahead.

**Human anchors:** A written card with who you are, where you entered, your exit time, and your return signal. A familiar personal object from home. A set verbal check-in every 15 minutes with a partner - name, direction of entry, time to turn back. If you go alone, talk to the log out loud at intervals. Edges wearing thin here is not just stone. Long stays make names, intentions, and memories feel eroded and vague. Externalising them keeps you attached to *your* world.

Bring a partner if at all possible, with a physical tether - rope, not voice - for city sections. Silence and recurrence make separation permanent very quickly.

### 4. How to move inside

- Come in during the brightest part of the dim day (it never gets truly bright) and leave well before the long dusk dimming, when cooling stone ticks and grazers come out low between the towers. That dim time is their migration time along the avenues. The avenues are their routes and the routes of whatever hunts them.
- Feels like an enormous city spread almost empty: kilometres of bare plateau between clusters, hollow plazas, empty courtyards. It was laid out for a population that never came, or left completely. There is no crowd to follow, no help to find, no centre to aim for. Do not try to map it in one visit. Pick one short sortie.
- Prefer travel just off the avenues on plateau when you can, to avoid the predator-prey corridor, and use an avenue only as a check, not a commitment. If you must use one, walk its edge, not its centre.
- When a landmark suddenly appears beside you - turn, stop, anchor yourself. Note time, pace count, compass, and the *exact* crack pattern, doorway side. Mark it as a recurrence trap in your log. Do not try to shortcut back to it from memory the next time it appears. Backtrack the way you came.
- Do not stay still for long looking. The travelers who stand still report the city shifting around them, dizziness from the double shadows and bent horizon. If you rest, sit with your back to a marked wall, feet planted, markers in view, and set a timer to move again.
- Fauna: You may walk all day and see none of the tall grey grazers, who look like architecture that decided briefly to be an animal. Keep distance, do not approach, do not touch, do not try to feed or eat anything mineral. If a grazer, normally slow and silent, is suddenly moving faster a hundred metres along, or a shadow on a wall disappears, a hunter is near. Leave the avenue perpendicularly toward a doorway or your markers, do not run along the avenue, do not make noise, and do not try to see it close.

### 5. Camp and duration

For an ordinary traveler, plan in hours, not days. If you must stay longer, treat camp as a physical system the city will try to blur: weighted tall markers around your perimeter, rope lines, a log entry every hour of what is where - and check them, because dust currents will re-lay ground cover and you cannot trust that camp looks the same as you left it.

Sleep away from walls and arches, ear protection if the ticking keeps you awake, but not so much you miss a groan. Stone will be cold under you all night. Your tent will collect dust, not dew - shake it out in the morning light, do not expect it to dry from condensation clearing.

### 6. Abort criteria - leave immediately if:

- Your water reserve is breached or your turn-back time passes, regardless of how close a tower looks
- You see the same plaza, arch, or doorway three times, or the horizon bends more noticeably
- You cannot confidently state your entry direction, your partner's name, or your exit plan without checking your written card
- You feel vertigo, unusual fatigue, confusion, or that you have always been here
- Headlamp fails, respirator seal fails and dust is thick, shivering begins, or a grazer is moving fast near you
- Your markers are covered, moved, or look unfamiliar

Leaving is not failure. The city is built to outlast a visit. Its erosion is established; it is not going to be hurried, solved, or finished by you.

### 7. After you leave

Expect a strange decompression. Sleep warm, drink water, eat familiar food, write a full debrief while memory is fresh - what recurred, what your logs say versus what you remember. Compare notes with a partner if you had one. If you notice gaps or a feeling that the place is still shifting around you, rest, ground yourself with routine and familiar places, and do not return until you have reviewed your logs and the experience with someone who did not go.

Bring home no metal, glass, or stone souvenirs. The place has lived without devices for a very long time. Leave your markers, take your weight with you, and leave only footprints in dust that will cover them anyway.

In short: bring all your water, all your light, all your warmth, and a way back that does not depend on this place making sense. Move slowly, mark physically, log constantly, anchor yourself to names and times outside the city, and leave before the edges of *you* start to wear as thin as the edges of things there.
