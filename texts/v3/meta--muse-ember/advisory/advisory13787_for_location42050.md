You are not visiting a landscape. You are visiting the inside of an animal that happens to be valley-sized.

That basin looks like stone because everything in it has been worn, smoothed, and settled for so long that nothing looks new. But the ground is a pavement of overlapping, breathing bodies, hand-wide, armored, eyeless, blindly tapping the damp film for food. The cracks between them are a continent of skitterers. The ledges are hung with soft, blind throats that boom and croak to feel the stone vibrate. Wanderers the size of houses — by your scale — part the pavement as they pass and leave clean wet tracks the pavement reclaims behind them. And over it all hangs a permanent haze of translucent, blind, thumbnail winged things you will hear long before you ever see one clearly.

For a normal human, the main problems are not predators. Nothing here hunts you on purpose. The problems are mismatch:

You are upright, heavy, eyed, warm, thirsty, and loud in a world that is low, light, cold-damp, eyeless, and navigated entirely by vibration and wetness.

Prepare for that mismatch.

### 1. Expect the sound and the cold first, before you see anything

The sound does not start when you arrive. It is already there under your feet: a low, wet pulse you will feel in your teeth and sternum, and on top of it an unending high shimmer of clicks, like rain on a tin roof made of a million tiny claws. Your own steps and breath will not register in it at all.

Bring real ear protection from the moment of transit, not after you land and realize you can't think. Filtered plugs that knock down the high clicking but let you still feel a wanderer's dull shift through your soles. Bone-conduction radio for you and your companions — voices will be drowned, and your voice is just a weird foreign drumbeat to the locals anyway. Don't shout. Don't drop a pack. A heavy knock travels through the film and the stone and everything curious will come to investigate it as a possible carcass or mating partner.

The cold is not winter cold. It's cellar cold, deep-water cold, the same at noon as midnight, with a lid of grey mist so low that beyond a few dozen paces everything blurs. You will stay damp. It's not a rainstorm you can wait out, it's drizzle too fine to splash, plus condensation rolling in waves, plus ledges dripping long after, plus the animals themselves exhaling stored water as mist when they pulse. You will be wet from the outside in and, if you go still, cold from staying still.

Dress like you're going to stand in a refrigerator that is also raining, forever: synthetic thermal base that wicks, waterproof outer shell, sealed hood, sealed cuffs, waterproof boots with socks you can change. No cotton, no leather, no canvas, no paper — skitterers and fungus will worry them apart in hours, and damp will rot them anyway. Bring spare socks, a microfiber towel for lenses and hands, and a means to keep skin unbroken. In that world, a scraped knuckle will be found instantly by skitterers, who specialize in picking at the dead and the wounded. Treat any break in skin as an emergency and cover it immediately.

### 2. You cannot walk normally on this ground

This is the hardest thing for an ordinary traveler to accept: the floor is alive.

The pavement animals are packed near-touching, two and three deep in places, lifting and settling in slow out-of-phase waves that take minutes to cross a flat. If you plant a 70kg heel on them, you are a wanderer to them — enormous, disruptive, crushing. You will also slip, because the bacterial film that is their pasture makes every surface slick, spongy in places, polished shell in others.

* Bring a platform if you can. A folding slatted mat, inflatable stepping pads, a short hover-sled, anything that keeps you *off* the pavement for arrival, eating, sleeping, and waiting. Stand on it for your first ten minutes. Let the skitterers investigate your boots. Let a wanderer pass. Let your eyes and ears adjust.
* If you must walk, walk slowly, with wide, soft, treaded soles that spread weight like a wanderer's broad foot, not a boot heel. Step, pause, let the clicking resettle. Do not run, hop, or stamp. You will cause a percussive shift, scatter millions of skitterers, and draw attention you don't want.
* Never step into the shallow pans — no deeper than a hand, trembling and reflecting the gloom — or into the upturned shell hollows, black and white and polished. That's where shells and droppings accumulate. It's a graveyard, and it's slick and confusing.

Hangers on the ledges drop mucus threads and snap at skitterers. Do not reach for them, do not duck under a cluster thick as grapes to shelter from drizzle, and do not touch a thread. It's sticky, it's meant for prey, and the body will slap at anything that vibrates like food.

If a wanderer comes your way: freeze on your platform. Do not wave, do not run. It cannot see you. It navigates by vibration through the wet film. Running is the loudest possible signal. It will part the pavement around you like a tide and pass, and the skitterers will resettle behind it. It has no interest in you, which is the only reason you are safe.

### 3. You will be infested if you leave any opening

Skitterers are as thick as flies in a heap, except they never stop and there are millions. They tap, worry, and pick. They will get in ears, nostrils, mouths, seams, packs, food.

Sealed is better than warm here. A light, smooth, hooded suit or poncho with sealed wrists and ankles, sealed goggles (not glasses, not contacts), and a respirator mask is not paranoia, it's hygiene. The winged haze does not land — it hangs in the mist feeding on moisture itself and on each other. Your warm exhaled breath is a beacon. So is sweat. Keep your mouth covered when you talk, even on the radio.

Your food and water must be in hard, sealed containers. Nothing open, nothing crumbly, nothing left on a mat. The locals will not steal sandwiches for malice; they will clean them to nothing in a frantic pick, and what they leave behind will be contaminated with film, fungus filaments, castings, and droppings.

### 4. There is no water here, even though everything is wet

There is no fresh water to drink. The pans, the condensation on your suit, the film on the stone, the beads on a hanger's skin — all of it is full of dissolved minerals, bacteria, fungal breath, animal waste, and the animals' own recycled breath. Do not drink it. Do not rinse a wound in it. Do not brush your teeth with it.

Bring all your water sealed, plus purification just in case for an emergency, but plan to carry enough for the whole visit. The same goes for food: nothing local is edible. The bacterial film is not a snack, it's the pasture. It will make a human violently ill.

You also cannot make fire, dry anything, or build shelter from the land. No vegetation. No twigs. No dry ground. For camp, you need a raised, waterproof platform of your own — a cot on stilts, a hammock between two poles planted on mats, a tent whose floor never touches the pavement. everything will be damp to the touch forever. Sleep will happen on top of that, not in it.

### 5. You will get lost, disoriented, and tired with no cues

From the air, this region is basin after basin, ridge after smooth ridge, all grey and black and dull wet white. In the mist, they are identical. There is no sun to track, no day-night temperature change, no shadow to read. The locals have no signal for sleep because there is no signal at all. You will need to import one.

Mark your arrival point with a bright, sealed beacon and a tether line if you are leaving the platform. High-vis color is essential — everything else is camouflage for you here. Wear it, mark your gear with it. Your eyes will strain in the colorless gloom; bring a low-intensity headlamp for close work, but don't flood the basin. For the locals, light is not information. For you, a warm 10-minute light cycle at your "night" will help you enforce sleep when the world gives you none.

Plan for sensory overload and monotony at the same time. The chorus never quietens as you go deeper; it just layers. The ground breathes under you in waves. The mist rolls, settles, drips, rolls again. Nothing hurries and nothing rests. Ordinary humans start to feel spatially lost and a little seasick from the moving floor, and simultaneously bored and anxious from never hearing silence. Short visits are better than expeditions here. Sit, watch a wanderer's track get reclaimed, watch the winged cloud darken a patch of sky and dissolve, and leave before you start to need the sound to stop.

Navigation by sound is possible — the deep pulse, the slap, the percussive shift, the whine — but do not trust it alone. Use compass, beacon, and a pre-planned turnaround time. If the drizzle thickens, the pavement opens wider, feeds faster, puffs more mist, and the clicking gets louder. Visibility will drop, footing will get slicker, and the hangers will be more active. That's your cue to return to the platform, not to push on for a better view.

### 6. Keep the world separate from you, in both directions

This is a closed, balanced loop: wanderers' droppings and pavement shells, grain by grain, smoothed into the ridges that contain the basin. New ground is made by life wearing itself down. There are no artifacts, no nests, no burrows dug in earth. The hollows are worn, not made.

Do not leave anything: no food scraps, no flagging tape on a ridge, no human microbes, no seeds. Sterilize gear before you go in, and decontaminate on the way out in a sealed bag. Skitterers and their eggs, winged things in a seam, film on a boot — all of them will happily hitchhike to another universe and find it very damp and very edible. On return, shower, inspect seams, ears, and boots, and treat your gear as contaminated until cleaned.

### A minimal ordinary-human kit

- Sealed waterproof suit/poncho with hood, sealed goggles, respirator
- Waterproof boots, wide soft soles, extra socks, skin-covering gloves
- Filtered earplugs + bone-conduction radio
- Hard sealed food/water containers, no open food ever
- Folding platform/mat for arrival, camp, and eating
- Raised waterproof sleep system, sleep mask, own light timer
- Bright beacon, tether, high-vis markers
- Headlamp (low), anti-fog wipes, microfiber towel
- Full first aid, especially for cuts and chafing; tweezers and covers for skin breaks
- Spare batteries and waterproof case — condensation will corrode contacts and fog lenses constantly

Above all, prepare to be unimpressive. You are not the apex here, you are not a climber or a sailor or a hunter. You are a warm, noisy, thirsty, eyed intruder standing on a breathing carpet of blind animals who are listening to the stone and drinking the rain through their skins.

If you stand still on your mat, keep your mouth covered, let a wanderer pass, and listen to the separate instruments — the deep sucking pulse, the wet slap, the percussive shift, the papery whine, the ceaseless bright clicking — you will understand what kind of place it is: old, crowded, cold, damp, active, and at rest in motion, with no plant in it and no silence in it.

Then leave before you start to walk like them.
