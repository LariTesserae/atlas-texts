**The Weeping Bowl**

Prepare for four things at once, all of which contradict each other:

1.  It is a freezer and a swamp. The floor is saturated slush over ice, water rises around your boots wherever you step, and nothing is ever dry except the wind-scoured rim.
2.  It is a bright, high, white desert. You are well above the clouds, in thin air, under a vertical white sun that bounces back up from stone and ice. There is essentially no shade.
3.  It is a town under constant construction. The ground heaves, tilts, freezes, floods, and melts on a daily cycle. Paths, channels, and house positions are different every morning.
4.  It is almost silent.

An ordinary traveler fails here not from one dramatic danger, but from getting wet, cold, sunburned, snow-blind, dehydrated, and altitude-sick at the same time, while stepping through a crowded, living town that is busy rebuilding itself.

### 1. Before You Go

Get medically cleared if you have heart, lung, blood-pressure, or severe dental sensitivity problems. The cold "gets into your teeth" — breathe through your nose or a scarf, and don't drink near-freezing water in big gulps.

Do two to three weeks of conditioning first: hill walking with a pack, squats and lifting, and walking slowly uphill while talking. At altitude, if you can't speak in short phrases, you're going too fast.

Acclimate if your route allows it. Ascend gradually, spend the first day at the rim doing almost nothing, and plan your return the same way. No one is coming up quickly from below the cloud-sea to rescue you.

Accept that rescue, firewood, dry shelter, and privacy do not exist in the normal sense. The only chimney tall enough to clear the roofs is the communal hearth-house.

### 2. Eyes and Skin — This Is the Main Event

Locals wear slitted visors of bone and smoked hide and keep their heads down. You need better.

* **Glacier glasses, not sunglasses.** Category 4 or equivalent, with side shields, a snug strap, and a spare pair in a hard case. The light comes from below as well as above, so ordinary sunglasses will burn you under the chin, nose, and lower eyelids.
* **A spare improvised visor.** A slit in cardboard or hide in an emergency. Your glasses will fog going from -cold air into the hearth-house and will ice up from your own breath. Carry a microfiber cloth *inside* your clothing.
* **SPF 50 sunscreen and SPF lip balm,** applied to nose, ears, neck, chin, and the backs of hands. You will not feel warm, you will not sweat much, and you will burn faster at altitude under a white sun.
* **A hat with a neck flap or a balaclava + neck gaiter.** Cover ears and nose. A balaclava will freeze damp from your breath — carry two and rotate them.

You will squint involuntarily all day. Rest your eyes in doorways and look down at the fish pools and ice. Give yourself dark-rest time in the evening if your eyes ache, feel gritty, or water uncontrollably — early snow-blindness.

### 3. Clothing System for Wet Cold

Cotton is useless. It will be damp by morning from the air itself. Bring:

* **Base layer:** Merino or synthetic, two full sets. Seal dry sleep clothes in a waterproof bag with silica and never take them out except to sleep.
* **Mid layers:** Fleece or wool, ventable. You will sweat in the sun while standing in freezing slush. Sweat that freezes in the layer is water against your skin all night.
* **Outer:** Windproof, waterproof shell jacket and pants with pit zips. Condensation beads on everything, roofs weep inside, lanes squelch. You need to shed rain, melt, and splashes, not just wind.
* **Hands:** Liner gloves *under* mittens, plus a third dry pair in a pouch. Cold stiffens fingers, and locals do constant fine handwork — bone needles, cord-tying, mending. You will need dexterity for tasks, warmth for waiting. Tape fingers to prevent frostbite before you feel it.
* **A headlamp.** Not for the bright day, but for interiors, evening when the light "drops abruptly," and for seeing the clear-ice lanes and fringe ice that grows inward over paths at night. Keep batteries in an inner pocket; cold kills them.

Everything you wear will stay faintly damp. Plan to hang one set to air in the communal hearth-house — with permission — and rotate.

### 4. Feet and Movement

This is where travelers get hurt.

* **Tall, insulated, waterproof boots** with aggressive tread, over waterproof socks, plus gaiters. Low shoes will fill with meltwater in one step. The floor is slush over an impermeable ice lens a hand's breadth down.
* **Microspikes / crampons.** Lanes are polished clear ice, wooden plank bridges slick with freeze, and basalt terraces worn smooth. You'll walk over last year's lanes and drowned storehouses visible *through* the ice, distorted and doubled. Depth perception is terrible in that glare.
* **Walking poles** for the rim and terraces, but shorten or carry them in town — lanes are no wider than two people.
* **Walk the lanes, not the pools.** Never walk on a pool lid unless locals do. Under the clear ice are fish schools so thick the water flashes. The ice can be thin over freezing water. A fall-through here is a hypothermia emergency in minutes.
* **Expect a new route daily.** Frost heave lifts a house a finger's width, a new lens shoves a retaining wall, a channel blocks and floods a different row. Do not trust yesterday's path, your parked pack, or a map. Ask every morning, and watch where locals step.
* **Watch for the burrower tunnels.** The forearm-sized furred swimmer paddles through melt-ditches between houses on tunnels that collapse and refill daily. Don't step on, block, or park gear on bank edges and planks unless locals do.
* **Don't move stones lightly.** Under every loose stone are mites, springtails, and beetles grazing lichen. If you must, wear gloves and put it back exactly.

No wheels. No wheeled luggage. They use sleds. Bring a small, waterproof 30-40L pack that fits those two-person lanes.

### 5. Food, Water, Sleep

There is almost no plant life, no turf, no wood to waste. Lamps burn fat and burrower dung. The food chain is fish, algae, bird waste, mineral film, and each other.

* **Bring your own ready-to-eat, high-fat food:** pemmican, smoked fish, chocolate, nuts, cheese in wax, dried meat. Foods high in water will freeze rock-solid. You should not expect to cook. If you have a stove, use it only in an emergency, sparingly, outside the town ditch system, and carry out all fuel and ash. Do not offer to build a fire.
* **Bring salt, vitamins, and electrolytes.** You will dehydrate in cold, bright air without feeling thirsty. Drink regularly, even though water is everywhere.
* **Do not drink from pools, channels, or frost cracks.** They are full of fish waste, bird guano from the rim above, algae, and the town's drainage. Water also *climbs* a few inches up stone faces here before freezing — drainage does not behave normally. Melt clean ice from the high rim weep only with local permission, or carry water up in an insulated bottle kept inside your pack.
* **Sleeping:** Houses are low, shared-wall, sod/skin/slate over stone footings sunk in slush, with condensation beading on the inside. They tilt and are re-leveled. Bring a sleeping bag rated far below what you expect, an insulated mat, and a vapor barrier. Expect to sleep on a floor in a crowded house, with wall-sharing noise of movement, ticking ice, and drip-drip. Light eye protection helps.

Waste: **Nothing rots.** Dropped food, tools, and trash freeze-mummify intact under slush and can be found months later, faintly glowing. Pack out *everything* — wrappers, food scraps, toilet paper, medical waste. Ask hosts where human waste goes. Never use a lane, channel, pool, or store pit.

### 6. Health Risks Specific to Here

* **Frostbite** of nose, ears, fingers despite sun. White skin that stays white after warming = stop, rewarm gently, do not rub snow.
* **Altitude sickness:** headache, nausea, breathlessness, poor sleep. Slow down, descend if it worsens. No alcohol to "warm up" — it cools your core and dehydrates you.
* **Infections from slush:** Keep cuts covered. Wash hands with warm water at the hearth-house before eating. Do not eat raw local fish or translucent worm-eels unless offered and you understand the parasite risk.
* **Cold and damp:** Earaches, chilblains, and tooth pain. Keep neck and jaw covered.

### 7. Animals and the Closed Ecology

This is not wilderness to explore, it's a closed, crowded food system.

* **Above:** Rim walls packed shoulder-to-shoulder with silent white cliff-birds. Keep off their nesting ledges. They arrive and leave without song. Flushing them could collapse nests and remove a food source.
* **Below:** Fish under pool ice, worm-eels in slush, the burrowing swimmer in ditches. Don't break pool ice to look, don't dam or divert a melt channel — the town's culverts and retaining walls deliberately direct water away from doors and toward the fish pools.
* **Plants:** Knee-high willow-thin shrubs on channel banks, lichen crusts, knee-high moss stains. They are sparse and slow. Don't pick, scrape, or trample them for "samples." Don't bring in seeds, soil, plants, or pets from your world — you could introduce something this sparse place cannot handle.

Don't take fish from drying racks, ice-storehouses, or bird eggs without a clear trade. That is the town's larder.

### 8. Social Preparation — The Most Important Kit

About seventy dwellings, wall to wall, roof overlapping roof, in a few hundred paces. A market hollow in the center. Everyone is carrying, bailing, re-tying, re-hanging doors, and re-leveling houses all day.

* **Speak low and short.** Breath freezes before a shout forms. Shouting is both physically useless and socially rude. Learn a few phrases, use gestures, use the market for trade.
* **Be useful before you are curious.** Offer to bail, carry ice blocks, haul sleds, hold a wall, mend cord. Don't just observe a construction site that is also their home.
* **Trade, don't buy.** Coins and multiverse credits are worthless. They trade fish for fat, for a stone blade, for a length of cord. Useful gifts: good cordage, bone needles, fish hooks, beeswax, salt, a single small knife (they keep prized knives wrapped in fat — wrap yours the same). Don't display a sack of metal or advanced tools; it disrupts their economy and makes you a target for theft in a place with no police.
* **Respect privacy in density.** Walls are shared, space is not private, store-pits are in floors, sleds are on roofs. Don't touch a roof, a storehouse, or a boat-sled. Ask before entering a house.
* **The dead are architecture.** They lay the dead in shallow ice niches in the rim wall, sealed in transparent weeping water, visible and smiling faintly. Never touch, lean on, climb, or photograph them casually. Walk past them as you would a grave.
* **Don't litter history.** Under the ice are tools, lanes, even houses from years ago, preserved. Don't pry them out. Don't drop anything you hope to retrieve later; the ice will take it.

### 9. Daily Rhythm to Plan Around

**Morning:** Check what heaved overnight. Ice lenses grow at night, channels shift, lanes ice over. Dress before you exit; the fringe ice on paths and the weep threads on walls are fresh.
**Midday:** Peak glare. Head down, visor on, sunscreen reapplied. Work will be frantic and silent — squelch, drip, tick-tick.
**Evening:** Light stays hard then drops abruptly. Get back to your house before the freeze sets in. Lamps are fat/dung — low, smoky light. Check boots, hang layers, secure your pack off stone faces where climbing water can freeze around it.

### 10. Packing Checklist (Condensed)

Glacier glasses + spare, balaclava x2, hat, liner gloves + mittens + spare, 2 base sets, fleece, waterproof shell, tall insulated waterproof boots + gaiters + microspikes, headlamp + warm batteries, insulated bottle, water filter (for rim melt only), high-fat cold food, salt/electrolytes, SPF 50 + lip balm, sleeping bag + mat + vapor barrier + dry bag for sleep clothes, microfiber cloths, first aid (frostbite, altitude, blister, cuts), cord, needles, hooks, beeswax, a small wrapped knife, sturdy tape, trash bag for *all* waste, small pack, no wheels.

### 11. What Not to Bring or Do

No campfire, no wood chopping, no wheelie bag, no heavy metal kit, no cotton clothing, no fresh food/plants/seeds, no pets, no loud speaker, no drone over the bird cliffs, no mirror to flash sun, no alcohol, no expectations of dry socks, shade, a signposted trail, or a quiet night's sleep in a private room.

If you can walk slowly all day in glaring cold, keep your eyes covered, keep your feet dry and your hands warm, speak quietly, carry your own weight, pack out every scrap, and help rebuild whatever tilted overnight — you'll be welcome. The bowl will look, from the rim in the evening, like a calm, established white and grey hamlet. Up close, it will be wet, crowded, bright, freezing, and never for a minute still.
