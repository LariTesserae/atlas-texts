**Sinderhold — a preparer's guide for an ordinary human**

This is not a ruin, a cave, or a wilderness. It's a working town of about 800 people squeezed, raw and still-cooling, into a fresh crack in hot basalt. 400 paces end-to-end. Three and four stories everywhere, a canyon of houses in the middle, and no sky at all.

You can walk it in ten minutes. You can also get lost in it for an afternoon, because almost nowhere is un-built. Every roof is the next house's floor, bridge-streets cross alleys overhead, and the whole space is layered — lane, bridge, cable, laundry, pipe — like a thicket.

And it is hot before you even start. 40°C at the stair-head, close to 50°C in the middle. That is 104-122°F, permanently, with humidity so high your sweat won't evaporate. For a surface-adapted human who is not acclimated, this is the entire problem. Everything else is secondary.

Plan for heat first, city second, sightseeing last.

### 1. The honest reality check

An ordinary traveler should treat a first visit as a 60-90 minute exposure, not an exploration.

- No cool escape: The "cool rooms" in the dwelling stacks, where fans reach, are 1-2 degrees less. People there call that cool. You will not.
- No air conditioning, no shade, no sky. Your body cannot dump heat by sweating because the air is already saturated and 40-50°C. You will lose water and salt just standing still.
- If you have any cardiovascular issues, diabetes, are pregnant, very young, elderly, or are on medication that affects heat regulation, do not go in unaccompanied, and do not stay down for long. Get medical advice first — this is general travel preparation, not medical advice.
- The descent itself drops pressure quickly. Your ears will pop on the straight flights down. The rock is young, sharp, and still ticking/pinging as it cools in fits. That is normal here. The floor heaves in fractions, buildings sit on screw jacks, doors swell with damp and jam. Don't interpret every crack as collapse, but don't lean on fresh walls either — they hold heat and give it back through your palms.

Spend a few days above first if you can. Let your body even start to notice heat before you descend. Eat, sleep, drink *before* the stairs.

### 2. Your pack — what to bring, and what to leave on the surface

Think: desert hiking + industrial site + wet cave + night city, all at once.

**Clothing paradox:** You need to be cool *and* protected.

- Loose, lightweight, quick-dry long sleeves and long pants, light colored. Not wool, not heavy cotton, not jeans that will stay damp for hours. Long sleeves protect you from accidental brushes with hot iron, steam, and dripping alkaline water, and from sharp basalt.
- A wide, thin hat or bandana. It will shade you from warm ceiling rain and drips, and double as a dust mask.
- No umbrella. Lanes are too narrow for two carts. An umbrella will hit cables, laundry, and bridge-streets.
- A very thin, breathable rain poncho or pack cover, worn over, not under. You'll need it for 10 minutes every hour or so, then you'll overheat in it. Be ready to take it on and off.
- Change of socks, and anti-chafe for thighs and small of back. You will stay damp. That alone causes blisters and fungal irritation fast.

**Footwear — critical:**
Thick, broken-in boots with stiff, rockered soles, ankle support, and a steel or composite toe. The floor is not soil. It's crushed basalt shards and glassy splinters that bite through thin soles and get finer underfoot. The lanes are narrow, wet, oily, and sometimes blocked by the little rail cart. You will be looking down a lot.

**Hands:**
Sturdy work gloves. Not for climbing — for not touching. Walls are hot enough to warm your palms after you let go. Valve wheels in the Engine Hall are worked in pairs because one person can't hold them long enough, even with gloves. Don't use walls to brace yourself.

**Lungs and eyes:**
This air has sterilized the place of flies, rats, pigeons, dogs, cats, insects entirely. The heat, dry steam, sulfur and hot iron smell, and process chemicals from castings, glass grinding, and furnace work are why. That tells you what it's doing to your lungs.

- A good respirator / P2-N95 or equivalent for near forges, the Loom Shed, and the Engine Hall. A cloth bandana is not enough for silica dust.
- Anti-fog glasses or sunglasses, even though there's no sun. Working hours the lamps burn hard white, furnace doors glow orange, and a tap can flash a brighter light the length of the canyon for a second. Your eyes will have to transition from dim amber to harsh white and back all day.
- Your own headlamp as backup, not as primary. The town is lit every 20 paces, but cables sag, lamps lean in the vent-cycle, and corners are dark. Don't rely on a phone torch — heat and humidity will fog lenses and kill batteries.

**Water, food, and heat-safety kit:**
- At least 2-3 liters of your *own* water per person, per short visit, plus electrolytes. Drink on schedule, not when thirsty. You will lose salt visibly — white on skin, salt on lips.
- No alcohol or extra caffeine before or during. Both worsen heat loss.
- Sealed, shelf-stable food. Salty, easy to eat without cooking. There are market stalls, and no flies land on them, but nothing down there is refrigerated in any real sense. If you buy local food, eat it hot and immediately.
- Burn gel / small first aid kit, blister plasters, painkillers, any personal medication in a cool, waterproof pouch. Keep it on your body, not in an overheated pack at the bottom.
- A small towel that dries fast. You will use it to wipe sweat so it doesn't run into eyes, and to dry hands before touching a door — damp doors jam, and you don't want to force them.

**Multiverse / electronics:**
- Leave a beacon, check-in time, and your return coordinate *at the stair-head*, not down in the city. There is no cell signal, no GPS, and no sky. A magnetic compass is unreliable in basalt and will confuse you in a fully built canyon.
- Assume your device will fog, sweat, and overheat. Put phone/camera in a dry bag. Condensation will form instantly when you step back from a boiler into cooler air — and vice versa. Wipe, don't blow.
- No fuel canisters, fireworks, gas cartridges, or anything flammable/expansive. Heat is free down there; they *borrow* it from rock and boilers. Bringing fire to a place that makes furnace brick and glass is both useless and dangerous.
- Batteries swell in heat. Keep spares cool until you need them, and don't leave a pack in the middle of the canyon while you wander off.

**What NOT to bring:**
- No pets, including service animals if you can possibly avoid it. There are no animals in Sinderhold because they cannot live there. Heat, chemicals, and no food/water suitable for them will harm it quickly. People keep no pets and could not keep them.
- No plants, seeds, soil, insects, or open food from another world. The ecology is fragile in the opposite direction: a few hundred square meters of pale succulents, bleached ferns, and moss in the gutters of Cistern Square, living on runoff the city makes anyway. They are tentative colonists. One pest, one weed, one heavy foot in a gutter could be an introduction event. The town itself is the thicket — don't step into its gutter plants and don't pick them.
- No heavy trade in fuel. They export things that are expensive to fire *above*. If you trade, bring things that are cool, fresh, dry, and useful: clean water, dried surface food, good un-heat-damaged textiles, paper (sparingly — it flutters in the vent cycle). Expect their products — castings, furnace brick, ground glass vials, riveted machine parts — to be hot, heavy, and sharp until they've cooled.

### 3. The descent and getting your bearings

The stair is fresh-cut, single straight flights, walls close enough to touch. Don't touch for balance if you can avoid it.

Go slow. Yawn, swallow, pinch your nose gently. Rest on the landing where the air changes and pressure settles. That is your last cool moment.

Once in, orient by absolutes, because everything looks the same: basalt brick, iron, smudged glass:

1. **Entrance is behind you, always.** The stair-head is the cooler end. Engine Hall is at the far end, 400 paces away, cathedral-scale, 30 meters high, with two great boilers. Heat, boiler-breathing, and valve work get louder as you go that way.
2. **Heat and air flow.** Hot damp air rises, gathers in the dome, weeps, then vents sigh open at the far end and pull air that way. If you feel air moving and paper fluttering, the cycle is turning. The middle of the canyon is hottest — "thick enough to drink."
3. **Count lamps.** They're strung every 20 paces on sagging cable bundles overhead. Counting helps when bridge-streets above make a lane feel like a different town.
4. **Cistern Square is your only open reference.** Not much bigger than a house plot, paved, walled, half-roofed with iron. A single pool, 3 meters across, steaming, too hot and mineral to drink. If you find it, you're roughly in the middle and can reorient.
5. **Look up carefully, not long.** You'll see more building than sky — because there is none. Balconies lean over doorways. Windows face windows across lanes too narrow for two carts and open onto a neighbor's kitchen. Do not stare into windows, lean over railings, or stand under laundry lines and pipe bundles dripping warm water.

For a first visit, do not try to explore the third-level bridge-streets systematically. Walk the main canyon end-to-end, find the Square, look into the Engine Hall from the doorway at a safe distance, and leave. The density rewards a second, slower visit. It punishes hurrying.

Navigation trick for ordinary humans: mark your route mentally with *sound*, not just sight. Drip counting from eave to gutter, the rhythmic clank of the press in the Loom Shed, the belt slapping, the cart on its fixed rail loop from Engine Hall to Square. This city is quiet in layers — rock and humidity muffle it, so sounds are intimate, like the next room. That is usually literally true. Keep your own voice low. Shouting carries into someone's home.

### 4. Timing: work, quiet, and the 90-minute breath

Sinderhold has no daylight. Workers' days are measured by lamplight.

- **Working hours:** Lamps hard white, furnaces glowing, presses and looms running, cart moving, people hurrying in work clothes. It's loud in a close way, hot in waves from furnace taps, and busy. Good for seeing industry. Bad for getting out of the way.
- **Quiet hours:** Lamps sink to amber, town dims, machines exhale, fans still. Heat ebbs a fraction. This is when the gutter plants bloom — tiny waxy flowers in the moss only then. It's also when you'll hear the rock tick and the dripping most clearly. Easiest time to learn the place, easiest time to feel its uncanny silence.

Superimposed on that is the cavern weather, about every 90 minutes:

Rock sweats dark, then warm fine rain falls from the ceiling for ~10 minutes, rattling iron roofs, spattering the Square, filling gutters. Then vents sigh open — you feel it before you hear it — and pull the excess up a shaft into darkness above. Heat and humidity build again.

Plan to be somewhere you can wait out the rain without blocking a lane — under a half-roof, in a doorway recess locals use, but not against a damp jammed door. Your poncho/hat goes on. Electronics go in the dry bag. Then it clears, relatively.

Do not try to do hard physical work down there. Lifting, climbing stairs fast, rushing between buildings — for a visitor, that's when heat exhaustion hits.

### 5. Water, eating, sleeping — hard rules

- **Do not drink the Cistern.** Don't drink gutter water. It's warm, alkaline, smells of sulfur and hot iron, and sits oily black in alleys. It is runoff, not a supply.
- **Do not splash it.** It will irritate skin and eyes if it sits, and everything stays damp anyway.
- **Conserve your own water.** Pipes sweat. Walls feel damp. A water jar from the Square will condense instantly. That's not a drinkable source, it's condensation on a hot world.
- If you stay over (experienced travelers only), sleep in a dwelling stack only if invited, with fans, and expect doors to be swollen, floors to settle, and dripping to count all night. Bring earplugs, a sleep mask for the white-to-amber cycle, and a dry change of clothes in a sealed bag. Your body will still need to acclimate for days. Most ordinary travelers should sleep *above* and visit down.
- Keep food sealed until you eat it. No flies doesn't mean sterile — it means heat and chemicals. Warm food spoils silently.

### 6. People and etiquette

Eight hundred people, almost all working. They live cheek-by-jowl, they built into rock before it softened, and they depend on the boilers like a body around a heart. They will make space for a careful visitor. They will not make space for a careless one.

- Don't block lanes, the rail cart line, or work pairs at valves. Don't touch valves, wheels, gauges, or boilers — even to pose. They are hot, dangerous, and operated in pairs for a reason.
- Don't knock on doors randomly. Doors jam; forcing them damages them and announces you to the whole stack.
- Don't stare into windows, photograph people up close in their kitchens, or flash a camera at a furnace tap. Ask, in low voice, and accept no.
- Trade fairly and leave the plants in the gutters alone. Those pale rosettes and white ferns are a point of pride precisely because they live on machine runoff. Step around them. If you see a local tending them, that's your cue to watch and learn, not to garden.
- Remember there is no animalsong, no birds, no hum of insects. The quiet is not emptiness — it's pressure. Some travelers find it deeply unsettling after an hour. That's normal. It means your brain is noticing the absence. Don't bring a music player to fill it. Listen to the boiler breathing and the stone pinging. That is the local soundscape.

### 7. Specific hazards to watch for

- **Contact burns:** walls, iron facing, boiler pipes, furnace doors, even "cool" door handles near shops. Gloves, no leaning.
- **Steam and ceiling rain:** Burns without flame. Keep forearms covered when you pass a boiler or a vent sigh.
- **Sharp floor:** shards, crushed brick, protruding screw jacks under buildings, rail tracks, uneven heaved slabs. Watch feet, especially looking up at bridges.
- **Overhead:** sagging cable bundles, pipes, laundry dripping, bridge-street edges. Duck not just for height, but for drip lines.
- **Moving cart:** fixed loop, slow, but it carries parts from Engine Hall. Listen for it. Don't stand on its line. Lanes are too narrow to step aside quickly.
- **Disorientation:** three dimensions of identical material. If you feel lost, stop, find the heat gradient, go toward cooler air and the sound of the entrance stair, or ask a worker — loudly enough to be heard, quietly enough not to enter their living room.
- **Screw jacks and fresh rock:** Don't sit on jacks, don't carve initials in basalt knobs on the ceiling ("infant stalactites" no more than a thumb's length), don't hammer the walls. It's still forming.

### 8. A sane first-visit plan

1. Day before: hydrate, sleep, gear check, leave beacon at stair-head.
2. Early descent in gloves, boots, respirator in pocket, 2-3L water on you.
3. Walk straight, slowly, to Cistern Square. Rest in its shade (such as it is), drink, check pulse/heat.
4. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, headache, cramps, confusion, stop *immediately* and go up. Heat illness advances fast in 50°C damp air. Don't "push to the Engine Hall."
5. View Engine Hall from a marked distance, mask on, gloves on, no touching.
6. Time your exit *before* a ceiling rain if you're carrying electronics, or ride it out under cover if you're not pressed. Exit up the fresh stair slowly — your ears will pop again, and you'll be tired.
7. On top, cool down, rehydrate with electrolytes, change socks and shirt, dry everything. Debrief while it's fresh — you'll forget which bridge-street was which within hours because everything is brick, iron, and amber.

Come back only if you were fine, curious, and respectful. The longer-term residents can live here for years and still find a new bridge-street above a lane they thought they knew. You, as an ordinary human, should find the fact that anyone lives here at all — hot, damp, dim, dense, quiet, and complete, with no outside — to be the main thing to carry home.

Pack for heat you can't escape, move like you're in someone's hallway, drink only what you brought, and leave the pale plants and the hot wheels exactly as you found them.
