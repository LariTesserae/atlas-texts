If you're an ordinary human, prepare to be bored, wet, cold, lost, and then astonished at knee-height.

This place is not hostile because it wants to kill you. It's hostile because it hasn't finished being made yet, and you are a warm, tall, dry animal built for sheltered lowland air. Everything here — the thin air, the water that can't go down, the wind that sorts stones like a factory — will work on you exactly as it works on the landscape, every day, without pausing for you.

Plan accordingly.

### 1. Your body is the main problem to solve

You will arrive already disadvantaged. If you step in suddenly from elsewhere, you won't have weeks to walk up from the treeline. Your lungs will notice the altitude before your eyes do: thin air, a sun that never gets high or strong, and a lot of hard walking uphill on loose ground.

Walk slowly the first day. Not because you're lazy — because a pack that feels fine at sea level will leave you breathless on a scree cone that hasn't slumped yet. Sharp, angular, freshly shattered stone does not give underfoot. It rolls. Ankles, knees, and calves will do all the work. Train for that before you go: loaded hill walking, balance work, carrying weight on uneven ground. 

Eat and drink on a schedule, not by thirst. The air dries you out, the wind steals heat even when you don't feel hot, and the light is so dim and lateral all day that your body misreads the time. You will be sunburned and on the edge of hypothermia in the same hour — UV is high at altitude and bounces off pale silt and lingering snow seams, while the wind finds every gap. Breath will fog at midday. It is not winter. It is just high, wet, and exposed.

You also cannot stay still. Morning frost forms on the moss and skins over the melt pools. Midday the springs pulse stronger, the sheets of water darken the silt, and the ground softens. Afternoon cloud builds on the knobs and the wind comes back to re-sort what it did last week. Night resets it all: water in cracks freezes, lifts stones by a millimetre, splits ice pools with a rifle-crack so loud in the quiet you will jump. A place that remakes itself nightly does not forgive a camp pitched in a low polygon or a route planned for afternoon.

Travel early, rest through the worst wind, never trust that midday will last.

### 2. Clothes: wet and wind are the same enemy

Forget cotton entirely, and be skeptical of down. Fog condenses on anything cold — rock, moss tips, your coat, a reindeer's back. Rain sheets across permafrost because it can't sink. Cliff faces weep for days after rain. You will not stay dry. You need to stay warm *while* wet.

Think in three systems:

**Base layer** — merino or synthetic, snug. No cotton socks, no cotton anything. You will sweat on scree, then freeze standing in wind.

**Insulation** — synthetic puffy, not down. Down lofts beautifully until fog or a weeping ledge drips on it, then it collapses. You need insulation that still works damp.

**Shell** — windproof *and* waterproof outer jacket and trousers with pit zips and full zips so you can vent without stripping in the wind. Wind here carries sand in low dense streams that sandblast rock to a polish. It will sandblast your face, your lens, the fly of your tent. A balaclava, a neck gaiter you can pull over your nose, and proper sunglasses — not just for sun, but for sand and sheeting glare — are essential. Goggles are better when the wind picks up. The light is silver-grey and coming sideways under cloud; shadows are blue and soft-edged and useless for judging depth. Your eyes will water constantly. Protect them.

**Hands and feet kill trips here.** Bring three pairs of gloves: thin liner gloves for fiddly work (GPS, stakes, camera, stove), warm mittens, and one dry spare pair in a waterproof stuff sack that never leaves your pack. Same with socks: at least three changes, rotated, hung inside your clothing to dry with body heat. Feet will get wet in saturated peat and sheet water no matter what boot you wear. Waterproof membranes fail when you wade through a polygon. Choose a stiff, high, ankle-supporting boot with aggressive tread and gaiters, and accept wet feet as a given. Dry them actively every night or you will get trench foot in exactly the conditions that look like summer.

Add a warm hat system — a windproof hat plus balaclava — and a head net for the weeks when midges hatch. In melt pools they rise in shimmering clouds so dense they look like heat haze. They will go in your eyes, ears, and mouth. Springtails and mites are working in the wet moss by the million; you don't want to drink that water unfiltered either.

Pack everything in dry bags within dry bags. Plastic will crack in cold. Zippers will ice. Keep batteries, stove fuel, and an emergency layer in your sleeping bag at night.

### 3. Shelter: there's nowhere to hide for you

There is no forest, no town, no wood to burn, no dry ridge to trust. Ground is permafrost a few hand-spans down, with bare silt, shattered stone, and patterned gravel repeating to the horizon.

Pitch a low, four-season tent, and expect to anchor it to rock, not soil. In sorted gravels the fines in the centre are soft silt, the cobbles at the rim are sharp and loose. Frost polygons expand a few centimetres a year and reset stones every winter; a stake in silt will heave out overnight. Use deadman anchors with rocks from a scree pile — not moss, not a patterned circle — and guys angled into the wind. The wind doesn't build to a howl; it arrives as a hiss of saltating sand across a sill and then slams.

Choose your pitch like the animals do: not in a fog-pooling basin, not on a moss mat, not in a lee hollow where snow seams linger, not where a spring will pulse stronger in the afternoon. A high, gravelly hummock with a boulder's wind shadow is luxury. And it will still be wet underneath you. Bring a thick, closed-cell pad plus an inflatable. Cold is conductive in wet ground.

You will have no fire. No wood, no peat you should burn — moss cushions that look like stains may be years old, lichen on a stable rock may be thousands. Build no fire, dig no latrine. Waste does not drain here; it sheets sideways and feeds the midges. Pack out all human waste, all food scraps. Your stove is your only heat for cooking, and it needs a full windshield — afternoon wind will kill a flame or blow fuel sideways. Keep the canister warm; cold fuel won't vaporize. Your food must be high-calorie, edible cold, and sealed against grit. Wind-blown sand will get into everything, including your mouth. Zip bags within zip bags.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere drinkable as-is. Glacial melt runs milky with flour and carries silt in braiding sheets that never commit to a channel. Springs weep from cracks, black melt pools sit still and look deep but freeze overnight. Filter with a pre-filter and purification, settle silt first or you'll clog it, and carry more than you think you need — dehydration is silent in cold wind.

### 4. Navigation: the view will not help you

You can walk for an hour and the view changes by a degree of tilt. For another hour there will be nothing to mark the hour by. Patterns repeat. Cloud caps form on high knobs in the morning and slide off by noon. Fog pools in basins and condenses on you. Ravens croak so far off the sound arrives before you see them, which tells you how little sound carries.

Do not rely on landmarks, shadows, or "that looks different." Shadows are blue and diffuse. The sun never climbs high enough to give you a true south. Your footprints in silt will be softened by melt or wind within hours. Circling is the normal human mistake here.

Navigate by instrument and discipline: GPS with batteries kept warm inside your clothes (cold kills them fast), a compass you know how to use without the sun, and a pace count — time-and-distance legs with a firm turn-back time. Mark your breadcrumbs in the device, not on the ground. Leave the patterned ground as you found it; cairns built from sorted stones just become part of the pattern and confuse the next person, and they disturb the sorting the place is doing anyway.

If you can't see the next knob, assume weather is making its own decision. It will. Light fails early and without colour — no long, warm dusk to warn you. Carry a headlamp even in midsummer. At night it will be a plain, dim dark, but the pattern of the ground will still be faintly visible. That is not enough to travel by.

### 5. Animals and ground shock

From a distance the place reads empty. Up close it is full, but thin and low.

Ptarmigan nest in the gravel patterns, perfectly matched to stone, and will explode upward in a whirr inches from your boot. Reindeer cross in thin columns, sometimes hundreds, steaming in fog, and take days to pass a point. A herd a half-kilometre away sounds like thunder on stone. Arctic foxes trot ridges at dawn, hares turn white against late snow, pikas whistle from rock piles and vanish, ravens ride updrafts along fault scarps.

Do not approach, chase, or try to get a photo by closing distance. Give herds a wide berth — they are moving across ten kilometres of empty ground on a schedule you are not part of. Do not step on moss mats, sedge hollows, or lichen crusts to get closer to butterflies that do not fly far or beetles under stones. A whole slope may have three moss cushions. Each is an island held together by a stone shading the wind and ground thawing a degree deeper. Your boot is a landslide to them.

Fresh scree below fault scarps is loose and will avalanche without warning, with a roar in this quiet that seems far larger than it is. Do not traverse steep, raw faces after a freeze-thaw night. Do not step onto ice-skinned pools in the morning; the thin night ice will break and you will be in black, numbing water to the shin. Do not drink from pools where midges are hatching.

### 6. Mental preparation

Prepare for stillness. For long stretches the only noise will be your step on wet gravel, a drip ticking from a ledge, wind coming and going with nothing to rattle. Then a pool splits, scree falls far off, hooves thunder, a raven rolls a croak across a basin — and the stillness returns, making each sound enormous.

Ordinary humans get unsettled by that. Day after day of grey-brown sameness, of everything moving on overlapping schedules that never sync, is draining. Kneel down. That is the trick the place offers. At knee height the moss mat has a world in it, the melt pool is shimmering with life, the patterned gravel is sorting itself in real time. Abundance here is not concentration. It's a thin film over a vast substrate, coverage over time and space. If you stay standing and staring at the horizon, you will miss it entirely.

Go in the narrow summer window — late spring will still freeze you at night, snow seams shrinking at noon and growing again by dark; too late and the freeze returns. Bring a margin for evacuation: there is no road, no signal, no one to come for you. Self-rescue gear, an emergency bivy, a repair kit for tent and stove, and the humility to turn back when cloud builds at midday are not optional.

In short: dress for wet wind, not for cold alone. Sleep off the moss, anchor to rock, filter the milky water, navigate by number not by sight, walk slow, watch your step, pack out everything, and expect to be briefly lonely and then suddenly surrounded by steaming reindeer, exploding birds, and a hiss of sand.

It's a normal place by the standards of cold, high ground. That's why it will test an ordinary human so completely.
