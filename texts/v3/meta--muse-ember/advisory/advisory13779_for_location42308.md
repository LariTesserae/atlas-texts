If you are an ordinary human, Urmesh will punish every assumption you bring from a watery world. It is not a cave to explore, not a desert ruin to wander, and not a jungle to forage. It is a sealed vessel, a great kiln-pot sunk under a plain. You do not visit it for long, and you do not visit it casually. You visit it prepared to be hot, dry, dim, and completely unresupplied for as long as you are inside.

Think of the inside as: a low dome you can walk across in under an hour, but where the ceiling makes it intimate and the empty distances make it feel enormous. The floor and walls radiate heat back at you. Shade does not cool you. There is no wind except the one the building makes for itself through its vents. And there is no water — not in the channels, not in the basin, not in the plants, not in the air.

Prepare for that, in this order:

### 1. Water is the trip

This is the one strange rule, and everything else follows from it.

- **You must carry in every drop you will drink, and every drop you might need for heat illness, eye flushing, or a mouthful for the climb out.** There is nowhere to refill, purify, boil, or cool anything. A filter is useless. The ornamental basin, the dry channels to the wall, the thick leaves — none of them contain drinkable water.
- **Do not drink the plants.** The broad paddle leaves, kiln-vines, resin-trees, and ash-tongues are lush but hold hot, turpentine-smelling resin, not water. It beads, drips, hardens, is toxic to swallow, will irritate skin, and is flammable. The sulfur-yellow flowers are spicy and irritant, not food. The hard pods that swell, tick, and burst are seeds, not fruit for you.
- **Your body will lie to you.** In this dry, hot, still air, sweat evaporates almost as fast as it forms. You will feel less sweaty than you are. Thirst will lag well behind loss. Sip on schedule, not by thirst — roughly a litre an hour inside in heat like this for a walking adult, more at midday, and bring extra electrolytes. By the time you feel dizzy, headachey, or your mouth is truly cracked, you are already behind, and you still have to climb out.
- **Treat water containers as gear, not an afterthought.** A metal bottle left on that floor will become scalding. A plastic bottle in the sun on the plain will heat up. Keep water insulated, off the ground, in shade *outside* where you can find it, and carry it in small, flexible loads. If you can make two short carries through the entry — water first, then you — do that. If you cannot, assume your pack must be small enough to fit the way in *and* the way out when you are tired and hot.
- **Plan the return on the same budget.** You will spend water on the shimmering plain before you ever kneel, and again on the way back. Arrive at the seam already hot and thirsty and you have wasted your margin.

Toilet, hand-washing, and food hygiene are also impossible inside. Go before you descend. Eat only sealed food, keep it covered from pumice dust, and wash hands with wipes after you are back outside. Bring a small waste kit if you may stay more than an hour. Do not use the granary, kilns, basin, or houses for this.

### 2. Heat that shade cannot fix

You will find the heat before you find the place, on the low plain of cracked mineral plates. Inside, the floor is hot underfoot even in deep shadow, walls radiate, and the low dome traps a hot layer at head height. Ducking a little helps. Standing upright under the ribs and light-plates puts you in the hottest air.

- **Time it for the cool edge of the cycle, not the middle.** Early morning is best: leaves are just unfurling, dust spirals are slow, floor plates have not yet begun their midday volley of snaps and crackles. Midday is when the translucent plates brighten a fraction, stones expand and pop like fired twigs, pods go off, and vent pipes sigh into a roaring flute-note. Evening deepens the dimness. Night is contraction ticks and house-creaks in animal-silent darkness — atmospheric, but hard to see glassy floor plates and narrow stairs in. For an ordinary traveler, a short morning visit of 60-90 minutes inside, with a hard turnaround time set *before* you descend, is the sane plan.
- **Dress for radiant heat, dust, and resin, not for sun alone.** Loose, light, natural-fiber long sleeves and trousers protect you when you kneel on the steps, brush a wall, or fall on shattered plates — and they let air move. A wide, soft hat protects from the outside plain, from overhead resin drips, and from bumping the low ribs. No heavy synthetics that melt, no shorts and sandals.
- **Footwear is safety equipment.** Thick, closed, lugged soles — boots, not sneakers. The paving is cracked, glassy, uneven, split by plant roots, worn concave on the entry steps, and hot enough to heat through a thin sole. You will be kneeling at the seam, so bring a folded mat or knee pad for the plain and the first steps. Test any floor before I putting weight on it; never go barefoot.
- **Rest is not recovery.** Sitting in the deepest shadow at the edge of the bowl will not cool you, because the stone around you is hot. Sit, if you must, on an insulated mat away from resin trees and pod clusters, drink, and know that the only real cooling is back up the 20 steps and out.

### 3. The way in — and the way out

At the top is a dark seam no wider than a hand, breathing hot pulses that smell of baked clay and crushed green leaves. You kneel to see it. Then 20 hand-cut steps descend.

Scout it before you commit. If your shoulders, hips, or pack do not fit comfortably with room to spare, turn back. That is not failure; the builders were lean people with hand tools, and the seam was not made for a loaded modern backpack. If you go, go slim, go with a buddy waiting outside if possible, and keep a rope or simply a clear plan to lower a pack by hand rather than carry it on the steps. Those concave steps are dusty and treacherous when tired.

Crucially, **test your exit early.** Go in a few steps, come back out, confirm you can climb with your full water load against the up-breathing heat. Keep at least a third of your strength, water, and time in reserve for leaving. The exit is up, into a breathing slit of hot air, on legs that will be trembling from heat and cracked plates. People overestimate the walk across the bowl and underestimate the climb out.

Outside, the plain shimmers and distorts distance. Take bearings and photos of the seam and plaza landmarks *before* you descend, because inside you will have no sky, no signal, no GPS under stone, and an echoing dome that throws sound in false directions. Leave a beacon, note, and return time with someone outside.

### 4. Air, eyes, and light

The light is perpetual dim dusk through pale mineral plates — greenish-brown, soft, never direct, strongest in the middle and falling to deep shadow at the edges. Colors lie: leaves that are dark green-black will look burgundy at the edges, sulfur flowers will dull. Do not trust color to judge hazards.

- Bring a **small headlamp with a warm, moderate beam** and spare batteries kept cool. You need to see glassy shards, root-split paving, the second-story stairs in houses, and resin on the floor — not to flood the dome. Keep it low. High heat kills batteries and electronics, so keep them off hot stone.
- Bring a **well-fitted dust mask/respirator with spare filters and clear eye protection.** Pumice dust is everywhere, lifting in slow spirals where the floor is hottest, thick in the granary husks, blown suddenly when the vent updraft crosses the plaza. The dry air will crack lips and nostrils fast; bring lip balm and a tiny saline spray, but remember oil-based products near resin are a fire risk — keep them away from plants.
- **Do not bring open flame, a stove, cigarettes, or large amounts of alcohol sanitizer inside.** Resin drips, turpentine vapor, dry husks, old kiln varnish, and fiber mats are all fuel from a world with no fire-suppression and no water. A flame here is not a light source, it is an incident.

### 5. What the city and the plants will do to you

The city is mostly empty space held in shape by streets. Houses can be fifty paces apart across naked dust. Avenues radiate from the stepped plaza with its rotted market stalls, kilns with blackened throats, a granary with doors warped shut, rope bridges over dry streets whose lashings are generations old. Everything has reached equilibrium — walls lean a degree and have stopped, dust fills every joint, vines grow into mortar and mortar grows around vines.

Walk slowly, as if on a ship. Expect:

- **Sudden sounds with no animal behind them.** Floor plates snapping, seed pods popping with flat echoes, sap ticking into a clay pot, leaves rustling like rain that never falls, kilns ticking as they cool at night. At night, with no insects or birds, your own breathing will sound loud. Do not follow sounds expecting life. Wear eye protection if you lean close to a swelling, ticking pod — it bursts, and shards and resin can fly.
- **Plants in the way, structurally.** Mats erupt in low seams, against walls, in building corners. A broad leaf can push through floorboards over a loom and neither yields. Do not step on mats, pull vines from ribs, or climb kiln-vines for a better view of the plates. They are gripping pumice and splitting stone; they are, in every practical sense, the inhabitants. Resin will get on gloves, sleeves, and hair if you brush them. Wash it off outside with soap; do not try to wipe it with your drinking water.
- **Old structures that look settled but are not safe to climb.** Second stories with narrow stairs, leaning annexes, warped granary doors, kiln mouths, rope bridges — admire from the floor. The dusty handprints in the kiln row are the last sign of animal life. Step around them, do not clear dust, move bricks, or take pieces. The city’s legibility is the point; your footprint should not be the next layer.
- **No bites, no stings, no wildlife — and no warnings either.** Nothing living will tell you where water is, where the floor is thin, or where the heat is worst. You must supply all that information yourself.

Mark your way without defacing: count avenues from the plaza, note the kiln row, the granary, the name **Urmesh** cut in fired brick over the inner gate. The bowl feels smaller than it is because of the dome, and larger because of the emptiness. Going out to one far house can quietly cost you twenty minutes and a litre you did not budget.

### 6. Mind and pacing

Ordinary travelers are often undone less by physical danger than by the strangeness: intimacy of the low dome, vastness of the dust, a lush green life that works without water, a town in perfect old equilibrium, and absolute animal silence. You may feel watched by unfurling leaves and listening pods, even though they are only following heat and light.

Give yourself a structure: descend, loop plaza → kiln row → one house block → granary exterior, and turn back whether you have seen “enough” or not. Sit occasionally, breathe slowly, drink, check the time. If you feel claustrophobic under the dome, panicky in the shadow edges, or disoriented by the echo, go back to the steps immediately — they are never far in distance, but they are far in effort, and you want to leave while you are still clear-headed.

### A minimal packing list for an ordinary person

Small daypack that fits the seam; 2-3 litres of water minimum for a short visit plus electrolytes; insulated bottle bag; thick boots and knee mat; loose long clothing and hat; dust mask + spare filters and glasses/goggles; headlamp + spare batteries; sun protection for the plain; lip balm/saline; gloves; small first-aid kit (blister, tweezers for pumice splinters, eye wash — use your own drinking water); wipes and sealed food; waste kit; watch; map/photos taken outside; trekking pole to test cracked plates and hot floor; no flame, no filter expecting a source, no pet, no large tent.

If your multiverse transit lets you step away instantly, test that exit *before* you need it, with your pack on. If it does not, assume you are on your own under that dome until you climb back into the breathing seam.

Come in the morning, kneel, breathe in that baked-clay and crushed-leaf smell, go down slowly, and leave before the midday stones start snapping. Urmesh is not a place to conquer or to live in. It is a place to witness briefly — a hot, dim, dry pocket where green life makes do without water — and to walk out still able to drink.
