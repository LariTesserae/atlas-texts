For an ordinary human, this is not an expedition. It's worse than that - it's a damp, cool, entirely ordinary-looking industrial hamlet that will soak you through, chill you, deafen you faintly, and then refuse to hurry up about it.

That is the first thing to prepare for. It is smaller than it sounds, and slower than it looks.

You can walk the whole place in twenty minutes and see all of it at once from the edge. There is no backcountry to retreat to, no city to get lost in, no anonymity. You will be seen from the start, on the low ridge where the road cuts through the mist. The horizon is close on every side: poplars behind you, a pale 10-15 metre stump of grey scarp ahead, fields gone to lush fallow to the sides. The land is new - that is the real oddity. A flat basin only a few metres above the water table, cut off sharp at the north and west. No weathering, no scree, no softening. Fresh stone faces, bright unweathered concrete retaining walls with corners you could literally cut yourself on, fresh cracks that haven't opened yet, newer buildings still glossy, older ones just beginning to rust at the seams.

And in that sharp newness, life has already moved in at full speed. That is what will govern most of your practical preparation. Your body, your clothes, your gear, and your patience are what need hardening, not your courage.

### 1. Pack for a northern spring that never ends

Locals keep a jacket over the back of a chair in June. You should too, all year. It is cool even in summer, a low shaded cool. Spring lingers, autumn lingers, the air stays cool. Rain is not a storm, it's a thin, cool drizzle that stops and then *settles*. Puddles form in the ruts and stay for days, skinning with pollen. Wet grass never quite dries. Inside the workshops, condensation gathers on corrugated roofs and cold steel morning and evening, long after the sky is clear, and falls in a very slow indoor rain.

Cotton will betray you. Pack only things that work wet, and a second dry set you never wear except to sleep:

* Waterproof shell with a hood - better than an umbrella in drizzle under loose trees - plus waterproof trousers. Wet grass will soak you above the boot in a minute.
* One warm insulating mid-layer, even if you arrive in high summer. Iron is cold to the touch at noon. You will get cold just sitting and watching.
* Well-wellington or sturdy waterproof boots with grip, not trainers. The ground is mossy brick, wet tarmac patched and re-patched, muddy puddle edges, and that fresh stone. Spare socks in a dry bag - this is non-negotiable. Cool damp feet for a day is how you get blisters, then trench-foot misery.
* Warm hat and gloves. Gloves are also for the new concrete and rusty metal, not just warmth.
* Dry bags for passport/multiverse papers, phone, power bank, book. Everything metal - your camera lens, your water bottle, your boot buckles - will bead with condensation in the sheds. Everything fabric left out will stay just-after-rain.
* Headlamp/torch. Electric light is off except in the sheds where work is close. Daylight is flat grey-white, overcast more often than not, glare on wet tin when the sun does come. At dusk the yard lights draw moths in a damp snow, and bats come out thin and fast under the conveyor bridge. Use a headlamp, keep it low.
* Sea-level practicalities: almost no mention of a shop, a supermarket, or a restaurant. There's a station house, a hall with a noticeboard, warehouses, a press shop, a sawmill, a powerhouse with two generators. Assume you bring your own food, toiletries, and entertainment, and ask at the hall/noticeboard first thing where to stay, where water is safe, and who to tell you've arrived. In a place this small, the noticeboard *is* the internet.

### 2. Your ordinary human body is the weak point

You are not immune to the local damp, rust, mould, and stagnant water, and the locals - plants, animals, people - have already adjusted. You haven't.

**Hearing and rest:** The sound never stops. Presses thumping every seven seconds, all day. A sawmill conveyor sighing every few minutes. A forklift beeping in a slow arc. And over/under it, louder: thousands of starlings roosting in the rafters of the open shed like tearing silk, frogs in every puddle croaking on the off-beat of the press, crows waiting politely for a saw cut to finish before screaming, pigeons in the eaves. Bring good earplugs. You may love the rhythm in an hour and hate it by nightfall. Sleep will be damp, dripping, and chorused.

**Skin and lungs:** Nettles and blackberries colonize every empty plot - and there are many empty plots, a press shop then a hundred metres of wet meadow, three houses then a soft timber stack. Ferns in culverts, moss up forklift legs and north sides of brick, cow parsley and meadowsweet thick between loose-canopied sycamore, ash and lime that stand alone as if planted for a single roof. Wear long trousers; you will snag and sting. Bring antihistamine, antiseptic, plasters, painkillers. Cuts from sharp new retaining walls, fresh scarp stone, rusty seams, and thorns need cleaning immediately - make sure your tetanus is up to date before you go. The sheds smell of forest as well as hot oil: condensation feeds mushrooms on the inner faces of timber, walls weep on dry days, starling droppings are in the rafters. If you have asthma or mould/pollen allergies, you will know it here.

**Gut and water:** Water here *waits*. There are no streams. Only seeps beading out of fresh stone at the foot of the scarp into shallow muddy pools where frogs have already laid, puddles dented by a crow's foot, rain barrels, and a patient drip under a valve that has been dripping since the pipes were laid and has grown its pool by a finger's width in a year. Do not drink from seeps, pools, or barrels. Do not wash in them. Do not trust a tap without asking - the pipes leak, the system runs to every building and to nowhere in particular, and the water smells of wet soil and oil at once. Bring a filter/purifier and your own supply for the first days. Store any food sealed - rats live happily in the warm wall cavities of the generator shed.

Clean your boots and gear between worlds, too. This is a young land with naive, unafraid fauna. Don't carry seeds, spores, or pond-water in your tread from the last world, and don't leave anything here. Anything you drop will stay for a very long time.

### 3. How to move in a place that is sharp, slippery, and unfinished

Walk. You don't need a vehicle, and the roads are a loop of patched tarmac already puddled in the ruts. Walk the grid in twenty minutes and you know it. But walk as if the land only just arrived:

- The edge is not a wall, it's a drop. At the north and west the earth simply stops. Don't lean on retaining walls to look over, don't climb the scarp, don't let a child or a dog near the margin where the damp air hangs a little lower. The fresh face hasn't settled, hasn't shed scree, and swallows are nesting on its ledges.
- Everything is slippery when damp, which is almost always. Moss on brick, wet grass that flops and grows again uncut, condensation making workshop floors slick. Watch your footing on culverts and timber stacks.
- The infrastructure stops abruptly beyond the last pole. Beyond that is field and fallow. Nice to look at, dangerous to wander into in ankle-deep wet meadow if you're tired, wet, and it's getting dark. You can see the whole place from the edge - use that. You don't need to enter the meadow to understand it.

Take your time. Nothing here hurries. If you need help, it will come at the same pace as the rust creeping. Self-sufficiency matters more because patience is the local speed.

### 4. The industry is real, and it's indifferent

This is a working hamlet, not a museum. A sawmill with a log deck, a metal shop making hinges, brackets and farm buckets, a powerhouse humming heat into damp air, warehouses with roller doors, a water tower that rings faintly in wind. Don't enter sheds without asking, don't touch presses, saws, conveyors, generators, or that elaborate pipework. Assume floors are wet from indoor rain, steel is cold and beaded, and tools are sharp, rusty, and possibly live.

The forklift beeps in a slow arc and likely assumes you will hear it and move. The press will thump in seven seconds whether you are ready or not. Stand well back from the conveyor bridge at dusk - that's the bat flight line. Do not block it.

Cats sleep on the motors. Leave them. They're using the one warm dry spot. Don't move them, don't try to pet them on a running motor, and don't drape your damp clothes over a generator to dry, however tempting. Ask where you're allowed to dry things.

### 5. The animals are residents, not scenery

They don't flinch at the thump. They time their calls to it. Treat that as a social rule.

Fox trots the road at the same time every evening, unafraid. Rabbits and hedgehogs snuffle through wet grass in the empty lots. Sheep from a smallholding beyond the scarp sometimes wander into the orchard and stand damp and placid between houses while people walk past with shopping. A crow will step into a puddle; a whole puddle of frogs will follow a croak.

Don't feed, approach, chase, or corner them. Watch where you step in long wet grass - hedgehogs are there. Walk around a sheep and let the owner fetch it in his own slow time. Don't pitch a tent in an empty lot; to you it's empty, to everything else it's a nursery, a burrow, a nesting ledge, a warm pipe for drying feathers. The orchard nobody has pruned and the blackberries nobody has picked are not an invitation to forage freely. Ask first, and know that a familiar name - sycamore, lime, cow parsley - on a land this new doesn't guarantee familiar edibility, allergy, or dose.

At dusk, don't stand under the starling shed if you can avoid it, keep food covered from rats, and use repellent near standing water for midges. Moths at the yard lights are a sign it's fully damp and fully evening.

### 6. Be a person in a place where everyone can see you

Because you can see everything at once, everyone can see you at once. Washing on a line, cats on motors, sheep in the orchard - this is domestic life at plain, even illumination. Don't wander into houses, gardens, or the generator shed in a meadow just because there's no fence. Introduce yourself at the hall, use the noticeboard, ask about the fox's time, the sheep, the water, the scarp edge, where to stay dry.

And settle your expectations about the weirdness. The place shouldn't hold together - industry and lushness, sparseness and a town, new stone and old trees, loudness and slowness - but it does because everything is patient. The water waits in pockets. The plants wait in empty lots. The animals wait on warm metal. The machines wait for the next seven seconds. The drip has been dripping since the pipes were laid.

If you arrive wanting it to perform, explain itself, or hurry, you will be frustrated. If you arrive prepared to be cool, damp, a little deaf, a little bored, and to wait with it - to hear a frog answer a press, to see condensation run down a wall on a clear day, to watch a pool grow by almost nothing - you will find it holds together very well.

**Final checklist to copy into your pocket:**

Warm waterproof layers + spare dry sleep set in dry bag; waterproof trousers; grippy waterproof boots + 3 pairs socks; hat/gloves; earplugs; headlamp; first aid (antiseptic, plasters, antihistamine); tetanus checked; water filter + own water; own food for 2-3 days; book; dry bags; gloves for sharp walls; repellent; notebook/paper map (assume no signal); polite introduction at hall/noticeboard on arrival; no climbing scarp, no entering sheds uninvited, no drinking standing water, no feeding animals, no leaving litter.

Go small, go dry where you can, go slow. It will meet you at the same speed.
