This is not a climb, a ruin, or a jungle, and you cannot prepare for it as any one of those. It is all three at once, bolted together, and your ordinary human body will be wrong for it in about six different ways at the same time.

Go in expecting to be cold and hot, dry and damp, deafened and bored, out of breath and overheated, all within a ten-minute walk. The trick is not toughness. It's layering, patience, and accepting that you will not be hurried and you will not be rescued quickly.

### 1. Before you go: the body you are bringing

**Altitude is the first gate, before the gate.**
The yard sits on a constructed summit with nothing below it but cloud-sea. The air at the rim is thin, clear, and cold enough to sting your nose. You will arrive—presumably by portal or lift, which is worse, because you get no gradual ascent—at that height instantly.

Prepare like you would for 4,000-5,000 metres, even if no one has given you an exact number. That means:

- Talk to a travel doctor, especially if you have any heart, lung, ear, or vertigo history. Tetanus up to date is non-negotiable—you will be touching rusted iron, boiler plate, and flaking machinery constantly, often with gloves off for ladders.
- Plan to do almost nothing physical on Day 1. Sit in the yard. Drink. Walk the sixty paces. Look down in short, deliberate looks—your inner ear *will* protest, and that protest is information, not weakness.
- Learn the symptoms of altitude sickness and of ear pressure, and agree with yourself in advance that a headache that gets worse, nausea, breathlessness at rest, or confusion means you stop ascending into the shaft and go back to the yard and rest flat. There is no clinic, no shop, no easy descent. A sprained ankle or a bad altitude day is a multi-day self-evacuation problem.
- Bring any altitude or motion-sickness medication you have used before, oral rehydration salts, and a simple first-aid kit you can use with one hand: sterile dressings, tweezers, antihistamines, burn care for hot metal, blister care, lip balm, and sunscreen you will actually reapply.

**Hearing and pressure.**
You will feel this place before you see it: a low, continuous roar that lives in your teeth and behind your ears, and near the sources makes conversation impossible. Swifts multiply that roar with screaming loops around the shaft. In the evening, thousands of frogs in condensation pockets are amplified by hollow steel until they are the second layer of the roar.

You need two levels of protection, and you need them on your person, not in your pack.

1. Custom or well-fitted filtered earplugs for general roaming. They cut the high screaming while letting you hear a rattling ladder or a warning shout.
2. Over-ear muffs for the lower engine floors, transformer rows, and anywhere swifts are tearing overhead.

Take them out to rest in the open yard, which is the closest thing to quiet you will get. Low-frequency pressure will still be felt in your chest and teeth even with plugs. Expect headache, a sense of fullness, and irritability if you stay deep inside without breaks. Sleep will be another matter—see below.

**Biosecurity, multiverse style.**
As an ordinary traveler you are a walking ecosystem. This place is a settled equilibrium, not a ruin. Frogs have made a pond out of a bolt head, a gear tooth, and a leaf axil. A seed carried in your boot tread or an apple core can become a new sapling in oily grit on a transformer, where birds will carry it to the roof trees. A fruit fly in your lunch bag will be farming itself on a warm pipe within hours.

- Arrive clean: shower, clean boots, sealed, sterile food. No fresh fruit, no loose seeds, no soil, no pets, no perfume.
- Pack out *everything*. Even a breadcrumb is a colonization attempt.
- On departure, shower, change, inspect for ticks, wash boots, and quarantine your outer layers for a few days before wearing them in another world. You will have walked through algae, efflorescence (which is alkaline and irritating), damp rust, rodent-run cable trays, and wasp-papered catwalks. You are carrying that with you if you don't clean it off.

### 2. What to pack, and why the contradictions matter

Pack for three microclimates that exist 40 metres apart: the cold, thin, windy rim; the warm, damp, still shaft; and the sun-blasted roof ring where metal holds heat like a stove.

**Clothing:** Merino or synthetic base layer that can get damp from condensation and still insulate; a warm mid-layer for shade and evening; a windproof, waterproof shell for sideways rain at the rim. A hat with a chin strap—wind over the void will take a loose hat, a loose map, a loose glove, forever. Sunglasses, category 3 or filtered: the sun is ordinary white noon, the shadows are hard, the concrete and steel reflect, and altitude intensifies UV. You will burn on your nose, under your chin, and the tops of your ears while thinking you are cold.

**Hands and feet:** Thin but tough work gloves for ladders, rusted rails, and hot metal—you will need dexterity and you will also need protection. A second, heavier pair for cold wind at the rim. Sturdy ankle-support boots with real grip for wet, mossy, algae-slick concrete and worn-concave steps, but not so insulated that your feet cook on sun-hot plate at noon. Gaiters keep grit, fern spores, and plant sap off your socks.

Heat is a burn risk. At midday, transformer boxes where lizards bask, copper runs, and iron terraces are too hot to lean on or sit on. Test with the back of a gloved hand. Assume every pipe, cable, and engine housing is either electrified, hot, or both, until proven otherwise—and even then, treat it as live. The production line on the lowest floor is still warm and moves one increment a month. The crane is frozen mid-swing. Nothing here promises it won't move very, very slowly without warning.

**Head and eyes:** A climbing-style helmet with a headlamp is oddly appropriate. It protects you from falling rust flakes and drips on the platforms, keeps your headlamp aligned, and gives you a place to clip glasses. You will need light: inside the shaft the sun is a moving patch, the lower floors are dim, and moths swarm electric lights thickly. Use a red-mode headlamp at night so you don't blind the frogs or attract a cloud of midges to your face.

**Respiratory:** A lightweight dust mask or buff. You will be breathing rust-dust, leaf-rot grit, mold spores from damp pockets, and pollen from roof trees. The air is thin, so you will mouth-breathe and dry out fast.

**Harness and vertical travel:** You do not fly between platforms. Swifts do. Everything else is ladders, catwalks, and a slow lift that takes an age per level. If you are not trained, do not freelance on the rim or on high catwalks. Bring a simple personal fall-arrest / work-restraint setup, practice with it before you go, and stay a full arm's length back from the railing unless you are clipped in. Wind gusts at the edge are sideways and sudden. A camera on a tether, a water bottle on a tether—small things go over and are gone.

**Water and food: bring it all.**
This is the most counterintuitive part. The tower is streaked with damp, it drips almost always, frogs sing, every joint weeps black tears. **There is no potable water.** Rain sheets off concrete into drains. Condensation collects in teacups of water that are warm, dark, still, and full of life. Don't drink it. Don't wash a cut in it. Don't fill a bottle from a leaf cup.

Carry 4-6 litres a day, plus electrolytes. Thin air + sun + climbing ladders will dehydrate you while your skin feels damp from shaft humidity and you think you're fine. Food: calorie-dense, sealed, no cooking needed. Cold burns more calories, and the lift is too slow to make multiple trips with a heavy pack. Make several light trips instead of one heroic load.

### 3. Arrival: the first hour

Come through the gate, close it. Sit in the yard. Let your ears adjust. You will notice the yard is mostly empty concrete, and the emptiness echoes—everything here was meant to be filled with machines and is now filled with air.

Do not rush into the tower. Walk the perimeter once, slowly. Find shade. Drink. Note where the sun hits the plate and where it doesn't; shade that is cool at 9am will be a heat trap at noon when the tower holds warmth.

Agree on communication before you need it. Inside, you cannot shout. Bring a small writing slate, learn a few hand signals—stop, water, wait, descend—and a whistle won't help much against the roar, but a hand on a shoulder will. If you travel in a pair, stay in sight, in touch, or in written contact at all times. A small mammal can live its whole life on one platform and never descend. A lost human can do the same, just less happily.

### 4. Moving through it

**Vertical is the landscape.** The whole place is one bounded, hamlet-sized footprint that is 150 metres tall. Getting lost is vertical: up a ladder to a platform with an engine shed, down another to a greenhouse cage of twisted glass, up again to trees rooted in engine housings with their canopies tangled in cable. Mark your route. A phone compass will lie in all that steel. Use a paper sketch of levels and a simple rule: always know how you got *up*.

**Three-point contact, always.** Steps are worn concave in the middle, cracked and re-cracked, moss-filled, slick with condensation in the morning. Vines have covered gauges—follow a root to find a dial, don't yank it. Ferns grow out of bolt heads. Grasses grow in expansion joints. Wherever you step, step on concrete or steel, not on green, and look before you put a hand down on a warm pipe or a sun-hot box.

**The shaft breathes.** A great wheel below completes one revolution in nine days and sighs a slow wind up the shaft. Hanging ferns lean into it. You will feel it as a change in temperature and dampness, not as a gust. At dawn, cold steel sweats. At midday, the top is hot and insects live their entire lives in the warmth around lamps on the roof ring. At evening, frogs begin.

Plan your day around light and temperature, not distance. Dawn for condensation and swifts returning, midday for roof trees and lizards on transformers (watch your step near them—they will not move for hours), evening for the frog chorus that will be beautiful and deafening and will make sleep interesting.

**Don't touch the workings.** Spiders string webs across idle gears specifically so they vibrate with the hum. Insects farm warmth on every warm pipe. Wasps paper the undersides of catwalks—don't brush a rail with your shoulder. Rodents run cable trays at night. Moths and midges swarm the lower lights—don't stand directly under them eating or you'll be eating them too.

Observe, don't handle. Frogs will move between teacup ponds in the drip-rain. If you step in a footprint-shaped hollow of water, you've flooded a world. Look down, step around, give the damp streaks a wide berth.

### 5. Eating, drinking, sleeping

Eat in the yard or on a dry platform edge, not in a condensation pocket. Seal food away from rodents and insects the moment you're done. The lush green is not food. Berries in oily grit, sap from ficus vines as thick as your wrist, leaves drinking condensation off copper—none of it is for you. Wash hands after touching any plant or rust before you eat.

Sleep is concrete, cold to the conduction, warm from the shaft, and loud. Bring a full-length insulated pad—concrete will suck heat from you all night even when the air is warm—plus a hat, earplugs for sleeping (softer than the daytime filtered ones), and an eye mask for the lamps and the early sun. Pick a spot in the yard where the roar is at its most distant and the wind is lowest, or a platform that is dry and away from wasp nests and rodent runs. Expect to be woken by swifts at first light and frogs after dusk. That's not a disturbance to complain about; it's the place's normal tempo.

### 6. The psychological preparation no one packs

You will be bored in a way modern travel rarely allows. A vine moves a handspan a year. A leaf takes three seasons to rot. The production line will not finish its task while you watch. The lift will not hurry because you are impatient.

Bring a book, a journal, a deck of cards, something to do with hands that doesn't require signal. Plan for time to drag and then to fly—you will look up from a fern and realize an hour has passed watching drips fall from pipe to leaf to bolt head in an exact, patient rhythm.

And be prepared for the feeling of the place itself: not ruined, not pristine, not empty despite how empty it is. A small mammal lives its whole life on one platform. You are a visitor to a complete world that has negotiated terms with machinery and will not renegotiate them for you. The correct posture is quiet, slow, observant, and a little awed. Don't prune, don't clean algae off copper, don't switch anything off or try to "fix" a leak, don't take a cutting for your other world, don't leave a cairn.

You arrived with an ordinary body in an extraordinary altitude, in extraordinary noise, in extraordinary damp. Your preparation is not to conquer that. It's to arrive fed, hydrated, warm, cool, protected in ears and eyes, steady on your feet, clean coming and going, and willing to move at the speed of a great wheel turning once in nine days.

If you can do that, you will see what the place actually is: a made mountain where industry and ecology have become the same humming thing, and where life is everywhere—exactly and only—where a drip can be caught.
