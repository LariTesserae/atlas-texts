If you go, pack like you're visiting a museum that is alive, and that cannot clean up after you.

This is not a frontier world waiting to be used. It is a finished world. A high plateau that was made, all at once, and made just so — and is staying just so. The first rule is not written anywhere, but you learn it in an hour: *nothing here has a surplus, nothing here goes to waste, and nothing here changes on net.*

A berry ripens and stays ripe. A bead of water swells under a ledge and hangs there, perfect, not falling. A doorstep does not wear. A tool dropped at noon is sharp at next noon. A lintel is half-carved today and will be half-carved tomorrow. A marmot sits in the sun in the same posture when you look again. Everything grows exactly as much as it dies. Everything consumes exactly what it makes.

You, the ordinary human multiverse traveler, are the anomaly in that picture. You arrive full of disequilibrium: you are hot, you are hungry, you exhale, you excrete, you cut yourself and expect to heal, you expect morning to become evening. The place will not help you balance any of that. It has no extra to give, and no way to absorb your extra.

Prepare accordingly. Short visits, complete self-sufficiency, and meticulous pack-out are not etiquette here — they are physiology.

### 1. Your body is the problem, so plan for it to be unsupported

**You will get cold, wet, and sunburned at the same time.**

The light is ordinary daylight, white-yellow, flat, at a fixed angle. It does not move, it does not dim, it does not glare. The air is highland-thin — your ears feel it, your breath shows and fades even with the sun on your neck. The stone sweats. The moss is mattress-thick and saturated. The shrubs have thick waxy leaves that hold dew in bright beads. There is no rain, no river, no wind to dry you. Brush through the green mat for an hour and your sleeves and trouser legs are damp, your skin smells of wet stone, and the damp will not leave you.

Bring real hill clothing, not summer clothing, despite the sunshine:
- Layered wool or synthetic insulation, a windproof (but here, really a *brush-proof*) waxed outer shell, like those waxy leaves. You need to shed dew when you push through plants, not rain.
- Hat, gloves, neck gaiter. No canopy, no forest shade, no cloud shadow that moves. High sun + thin air = burns under the chin and nose even though there's no glare.
- Stiff-soled, ankle-supporting boots with aggressive tread, plus gaiters and spare socks, and a foot-care kit. The rock is newly split. Cliffs are clean and vertical, scree is angular shards and splintered boulders that have not rounded. Edges are still sharp enough to cut a sole. Under the lush moss you cannot see if you're stepping on shard, hollow, or a ankle-twisting slab. Your boots will get wet from condensation and moss and will stay wet. Trench foot and blisters are a bigger risk here than any predator.
- Trekking poles, not for climbs so much as to probe ahead in the green and test fracture blocks.
- Sunscreen (it won't expire or spoil here, a small mercy), sunglasses for eye-rest even without glare, and a blackout sleep mask. The light never changes. Without a true night you will not get a true sleep cue.

**Assume injuries do not progress the way you expect.**

This is the most important medical intuition to carry: in a perfect equilibrium, growth and decay cancel. A cut is growth trying to happen. An infection is decay trying to happen. Locals do not age — the young stay young, the old do not get old. That is wonderful until you are the one who is bleeding.

Do not count on a wound to heal neatly, or a cold to run its course, or food poisoning to pass in a day. It may hold at exactly the stage you left it. Therefore avoid injury as a primary strategy, and bring the kit to close, clean, and protect yourself as if you were your own surgeon:
- Sterile dressings, closure strips and sutures if you are trained to use them, antiseptic, tetanus cover, antibiotics, pain relief, blister care, splint material.
- Do not gamble on the scree. Three points of contact on fracture planes, always. A fall here is not like a fall on soft old mountains; there is nothing soft to land on.
- Moth-dust and moss spores in thin, still air will irritate lungs. Bring a simple respirator / dust mask for kilns, workshops, and cistern rooms. Kiln smoke rises in a thin straight column and *stops*. It does not disperse. Do not sleep in a kiln, workshop, or granary room where smoke, CO, or your own exhaled air has nowhere to go. Still air pools trouble.

**You are a zero-surplus visitor in a zero-surplus world.**

There is abundant life, but no extra life. The frogs in the hand-pools, the berries, the sheep chewing in stone pens, the ptarmigan-like birds mottled like stone — they are not a larder waiting for you. They are balancing. If you eat a patch of berries, to stay in balance something else, somewhere, must do without. Locals live by taking exactly a replacement and no more. They cannot feed you generously, and you should not forage generously.

Bring all your calories with you. Dense, preserved, no-cook food preferred. The upside is real: nothing spoils, rusts, or dulls here. Your food will keep, your metal tools will stay sharp, a cache you leave in a dry granary will be there, exactly as left, when you come back in a year. Use that. Cache, don't forage.

### 2. Water: there is no river, and the wells are an idea

This is the trap that will dehydrate you while you feel damp.

There are no streams, no rivers, no rain. The aqueduct channels in town carry no water. The cisterns are cut deep and empty. The damp is in pockets: pools no larger than your hand tucked between tilted slabs, frogs motionless in them; condensation beading on the undersides of overhangs, on stone, on waxy leaves, and *staying there*. It looks like it should drip. It does not.

You collect water the way the locals do, with fiber and patience:
- Carry 3+ liters of water *in* with you, plus a wide-mouth container, a large absorbent cloth or sponge, and a filter.
- Drag the cloth through shrub mat and moss in the morning, wring it into your bottle. Squeeze moss cushions — they are saturated through. Wipe leaves. Let a woven condensation wall you string between two posts between your house and the shrubs gather for an hour, then wring it. It is slow, steady work. Plan an hour a day for it.
- Hand-pools can be sipped from only after filtering — frogs live in them, and in equilibrium, bacteria live in them at exactly the dose they live at forever.
- Do not trust a well, a trough, a bead under a ledge. If you touch that bead, you have no idea whether you have broken a perfect balance to release a drop that will never be replaced at that spot. Leave beads alone.

Because you will be constantly brushing wet green, keep one dry layer and your sleeping system sealed in a waterproof sack at all times. You will not be able to dry things by hanging them in sun and wind. There is no wind.

### 3. Fire, shelter, and the empty city

The towns are enormous and almost empty. That is your gift. A city-sized grid of wide slab streets following the fracture lines, plazas larger than need, granaries, kilns, workshops, long low houses with small thick plank doors and holed turf roofs. Zones for craft, storage, animals, all planned, all Euclidean, all hand-made of stone, turf, wood, woven fiber, fired clay. No metal to speak of. No glass. No wheel ruts deep enough to hold water.

You will not find an inn, a market, or a crowd. You *will* find an empty house in every valley, three valleys away from the next cluster you can see from a ridge. Take an empty one — ask the one person you see that day if it's truly empty, then use it. Elevate your bed off the damp floor. Turf roofs shed the dew. Stone walls hold the cool.

Do not count on fire. Wood here is in balance; smoke columns go up and stop. Kilns are charred but the system is not arranged to burn for you. Bring your own stove and canisters, minimal and contained. If you light a fire, keep it tiny, ventilated, and extinguish it completely — ash does not weather away here, it remains, an accumulation in a world that does not accumulate.

Similarly, do not count on local repair. If you break a zip, a strap, a lens, a burner — there is no metalworker who will fix modern gear, no glassblower, no shop. Bring spares of everything critical, repair tape, cord, needle and thread. Your gear will not rust or get dull here, so invest in quality once and it will serve the whole trip.

### 4. Sanitation: nothing rots, so you must carry everything out

This is where most travelers would ruin the place without meaning to. There is no decay. No composting toilet will compost. No biodegradable wipe will biodegrade. No dropped crumb will be taken by ants and gone tomorrow. No latrine pit will break down your waste into soil and close the loop — the loop is already closed.

**Pack out literally everything you produce:** urine, feces, toilet paper, food scraps, gray water, cigarette ends, bandages, hair clippings, nail clippings. Bring sealed waste bags, a urine container, trowel — and then take it all with you when you leave. Burying it is pointless; it will sit there forever, exactly as buried, a little pocket of disequilibrium for the next person to find unchanged.

The same applies to noise and smell. There is no wind to carry them away. Bells on the sheep are silent. Birds call once, clear and spaced. A hammer taps far away, occasionally. Your steps on slab streets ring and are too loud to yourself. Shout and the sound does not get lost; it stays where you made it. Move quietly, camp away from pens and nests, do not clank, do not play music outdoors expecting it to dissipate. It won't.

Leave tools exactly where you found them. A flint hammer lying by a lintel is not abandoned — its owner is on the next street, moving sparsely, and will return to use it in the same way tomorrow. Picking it up and moving it is a change that will never be undone or worn away. The same for a basket, a half-carved stone, a pen gate. Look, do not rearrange.

### 5. Navigation and time: the sun will lie to you

The sun does not move. Clouds, few and thin, do not drift. Shadows do not swing. You cannot tell time, direction by shadow, or weather by sky. Everything looks uncannily the same when you return to it — because it is.

- Bring a compass, a mechanical watch, and a paper logbook. Mark tallies on a doorpost or in the book: *Day 1, ridge, town A. Day 2, moss mat, water collected.* Without external markers you will lose days in the monotony. Travelers report standing in a street for what felt like an hour and finding the same swallow making the same tiny adjustment, back and forth.
- The built landscape is your map. Streets follow fracture lines. Towns occupy only the flatter benches. From any high bench you can survey three valleys of pale roofs. Climb early, take a bearing, sketch the grid, then descend. Do not rely on "I'll recognize it when I get back." You will — too well. Every green mat looks like every green mat, because the ferns are unfurled to the same size forever.
- Your exit anchor / portal must be independent of local light, weather, season, or growth. Test your return frequently, even for short trips. Do not let the stillness seduce you into "just one more day." The place is comfortable precisely because it asks nothing of you to change. That comfort is the hazard. Locals have adopted it for a lifetime: their crafts do not progress toward completion or decay. If you notice you are repeating the same walk, same meal, same hammering motion without progress, leave.

### 6. The people

You will see one here, one there, in woven wool, carrying a basket, hammering at a lintel that is half-carved. They are not hostile, not particularly forthcoming, and not in need. There is no surplus to trade, no hunger to relieve, no technology to teach that would not be a violent disequilibrium. Glass, metal, wheels, engines — they have none, not because they are behind, but because the world they inhabit does not need them and will not wear them in.

Expect slow, sparse contact. A conversation may be a few words spaced like a bird call. Do not try to finish their work for them, do not try to organize them, do not leave gifts that will sit untouched forever. Observe, greet simply, thank them, move on. They will be there, in the same place, doing the same thing, when you return — which is both reassuring and lonely.

You will also be startlingly alone. Even in a city, you may walk leagues of moor and empty fields — crops at perfect ripe stage inside low walls, empty pens, an entire street of golden thatch with cold chimneys and undisturbed birds in the eaves — without seeing a soul. Bring your own company, your own entertainment, a journal. Silence here is not emptiness; it is life holding its breath. It wears on ordinary humans after a few days. Travel with at least one other person if you can, to be the one changing, talking, inconsistent thing in your sight.

### A short pack list, distilled

**Carry in (and carry out):** all food and calories, 3+ days water + collection cloth/filter, stove + fuel, full waste pack-out kit, first-aid including closure, spare socks/boots care, repair kit and spares, compass + watch + logbook, blackout mask, dust mask, warm layers + waxed shell, stiff boots + poles, entertainment/journal, dry sack for sleep system.

**Do not carry in expecting to use:** composting toilet, reliance on local wood/fire, reliance on wells/cisterns/rivers, reliance on foraging/hunting, reliance on local repair, reliance on sun/time/weather cues, anything you plan to leave behind because it will "break down."

**Do not do:** move tools or stones, pick whole patches, burn timber, shout, sleep in unventilated kilns, touch water beads or move snails/frogs/sheep from their exact spots, try to homestead or stay indefinitely.

Go for vistas, for the uncanny pleasure of a world where a berry stays glossy, where smoke stops mid-air, where you can see a town three valleys away and hear only your own too-loud step. Go briefly, go prepared to be the only thing in the landscape that is out of balance — and to take that imbalance with you when you leave, so that when you look back from the highest fractured bench, the pools are still glittering, the moss is still green, the sheep are still there, and the bead under the ledge by your foot is still there, swollen to its perfect size, waiting to fall and not falling.
