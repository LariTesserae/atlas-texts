**Istrenkor**

Do not think of this as a place to *visit*. It is a place to be *in*, for as long as you can stand it, and there is no one there to help you. There are no shops, no shelters, no human inhabitants described or implied, no plants to forage, no wood to burn, no view to reach, no sunrise to wait for. The city is not built - it is precipitated, still growing, and the animals are not in the city, they are part of its plumbing.

An ordinary human traveler has to bring everything that a normal place would give them, and give up everything they normally rely on: dry ground, silence, sunlight, solitude, a finished road, and hurry.

Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Prepare Your Body and Your Expectations

**You will be high, thin, and cold in a way you have not felt.** The air hurts in the back of the throat. Breathe slowly through your nose, or better, through a filter (below). Even with the mist you will dehydrate quickly from breathing effort and cold. For a week beforehand, do endurance work in cold, wet, loaded conditions if you can. Ordinary fitness is not enough - you need to be able to walk slowly for hours on sharp, uneven, perpetually wet stone without losing patience.

**You will not see the sun, and you will not get a day.** Light comes from below - amber warmth leaking from tower joints, the dim blue-white glow of animals, faint sparkle in stressed obsidian, and pools that contain their own tiny lights. Your internal clock will fail. Bring a clock, a sleep schedule, and accept that "second day" is only a convention. Sleep when you are tired, not when it is supposed to be night.

**You will learn to wait.** Everything here moves at geological pace. A pool fills a centimetre a year. A ridge rises while you stand on it. A bridge that needs to be thicker is thickening *now*, over months. A wall that was soft yesterday is firm today, and a doorway warms a fraction before you reach it because it reads your heat and makes room. If you move at human speed you will stumble on fresh basalt, get in the way of a tide of animals, or step into a crack that is planning to be a stream. Waiting is not being polite here. It is how you stay safe, how you hear properly, and the only way you will ever feel dry or warm - which you won't.

**You will never be alone.** In permanent night, in cold, with no plants, you expect scarcity. You get the opposite. Blind grazers the length of a forearm shuffle shoulder-to-shoulder in tides that take an hour to pass. Thousands of large, slow, translucent midges - drifters - hang in the thermal columns over the city's vents and settle on your face like cold snow. Hairy clingers process in long lines up walls. Pale, long things swim just under black pools. All of them glow a little. It is language, warning, and excess energy, and you will learn to navigate by those moving constellations because there is nothing else.

That means psychological preparation too: constant low drone felt in your teeth, a thousand drips amplified like bells in stone, a groan through your chest once an hour or once a day as a new fracture opens, and at times a deafening chittering chorus that becomes pressure behind your ears, then stops. You need to be able to sit in noise and wet and glow and not need it to stop.

### 2. Head-to-Toe Gear

**Breathing:** A well-fitted respirator or buff plus a particle filter is essential, not optional. The mist is not clean water. It carries mineral aerosols, ozone, ammonia, faint sulfur, microbial film spores, and drifters you will breathe in by accident. The cold, thin air will also burn your throat raw. A neoprene-type mask or filtered half-mask will warm and humidify the air. Test it at home - claustrophobia kills here because you cannot see 50 metres in mist.

**Eyes:** Anti-fog goggles or close-fitting glasses with a spare anti-fog cloth in a pocket, and a balaclava that can cover your face when drifters are thick. You will be navigating almost blind by human standards. Do not bring clear contacts as your only option; mineral mist will irritate them.

**Head and hands:** Insulated, waterproof head cover that doesn't trap heat too much - you will be damp, not sweating. Gloves are mandatory for touching anything. The rock is fresh basalt and obsidian fractured in clean, recent planes that cut like glass. Edges are absurdly sharp, even on "ancient" looking things, because nothing has had time to wear them smooth. Bring slash-resistant work gloves, not ski gloves. When you must touch a wall - and you will, to feel your way - you need to detect the piezoelectric stone flexing and the filaments thickening without tearing yourself.

**Clothing:** No cotton. No leather. No wool left alone. You will be wet to the bone in a fine drizzle that never becomes rain, condensing on every surface. Dress in layers: synthetic or merino base, insulation that still works when damp, and a waterproof breathable shell that you accept will stay wet on the outside. The ceramic slabs of the lower streets, strangely, never seem to wet. The stone beside them always does. Anything that wets out, sags, or insulates poorly when saturated is useless.

**Footwear:** This will make or break you. You need:
1. Puncture-proof, slash-proof soles for obsidian.
2. Thick insulation *in the sole*, because cold lives in the stone and comes up through your feet even while geothermal heat warms the rock underneath. Thin-soled boots will numb you in minutes.
3. Deep lugs for slick microbial film and mineral sheen - but not so aggressive they wedge into soft filaments.
4. Above-ankle support, tied before you arrive. You may arrive standing on fresh rock in mid-tide.

Carry two spare pairs of socks in a dry bag *inside* your pack, and a thick foam sit pad. There is no dry ground to sit on. Every natural surface is weeping, alive with grey translucent microbial felt, or a route for grazers.

**Light:** Bring almost no light, and make what you bring dim, amber or deep red, and controllable. Harsh white light, UV, and especially flash will blind you in mist, destroy your night adaptation, and interfere with the animals' glow-language. The fauna navigate by heat, vibration, electrical fields, and smell - your flashlight is to them a shout. A small, low, warm lantern for camp and a dim headlamp you can turn to almost off is enough. The place lights itself, poorly, and you must adjust.

Do not bring a compass and trust it. Fresh basalt is magnetic, the stone is stressed, and the post-technology of the city works with electrical fields. Do not bring paper maps - they will be pulp in minutes. Do not bring a camera expecting vistas - there are none. Mist closes at 50 metres, darkness at 100.

### 3. Food, Water, and What Not to Drink

Bring all food and water, sealed and scentless. There is nothing green, nothing edible, nothing to forage. The microbial felt is food for everything else, not for you. Do not eat it, do not scrape it, do not try to filter pool water.

The black pools and runoff are mineral-laden, faintly acrid, salted enough not to freeze a few degrees below sense. They look still and drinkable. They are not. They carry the dissolved building material of the city, the minerals of the dead grazers who fall in, ammonia and sulfur. Drinking them will make you sick and there is no help.

Bring electrolytes. Cold, altitude, and filter-breathing will make you lose them while you feel wet all over.

Do not bring a campfire. There is nothing to burn, and open flame will confuse heat navigation, potentially damage phase-change membranes that inhale mist and exhale it where needed, and draw drifters in a column you do not want to breathe. Cold rations only.

### 4. Finding Your Way When There Is No Way

Istrenkor is urban at regional scale: streets at foot level, second and third storey platforms and filaments strung between towers, and below, vaulted tunnels and cuttings lit from underneath. From a high point you can see more glows on other ridges, then mist takes them.

You cannot get a view. You must navigate in three dimensions, in layers, with 50 metres of visibility.

1. **Use a tether or guide line.** Start with a fixed point - the ceramic-slab street level is most stable - and lay line as you go. The city re-grows and re-paves under animal feet; marks you paint will be overgrown, walls will change density, a route you took yesterday may be thicker today. A physical line does not care.
2. **Learn by sound and glow, not sight.** The drone tells you where the deep lattice is. The bell-drips tell you about hollow towers and water channels. A sudden brightening of drifter column over a vent tells you heat is rising. A tide of grazers leaving a mineral sheen and thicker film in their wake tells you a street was just crossed - follow it backwards if you are lost.
3. **Memorise by touch.** With gloves on, practice in fog at home: close your eyes, count steps, feel a doorway warm before you reach it. Here, doorways *will* warm. Do not force them, do not lean your pack against a wall to rest. It is deciding, slowly, how dense to be.
4. **Stay on ceramic where you can.** The matte slabs don't wet and are less sharp than raw rock. But do not camp on them if they are in a grazer tideway - you will be run over, politely and inevitably, for an hour.

Move slow. When you hear the deep groan of a new fracture, stop and step back from fresh cracks and thin black sheets of water. The rock is still being born. It will not throw you far, but it will cut you, it will deepen a pool under your feet by a fraction, and water will immediately thread the crack.

### 5. Living With the Animals and the City

These are not rules of etiquette for sentient hosts. They are rules for not breaking a closed loop.

**Never step into a grazer tide. Stand aside and wait.** Hundreds of them, shoulder to shoulder, moving in a deliberate shuffle, eating film, eating their own dead, filtering mist with feathery gills. They are blind and will not see you. If you try to push through, you will fall on their slick backs, injure them, and foul your boots with mineral sheen.

**Do not block vents, walls, or channels.** Drifters hang over vents. Clingers process up walls filtering condensing water and harvesting microbes from ceramic. If you stand under a vent to get warm, you will breathe thousands of them and disperse the column. If you lean on a wall, you block a procession and break a filament the city is growing.

**Do not touch pools, do not wade, do not poke.** Pale, longer things swim under the surface and stir the reflection so the lights below seem to move. The ecologic loop is: film feeds grazers, grazers feed drifters and dead fall into water, water carries minerals to the lattice, lattice exhales heat and moisture, growing more film. Your trash, your dropped food, your chemicals will be built into a tower. Carry everything out. Leave no trace because here, trace becomes architecture.

**Do not mimic the glow.** Flashing a light in patterns might be read as warning or language. You do not know the grammar. Keep lights low and steady.

**Do not cut, pry, or test the structures.** A broken span does not crumble, it re-grows inch by imperceptible inch. You will not damage it permanently; you will stay in its way while it heals, and you risk your hands on sharp, regrowing edges. Trekking poles: use rubber tips, use them only on raw stone, never poke a membrane, filament, or tower joint.

**Wash your boots and hands when you can, with your own clean water, sparingly.** You will accumulate film and sheen. It is not poison, but it is a living culture from a closed system and you do not want it in cuts, in your lungs, or carried to your next world.

### 6. Rest, Noise, and Emergency

Camp above ground if possible, in a hammock or on a thick pad on a ceramic platform, *not* on raw rock and not in a tunnel where sound amplifies. The city does not get quiet. Bring foam earplugs for sleep, but sleep with one ear open - you need to hear a crack groan or a chittering sudden stop.

There is no rescue. No one is coming through fresh basalt, mist, and a city that re-grows behind them. Your emergency kit is personal: wound care for deep, clean cuts from obsidian (mineral water will make any cut harder to heal - clean with your own water, seal it), treatment for hypothermia (you will be wet and cold long before you think you should be), and extra filters, socks, and gloves. When in doubt, stop, sit on your pad, put on your mask, and wait for a tide to pass. Hurry is the most dangerous thing you can bring.

### Final Checklist for an Ordinary Human

- Filtered mask + spare filters, balaclava, anti-fog eye protection
- Slash-proof gloves x2, insulated puncture-proof boots, 2 spare sock sets, dry bags
- Synthetic layering system, waterproof shell - leave cotton and leather at home
- Dim amber/red headlamp + tiny warm lantern, batteries sealed
- Guide line / tether, 50-100m, gloves to handle it
- Thick sit/sleep pad, hammock if you can carry it
- All food and water for twice your planned stay, electrolytes, sealed and scentless
- Foam earplugs, clock, notebook with grease pencil (paper will fail)
- First aid for cuts and cold, tweezers for obsidian splinters
- A pack you can carry in and out entirely - nothing to leave behind

Leave behind: anything you expect to dry, anything you expect to stay clean, anything you expect to be finished, anything that assumes sun, soil, wood, silence, or a view.

If you can accept being cold, wet, dimly lit by living things, surrounded on every surface by life eating life eating stone, with no outside to the loop, then Istrenkor will become, by the second day, almost ordinary. That is the most unsettling part - and the proof you are prepared.
