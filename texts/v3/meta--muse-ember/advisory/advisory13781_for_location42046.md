The Still Shelf will not attack you. That is not the danger.

Nothing here will attack you, feed you, warm you, warn you, or rescue you. It will simply do none of the things a living place does for an ordinary human body. It is a finished world - high, cold through the soles, perfectly dry, perfectly still, with a sun that does not move and a floor that is two-thirds alive.

Prepare as you would for a place that exists only to omit things:

**No water. No fuel. No food. No shelter. No decay. No change. No way to find your way by eye.**

Plan for a short, fully self-contained visit. The previous humans whose cairns, obsidian tools, and one slab shelter you will find did not stay. They left a mark and left. Follow them.

### 1. Decide the visit before you arrive

Go for hours, not days. 6-10 hours is plenty to understand it. Do not plan to "camp for a few days and see." Because nothing changes, you will lose an accurate sense of time very quickly, and boredom will make you linger past your water.

Set a hard turn-back time in advance and carry it in *two* places: a wristwatch with a loud hourly alarm, and a companion or base beacon that will alarm you. Your own sense of "we've been here a while" means nothing when the light, shadow, and temperature never change.

Before you go through, stage your return at home. Send your entry point, planned exit time, water budget, and a dead-man check-in to someone outside. There is no one on the Shelf to find you, and no local resource to help you if you are hurt - no wood for a splint, no stream to wash a wound.

Carry a loud, personal locator beacon and a simple audio beacon for base camp. In that silence and that repetition, a soft repeating tone you can hear is worth more than any landmark.

### 2. Water is the whole expedition

This is the non-negotiable. Water in any form does not exist here. What looks like a pool is dry salt cracking like ice. What looks like a riverbed is a dry groove worn smooth and abandoned. Your filter, straw, fishing kit, water purifier, and optimism are useless.

You are the only wet thing on the plateau.

Bring *all* of it, sealed, and more than you think:

* For a normal day visit in thin, dry air: minimum 3.5-4 litres per person, plus 1 litre emergency reserve you are not allowed to drink except to return to your entry point. Thin air + mouth breathing + exertion on an undulating plateau will cost you about a litre a day just in breath. Your breath will not fog here. That means you are losing moisture you cannot replace.
* Carry it, don't cache half of it far away. Put the emergency reserve *at the entry point*, where you can see it.
* Bring electrolytes. Beasts, dust, and salt crusts are salty everywhere. You will be tempted to lick, eat, or melt salt for water. Do not. Dissolve the salt in your own ration and you will get saltier.
* Bring a urine bottle and a full pack-out system. Nothing rots here. Human waste, food wrappers, toilet paper, a dropped glove, a tent peg - they will remain, clean and sharp, for longer than this civilization has existed. Your footprints already will. Do not add permanent pollution to a pristine finished world.
* No cooking. No hot food, no rehydrated meals. Bring cold, sealed, calorie-dense rations that need no water: bars, nuts, dried meat, hard cheese in sealed packs. You cannot make fire - there is no wood, no dry vegetation to speak of, and low oxygen at altitude makes flame pathetic anyway. A needle-grass tuft once per hundred paces and a stone-flower that has not seeded in centuries are not food, fuel, or shelter. Leave them alone.

Lip balm, eye drops, moisturizer, and nasal saline are not luxuries. The air carries no damp, ever. Skin cracks, eyes sting, and dust is fine, pale, and dry.

### 3. Air, cold, and the ground under you

It is high, and your body will know it before your eyes do.

* **Thin air:** Reduce exertion. Do not run, climb fast, or carry heavy loads and expect to breathe normally. Spend a day or two acclimatizing before a long walk if you can. If you are prone to altitude sickness, bring a small personal oxygen canister, or shorten the visit. The sky-blue horizon is thin, honest, and unforgiving.
* **Cold through the feet:** The chill does not come from the air. It comes up through your soles and stays. Ordinary hiking boots will fail you in an hour of standing on the breathing slabs.
  Wear insulated, high boots with thick thermal insoles *inside* both boot and sock. Bring an insulated ground pad for any sitting or sleeping - you cannot sit on the stone itself without losing heat.
* **Sun and shadow:** Light is normal, white-gold, and honest. That does not mean gentle. At this altitude with no atmosphere to speak of and black, sharp shadows that never move, UV is high and there is no shade to retreat to. Sunglasses, wide brim, sunscreen on nose and neck, even though you feel cool.
* **Dust:** Kick it and it hangs, then settles straight down. It is silicate dust and glassy stone grit with a metallic tang. Wear a well-sealed respirator (P100/N95 minimum) and close-fitting goggles when you are moving through drifts or examining the salt patches. Do not breathe deeply through your mouth while walking. It will be in your lungs, your camera, and your boots for days because it never washes away *there*.
* **No wind:** Your tent will not flap. Your windbreaker will not be tested. But also, there is no wind chill and no cooling breeze. You will overheat under exertion in still air despite the cool ground. Layer, vent easily.

### 4. Navigation: You *will* walk in a circle

This is the second most dangerous feature, after thirst.

The Shelf repeats itself. Seven beasts around a salt patch with the large one cracked on the left. A slab rounded like it's been held in a hand. A cairn, then a near-identical cairn on the next rise. Basins so shallow you walk down without noticing. In a place where everything is a copy of something else, at every scale, your eyes will lie to you calmly and consistently.

You *will* walk in a straight line and find yourself following your own footprints back. The sun does not move, so it gives you no time and no reliable compass bearing except one fixed reference. Shadows point the same way forever. There are no stars, no birds, no wind-blown cues.

Do not navigate by terrain. Navigate by what you bring:

* **Breadcrumb with unique markers.** Every 15-20 steps, drop a tall, bright flag in a color that does not exist here - international orange, acid yellow, magenta. They must be tall enough to see over a rise, weighted so the breathing stone won't tip them. Count them. Know that the pattern will try to convince you a flag you placed is a natural repeating feature. Only your count matters.
* **Inertial navigation.** Bring a real dead-reckoning unit / GPS if your multiverse kit allows it, but do not trust a phone compass alone. Mark the sun's fixed position at arrival on your watch: "Sun is at 10 o'clock from entry." From then on, deviations are measurable.
* **Audio beacon at base.** A low, steady tone is easy to localize in dead silence. The place makes almost no sound - your heart, a distant tick of cooling stone, your muffled steps. A tone cuts through the monotony without shouting.
* **Companion protocol.** If you go with someone (and you should), every hour do a verbal check: "My name is ___, base is ___ direction, I have ___ litres left." If either person cannot answer clearly, you turn back *now*. Repetition, timelessness, and sensory starvation mimic early confusion.
* **Do not use cairns, tools, or the shelter as landmarks.** They are spaced to be identical, and they are archaeology. To touch them is to destroy a record preserved because nothing decays. Photograph from distance, with a scale object in frame - everything here looks the same size in photos because there is no familiar scale.

Stay on the plateau. Do not attempt to descend the smooth, ancient slopes to see the darker country below, and do not try to jump to another floating table. There is no water, no handhold, and no rescue route down there.

### 5. Walking on a living floor

You cannot put your foot down without deciding whose back you are stepping over. The dominant beasts - domed, armoured, bread-loaf to dog-sized, eyeless, mouthless in any familiar way - are not scenery. They are fatty, salty, crystalline, walk with dreadful slowness, eat dust, photosynthesize through their rims, do not drink, do not sweat, do not rot. When they "die" they harden in posture and join the pavement. Their children bud in exactly the same posture in exactly the same place.

You cannot reliably tell a sleeping beast from a boulder, or a finished beast from a live one. Neither, probably, can they.

* Walk as if every dome might be alive. Slow, test weight, step *between* them. Coin-sized crustlings cluster on larger slabs like barnacles - a normal boot will crush dozens without feeling it.
* Bring 2-3 short rollable walkway mats (foam or sling panels). Leapfrog them across the densest herds. They protect the beasts, protect your ankles from the breathing slabs, and protect your feet from the cold. It will feel ridiculous. Do it anyway.
* Beasts on larger slabs breathe, faintly, through mineral plates. A whole slab may rise and fall a centimetre under you over minutes. Do not stare down at it trying to confirm. It causes vertigo and a feeling that the world is swallowing you. Check your footing, then look at the horizon.
* Mote-drifters hang motionless in the air, translucent, casting no shadow, drifting neither up nor down. Do not wave at them, brush them, or try to catch them. Their bladders are full of dry spores. Leave the air as still as you found it.
* Do not eat them, lick them, move them, or use a shell as a seat or a tool. They are not food - crystalline, salty, and dry-chemistry based - and moving one breaks a pattern that may be thousands of years in the making. The obsidian tool with a huskback resting against it as if a companion is not an invitation. It is a warning that prior travelers understood this.

If you cut yourself, the blood will not disappear. Cover wounds immediately and thoroughly. There is nothing to clean them with locally except the sterile supplies you brought.

### 6. Sleep, time, and mind

At "night" - and the text describes a deep clean blue sky while the sun hangs in the same place - it never truly darkens. You will not get sleepy from dark. You will get tired from exertion and confusion, which is dangerous.

Bring your own night:

* Opaque sleep mask, earplugs that don't entirely seal out the stone ticks, and a strict sleep schedule by watch, not by feeling.
* An insulated tent *on top of* a full-length pad. The ancient shelter of stacked slabs is not yours to use - the beasts have moved in through the doorway and arranged themselves inside, and its awl and fibre bundle belong to history.
* A routine: journal entry every hour, even if it's one line. Write the time, water left, flag count. Hearing your own voice on a recorder every hour is a sanity anchor in a place with no birdsong, no wind, no buzzing. Your blood will seem louder than the world. That is normal. It will unnerve you. Expect it.
* Bring a familiar scent from home - a handkerchief, a soap pad - sealed until needed. The smell here is cold rock, salt, and metal, and *nothing else, ever*. When everything repeats and nothing changes, a small, personal, non-repeating smell will bring you back to yourself.
* Music or a metronome in one earbud only. Two ears sealed off will make you miss a change in the slab breathing under you, or a companion's voice. Keep one ear open.

Expect derealization: déjà vu in blocks, the sense that a herd you passed is exactly the same herd, that time is both stuck and racing, that you have been walking for a week across a rise that takes a week to cross and yet your legs are fresh. Do not argue with it. Check your instruments, your flags, your watch. Trust the things you brought, not your intuition.

### Suggested minimum kit, per person

**Water & body:** 4L+ sealed water, electrolytes, urine bottle, pack-out waste bags, cold rations, respirator + goggles, sunglasses, sunscreen, lip balm, eye drops, insulated boots/inserts, layered clothing, insulated pad, tent, sleep mask, medical kit + sterile saline wipes + splint, personal O2 if needed
**Navigation & time:** Wristwatch with alarm, 20+ tall bright flags + weights, audio beacon, inertial/GPS unit, notebook + pencil, headlamp (not for darkness, for looking into black shadows), camera with scale reference
**Pavement protection:** 2-3 rollable mats, trekking poles (for testing slabs without committing weight)
**Do NOT bring:** Water filters, fishing gear, firestarters, wood, camp stove expecting to cook, empty containers hoping to fill them, a pet, anything you plan to leave behind

### On the ground, three rules

1. **Leave only footprints you must, and plan to follow them back.** They will not blow away. Minimize them with mats, move slowly, and do not wander for a better view.
2. **Touch nothing that looks old, repeated, or alive.** Cairns, tools, shelter, stone-flowers, needle-grass, mote-drifters, salt crusts. Photograph, don't handle.
3. **Turn back on schedule, not on feeling.** When the alarm sounds, retrieve your flags on the way out - every single one - and check that your emergency water is still sealed at entry.

The Shelf is overwhelming not because it is hostile, but because it is complete. An ecology with no waste, no growth, no season has closed its loop. An ordinary human does not fit into a closed loop. We need influx and outflux - water in, waste out, day then night, new shapes. Here, you are the anomaly, the temporary wet, breathing, decaying thing that will walk for a few hours and leave sharp prints in dust that will outlast you.

Visit briefly, respectfully, and fully supplied. Let it stay finished.
