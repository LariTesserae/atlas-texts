For an ordinary traveler, this is not a tourist site. It is a sealed, closed, still-wet-born pocket of rock that has been warm, dry, loud, sharp, and exact for as long as anyone has ever seen it — and you are not built for it.

The runners are armored against its floor. The gliders breathe through their crystal wings and eat lamplight air. The stone-grass lives on electricity. You are a warm, wet, weathering animal carrying your own weather with you. Your preparation is not about adapting to the place. It is about borrowing enough protection to walk in, briefly, without breaking it, and walk out again.

### 1. Before You Descend

**Go short, go with a plan, and do not go alone.**

The chamber is only about eighty meters by thirty, but it will take you an hour to learn how to be in it. Because nothing changes, there is nothing to wait for and nothing new to find by waiting. Set a hard time limit before you go down — for most humans, a first visit should be measured in minutes, not hours — and tell your spotter at the top of the shaft exactly when you expect to come up.

You will need a spotter for the shaft itself. The entrance is not a door, it is a ladder, a lift-cage, or a slope of cut rock, just wide enough for one person with a pack. You will be coming down hands-first, under kilometers of rock, with pressure popping in your ears long before the air goes warm. If you fall, slip, or freeze in the cage, no one in the chamber below will hear you over the roar. Leave a rope, leave a timed check-in, leave a way to be hauled back up.

Train for the descent, not just the chamber. Practice climbing and descending in full kit, with gloves on, carrying your water inside your suit rather than in a bottle you can drop. An ordinary, fit human will get tired, hot, and deafened faster than they expect. That is not weakness. That is the environment working on a mammal.

Eat, drink, and use the toilet *before* the shaft. There is no clean, sanctioned place to do any of it below.

### 2. What You Have to Wear

Think of the suit as two suits: one to protect you from the place, one to protect the place from you.

**Dryness and dust are the first assault.** The air is warm as the inside of a closed hand, and absolutely dry all the way down. Your mouth will be paper in minutes. Your eyes will sting faintly. The grit underfoot — crushed mineral, metal filings from the lathe, salt blooms, the fine metallic powder excreted by the stone-grass — never cakes, never clumps, never settles in the humid way surface dust does. It powders your boots and stays airborne in a way that gets into everything.

Bring:
- A sealed helmet or full-face respirator with a fresh, high-efficiency filter for fine metal and mineral dust. An open face or a cloth mask will leave you coughing dry grit for days. Sealed goggles are essential; the dust and white salt-frost on the walls are an irritant manufactured to sting.
- A closed or semi-closed breathing system if your guild provides one. This is not vanity. Your exhaled breath is the first water this place has ever received. A multiverse survey protocol will consider every breath, every drop of sweat, every spill, a contamination event in an anhydrous system where nothing has ever rusted or rotted. At minimum, wear a proper respirator and never talk unnecessarily. Ideally, bring your humidity with you and take it back out with you.
- Synthetic, close-woven clothing. No leather, no canvas, no untreated wood buttons, no paper notebooks — they will dry, crack, and shed organic fluff into a place that has never had it. Layers that wick *inside* the suit and are sealed at wrists and ankles. Your sweat will evaporate invisibly in that warm dry air and you will dehydrate without feeling wet. Plan your intake as if you are exercising in a desert, because you are.
- **Sealed water, sealed only.** A hydration bladder *inside* your suit, sipped through a tube. No open bottles, no wet wipes, no damp cloth for your face. A spill down there is not a puddle. It is the introduction of an ocean to a world that has never made mud, and it will start corrosion, dissolve salt, and stain the sharp young mineral in ways that will persist forever. Bring more than you think you need; there is no source to replenish it.
- **No food inside.** Eat before and after. Crumbs become runner feed. The runners graze the dust, the lathe filings, the pellets other creatures drop, and the salt. You are not part of that cycle. Every crumb is an alien nutrient entering a perfectly balanced loop.

**The floor and walls will cut you.** This is the second assault, and the one that sends most ordinary travelers home. The surfaces are unweathered — fresh precipitated anhydrite, basalt glass, plates of pale crystal split to edges like paper cuts. The runners tap across them unharmed on six armored legs. Your boots are not armor.

Bring puncture-proof, hard-soled boots with thick soles, and a spare pair of sole inserts. Add puncture-resistant gloves, knee and elbow pads, and a hard hat for the low edges of the vault and the cable canopy. Do not kneel, do not sit, do not put a hand on a wall to catch yourself without a mat or pad underneath. A fall here does not mean a bruise; it means opening your boot or your glove on a lance that has never been dulled by rain or time.

**Sound, light, and electricity are continuous.** The roar does not come from the kiln in a way you can move away from. It is in the floor, in the metal, in your teeth, at the same volume by the wall as by the engine. Ordinary factories vary; this one repeats bar for bar, forever. Foam plugs held in a muff will help, but bone conduction will still carry the hum. Decide on hand signals and a written slate *before* you go down. Shouting will fail, and radios may suffer from the electrical fields of the cables and stone-grass.

Light is dim, amber, and scattered — caged lamps, machine slit-windows, bioluminescence where current passes. It is enough to see, never enough to feel cast by. Bring your own low amber or red headlamp, at low intensity, to preserve your dark adaptation. A bright white flood is cruel to you, blinding in the dust, and cruel to the gliders and the lamp-orbiting swarms, who live in that dimness. Do not wave your light into the swarms overhead. They never land, and they are food for everything else.

Treat everything electrical as live. Stone-grass — those black, wire-thin tufts five to ten centimeters high along current seams — *conducts* and glows faintly when current passes. The kiln pipes are hot, the overhead cables are a second ceiling, the control cabin is wired to the walls. Do not touch to steady yourself. Do not step on the tufts. Your ordinary instinct to grab vegetation for balance will earn you a shock in a place full of sharp teeth.

### 3. How to Behave Inside

Keep your movements slow, quiet, and economical. The runners are blind, but their antennae are tuned to vibration and electric field. To them, you are a new, hot, wet, electrically noisy vibration in a world that has no new vibrations. Walk, do not run. Do not kick up dust. Do not try to move a runner aside — they will tap around you if you give them time.

Do not touch anything you do not need to. The tools lie where they were dropped, edges still bright because nothing corrodes. The bunk beds are still made. The shrine in the corner still has its single wrench laid upon it. The preserved bodies — of runners stiffened into perfect crystal statues, and, if the partial remains are human, of workers — are part of the density of the place. Disturbing them will not decay away. Here, an object moved stays moved, and a footprint stays sharp beside the footprints of the first workers, who are still there and still sharp.

Do not try to turn anything off, collect a souvenir, or "improve" the loop. The conveyor carries the same ingots from kiln to wall and back for no reason you need to understand. The lathe shaves the same piece. The fan stirs the same air. The runners split and stiffen at exactly balanced rates so the population never grows. This is not a broken factory. It is a closed ecology of dust, heat, noise, and bodies, running on kiln waste heat and grid waste current. Interrupting it — unplugging a cable, pocketing an ingot, knocking over a crystal glider — is not a prank. It is a change to a system whose entire strangeness is that it does not change.

Keep to the hundred paces between walls. It is easy to get lost in a room that gives you no gradients: warm by the kiln is exactly as warm as warm by the far wall, loud by the engine is exactly as loud as loud by the lockers, and the kiln is always in the center. Count, leave a guide line if you are surveying, and do not trust your sense of direction. Your ears cannot triangulate the source. Your skin cannot find the cool wall. Only your counting and your light can.

Respect the hand-sized things that are the color of the wall. You will see them only as a ripple in dust, or not at all. Do not lean on walls, do not press your back to a pipe, do not assume a bump is rock.

### 4. The Psychology, Which Is Worse Than the Heat

Prepare to lose your sense of time. There is no night, only dim that is always dim. There is no variation to anchor memory — the clank will never miss. After a while you will stop waiting for it. That is the preparation *and* the warning. Boredom in a continuous present tense becomes a kind of trance. Travelers report setting their breathing to the machine cycle without meaning to, and then being startled when they realize they do not know how long they have been doing it.

Bring an external timer and a pre-written checklist on a sealed slate. Write prompts for yourself in advance, because inside you will find yourself writing the same observation in different words for an hour. The place looks as if it was just made, yesterday, and will be exactly like this tomorrow. The strangeness is not that it is old. It is that it began and was forbidden to continue.

Expect the sensory after-effects. The ozone, hot oil, and burning dust smell will stay in your nose. The hum will stay in your teeth after you ascend. Your mouth will stay dry because you *are* still dry. Do not interpret the quieting of your hunger as adaptation. You are still a human who needs water, now farther from your water than you think.

And expect the footprints. Every step you take in those grooves will be preserved, sharp, beside the first workers' steps. You will inevitably become part of the pattern of movement that produces no change. Walk as if you have to live with your own trail forever, because here, in a sense, you do.

### 5. Coming Back Up

Decontaminate at the top. Brush and vacuum your boots, suit, and pack in a contained area before you go back into normal air, normal humidity, normal rust. That anhydrous grit — metal filings, salt blooms, new mineral dust from a world that never weathered — is a multiverse contaminant if you carry it home in your soles, and it may behave very differently when it finally meets water.

Drink, eat, and rehydrate fully. Check your hands, knees, and feet for fine paper-cuts from crystal edges; they will be dry, pale, and easy to miss until they sting. Change your filters. Sleep, if you can, in a dark room. You have just spent time in a place with no night; your body will ask for one.

If you have done this right, you will have left almost nothing, taken almost nothing, and changed nothing — which is the only way an ordinary human is allowed to visit a place that has stayed new, and stayed new, until its newness *is* its history.
