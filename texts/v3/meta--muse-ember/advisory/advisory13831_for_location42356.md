If you are an ordinary human, not an enhanced, not a mage, not a machine, this is a place that will try to convince you that you are not built for it. Not because it is violent — although it can be — but because every human assumption about how to navigate, how to rest, how to find water, and how to keep going, is quietly reversed.

Prepare for that reversal first. Gear is secondary.

This is a high plateau that is permanently dark, permanently wet, and permanently warm from below. You are coming up out of the low country by an unfinished road into thin air that feels warm. There is no sun to orient by, no stars, no dawn, no clear horizon. The sky is a low, thick, material dark that presses on your head. What light exists is *made* — by fungi, by flowers, by sodium-orange lamps, cyan work-lights, blue harvester headlights, amber signal blooms.

Everything else follows from that.

### 1. Prepare Your Body Before You Prepare Your Pack

**Altitude in warm air is deceptive.** You will be breathing thinner air while your skin is beading with warm condensation. You will not feel cold, so you will not drink as much as you should, and you will overheat under exertion in humidity that never dries. Work up to the road in stages if you can. Rest a day in the low country, hydrate with electrolytes, not just water. On the first day on the plateau: walk slowly, plan for half your normal distance, and expect shortness of breath, headache, and irritation out of proportion to the effort.

**You will not get a proper night.** There is no night/day cycle to sleep on. The living glow is steady and low; the industrial glow pulses. Frogs start at no particular time. The factory heartbeat comes through your boots all day. Your circadian system will start to drift within 48 hours.

Bring a strict external clock — a watch you trust, not a phone's light, not the plants. The plants near the oldest smelters open and close in perfect time to its pulse, amber then white, like signal lamps. Local people — if you meet anyone at the occasional hut — will probably time work to that. You should time sleep, meals, and medication to *your* watch on a 24-hour cycle, whether it is dark or not. It always is.

Bring vitamin D. Bring a sleep routine that does not depend on darkness falling. An eye mask is useless. Earplugs are useful for frogs and pumps, but dangerous if you plug both ears — sound is your main navigation system here. Use one earplug, or moldable plugs you can pull out instantly.

**Eyes need an hour to learn.** After an hour in fungal green smear, you will navigate better than with a harsh white torch. Arrive at dusk equivalent — which is always — sit still for 30-60 minutes before you try to do anything complicated. Keep your main light low, steady, and warm-amber if possible. Harsh, pulsing white light is what industry does; it attracts, it confuses echolocating birds and bats, and it destroys your adaptation. Use a headlamp on its lowest setting for walking, and a small lantern you can leave hooded for camp. Learn the colors before you need them: steady green = fungi on wet logs, footing; orange flicker through leaves = a building, warmth, possible shelter, possible danger; cyan = work-light, active machinery; a low, steady blue-white moving light = a harvester or walker, and *everything that hunts by that light*.

### 2. The Pack: What the Plateau Eats

Water is everywhere and safe almost nowhere. Water sits just under the surface of marsh flats, never deep enough to be a lake. Runoff carries a faint chemical tang that fungi love. Rivers form for two days after a vent blows and vanish into a crack that steams. Upper marsh water can flow slightly uphill under the pull of condensation towers.

You must bring purification and assume all surface water needs it. A filter plus a chemical backup, and a way to boil without making a big open fire — your heat becomes rain half a kilometer away, feeding the marsh, and attracting heat-seeking pollinators. A closed canister stove, small, is enough. Do not camp on warm ground. Put your palm on a stone and expect it to give back heat; that stone may be a pipe, a vent, a steam column where translucent, blind animals hang and drop one by one to drink. If the ground steams under your boot, move.

Clothing must be quick-dry, never cotton. Warm rain will wet you to the bone in minutes, and condensation will bead on hot metal, on leaves, on you. You will stay damp. Hypothermia here is not freezing — it is prolonged wetness plus wind plus thin air plus exhaustion. Bring: a lightweight rain shell you can wear *over* a breathable warm layer, waterproof pants, and at least two full changes of socks and base layer in dry bags. Bring spare boot laces and a chemical boot dryer. Boots will not dry overnight by themselves.

Footwear is the single most important item. You need high, waterproof, ankle-supporting boots with aggressive tread and, ideally, a midsole that insulates from warm ground. Basalt slumps and weeps — rust-colored streaks, slick algae on mossy rust, rounded outcrops like sleeping animals. Stone ticks and leaks before rain. The mire will suck and steam. Bring gaiters. Bring trekking poles; you will need them for slumped cliffs, flooded tire ruts, and ground that is a black moving mulch of worms and beetles you will hear before you see. Do not step into standing water near a walker or fence line if you can avoid it: electrified joints arc blue when they step through water, and ozone smell means a fence is live. That same fence is being used by migrating animals as a guide-wire, stupidly and cleverly. Treat every wire, every silo rim, every cable as live when wet.

Gloves matter: insulated leather for rust, sharp basalt, hot pipes, and electrical brushes; thin waxy-leaf-proof gloves for pushing through vine mats so dense you cannot see soil for weeks. Ferns the size of umbrellas, orchids hanging from cables, sulfur-yellow fungi — they will snag, stick, and stain. Some plants have finished negotiating with machines and will turn toward the nearest smelter. Do not cut or block them.

Respiratory protection: a fit-tested P2/N95 or elastomeric mask with filters for spores, mist droplets, seeds, and chemical mist near smelters and venting towers. The air is crowded from the ground to 20 meters — spores, midges in clouds that move like a single animal, hand-sized moths, biting things that come for heat as much as blood. Bring repellent and a midges net, but know a net will trap condensation. Bring anti-itch, anti-fungal powder and treatment. The truce of erosion means everything is damp and growing: fungal skin infections, blisters that won't dry, and tetanus from rusty, mossy metal are your most likely medical problems. Bring a full wound kit, antiseptic, tweezers, and the discipline to clean and fully dry feet every night, even when tired.

Electronics: assume constant condensation. Dry bags, silica, grease pencil on a waterproof notebook — ink will run, paper maps will be lies anyway. Horizons are rumors; a day's walk can be a change of climate. Distance does not guarantee sameness, and weather three kilometers away can be clear while it rains straight down on you, warm as bathwater. Do not rely on a forecast or on satellites. Learn to read the plateau by ear as you walk: low hum = pump-house ahead, probably drier footing; sudden repeating metallic ping = cableway swaying overhead with a load, things fall off, do not stand under it; thickening frog chorus = flooded, warm ground ahead; ozone = live fence. Echo can last a full minute in wet basins. Shouting will carry, disorient you, and call things in. Speak low, use hand signals, use a whistle sparingly — a short, low one, not a blast.

### 3. Shelter, Food, and the Built Things

There are no cities, no towns. There are single buildings, days apart: a smelter, a pump station, electrified silos, a small domestic hut with a generator, antenna, and laundry line limp in the damp. Each is half-sunk in its own way. Machines still run autonomously, tending themselves, sending harvester crawlers and long-legged walkers across the mire. Animals nest in flues. Plants specialize in waste heat.

Do not assume a building is abandoned, empty, or welcoming. Do not assume you can charge a device because there is a generator. Some huts may have an occupant; most will have machinery that does not know you are not part of its shift. Approach from downwind, from the light side, announce yourself quietly, and be prepared to walk on. If you do shelter in a building, assume it is warm, damp, and inhabited by frogs in the cooling tower, insects in the vents, and nests in the pipes. Do not break a window that vines have already made their home in. Do not sleep against a warm wall that might be a flue.

Never camp on marsh flats where water sits just under the surface, never under a weeping cliff face that has been ticking for longer than the machines have existed, never under a cableway, never on a steam crack. A hammock or raised mat with a waterproof tarp strung under trees is safer than a ground tent. Build your camp for rain arriving from above and water arriving from below and from the sides, all at once.

Food: do not eat the engineered grain that has gone wild, glowing along the seed-heads, followed by rat-like grazers eating spillage and a fast, wet predator using the harvester's headlights to hunt. Do not eat glowing fungi, however inviting. Local water, local fungi, and local grain are part of the cycle — heat is rain, runoff has a chemical tang. Bring all your own food for the entire traverse plus 50% extra. There is no resupply. The herd of large animals with bobbing green eyes moving through the mire does not want your rations, but the midges, moths, and burrowing things in the mulch do.

### 4. Mental and Multiverse Protocol

This place is actively, impatiently alive. You will brush past another creature every few steps, and it will brush past you. That is normal. Panic is also normal: the dark has weight, the air is crowded, sound never stops, and you cannot see the edge of anything. Prepare a routine: same time to eat, same time to check feet, same time to log by sound what you heard. If you lose the routine, you will lose time.

Two multiverse rules matter here more than usual:

**Do not bring the sun with you.** Do not bring sun-dependent pets, plants, or seeds expecting to grow them outdoors. Do not bring a large open flame or a loud generator to “light up” the night — you will alter the microclimate, create rain, and attract everything that has learned to live inside made light. Bring dim, steady, shielded lights.

**Do not take the plateau with you, either way.** Before you leave, shake out clothes, brush boots, inspect seams for vine mats, orchid seedlings, fungal sheets, and seeds on the wind. Spores and seeds are already in the air; you will carry some. Clean gear at the roadhead before you re-enter the low country or any other universe. Quarantine your tent and boots. The plateau has incorporated fallen machines the way it incorporates a fallen tree. It will incorporate your litter, your toilet waste, and your dropped gear just as efficiently. Pack out everything. Use a wag bag on the marsh, bury nothing in ground that is water just below the surface.

File your plan with someone *off* the plateau who knows you are not expecting rescue from a town that does not exist. Give them your entry road, your intended direction by sound-marks and light-marks, and a hard return date based on rations. Autonomous harvesters will not stop for you. No one with a radio is guaranteed to be at that hut with the antenna.

### 5. A Minimal Checklist

- Headlamp (low, amber-capable) + hooded lantern + spare batteries in dry bags
- Water filter + purification tablets + 2L personal capacity, electrolytes
- High waterproof boots, gaiters, poles, 2x sock/base changes, rain shell
- P2/N95 mask + spare filters, repellent, anti-fungal, eye protection for spores
- Insulated gloves, work gloves, first aid, tetanus up to date
- Waterproof notebook, grease pencil, watch, whistle (low)
- Hammock/raised sleep system, tarp, closed stove
- All food, 50% extra, no local foraging
- Earplugs (one-sided use), sleep routine, vitamin D
- Clean-out kit for departure quarantine

On the first day, you will stop looking for the sun and start looking for sources. That is correct. Follow them slowly, with your ears first, your eyes second, and your light last. The plateau is vast, old, settled, and truce-bound, not peaceful: slope creeping, rust flaking, root splitting a pipe, seed splitting a silo door, predator and prey negotiating, machine and moss negotiating, rain and heat and rock negotiating. You are a temporary guest in a negotiation that has been going on for longer than the machines have existed.

Walk like a guest. Keep your light low, your fire smaller, your steps out of the steam, and your time by your own clock.
