This place will fool you in five ways. Prepare for the opposite of what it first looks like.

**1. Lush does not mean wet ground.** There are no rivers, no lakes, almost no standing water. Water lives in the air, in fog, in a thin damp layer a few centimeters under the clay, in the pores of plants and buildings. You will be damp to the bone while standing in a desert.

**2. Empty does not mean silent or safe.** The five to ten kilometers of bare clay between city-nodes are the point. Sound travels forever here, herds travel forever here, and you will travel forever here if you are unprepared. A node is not a town with help in it.

**3. A city does not mean civilization.** These nodes have no shops, signs, doors you can rely on, or obvious residents. They are hollow, breathing waterworks - grown structures of a warm, seamless, amber-and-pearl material maintained by living nanolattices. Rooms open when the lattice wants them open. Walls go phase-thin. You cannot knock, pay, or ask for shelter.

**4. A building does not stay a building.** Spires bloom and contract over the course of a day. A hall open in the morning can close by night with its garden folded inside it. Never treat any interior as stable, permanent, or yours.

**5. Young ground does not mean soft ground.** The plain is recently laid down. It is sharp, cracked into knife-clean polygons, cut by vertical-walled hollows a few meters deep with crumbling edges. It settles daily. New cracks open with a sigh. Maps, paths, and even the ground itself are a draft.

If you prepare for those five inversions, you will be fine. If you prepare for an Earth-like lush valley with a town in it, you will be cut, lost, deafened, and rationless within a day.

### Timing and Physical Preparation

Aim to arrive in a dry interlude, mid-morning to early afternoon. That is when visibility is best and dripping is thinnest.

Dawn and dusk are saturation hours: fog rolls low, condensation beads on everything, then drips for hours without ever quite becoming rain. That is when insects in the fog swell to a white-noise blanket, when plants make their visible growth pulses, and when you will be wet, blind, and unable to shout a meter. Do not plan to hike then. Plan to be inside a tent or a known, stable node by then.

You need heat and distance fitness more than speed. Heat comes up from the clay as well as down from the dim, haze-thickened sun. Sweat will not evaporate well in saturated air, so you will dehydrate while feeling damp and not particularly thirsty. Drink and salt on schedule, not by thirst.

Train for long, slow, flat walking with ankle work. Five kilometers between nodes sounds short. On sharp polygons, in humidity, with no shade, testing each plate for a trip edge, it is a two-hour expedition. Ten kilometers is a half-day commitment. The plain continues for a hundred kilometers before it rises. Do not attempt an unknown hundred kilometers alone. Travel node to node, or not at all.

Acclimate for at least a day of just listening and looking at your first node before you try to cross. Your brain is going to be slowed by the infrasonic hum - a pressure in teeth and sternum from the city lattice that never stops. Thoughts will feel thick. Make decisions in writing, in advance, on a checklist. Do not trust yourself to improvise clearheadedly at dusk in fog.

### What to Pack, and Why

**Feet, skin, and clothing:** This is most of your survival.

- Stiff, ankle-high, cut-resistant boots with thick soles. Polygon ridges are only a few centimeters high, tilted, and hard enough to slice leather and skin and trip you all day. Low shoes are a mistake.
- A trekking pole or stout cane. Not for climbing. For probing hollow edges in fog, testing a plate, and finding phase-thin walls and invisible drop edges. In dim, shadowless light you have almost no depth cues.
- Gloves, even in the heat. For sharp clay, for testing walls, for climbing out of a hollow.
- Breathable, quick-drying, loose clothing. No insulation, no heavy waterproofs. A light warm-drizzle shell, a hat, spare socks in dry bags - plural. Your feet will stay damp. Friction, fungus, and trench-foot are your medical risks, not cold.
- A fine mesh veil or respirator cloth for dawn and dusk in thickets and fog. There are minute translucent insects living *in* the fog itself, plus moss, lichen, and spore allergens in the damp islands. You do not want to breathe them for hours.
- Sun and heat protection despite the dull coin sun. Haze diffuses light, it does not remove heat or UV. You will burn and overheat without noticing a sharp shadow to warn you.

**Eyes, ears, and brain:**

- A headlamp and a soft lantern. You will need light every day. There is no blue sky, no sharp shadows, no night/day contrast to judge edges. Diffused light helps you see the translucent buildings and phase changes better than a harsh beam.
- Ear protection - good plugs or muffs - *and* a plan for when not to wear them. The sound here is constant, layered, and overwhelming: condensation tonking in hollow spires, wind making chords through them, insects, megafauna foghorns that carry ten kilometers, even distant herd steps. Shouting does not work in saturated air. Infrasound will get through plugs anyway. Bring a whiteboard/slate and light signals for your party. Do not rely on voice.
- Sleep mask and maximum ear protection for sleep. Silence does not exist here. If you do not sleep, you will make bad decisions on the plain.
- Laminated paper map, notebook, and pencil in waterproof cases. Paper will get damp. Electronics will get damp. Condensation will form inside everything.

**Water and food - bring it all:**

Assume no node will give you anything. Bring 1.5x the food you think you need, salty, lightweight, and non-perishable. Do not forage. Island plants grow toward sound as much as light, feed on minerals and damp, and live on each other. Nothing there is described as edible to a human. Moss and lichen are scenery, not salad.

For water, carry at least 2 liters per person for even a short 5km hop, more for 10km, plus electrolytes, and expect to refill only at a node after testing.

Do **not** drink from the hip-sized green-black pools in hollows. They are warm, stagnant, funnel-collected runoff, and full of thousands of translucent shrimp-like animals that breed and die in a day. They are a habitat, not a tap.

Do **not** drink building drip untested. Buildings harvest fog in microscopic capillaries, mineralize it, and release it on schedules no one controls. It may be rich in iron and other leachates from the lattice. It could be wonderful, it could be laxative or toxic to you.

Safer collection, if you must: fine mesh sail or cloth strung to catch dawn fog, then filter and sterilize everything - boiling, UV, or chemical purifier rated for minerals and microbes. Collect away from thickets. Test building trough water only in small amounts at first, over days.

And do not dig for that damp layer a few centimeters down. You will undermine sharp hollow edges and destroy the capillary sponge the whole ecosystem depends on.

**Navigation and communication:**

The plain is featureless, the fog thickens, glowing etched paths between structures fade underfoot, and the ground makes new cracks daily. GPS may work, but the lattice hum and phase material interfere with compasses and electronics. Your multiverse anchor / return beacon needs extra protection: insulated, waterproof, desiccant-packed case. Hum and damp are not kind to field electronics.

Bring: compass (and know it may drift near a node), step counting, laminated grid, and a set of high-visibility trail flags or prism reflectors you can leave and retrieve. Mark your exit from every building the moment you enter. Never rely on remembering which phase-thin doorway was yours.

Party tether - a simple line between walkers for fog or whiteout drizzle - and a loud light whistle for herds to hear, not people. Leave a full itinerary and check-in schedule with someone outside. If you are lost, sound is your best signal: fauna here navigate by city echoes, and sound is the only thing that connects this place. Move toward a known node resonance by daylight, signal with light and rhythm, do not wander in circles.

**Shelter:**

A one-person tent with a real insect mesh, plus a hammock if you can manage it. The ground is sharp, damp, uneven, and churned by burrowers. Hammocks keep you off it. Tent stakes will not hold well in thin crust over damp clay - use rocks, dead thicket fibers, or sand bags.

Do not camp in a hollow. They collect water, attract pool fauna, have collapsing walls, and are where burrowers dig. Do not camp on a glowing path or blocking a building's basal breathing pores - those slow exhalations of warm damp air feed the vines, and you will wake in mold and insects.

Do not plan to sleep inside a spire unless you have watched it for a full breathing cycle and have a guaranteed exit. Rooms close. Gardens fold. Vines thicken toward sound - if you snore, hum, or play music near vines overnight, do not be surprised to wake entangled.

### Moving Across the Plain

Walk the polygons, not between them. Test with your pole. Keep your weight centered. After warm drizzle, the crust softens and hollow edges crumble: give all hollows a two-meter berth even when they look dry.

Carry a 3-5 meter cord or collapsible ladder. If you fall into a vertical-walled hollow, do not try to climb crumbling clay with your fingers and do not dig the edge wider. Anchor back, rope down, get out before it slumps further.

Follow glowing/etched paths when you can see them, but do not trust them to last. In rain they fade. Mark turns.

Do not follow herds. They test the clay beautifully, step polygon to polygon, and know where damp is - but they are migrating at continental scale, with predators pacing at the exact same unhurried tempo behind them. A herd line can be kilometers long. If one is between you and your node, wait. Do not try to cross through it, stand in its path, or run. Running introduces vibration and panic into a slow system. Slow, sideways, and yielding is how everything here moves.

The predators are not fast. They do not need to be. Treat any large animal pacing a herd as lethal by size alone. Give them hundreds of meters. Observe their direction for information, never follow.

Burrowers are the hidden hazard. They churn the young ground, open and close hollows, and dig up buried cables. If you feel ground vibration, see fresh crumble or an exposed cable, stop and step back. Exposed cables are not handrails, not bridges, and not wires to touch. Step over them, don't step on them.

Winged flocks are weather. When the haze darkens in one place, get low, cover your head and pack, and wait. They will pass as a darkening, not as individual birds you can dodge.

### Working a Node

Approach from the path. Stop outside the cluster. Watch for a full slow breathing cycle - buildings exhale audibly through pores at their bases. Listen to the chord the wind is making through the spires. Note which domes are open and which are closed. Then enter.

Test every wall with a gloved hand before leaning. A wall can be solid from the road and phase-thin up close. It is warm, seamless, translucent in places, pearl in others. Do not push through, do not try to tape, nail, or anchor anything to it. Earth adhesives and fasteners are meaningless here, and the lattice may react by flowing, closing, or thinning further.

Keep to ground floors. The spires are extravagantly hollow - cathedral-sized voids with gardens climbing inner walls toward dim light, no railings, no floors where you expect them, and water that occasionally runs upward a little way inside a phase-thin wall. Falling inside these structures is a real risk and would be disorienting in shadowless light.

Do not dam or drink from capillary forests, troughs, or pores without testing as above. Do not block a pore. Do not pluck moss, lichen, funnel-leaves, or vine cuttings. The buildings, plants, and fauna are one tuned system: dung and urine from fauna enrich the gardens, gardens feed insects and pollinators, buildings tune the microclimate for all of it. Your waste, too, is foreign. Pack it out. Leave no trace beyond footprints in damp clay, which will heal.

Do not make loud music, banging, or prolonged shouting inside a node. Vines grow toward sound. Fauna navigate by echoes and will alter course to pass a node for water and resonance. You may summon a thicket to your tent, or a predator that has learned nodes mean prey and water.

Thickets at nodes dart, nest, sing, and pollinate. Admire from the edge of the path. Do not push in. You will flush nests, tangle in vines that root from building-made soil, and blunder into insects at their worst at dusk.

### Sound, Mind, and Health

Pre-commit to a routine because the place will erode your judgment slowly and pleasantly. Infrasound, never-ending dripping and humming, and dim light will make you anxious, nauseous, slow, and suggestible without you noticing. That is not weakness. That is physics.

- Keep a daily written log: water in, water out, distance, node reached, exit time. Review it at lunch.
- Plan your dusk camp before noon. Once insects swell, you will not be able to think, talk, or navigate.
- Carry first aid for cuts (sharp clay will make them), heat exhaustion, dehydration, fungal skin infection, insect bites, allergic reaction to spores, and eye irritation from fog/insects. Antihistamines, antifungal, wound care, electrolyte salts, and a proper filter/sterilizer are non-negotiable.
- Air is warm and damp to breathe. If you have asthma or severe allergies, this place is a poor choice without medical advice. Condensation on your gear will breed mold in a day. Dry everything, every dry hour, with a line inside your tent.

### A Sample Day

Mid-morning: leave node with full water, flags marking your exit, tether on if fog is light. Pole-test polygons. Cross 5km in 1.5-2 hours with rests.

Early afternoon: arrive next node. Watch buildings breathe for an hour. Collect and sterilize water if needed. Eat, dry gear, log.

Before dusk insects: set tent/hammock on high, flat clay away from hollows, pools, pores, and vine lines. Eat, ear protection in, sleep mask on. Accept that you will still hear the world through your ribs.

Repeat. Do not push a second crossing in the same day unless you have already done the route once and know the timing.

### Final Checklist

Boots, pole, gloves, veil, headlamp, lantern, ear plugs + muffs, eye mask, shell, hat, spare socks, dry bags, hammock/tent, rope, flags, laminated map, slate, compass, water filter + sterilizer, 1.5x food, salts, first aid, antifungal, antihistamines, desiccant, protected beacon, itinerary left outside, and a rule written on your hand: **No pools, no untested building water, no thicket shortcuts, no sleeping in closing rooms, no crossing herds, no hiking at dusk.**

Go slowly. This place rewards stillness and punishes hurrying like almost nowhere else. It is not empty. It is spaced. Your job as an ordinary human traveler is to fit, briefly and carefully, into the spaces between its breaths.
