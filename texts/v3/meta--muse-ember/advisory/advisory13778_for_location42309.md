If you take one idea with you before anything else, take this: you are not visiting a reef, a wreck site, or an underwater city. You are visiting a cold, sunlit, almost empty tabletop under a sea, where everything that is there is far apart, everything that moves moves in a fixed pattern, and nothing ever cleans up after itself.

An ordinary human body was not built for duration there. It is not instantly lethal — the light is good, the water is clean, the pressure on top of the table is manageable — but it will tire you, cut you, deafen you, and lose you very efficiently if you go as a tourist. Prepare as for a long industrial expedition in a desert, except the desert is liquid, 2-3 degrees, and loud enough to hurt through bone.

### 1. Understand what your body will feel

**The roar is the environment, not an event.** It is turbines on the rim, currents pouring over an edge, shoals and whales calling at a distance, all merged into a low note in your sternum, a hiss, and a high whine. Covering your ears will not help. It goes through gloves, through your skull, through the floor.

You need real hearing protection, not just earplugs for a swim: sealed plugs *plus* insulated muffs/hood integrated into your suit, rated for continuous broadband noise. You need to assume you cannot shout and be understood at arm's length, and your own voice will come back distorted. That means no voice-dependent safety plan.

Bring electrical comms — bone-conduction or throat-mic with a visual text display, and a simple push-to-talk that works on your suit. Bring a non-vocal backup: strobes, a tap code on a tether, a light signal. If a buddy is 20 metres away in clear water you can *see* him, but you will not hear him call for help.

Long exposure matters even with protection. Bone-conducted vibration for hours leaves people exhausted, nauseous, with ringing teeth. Plan work in shorter rotations, rest in a vehicle, and expect your concentration to degrade faster than it would in silence.

**Cold, slow, and sticky.** At two degrees the cold is not from wind, it is from everywhere at once. Movement is slowed by drag. Metal sticks faintly to a glove. Your breath on a rebreather doesn't fog, it strings away sideways in bubbles — fine, but a reminder that exertion makes a lot of bubble noise and loses heat fast.

You need a proper dry, heated system for cold water, not a wetsuit for an afternoon dip. Active heating, insulated hood and hands, and a calorie budget: in cold water you burn fuel even when you are just hovering and listening to the roar. Keep excursions shorter than you think, bring external heat, and bring more food than you want. There is nothing to forage, nothing green to eat, and the animals are not food in any practical sense. Everything you eat and drink — and everything you exhale through a scrubber — you carry in or make room for.

**Sharpness is continuous.** The table is young. Basalt columns with mirror facets, volcanic glass, granite broken fresh on the diagonal, edges that will slice a glove if you brush them. There is no sand blanket to soften a landing, no silt to cushion a fall. You will drift and sink slowly rather than fall, which makes it easy to settle onto an edge you didn't notice.

Thick cut-resistant gloves and boots for *contact*, not just insulation. Over-gloves you can swap when sliced. A repair kit. Treat every rock contact as a potential cut, even if you are in a vehicle and only reach out to steady yourself. Cold, clean water may be low on bacteria, but a cut that won't stop bleeding because you are in a current, and that you cannot properly dress in gloves, is still serious. Bring a bleed kit you can use one-handed, inside your suit system.

### 2. Breathing, buoyancy, and staying on the table

You will be on apparatus for anything longer than a few minutes. That apparatus defines your limit: gas, scrubber, heat, and communication fail before your courage does. Use a closed system or surface-supplied umbilical *only if* you have a vessel above in the bright surface, which you probably do not. For a multiverse traveler this usually means a self-contained rebreather or a small submersible with its own life support, with gas calculated for the return trip *plus* the same trip again.

Neutral buoyancy is a skill, not a setting. The surface is close and rippling white above you, the table is below you, and in water so clear there is no dust or plankton for your brain to judge motion against. People become disoriented up/down very quickly. Bring a depth gauge, compass, and artificial horizon you can read at a glance. Practice in a quarry or training tank first until you can hold depth without thinking.

Most important: stay off the rim. On top of the table the pressure and light are survivable. Over the side, the drop continues for a very long way into dark water where pressure would crush you. The rim current is a permanent sheet pouring over that edge, bringing food and animals with it — and it will carry a careless swimmer, scooter, or submersible over with it. Mark your limit, use a tether or a planned turnaround depth/alarm, and treat any current that accelerates toward the edge as a no-go zone. You do not explore the sides in a leisure suit.

### 3. Movement and navigation in a place without landmarks

This is a continent-sized plain. Between one machine and the next may be hundreds of metres to kilometres of nothing. You may travel for an hour and see no living thing, then pass a shoal kilometres wide. Your visual system will lie to you about distance because there are no plants, no kelp, no size references. A whale that looks like a change in light can be enormous and close, or far.

You cannot walk this on foot for long, and you cannot swim it usefully. You need powered propulsion — a scooter for short scout moves on the table, a small submersible for anything that might take a day. Your own legs are for emergency station-keeping.

Navigation must be instrumental. The currents are fixed — this square is always rough, that valley always carries fast water from the left — so locals and previous travelers will have maps, and you should get them, but do not rely on memory. Bring:

* A chart plotter / dead-reckoning log with compass, depth, and distance log or Doppler velocity log
* Pre-planned waypoints along the things that *do* exist in straight lines: pipelines, cable lines to rim turbines, harvester tracks. Those are your handrails across an otherwise featureless plain. Follow them in, follow them out.
* A rangefinder/ sonar for judging the scale of animals and machines. Do not trust your eyes.

Lost here does not mean lost in a forest; it means you are in bright, clear water with perfect visibility and nothing to orient by except the white ceiling above and the glass below. If you lose your nav, stop, conserve gas and heat, and return to the last known line — usually a cable or pipeline — rather than pushing on hoping a machine appears.

Currents are weather. There are calm lakes that sit in the same place forever and churn violently if you cross their boundary. You will be pushed sideways, your bubbles will be taken sideways, your scooter will yaw. Map them, cross them at right angles with power, and anchor/wait only in known calm water.

### 4. Animals and machines: abundance at a distance

The animals are not hiding, and they are not interested in you, which is good and bad. Grooved whales on rim routes, sleeper sharks hanging motionless in crevices then unfolding without hurry, crabs and octopuses living in cracks because there is nowhere else, silver fish in vast shoals above the glass.

Do not approach, touch, feed, or try to wake anything. The sharks are called sleepers for a reason. The whales are so large that their approach is slow and unstoppable; you are the small, noisy, bright thing. Keep distance, keep your lights off unless needed, and keep your communications pings low — their low calls already travel enormous distances, and yours will be an intruder in that channel.

The machines are industrial and unmanned. Rim turbines as large as hills turning at unvarying speed, a factory module flat on the rock with one lit window, a harvester crawling in a straight line scraping nodules, a tipped platform preserved exactly as it fell a century ago. There are no towns, no lights clustered, no visitor center, no one to ask for air.

Do not touch them, do not try to charge from them, do not mistake an amber beacon on a half-buried housing for a rescue signal. They are electrified, heavy, and designed to work for years without a human present. In a place where nothing rots and nothing dulls, a wreck from a hundred years ago is still sharp, still leaking, still dangerous. Treat old gear exactly as you would a fresh one.

### 5. The psychological part nobody packs for

The trick of the place is equilibrium. Inflow equals outflow. Light in equals light out. Noise in equals noise out. The rock does not weather, the currents do not seasonally shift, the harvester does not speed up. If you leave for a year, the system will be in the same squares when you return.

That is comforting for navigation, and corrosive for a human mind. There is no change to watch for, no storm to wait out, no growth to mark progress. Just bright emptiness, a permanent roar, and long stretches where you see one machine and hear everything else.

Ordinary travelers panic in two opposite ways here: claustrophobia from the noise and cold *in* an open volume, and agoraphobic drift from having no walls, no plants, no horizon. Train for both. Practice long, boring transits under load with a buddy, with timed tasks, with communication failures. Practice doing nothing while conserving heat — meditating, listening to your own breath apparatus, watching a depth gauge — because in current lakes and at machines you may need to hold position for a long time.

Go with at least one buddy, tethered or in close visual range, with a written plan and a hard turnaround time based on gas and heat, not on "when we see something." Solo travel here removes your only landmark that moves.

### 6. Multiverse traveler specifics: leave no permanent trace

Because nothing grows and nothing rots in any hurry, *you* will be the only thing that changes this place. A dropped glove, a food wrapper, a streak of grease, a fin kick that breaks a glass facet will look exactly the same in a year. Pack out everything, including waste. Bring sealed waste containers. Do not leave food, do not leave markers, do not carve, do not “improve” a machine.

Your return mechanism — portal, jump, extraction — must be tested for this medium. Sound, current, cold, and buoyancy all interfere with delicate transits. Arrive with your return already anchored on the table, not over the rim, and test it unloaded before you wander. If your exit pulls you even slightly toward the edge current, it will win. Plan your exit point inland, in calm, mapped water.

And do not expect local help to arrive. There is no local population to rescue you, and the machines will not notice you are in trouble. Self-reliance is not a virtue here; it is the only plan.

### A short packing checklist

- Heated dry suit system, hood, cut-resistant over-gloves and boots, spare gloves
- Rebreather / submersible life support, gas for 200% of planned trip, scrubber, bailout
- Hearing protection integrated into hood, visual comms display, strobes, acoustic pinger
- Compass, depth, artificial horizon, distance log, sonar/rangefinder, paper charts of fixed currents and pipeline/cable lines
- Powered propulsion with reserve, tether, anchor for calm lakes
- First aid usable in gloves, shear/bleed kit, repair kit for suit and mask
- All food, water, waste containers for the full duration
- Lights (for crevices and under machines, not for signaling whales), eye tint for the unfiltered daylight
- Hard turnaround criteria written down, buddy plan, abort signals

Go in expecting to be cold, loud, bored, and small — and to find the place exactly as you left it when you come back. If you prepare for that, you can spend a day above the glass watching whales pass along the rim, turbines turn without variation, and light plunge down through empty water, and come home with the sense that you stood for a while on a table that has not aged, under a sea that is itself the sound.
