The High Sump is not a place you visit so much as a place you are temporarily absorbed by.

Pack for that. Not for a trek, not for a beach, not for a jungle — though it will borrow from all three. As an ordinary human traveler you can arrive, breathe, eat, sleep and get out. You cannot fix it, light it, dry it, or speed it up. Preparation is mostly learning to subtract: metal, dryness, distance, and your usual expectations about cities.

### What it will actually feel like

Expect to be damp within an hour and never dry again. The basin is high, so your lungs will feel a little short on the spires, but the air is not thin and cold like a mountain. It's heavy, warm, saturated. You are living inside the top layer of a perched sea — a shallow, warm, closed inland ocean between still-rising volcanic ridges.

Sweat does not evaporate. Clothes do not dry. Stone is warm in the shallows. Houses hold the day's heat and other people's sweat. The only reliably dry, warm air is around the communal hearth-house for each cluster, and everyone knows it.

Light is dim forever, a late afternoon seen through two thicknesses of water and cloud. Visibility is usually fifty meters, then mist. At night it's complete and close. Sound is muted — rain making a continuous hush, paddles that don't splash, voices dissolving after a few meters. No birds to speak of, almost no large animals. Fauna is midges, amphibians, a few eel-like fish in spring pools. The place is dominated by plants, by people, and by buildings slowly turning back into rock.

Time is slow and heaving is slow. A fern unfolds, a crack opens, a house gets re-leveled by a hand's breadth. Don't come with a tight schedule.

### Before you leave: train and vaccinate for damp

1. **Feet and skin are your weak point.** You will be in warm, opaque, jade-green water constantly, on wet basalt that is sharp because erosion hasn't rounded it yet. Trench foot, fungal infections, macerated skin, and cuts that say infected are the occupational hazards, not predators. If you are prone to athlete's foot, eczema, or asthma, sort it out beforehand. Tetanus up to date is not optional — you're walking on freshly cracked rock and hand-quarried stone.

2. **Eyes and lungs.** Constant dim light, algae in the air, mold spores, midges in drifts, and steam from hot springs. Bring a fine mesh veil / hat for midges and mist. Your eyes will adjust in a day or two, and then a sudden Guignol-bright headlamp will be painful and rude. Bring a dim, warm, low lantern. Locals' lights bloom like fungi.

3. **Fitness for wet climbing, not for altitude.** You will climb hand-deep steps cut in rock, balance on sagging rope bridges, duck from raft to causeway to roof-garden. You will paddle, in a low, quiet stroke, in a reed or bamboo boat that will have very low freeboard. Practice swimming in warm, completely opaque water. You will not be able to see your hands, the bottom, or the eel-shaped things in the spring pools. That's normal. Don't flail.

4. **Mental prep.** This is a quiet, green-black, monotonous world. A traveler who needs glitter, birdsong, horizons, and hard shadows will get restless or lonely in 48 hours. Learn to listen for small sounds: a raft lapping a pylon, a spring breathing, stone on stone from a spire across the basin arriving late. Speak low. Shouting doesn't carry.

### What to bring — and what to leave

Think disposable, wettable, and non-metallic. Anything metal rusts to paste in a month. Anything leather, paper, cotton, or book-like will rot, mold, and be eaten by vines. Anything electronic will fog, short, and die, and if you drop it in the lake it will in a generation mineralize into a perfectly preserved fossil in a foundation.

**Wear:**
- 3-4 sets of synthetic, quick-draining layers — not because they will dry, but because they are light when wet, don't smell as fast, and can be rotated at the hearth to get *less* wet. Wool as a warm mid-layer only; it stays warm when damp.
- A proper waterproof shell for rain that never stops being soft, and a wide hat. You'll still be damp underneath. Accept it.
- Two pairs of shoes: closed-toe, soft-soled but cut-resistant water shoes / reef boots with excellent grip for slick basalt, and a second pair to keep slightly drier for sleeping. Barefoot is for children who know the spires. You will cut yourself on fresh plates and mineral veins.
- No fancy pack. Bring a small, soft duffel that can get wet and be hosed — metaphorically — at the hearth. Weight matters on rafts. Half of what you think you need, leave on the rim.

**Tools:**
- A stone or bone knife, a few obsidian or flint flakes, replacements. If you must bring metal, bring it as a gift, not as a tool. A fishhook will be lost in a week.
- Resin or beeswax in small tins for sealing boots and packs, shell-lime for small repairs, rope and cordage you don't mind losing.
- A collapsible paddle is less useful than learning to use theirs. Take the local boat.

**Health kit, small but serious:**
- Antifungal cream and powder, antiseptic, bandages that work wet, antibiotics if your medkit rules allow them, antihistamines for midge/spore reactions.
- Electrolytes. This is the paradox: you are surrounded by water and you will dehydrate. Sweat pours, it just doesn't evaporate. Sip constantly.
- Sunscreen, strangely. Direct sun is a rumor, but on the rare clear moment, wet high-altitude skin and water reflection will burn you before you notice. Sunglasses are for the rare clear moment, not for fishing.

**Food and water:**
Do not drink the lake. It's opaque with silt and algae, warm, fed by mineral upwelling springs. Do not drink from spring pools. You can collect rain off a roof with permission, but you'll need to boil/filter it — and fire is precious.

Locals live on algae cakes from floating gardens, root vegetables from raft gardens, and very sparse trapped fish. It's nutritious but not calorically dense for a stranger used to more. Bring dense, shelf-stable, wet-tolerant calories: nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, jerky in waxed cloth. Expect to trade most of it or eat it cold. There will be no personal cookfire. Food is cooked slowly, communally, at the hearth-house. Ask to join; don't ask to light your own.

**Trade and gifts:**
No coins. Markets run on lineage, knots in rope, labor, and memory. Bring things they value and can use without metal: salt, good spices in small amounts, woven fiber mats, resin, shell lime, quality obsidian flakes, tea. Bring your labor too — weeding a fern that's sealing a doorway, carrying stone, re-leveling a platform. That's currency.

**Knowledge:**
Hire a guide, preferably a child or young person from the neighborhood you're staying in. Children know which platform belongs to which lineage by the knot. You need that literacy. No map will work: causeways sag and shift, a crack heaves a few centimeters and a street plan from last generation is abandoned underwater, preserved, with straight lines of stone on the bottom. The city is continuously persuaded back from plants.

### Getting around and living there

Travel is by boat, causeway of packed stone and woven reed, lashed platform, rope bridge, and carved steps. There are no empty lots; neighborhoods are one per spire, one per raft-field, one per submerged shelf, all touching. Houses are stacked: stone ground floor half in water, timber and fiber room above, garden roof shading the doorway below.

Sleep above the waterline if you can. Ground floors are cool, wet, and mossy. Upper rooms are warmer, damper, and condensing on the inside walls. Mist goes *through* houses. You will sleep with rain on the mats above you and water running in rivulets down the walls. A hammock or a raised mat is better than a bedroll on stone.

Don't close doors for long. A door left unopened for a season gets sealed by growth — giant ferns from eaves, vines rooting in mortar. It's not neglect on their part; it's maintenance. When you leave a room, leave the door ajar. When you leave a neighborhood, don't clear the weeds from their walls; they are part of the structure now.

Wash in spring pools carefully. They look inviting and warm, and the bottom is foundation stone, old walls, and springs bubbling up that can be too hot underfoot. Test with a hand, then a foot. Some pools are communal washing places; some are shrines of spiral growth that doesn't rot. Don't take cuttings, don't move a mineralized log — that log is a foundation now.

### Safety, geological and otherwise

The land is still being made. You can sit on a spire that's too warm. Shelves are knife-edged and slick. Water pools and seeps in sheets rather than forming rivers. Nothing is worn smooth, so nothing is predictable. Move slowly, three points of contact, assume rock under water is sharp and hot.

There are no tides and no outlet, so the lake level is stable over a visit — but rising and falling by a hand's breadth over a decade, with the ground rising to meet it. That means yesterday's causeway can be ankle-deep today, next year's can be a ledge.

Don't expect to be heard if you fall. Sound is absorbed. If you are separated in mist, stop, get low, listen for stone-on-stone, for breathing springs, for a raft. Your guide will have methods — a low call, a knotted rope left at a junction — that work at 50 meters. Learn them before you need them.

At dusk the spires disappear one by one. Navigation back to the rim is nontrivial in that state. Do not leave guidance to your own sense of direction. The rim is not visible from the water; the basin has no coast, only slopes going down into it.

### Small etiquettes that matter

- Speak low, step from platform to platform, not from roof to roof unless invited. Roofs are gardens and other people's shade.
- The hearth-house is the dry room and the kitchen and the meeting place. Dry your socks there in rotation, share food there, don't hog the heat.
- Don't build, don't level, don't straighten. A foundation course underwater with no wall is not waste, it's memory. The previous generation tried a street there and the rock moved.
- Don't document with flash. Use your dim lantern. Don't take the strange things: vines growing down from bridges rooting in air, rain falling sideways off steam onto the underside of a staircase, preserved foundations covered in non-rotting spiral growth. Look, learn, leave them.

Isolation in the High Sump has produced a very competent, patient people. Tools are hand-tools, bone and stone. Buildings are massive where they meet water, light where they meet air. It's primitive in the sense that nothing is finished and much is temporary, not in the sense that it's a camp. It's a city whose entire maintenance culture is renewal against growth and heave.

As a traveler, your job is to be light, wettable, quiet, and useful. Bring the calories, the antifungal, the extra socks, the salt, and a willingness to paddle poorly for a long while at a soft, splashless pace. Leave the metal, the paper map, the expectation of dry, of sun, of birds, of a view.

If you do that, you'll find what most people miss on a first day: that it feels like being inside a warm lung, or a terrarium, or a bath that's been left running for a thousand years — green, black, ochre, and gold, dim and dripping, and built by people who have learned to live by knotting a rope, leveling a house, pulling a fern off a doorway, and doing it all again tomorrow.
