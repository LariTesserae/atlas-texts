The Sinterhouse is not hostile. It's unfinished, and it is indifferent, and that is exactly what will hurt an ordinary human body.

There is nothing here to help you: no water, no shade, no soil, no animals, no people, no trail, no artifact, no wind to cool you. Everything you need to stay alive, oriented, and able to get back out you have to carry in with you — and keep carrying, on a timer, because your body will not tell you honestly when it is in trouble. Sweat evaporates before it can run. Breath comes short on the ridge. Dust does not settle, does not wash away, and does not leave your lungs once you breathe it.

Treat it less like a destination and more like a furnace room that happens to be growing a garden.

### Decide if you should go, and for how long

This is not a place for a child, or for anyone with heart, lung, heat-sensitivity, or balance problems, or for anyone who panics in enclosed, silent, same-looking spaces. Note that down before you go, because inside you will want to stay longer — it is beautiful in a way that contradicts everything around it.

Plan for minutes, not hours. Twenty to forty-five minutes inside the pocket is a full visit for a first-time human. An hour is long. Heat does not peak and fall here, it accumulates, the day-night cycle is muffled by the vault, and the farther into the middle you go, the older the pocket is and the worse direction and time behave. If you want to camp, camp well outside it, on a safer part of the ridge, with your own water and your own clock.

Go early. There is no cool morning dew, no rain, no relief, but heat is still building from the moment the ordinary white-gold sun finds the slit. Earlier is less accumulated.

Never go alone. Voice is swallowed here, help cannot hear itself, and echo sometimes arrives before the sound has finished leaving your mouth. You need a second person, or at minimum a continuous line between you and the outside to follow back.

Set your return anchor *outside* the slit, in plain daylight. Do not put a portal, camp, or cache inside the pocket. The floor is still setting. A crack seals in an hour. A footprint fills in like cooling concrete deciding to forget you. Anything left still for long becomes a nucleus for new crystal. An anchor left inside can be worked loose, grown over, or lost in the loop.

### Physical preparation

Acclimatize on the ridge for at least a day beforehand if you can. Thin air + heat + a respirator is a different kind of effort than sea-level hiking. Walk slowly, talk less, and get used to being breath-short without sprinting.

Day before and morning of: sleep, eat normally, load up on water and a little salt. No alcohol. Do not start dehydrated hoping to find water on site — there is none. There is no seep, tarn, or dew to find. If it looks wet, it is not water. It is ruby resin.

Condition your feet. The ground is hot not from sun but from below. It radiates up through rock as if the mountain is still cooking. Standing still burns through ordinary shoes. You will be walking, pausing to look, standing balanced — you need thick, insulated soles, broken in, high enough to keep powdered mineral out. Not running shoes. Not metal studs, which will conduct heat and spark risk is real near the plants.

### What to pack

Pack light, but pack sealed. Everything open will fill with fine, dry dust that nothing here can wash out.

**Breathing and eyes:** A well-fitted respirator — P100/N95 or better — and sealed goggles. This is non-negotiable. The atmosphere is hot, thin, metallic, smelling of iron, scorched sugar, and ozone. The dust is pale, sharp, powdered at the edges, and it stays airborne. There is no water to clear it from your lungs later. Sunglasses are good for the ridge, but swap to sealed goggles at the slit. A climbing helmet is sensible: the vault is low, made of black glass and rust-red slag, fresh crystals are pushing up, and cooling rock ticks and pings and sheds chips.

Facial hair breaks a seal. Practice wearing the mask on the climb before you enter.

**Clothing:** Loose, complete coverage. Long sleeves, long pants, hat with a brim or neck flap, thick leather or heat-resistant gloves. You will brush plants, rock, and resin, and you do not want skin on any of it. The resin weeps thick as warm honey and does not dry. The leaves are waxy, ribbed like corrugated iron, some enamel-smooth and translucent. Some edges will be sharp, some hot, some slick with resin. Gloves are your buffer.

Avoid anything loose that can catch on a ribbed leaf or a growing crystal lip.

**Water and food:** All of it yours, all of it sealed, in a pack with sealed zippers. Drink on schedule, not on thirst, because thirst will lag behind evaporation. Set a silent timer: small drinks every 15-20 minutes, more if your pulse is racing. Eat a little salty food before. Keep food sealed and in your pack, never on the floor.

Do not bring water *for* the place. Do not pour it out, do not try to water the thickets. They do not drink. Their roots braid around hot veins after heat, and they breathe mineral vapor. Extra water would boil, contaminate their chemistry, and waste the water you need for your eyes and burns.

**Footing and movement:** Stiff, insulated boots as above. Train yourself not to stand still. Shift your weight, keep moving slowly. If you feel your footprint starting to soften or fill, wiggle free immediately. Do not pose for a photo in the middle. Do not set a pack down on the floor — it will start to sink, seal, and grow around itself.

**Navigation and time:** A continuous tether from your outside anchor to you. Rope, cord, bright line — unspooled as you enter, never cut, never tied off inside. Your compass, GPS, and sense of east will lie to you.

Inside, walking east you will come back from the west, having passed the same thicket twice from opposite sides. Uphill and downhill are the same slope. The ceiling garden is not a reflection — it is the same garden continuing root-to-root, and at the meeting point leaves interleave impossibly. You will count three islands on the floor, a fourth on the ceiling, and then miscount them because you have looped.

Do not try to map the center. Stay near the perimeter, near the slit, near your line. Treat the middle as a no-go zone. If disoriented, stop, find your line with your hands, follow it out. Do not try to shortcut.

Bring two timepieces and trust the one you synchronized *outside*. Time here accumulates rather than passes. A leaf half-formed this week will be half-formed next week, then complete in a blink when you are not looking. Seeds are older than their parent plants. Your watch may drift, your perception will certainly drift. Keep checking your outside time and leave by that, not by how long it *feels*.

**Communication:** Assume silence. Speak quietly, in short phrases, facing your companion. Agree beforehand on hand signals and line-tugs: one tug = check in, two = I am moving, three = I need to go back. If you hear your own echo arrive with a strange coldness before you finish speaking, or hear ticks and pings that sound like footsteps, do not answer it. Step back toward the entrance. Cooling slag is not something approaching you.

**First aid:** Small kit, all sealed. Bandages for sharp crystal cuts. Burn care for steam and hot rock — plants vent. Keep a small dedicated bottle of your drinking water, marked, for eye wash only. Do not use it casually. If resin gets on skin or gloves, do not wash it off with your drinking supply; wipe, peel what you can, bag the gloves, and clean properly outside.

**Camera / notebook:** Light inside is mercifully normal — true colors, expected shadows. A camera will work. But keep the lens capped between shots; dust will coat it and nothing washes it clean here. A paper notebook is fine if kept in a bag. Do not leave markers, coins, tape, or gear behind to “mark” a spot. They will be sintered into the floor and become part of the growth.

**What not to bring:** No live things. No seeds, soil, plants, pets, insects. No food scraps left behind. No matches or fire — there is nothing to burn, and many things here are hot, resinous, and under pressure. No plans to collect resin, leaves, seeds, or crystal. They are not specimens, they are engines, and handling them will burn, cut, stick, shock, or upset the temporal oddity of seeds older than their parents.

### On the ridge and entering

Climb slow. The sun is ordinary, the glare off pale, sharp stone is not. The air is thin, the heat is a constant pressure rather than weather, and there is no wind to speak of. Air moves only when a plant breathes out, occasionally, and reluctantly.

At the slit, everyone stops. Goggles on, respirator sealed, water taken, timer set, line tied to the outside anchor, time noted aloud. Check pulses. Sweat may not be visible — a racing heart and dry mouth are your indicators.

Enter low and slow. The pocket is perhaps ten paces across if you measure honestly, and then the proportions stop being honest. The vault is low. The floor is black glass and rust slag still becoming floor, new crystals pushing through old. Walk on the most established-looking stone near the edge, not through the middle of a thicket.

### Inside: How to behave around the gardens

The gardens will surprise you twice: first because they are so lush after meters of empty, shining mineral floor, and second because they are not fragile. They are industrial. Some are intakes, leaves angled to catch light, fins to shed heat. Some are smelters, bellows-swelling over days to drive salts up and lay down material layer by layer like a hot-printed pipe. Some are reservoirs, storing ruby fluid under pressure until a joint pings and hisses hot vapor.

Keep at least a pace back from any thicket. Do not lean against it, do not shade its leaves, do not stand directly over its root braid where heat concentrates. Listen for that slow tick, ping, and sigh. If a stem swells, hisses, or vents, step back and wait until it settles. The vapor is hot and carries mineral dust. It is not a sigh to lean into.

Do not touch. Do not brush a leaf to see if it is real. The resin will not dry, the ribbed edges can cut, and the plant is not a plant in any way that forgives handling. Admire from a distance. Their smell — hot resin, ozone, scorched sugar — will get stronger near them, and your mask, not your nose, should be doing the work.

The flowers do not open for pollinators. There are no insects, no birds, no lizards, nothing that moves under its own power. When a flower opens, it opens like a furnace door, glowing orange at the throat, and sheds pollen as a brief, silent spark that arcs across empty floor to the next plant. Cross-pollination is electrocution.

If you see an orange glow, freeze, then leave the immediate area. Do not stand between two thickets. Do not reach out to interrupt a spark. Do not carry anything long and metal. Wait until the glow is dark again, then move.

There are no animals to watch or avoid. That emptiness is part of the strain. You will hear your pulse in your ears for meters at a time. That is normal here. Breathe through the mask, slow, and keep checking your companion by touch or line.

### Orientation and time protocol

Assume you will get lost in the small. You will see the same thicket from the other side and think you have made progress. You will look up at the ceiling garden and lose up entirely. Up and downhill will feel identical because they are.

Rules that help ordinary humans:

1. Count nothing. Pace-counts loop.
2. Follow the line out when in doubt. No heroics in the center.
3. Note outside time every few minutes.
4. If your companion’s voice sounds delayed, early, or echoed cold, move back to the edge.
5. Leave before you feel you need to. By the time you feel dizzy, hot, or panicked, you have already accumulated too much.

### Leaving and decontamination

Follow your line back to daylight. Do not exit by a new “shortcut” through the center or up toward the ceiling garden. There is no up exit.

On the ridge, still masked, rehydrate, sit in whatever shade your pack and body can make, and check each other for pulse, dizziness, cuts, burns, resin, and dust in eyes. Then decontaminate before you go anywhere else in the multiverse.

This is crucial for a traveler: bag your outer gloves, wipes, and any dusty layer you can spare while still outside. Brush boots thoroughly *outside* the pocket. Inspect cuffs, seams, and pack straps for tiny crystal nuclei — because a pebble here is not a pebble, it is a nucleus, growing. You do not want to carry a growing crystal, a seed older than its parent, or resin-coated dust to the next world. Wash your hands and face with your remaining water when you are safely clear, then shower properly as soon as you can. Breathe easy, but mention the exposure; mineral dust that does not wash away in its home may still irritate you for days.

Do not leave anything behind, not even a footprint if you can help it — the place will erase it anyway, but you should not add trash, food, water, or biology to a world that is still deciding its rules.

### Quick checklist

- Outside anchor set, line ready, companion agreed, time synchronized outside
- Respirator sealed, sealed goggles, helmet, hat
- Insulated, high boots; long sleeves/pants; thick gloves
- Sealed water (drink on timer) + sealed eye-wash bottle + salty food
- First aid for cuts/burns, bags for contaminated items
- No live material, no collections, no leaving gear
- Plan: perimeter only, no center, 20-45 minutes max, leave by outside clock

The Sinterhouse will not last in the form you see it, and it has not lasted yet. In a hundred years it will have spread, folded, or become something else. Your job as an ordinary human traveler is not to tame it, water it, name it, or finish it. It is to visit briefly, with your own air, your own water, your own footing, and your own line back to the ordinary sunlight at the slit — and to leave before an embryonic world, still learning its own rules, starts teaching them to you.
