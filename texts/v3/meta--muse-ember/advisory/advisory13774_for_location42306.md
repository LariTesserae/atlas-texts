If you are an ordinary human, arriving by the usual multiverse way - a step, a portal, a drop with a pack and no one waiting on the other side to share the view - this place will not meet you halfway. It is not a mountain to climb. It is a finished surface that has been lifted, intact, to a height where there is nothing left for weather to do except riot on its skin.

You have to arrive self-contained, briefly, and already braced for the two things it guarantees: emptiness and noise.

### 1. Reset your expectations before you go

There is no path, no settlement, no summit to aim for, and no one else. Other tables may rise out of the cloud sea far away. They are not reachable. Do not try to cross to them. Your return must be anchored where you arrived, on ground you have tested, and your visit should be planned in hours, not days, until you have learned it. A first visit that turns into a night out there is a survival situation, not a camp.

The light will lie to you. It is plain midday light, warm on your shoulders, warm wind on your neck, even though you are two to three thousand metres above a sea of cloud and the air that high should feel thin and cold. It is thin - you will be breathing hard on a slope you can barely see - but the sun on polished stone makes it feel like a warm day anywhere else. That warmth is real, and it is a trap. The stone itself is warm and slightly damp under your palm, sweating in full sun, and when the rain comes and the wind flattens you, wet clothes and a scouring wind will chill you faster than you expect. Dress for a warm, bright, windy day, and pack for a cold, wet storm that can arrive in three minutes.

You will walk for an hour over naked, bone-smooth slabs - grey, iron-red, pale pink, polished black where water runs in a film - with no bush, no outcrop, no track, nothing to cut yourself on and nothing to hold onto. Then you will step off a polished bowl no bigger than a house and be dripping overhead from ferns as broad as hands, moss as thick as a mattress under your boot, rosettes spiralling clockwise around a weeping point in the stone, cushions of gnarled shrubs studded with white and magenta flowers that only open when wet. That contrast is the whole place. Everything valuable - water, shelter of a sort, life - is compressed into those damp finger-dips. Everything dangerous - the edge, the wind, the long empty tilts - is in between.

### 2. Your body

**Altitude.** You are not just high, you are *above weather*. Spend days beforehand at 2,000-2,500m if you can. If you cannot, assume the first hour will give you a pounding head, short breath, and slow thinking. Bring whatever altitude medication your doctor approves for you personally, and electrolytes and food with salt. You will dehydrate without sweating obviously, because wind and sun and high air steal water from breath and skin while your boots stay damp from the stone itself.

**Sun and eyes.** No tree, no shade, no bush. Sun reflects off flat stone and off the cloud sea below. Hat with a chin strap that will survive a gust, sunglasses, high-factor sunscreen on face, neck, ears and the back of hands, lip balm. Also bring clear wind goggles. Rain here sometimes falls *up* - lifted off the cliff by an updraft from below - and wets eyelashes from underneath, and mist is wrung out on the windward side of every stone. Ordinary glasses will fog and spot and you will be blind in the exact moment you need to see a weather curtain coming.

**Feet and hands.** This stone is not sharp. That is the danger. It is polished, warm, damp, and in places hollow. Luggy hiking boots skate on wet polished rock. You want soft, sticky rubber - approach shoes or low trail shoes with a climbing sole that actually grips wet granite - with good ankle support underneath, because slabs are uneven in a subtle way, pebbles between them are river-round, and moss pockets will take your foot to the calf and then grip and twist. You will be damp all day. Bring two complete changes of socks in waterproof bags, blister care, and expect to stop and dry feet even though there is nowhere private or sheltered.

Hands: the stone won't cut you, but you will steady yourself on it constantly. Bring light gloves for wind and for sliding on wet slabs. Do not test slabs by standing still on them. A ringing, low, pot-like tone when tapped, a vibration you feel in your teeth if you stand too long, means a thin shell over a hollow where ancient rain is still travelling drip by drip. Move on. Do not enter those hollows. The flat, earless, leather-like thing that lives under some of them only comes out when rain drums loud enough to hide its breathing. You are lucky to see it twice. You need not see it at all.

**Hearing and mind.** At rest the plateau is loud: wind pouring over the edge and accelerating across stone, roaring like surf through flat leaves, finding worn holes under slabs and making them sing a low organ note that overlaps another and never resolves. In a storm it becomes deafening white noise - wind, rain hitting stone at a slant and bouncing up, pebbles skittering, plants thrashing, and the whole table booming from inside, thunder rolling continuously in the cloud deck *below* you, felt in your soles. You will not hear your own shout. Your thoughts will have to wait for a gust to pass.

Bring filtered earplugs for the storm and solid earplugs for sleep, but train with them before you go so you are not suddenly deaf to balance. Bring a notebook and a pencil, not because you will write poetry, but because speech will fail and writing is how you will think, mark distances, and leave a note on your pack if you are separated from it.

Loneliness is a physical force here. The ordinary light, the warm damp, the vacuum of polished stone for hundreds of paces in every direction, the drip that continues long after rain has stopped from stone that looks dry - as if the plateau is remembering water - will unbalance you. Talk to yourself out loud when it's quiet; it's normal. Plan your time in small tasks: to the next bowl, to the next ring of condensation, back uphill.

### 3. What to carry

Carry everything, because there is nothing to gather.

**Water: at least 3-4 litres, plus purification.** There are no streams. Water arrives in a three-minute cloud tear, as condensation beading on the underside of stone, as mist pulled up the cliff. It pools only in hollows, for a day at most, then smells of iron and warm dust, of fermented leaf litter and crushed fern. The moss is black-green and swollen, but pool water is not safe to drink untreated, and licking condensation or relying on the stone sweating will not keep you alive. Purify any pool water as an emergency only, and do not count on it.

**Windproof, waterproof, low profile.** A close-fitting shell jacket and trousers that seal at wrists and neck against upward rain and sideways storm rain. A pack cover. Everything in dry bags. No loose straps, no hat without a strap, nothing that will become a sail.

**Shelter and fire - carried, not made.** There is no wood, no tree to stand under, no peg-friendly ground. If you are caught, you will need a bothy bag or a very low tent you can pitch in the lee of a shrub cushion or a slab in a stone bowl, anchored in cracks, not stakes. It will be buffeted and torn if you pitch it on open stone. A small canister stove and fuel, because you cannot make a fire, and a warm drink in a storm is medicine. For wind, the cushions and bowls are your only windbreaks - use them, but do not crush the moss to make a bed. Step on stone where you can; moss is the whole ecosystem of that pocket, and it only grows on the windward side of stones, fed by mist before a drop ever falls. It is not a spring mattress for you to lie in all night.

**Navigation.** A compass you have tested, a map of the plateau if your multiverse office has one, and a method for dead reckoning. There are almost no landmarks. The plateau is gently domed and tilts, with almost imperceptible patience, toward the edge. You will not feel yourself descending to death until you are close. The rosettes spiral clockwise around their wet point, not around north. The plants lean and flatten and re-root with wind, they do not point home. Moss on the windward side tells you wind, not direction.

Count paces through empty stretches. Mark your return point with something bright you will absolutely remove later - a length of cord tied in a crack, as the old condensation bowl in the description was tied. Do not build cairns. One dead-simple cairn of three hand-stacked round stones, sunk to its second course in moss, is already there. Someone left it, and the plateau is polishing it back to nature. Leave it. Leave the flaked dark blade wedged in a crack, and the shallow pecked bowl meant to catch drip. They are not souvenirs, and they are not instructions. They are warnings that people have come with simple tools and the place has erased almost all of them.

**Signals.** A mirror, a bright panel, a headlamp - but assume a whistle or your voice will be swallowed. The single hawk that screams once high above and is gone is not going to help you. A personal beacon of whatever kind your traveler kit uses, plus a physical signal you can make from a bowl: bright cloth tied low where wind can see it, because no one will see you from the cloud sea.

**Food.** High-calorie, salty, easy to eat with gloves on, no cooking dependency. You may see nothing with a face in an hour except a thumbnail beetle moving slowly through moss, or that hawk thrown sideways. There is nothing to hunt and nothing to forage. The flowers and ferns are not food.

### 4. Movement and the edge

Walk slowly. Prepare to watch a curtain of weather approach from a mile off as a dark smudge on the stone. You can hear it before it reaches you. That is your early warning. Light, shadow, wind, rain, heat, in no settled order.

When you see it coming:
- Turn away from the edge first. Get at least many tens of metres back from any drop. In cloud, whiteout rain, or noise, you cannot see a 2,000m drop until you are on the lip where wind pours over and accelerates.
- Get off moss pockets - they will be thrashing and impossibly slippery.
- Get off a ringing slab. Brace low behind a slab or in a shallow bowl, sitting, pack secured, loose items inside. Do not try to outrun the storm across empty stone; the wind will throw you sideways as it throws the hawk.
- Do not stand tall on wet stone. Thunder is rolling below the rim, inside the deck. The plateau itself is not a tall lightning rod, but wet polished stone conducts and there is nowhere dry to crouch.

After it passes, do not trust the look of the stone. It will still be dripping, sweating, booming faintly. The dripping echo continues long after the rain has stopped. Step as if it is still wet - because under the skin, it is.

Uphill is safety. If you are ever disoriented, go to the highest domed ground you can find away from other tables' edges, rather than following a line of bowls downhill toward the rim. Bowls collect water, and water collects the edge.

Do not follow seep lines under slabs to drink. Do not put your head into a hollow to listen to the organ note. The stone will answer a step with vibration, and that is information, not invitation.

### 5. Etiquette with a living, dying place

This mountain finished dying a long time ago. It has stopped eroding because there is nothing left to take off it that would change its shape. It is dead in its bones and alive on its skin. It will not rearrange itself for you, it will not keep your path, it will not remember you.

Step lightly, go briefly, leave nothing: no cairn, no tape, no waste in a moss pocket, no footprint in the swollen moss. Do not pick the flowers that open when wet. Do not move the beetle to look at it. If you see the leather-flat animal because the rain is loud, give it the hollow back.

Bring out everything, including if you must empty your pack to tie down in wind. Condensation rings will form in full sun on vertical faces around the rosettes, and dew will form even when you are sure it's dry. Your gear will be damp in the morning even if it never rained. Dry bags are not paranoia.

### 6. If you are stranded

You will be warm, damp, deafened, and entirely alone above clouds with no signal to the lowland. Sit in a bowl, out of wind, out of the drip line. Conserve water. Conserve voice. Write down time, direction of wind, what you saw. Watch for the next curtain - storms here tear apart and reform a hundred metres away, so a clear patch is not the end. Signal at midday when light is ordinary and your panel will flash off flat stone, and again when a hawk or any life is high and might see movement.

Above all, do not wander in search of a lower way off. There is no lower way. There is only the table, the cloud sea, another table impossibly far away, and the drip still falling somewhere under your boots in the dark hollow of the old world.

Prepare as if the plateau will grant you one good, bright, ordinary hour - and will make you pay for every extra minute you assume it owes you.
