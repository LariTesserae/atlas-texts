You are not entering a cave. You're slipping under the skin of a world that hasn't finished deciding what it is, into a bright, cold, damp closet that is still being born.

That matters for an ordinary human. You will not be warmed by the light, you will not be able to see your way out, you will not be able to drink, and you will not be able to trust your eyes, ears, or sense of direction for more than a few minutes at a time. Prepare less like an explorer going far and more like a guest going briefly into a newborn room: small, quiet, tethered, and out again before the cold settles in.

### The non-negotiables

**1. Never enter solo.** One person inside, one person kneeling at the crack outside. The room is only about six paces across, two metres high, and you cannot see the entrance once you're in. It is bright everywhere and from nowhere, so there is no doorway to look back toward. A second human's whole job is to hold the rope, keep time, and notice if your signals stop.

Bring no more than two people total at the site. Three bodies will fill the pocket, stir the condensation, block the light, and make a small cold room feel frantic.

**2. Go in on a tether.** Thin, bright orange or red cord, 8-10 metres, anchored to a stone *outside* the crack. Feed it in as you enter and keep a hand on it at all times. Let it lie along the floor, not tied to the growing walls - the walls are spongy, waxy, and may soften and forget a knot. If you wander, the room will lie to you about its size: larger if you turn your back to the glare, smaller if you face it, with corners that move when you aren't looking. The rope is the only honest straight line.

**3. Stay short.** Fifteen to twenty minutes for a first visit. Thirty is a hard limit for anyone. There is nothing to collect, no summit to reach, no good camp to make. The cold does not hit you like air temperature; it sinks, first into your knees, then into your breath and core, and stays after you leave. A condensation cycle - film, beads, brief drizzle, evaporation - runs every few minutes. Use that as your clock, and a mechanical watch outside. Set a turnaround before you enter, based on cold and fatigue, not on what you want to see.

**4. Bring everything back out.** This is a place that is still forming. Things left there do not stay “left.” They sink halfway, get skinned over, get claimed mid-construction like the unfinished cairn by the far wall. Do not leave a glove, a wrapper, a drop of blood, a footprint if you can help it, and absolutely do not finish anything: do not add the fourth stone, do not close the spiral, do not complete the broken arc. The rule here seems to be that nothing is obliged to finish becoming what it is. Helping it finish may be how it seals, or opens, or decides to remember you.

### What to wear

Dress for cold damp, not for bright light.

- **Dark, saturated clothing.** White on white is the whole environment. Pale gear disappears; you will lose your own hands in it. Black, deep red, dark orange - anything that makes a clear shape against bleached stone and casts a normal, downward shadow. Your companion outside should be able to spot you through any accidental gap.
- **Layers that wick, not cotton.** Merino or synthetic base, a fleece mid-layer, a light wind/damp shell. The room is damp without ever quite being wet. Moisture will condense on you, your glasses, your breath. Cotton will get cold and stay cold.
- **Warmth for knees and feet first.** The description is precise: the cold settles in your knees. Wear padded leggings or knee pads, thick socks, and insulated boots with a grippy but shallow sole. You will be kneeling, crouching under a two-metre ceiling, and stepping on stone that is still doming and sloping. Carry a small foam sit-pad. It is also a dark, matte reference object you can put down and pick back up - useful in a room with no contrast.
- **Hat, thin grippy gloves, a buff.** Your head will be close to the ceiling, where the slugs are plastered upside-down and the hanging filaments descend. Do not touch the ceiling with your head or hands. Gloves keep unfinished stone and mineral exudate off your skin and keep your hands functional on the rope. The buff works as a scarf, a nose-warmer, and a quick fog-wipe.
- **Cover cuts.** Damp, mineral-rich, teeming at a microscopic scale. Bandage anything open before you go in. No perfume, no strong-scented lotions or insect spray - the air is still and the fauna are feeding on condensation and exudates, not on you, and chemicals are an intrusion here.

Do not wear a backpack. It will not fit the crack, it will scrape the ceiling, and it will catch on crystal teeth pushing up through the floor. Use a slim waist pack or pockets. Empty your pockets before you enter.

### What to carry - and what to leave

Carry in the waist pack:

- A **warm, low light**, not a bright white one. The ambient light is cold blue-white glare from the ceiling itself. It bleaches everything and kills depth. A dim 2700-3000K headlamp or handheld, angled down, will restore color: pale lime moss from grey stone, shiny mucus trails from wet rock, your own boots from the floor. Keep it low and steady. No flash, no strobing, no shining up at ceiling slugs or into puddles.
- **Amber or blue-blocking glasses**, plus your regular glasses with anti-fog if you need them. Staring at that ceiling makes the edges of your vision get brighter, not darker. It produces afterimages, pressure in the ears, and a high cold ringing when the slugs move. Look down, look sideways, blink a lot. Do not stare up to “get a better look.”
- The rope, the foam pad, a pencil and a small waterproof notebook.
- About 750 ml of your own water, room temperature, and one sealed high-calorie bar. There is no water to drink here. The film beads, chooses a direction - down, sideways into the wall, sometimes *up* toward the ceiling - and vanishes. The shallow mirrors are not water. Do not cup them, do not drink from them, and do not use them as mirrors. Your reflection in them is a breath older than you and sometimes moves when you do not.
- A tiny first-aid patch: plaster, antihistamine if you are prone to mold/mite reactions, your usual headache tablets. The floor is a carpet of mites, springtails, and hair-thin nematodes under the glare that is invisible at your scale. You will walk among them, not on a sterile floor. Brush off before you leave.
- A small brush for exit decontamination.
- A mechanical watch if you can. Your phone will fog, drain in the cold, find no signal under the skin of the world, and its glass screen will only add another wrong reflection. Leave the phone, compass, GPS, mirror, stove, tent, and any tool made of metal or fire outside. The makers of the spiral and cairn used only bodies and patience. Bring respect, not equipment.

Leave outside: bulky gear, mirror, food to share, samples, seeds, anything you would be tempted to leave as a marker. Your footprints in the damp film stay bright, then fill in from the edges. The floor is trying to forget you. Let it.

### Going through the crack

Warm up, eat, drink, use the toilet, and empty your pockets *before*. You will enter sideways, on an exhale, shoulder first, through a hand-wide gap between stones that are still growing together, pale and damp as unfinished clay. Do not brace hard with your hands - the walls give, the stone may be setting, and a push could deform it or pinch you if it shifts.

Pass nothing bulky ahead of you to “test” it. Go slow. The cold air you slip through will stay in your knees. Your companion outside keeps tension on the rope but does not pull unless you signal.

Once inside, freeze for a full minute. Do not try to orient.

Let your eyes adjust to a light that has no source and no shadow. Let your breathing slow - it will sound too loud, then strangely muffled, as the spongy walls swallow sound. Listen instead for the room: a granular tick as a crystal sets, a wet sigh as a pore opens, a distant tick-tick of many legs filtered through cloth. That ticking you feel in your molars is not imagination. If it quickens, pause. Something is growing or shifting.

### Inside: how to see, move, and stay

**See with your edges.** The moss is pale lime and appears most clearly when you are *not* looking straight at it. The hanging filaments from the ceiling are translucent until they block the light. Sweep your gaze slowly, look down at the floor, look peripherally, then look away. A warm lamp raking low across the stone will do more than your normal vision.

**Move in short arcs and stay near the rope.** The floor is domed in the centre and re-slopes to the walls. Walks that should take three steps can loop you back to a different condensation stage, with a new crystal popped up where there was none. Step only on bare, clean stone when you can. Avoid moss patches, fungus fingers, shiny trails, crystal teeth, and the shallow puddles. Everything is unfinished and easily damaged, and some of it is alive at scales you cannot see.

**Do not touch the signs.** The three-stone cairn half-sunk into the floor, the wet spiral clawed into the soft wall, the upright broken arc that holds condensation longest - small things shelter under it. Look, photograph without flash if you must, and leave them exactly as incomplete as you found them.

**Expect the wrongness and name it, don't chase it.** Shadows here fall upward and sometimes arrive a moment before the animal that owns them, pooling on the ceiling. A patch of darkness roughly animal-shaped hunts the blind millipede-like things along the wall-ceiling junction, moving seconds before its body. When it passes over you, the cold deepens. If you feel that, do not run. Crouch low on your pad, stay still, breathe slowly. The blind fauna cannot see you; a panicked human thrashing in the film is far more conspicuous. Let it pass. Do not follow shadows to see what made them.

If your ears ring with pressure, if colors bleed - a green afterimage hanging in the air, an ochre smear on a white wall that vanishes when you approach - lower your head, look at your dark gloves, breathe through your nose, and stop moving for a few breaths. This is the place fraying, not you breaking. But ordinary humans get migraines and vertigo here. If it intensifies, follow the rope out. There is no prize for endurance in a room this size.

**Do not sit still too long.** The bare floor looks empty, but at mite scale it is crowded. A few minutes on a pad is fine; do not settle your weight into the spongy wall, do not lean on the waxy skin of rock, do not let your bare forearms rest on the floor. When you stand, brush your clothes and gloves down in a corner before you move.

**Keep your head down.** Two metres is not high when you are tall, crouched, and looking up. Ceiling slugs leave bright mucus routes, the too-many-eyed spider hangs in strands of condensation stretched between crystal teeth, and the shrimp-shaped translucent things dart not through water but through damp air and film. You do not need to touch any of them to share the room. Duck, do not stretch, do not reach for the web.

### Getting out

Follow the rope hand over hand, counting condensation stages if you can. When you slip back through the crack into cold outside air, your companion should already be holding your pack, water, and sunglasses. Do not stand up immediately and stare at the bright plain above - your eyes will be bleached and your knees will still be cold. Sit, warm your core, look at something dark and distant.

Then decontaminate like a multiverse traveler, because you are one: shake and brush boots, gloves, and trousers *outside*, check skin and socks for hitchhikers, put used gloves in a zip bag, and count your gear against what you carried in. A mite or nematode that lives in a still-forming pocket should not get a free ride to another world.

Wash up, change into dry socks, and eat something warm. Expect to feel the cold in your knees for a while. That is normal. Expect also to remember the room as quieter, brighter, and emptier than it was. It was feverishly alive the whole time; you were just too large to see most of it.

If the crack ever seals while you are inside - stones growing together is the whole nature of the place - do not scratch the walls to make a new way. Stay low on the central dome, conserve your lamp and water, keep the rope in your hand, and signal with two slow light pulses every minute. The pocket breathes in cycles of setting and unsetting; a way that closed may, in a few cycles, soften again. Your outside companion's job is to wait, keep time, and not enter after you. That is why you tethered, why you kept it short, and why you told someone outside exactly when you would be back.

Go in small, go in dark-clothed and warm-lamped, keep one hand on the line, look with the edges of your eyes, touch almost nothing, finish nothing, and leave with everything you brought - including the cold.
