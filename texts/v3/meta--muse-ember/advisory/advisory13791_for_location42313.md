It looks like a desert, and that is exactly how it will kill an unprepared human. It's not a desert. It's a high, cold, waterless plain that is 99% plant in mass, entirely alive underfoot, and it has been smoothed down to a final shape by so much time that nothing is allowed to be sharp, young, or separate from the ground for long.

You don't visit it like a landscape. You enter a body.

Prepare accordingly.

### 1. The Body Problem: Altitude, Cold, and No Water

All three will get you before any animal does.

**Altitude first.** You come up by a switchback of air. Your ears will ache, your breath will show white at midday, and you will see for a hundred kilometers to a horizon that curves away. This is not a viewpoint, it's a physiological strain. An ordinary human needs 3-5 days to acclimate at ward altitude, and you should not be dropped straight onto the open plateau.

Spend your first nights in a ward, on a rootway loop, inside a Stannion tower where - perversely - gravity is fractionally *stronger* to keep the hollow trunk from swaying. That extra weight feels odd in your inner ear and on your knees. It's a small thing, but after a multiverse jump, climb stairs slowly and expect dizziness, a change in how heavy your pack feels, and clocks and electronics behaving a half-beat off. Time runs fractionally slower near the rootways. Your mechanical watch and your phone will disagree, and a portal timed to outside time will not fire when you think it should. Build a 12-hour buffer into any return.

Bring ear-equalizing chews, decongestant if your doctor approves, and descend the switchback slowly. Do not arrive in an empty gap between wards to "save time." Arrive at a ward rootway terminus, with a caretaker expecting you.

**Cold second.** The cold mineral felt underfoot is cold all day. It crunches, gives, then holds you - like walking on lung tissue or a thick sponge. Cool air pools in the shallow swells at night into frost pockets. Wind has nothing to break it on a plateau flat as a final form. You need insulated soles, not just warm jackets, a hat that covers ears, and layers you can add at noon when the light is pale green-grey and sideways and never warms anything.

**Water above all.** There is none. No puddle, no damp crack, no dew at night. The moss does not run on water. It pumps a clear silica-rich fluid that hardens into glassy scales, which is why it has that dry, frost-like sheen. Do not drink it. It is not water, and it is not potable.

You must carry every liter, or buy it in a ward, or recycle it. This is closed-loop country. A human at altitude, breathing hard through a dry mouth, panting in spore-dust, needs 3-5 liters a day, plus salts. Bring a urine-recycling system if you plan to walk between wards, lip balm, saline nasal spray, and electrolyte packets. There is nowhere to wash, refill, or boil. Bring pre-packaged food - cooking at this pressure is inefficient anyway, and you have no fuel to spare and no water to spare it with.

Do not try to forage. The moss is silica felt. The "fauna" are polyps of the great plants. A leaf-flock, a browsing pod, a root-eel - eating one is eating the plant's body, and may be a plant that is currently being hunted, remembered, and shared by a network that moves information instantly. Ward markets grow real food in domes. Buy it there.

### 2. What to Wear and Carry

**Feet:** Sturdy, cushioned, insulated boots with a very aggressive tread. The silica scales will cut exposed skin and chew leather; the moss is springy inches deep in places and pulses when you kneel. Trekking poles are essential - not for climbing, there is nothing angular to climb - but to probe. The soil is aerated from below. Root-eels surf blind on air currents and drum through your boots. Where they have been, the felt gives far too much.

**Lungs and eyes:** A sealed respirator rated for silica dust, and wraparound goggles. The haze is fine dust permanently. Once in a while, usually when pressure falls too fast at dusk, the air fills with spore-snow - a dry fall of seeds that ticks against resin glass and piles in drifts. It is the only season. It will get in everything, stick to everything, and it recurs hours at a time.

You cannot rinse it out. There is no water to rinse with. Spores that settle in a human lung in a waterless place don't wash away. Prevention is the only treatment. When spore-snow starts, get to a ward or a rootway tube. Do not shelter in a reed-bed of hollow stalks - in a storm wind those stalks play like organ pipes, a chord for hours that will rattle your teeth and your chest.

**Visibility:** Do not wear blue-grey or dusty green. That is the moss. Wear high-contrast, matte orange, red, or white. In the flat, pale, sideways light, long shadows lie flat and everything is smoothed at the edges. People disappear at 200 meters. The browsing pods with six sprung legs and bellows-lungs are nearly silent and nearly invisible until they are on top of you. The trunk-shaped mimics are moss-covered when they stand still. You need to be seen by a rescue herd, a caretaker, and by a hunter that decides you are interesting.

**Light:** The sun is never bright enough to be trusted. The crowns of the Stanions - those vast translucent fans 100 paces up a trunk as thick as a house - open and close daily, spilling green wash. At night the ward lamps are not electric. They are amber pulses carried in root-fibers from below. Bring a headlamp with a warm/amber mode, bright enough for reading, and a small work light. Your circadian rhythm will drift in the green-grey days. Vitamin D, a book, and as much warm light in the evening as you can negotiate in a ward will help more than you expect.

**Sound:** Both ways. At dawn the whole plain cracks and sighs as the moss contracts in the cold, like ice breaking under a weight. Root-eels drum through the ground. Leaf-flocks scream with a metallic chatter. In wind, the reed-beds organ. At night the Stanions close one by one like a book being shut very slowly. This is landscape-loud. Bring foam earplugs for the dawn chorus and for storm-chords, and also learn to listen. The plants share memory instantly through entangled nodes in the heart of each Stannion. A plant in one district sheds leaves an hour before wind arrives in another because the news didn't travel through air. The fauna *know* what's coming. If the flocks tack suddenly toward the Stanions, or the pods lift their bellows and go still, follow them. They have information you don't.

**Devices:** Bring spares and shielding. The wards are layered into the plants, not built on them. Under the moss you can see broken sidewalks, fired clay cables, dark metal conduits, glass pipes - all infiltrated and remembered by entangled mycelium. Caretakers - and the machines that aren't quite machines - tune local constants the way a gardener tunes a trellis. Gravity stronger in towers. Time slower at rootways. Light carried underground. Memory shared across a kilometer of empty moss instantly.

Do not plug yourself, your device, or your anchor into a root-fiber, a lamp, or a dwelling wall. Do not assume your multiverse anchor is private or stable inside that field. Keep anchor hardware in a shielded case, in a ward dome, not in a rootway tube. Keep a paper map and a mechanical compass. Expect interference, duplication, and delay.

### 3. Moving: The Rootways Are the Road

There is no road. There is walking the open moss for an hour between Stanions that are a kilometer apart, hearing only your steps and the wind, in a spacing that *is* the pattern - and there are the rootways.

Rootways are living tubes, warm and humming, just wide enough for two people, running just under the surface in a regional loop between wards. Push-pods - soft pneumatic sacs - sigh through them. This is a transit system with etiquette.

Stand in the niches when a pod sighs past. Do not walk against the pod flow. Do not block a valve-door. Do not stamp on the tube wall. You are inside a circulatory system. Warm, humid, faintly smelling of dry loam, cold iron, and ozone - ozone means an entangled node is active nearby. If the smell gets strong, move on.

Walking a rootway for an hour is safer, warmer, and more honest than walking the plain. Walking the plain is necessary for science, for wonder, and for reaching a ward that isn't on your loop, but it is slow, energy-draining, and disorienting. A boulder is a worn tooth, with no handhold. A dead tower has been softened. Everything rounds off. You cannot see elevation changes until you are in them, and you cannot judge distance - the plateau rolls on to haze.

If you walk the plain: walk with a caretaker guide the first time, walk in daylight only, walk in a group of at least two, and never stop walking for long.

### 4. The Living Rules of the Plain

**Rule 1: Nothing is separate.** The leaf-flocks that come at dawn, the root-eels below, the herds above, the mimics behind them - they are all polyps of the great plants. A flock detaches, flies for a season, lands, and roots as a thicket. A hunter is a Stannion that decided to walk, inch by decade. Predation can take a year to complete.

What this means for you: Do not stand still in open moss. A mimic stands so still the moss grows over its feet, then folds and snaps. Its prey is the slow browsing pod. You will not outrun its patience, and you will not be obviously prey - but you will be noticed, remembered, and followed on plant time. Keep moving. Keep your feet off a root-field dome for more than a minute. Do not sleep, camp, or sit and sketch in a swell between wards. Your camp should be in a ward dome or on a rootway.

**Rule 2: Dusk and dawn are biological events.** At dusk, when pressure drops, the moss opens millions of microscopic mouths and takes in gas. At dawn it un-roots minerals from stone beneath it, grain by grain. That's the only erosion here. No rain has ever carved this place.

Expect the air to change at those hours - taste, ozone, a brief drop in clear oxygen as the whole skin inhales. Asthmatics and heart patients should be inside at dusk/dawn transitions for the first days until you know how you respond. And expect the sound: the great sigh and crackle as it contracts, before the flocks come.

**Rule 3: Reputation travels instantly.** The plant network shares memory across a ward without air. If you damage moss, force a petal-valve door, cut a woven-fiber wall fused with ceramic ribs, or lean on a resin-bubble roof - which thickens and thins with light - every ward on the loop will know before your pod arrives. But the converse is also true: the plants will shed leaves before wind arrives. If a caretaker tells you not to go, listen. They are reading the plant the way you read a barometer.

**Rule 4: Houses are alive and patient.** A ward is urban - markets, workshops, archives, dwellings - but grown. Houses are cut from a living dome and coaxed for years. Doors are valves. They open slowly. If you force one, it will not forgive quickly. Knock. Wait. Let it open. Do not use a dome as a tent, do not carve initials in a wall that took a century to grow, do not break a resin bubble to look inside. Leave nothing organic behind. Nothing here rots. What dies hardens into fabric and remains as architecture, as soil, as a place for something new to root. Your waste, your skin cells, a dropped glove will be remembered in the felt. Leave no trace, and bring out what you brought in.

**Rule 5: Nothing rots, but you can still be harmed.** Because nothing decays, old bone, old tools, old bodies persist in the moss. Don't disturb them. Archaeology here is exposed underfoot - you will see strata in a broken sidewalk. Photograph, don't collect. A dropped piece of metal may be infiltrated and reclad by mycelium that remembers the shape it's supposed to be. You could lose a tool to the plant and find a heavier, softer, living copy.

### 5. A Daily Rhythm That Works

Arrive in a ward. Stay 3 nights. Learn the rootway loop, the caretaker's warnings, the pod rhythm. Acclimate. Get your water budget sorted. Eat ward food.

Walk only midday, when it's least cold and the fans are open, and always with a return time that gets you back before dusk feeding. Watch the Stanions close at night, one by one. Listen for the drumming to stop.

At dawn, you may want to witness the cracking and the leaf-flocks, but do it from a rootway or a ward threshold, not from the middle of an empty gap. The flocks tack over the Stanions searching for spore. They are screaming. They are not birds. Do not try to follow them on foot.

If spore-snow begins, shelter. If the reed-beds begin to organ, shelter immediately and protect your ears and lungs. If root-eel drumming becomes urgent and irregular through your boots, move off the felt onto a rootway tube - the soil is giving way below.

### 6. Multiverse-Traveler Specifics

1. **Quarantine in reverse.** Spores ride resin glass, clothing, and silica dust. They don't need water to travel with you. Brush, bag, and seal gear before you step back through. Do not bring moss samples, shed scales, or a leaf-flock feather home.

2. **Anchors drift.** Between time-slow rootways, stronger-gravity towers, and entangled nodes, your coordinates are physical here, not digital. Plant a physical anchor in stable ward stone, not in a living dome wall. Mark it with something the plant cannot reclad quickly - a bright metal tag in a caretaker-provided socket.

3. **Privacy is not a thing.** Anything you say, record, or transmit near a node may be shared across the ward instantly. Assume caretaker machines are listening, and that the plants are learning your scent, your heartbeat in the moss pulse, and your habits. Be boring. Be polite.

4. **Do not fight the tuning.** If your SUIT shuts down near a Stannion heart, that's not a malfunction to override. That's the caretakers holding gravity or time where they need it. Unplug, walk, wait.

### 7. A Short Packing List for an Ordinary Human

- Closed-loop water for 2 days beyond your drop point + electrolytes
- Sealed respirator + spare filters, wraparound goggles
- Insulated, cushioned boots, poles, warm hat/gloves, layers for wind
- High-vis orange/red outer layer
- Amber headlamp + small lantern, batteries (ward charge is slow and inconstant)
- Foam earplugs, eye mask for green-grey nights
- Mechanical watch, paper map, physical anchor tags
- Shielded case for electronics and anchor
- First aid for altitude, cuts from silica, dry-eye and nosebleed: bandages, petroleum, saline spray, altitude medication only as prescribed
- Pre-packaged food, ward market credits/barter (the caretakers value intact tools, filters, and patience more than money)
- Sealed waste bags, laundry bag for spore-contaminated clothes

If you have uncontrolled asthma, cardiac disease, severe ear problems, or you cannot moderate your pace for days at altitude, this is a hard place for an ordinary body. It is not impossible - the wards will keep you alive if you let them - but it expects you to arrive humble, watered, masked, and willing to move at plant speed.

Stand, eventually, in a gap halfway between two wards where the moss is thin and you can see the plateau roll away to haze. You will hear the city hum, the root-eels drum, the skin sigh as it cools, and something winged chitter where you cannot see. It will be cold, loud, bright in a dim way, and utterly alive under your feet.

Don't try to master it. Walk it, carefully, on the rootways, and let it remember you kindly.
