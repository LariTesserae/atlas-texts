For an ordinary human, this plateau isn't a hike. It's a week of being wet, cold, slow, and slightly out of sync with cause and effect — on ground that is itself alive, breathing, and not especially interested in you.

It has no trail, no shelter, no hand-made landmark, and no sharp edge to climb. It's broad, rounded, polished by eons of rain and ice into stones like eggs and old teeth. You will walk on plant, not soil: a continuous spongy mat of moss, liverwort, fern, lichen and root-fur, miles across, that squeaks, sighs, remembers your footfall for a minute, and flexes under you. Under it is black water, under that is stone, under that is fermentation. Almost nothing here is solid in the way your body expects solid to be.

Prepare for that, not for a mountain in the ordinary sense.

### 1. Reset your expectations first

You will not get warm. The sun is a pale disc behind a milky, cold sky, staining mist grey-blue. Dim is the default. Your breath fogs, plants bead and drip when it isn't raining, mist rises off bogs and cliffs and your own sleeves. Color stays olive, bone, black water, rust.

You will not get dry. Everything is near the point of becoming water again. The ground gives with a wet squeak anywhere. Cliffs weep in cold curtains, pools lie so still they look solid until you ripple them, streams braid wide and slow through the mat and vanish under it. Water isn't just *on* this place, it's *in* it, just under the surface.

And you will not get company, or emptiness, in the usual pattern. For hours, maybe a quarter-mile at a time, there's nothing taller than your shin — wind combing the mat. Then a sentinel: a single boiler-barked trunk-tree with a slow-turning rosette crown far above you, a boulder entirely swallowed by fruiting vine, a hollow stem arched over a pool, a cluster of ribbed piston-plants thick as towers. Sparse giants in a huge empty wet skin. Then, suddenly, a mile-long band of shaggy grazers comes over a rise, stilt-legged, moss-and-flower-backed, steaming, advancing like weather and shearing the mat to black churn. Then flocks blacken the sky for ten minutes, screaming, and are gone.

This is also a working landscape. Smelter-roots in iron seeps extrude amber resin that runs hot-sweet, smelling of hot oil and copper, then hardens glossy. Distillery bogs swell and contract in slow shifts, exhaling vapor that condenses and runs back in rivulets. Piston-plants on cliffs inflate with a groan and slam shut with a hiss of a gate, keeping a rhythm you feel in your bones if you lie in the mat. After rain — which is to say, always eventually — blue pulses of electrified mycelium run in straight, branching, production-line waves across the surface, sparking where moss is thin. Windward turbine-stalks click and fling white spore plumes like steam.

Don't go to harvest any of it. Don't touch fresh resin, don't camp on a bubbling bog, don't try to use a hollow stem as a bridge over a pool, and don't try to climb a sentinel. It's grown architecture, not built.

### 2. The body: cold is the main danger

Ordinary human failure here isn't altitude or predators. It's hypothermia, immersion, trench foot, exhaustion, and getting tangled in mat that collapses.

Train for ankle strength and balance on soft, uneven ground. Walking a mile here costs double on firm ground because every step sinks, flexes, and breathes back. Expect a slow pace — 1 to 1.5 mph in good conditions is honest.

Acclimate to constant wet-cold. No cotton. No down. Anything that insulates when wet only: synthetic base layers, synthetic mid insulation, a full waterproof shell with hood, spare gloves, spare socks in dry bags, a balaclava. Condensation never stops, so add vapor-barrier socks if you run cold, and accept that you will never be dry, only dry *enough* under the shell.

Feet matter most. You need high, stiff, waterproof boots with ankle support, gaiters, and waterproof over-socks or thigh waders for the braided streams. Polished stones are slippery even when they look soft. Slush holds its shape deep into summer and cracks like a rifle course underfoot — test it with a pole before trusting it. Leave trail runners at home; soggy ankles in bog mat will end your trip in a day.

Carry trekking poles — composite, not metal, for reasons below. Probe ahead where the mat is thin (you'll feel cold stone through it) or suspiciously thick and spongy. You are walking on decayed ancestors upon living descendants. Some patches will rise a foot overnight on gas pressure, crack, flood, and settle flatter. Some look like ground at dawn — a raft of root and moss — and break apart by noon.

### 3. What to pack: everything must survive condensation

Assume your tent, camera, phone, and boots will fog from the inside forever. Pack for mildew, not just rain.

**Core kit:**
- 4-season tent, but pitched on a hummock, not a hollow. Use a groundsheet/disc to spread your weight so you don't sink. Mat gives no reliable stakes — use dead-weight anchors: stones, stuck poles, sandbags of wet gravel. High hummocks are rockier, drier, safer from overnight flooding and gas build-up.
- Bothy/emergency shelter, insulated mat (double layer — cold comes up through wet mat all night), synthetic sleeping bag rated 15-20°F lower than you think.
- Headlamp with spare batteries kept warm on your body, plus a dim red light. Even at midday the light is undecided.
- Paper notebook and pencil — touchscreens die wet, and you'll need to log a world that doesn't keep simultaneity straight.
- Bright clothing: make yourself hunter-orange or turquoise against olive/bone. Mist and repetition erase distance.
- Earplugs. This place is loud in assaults: bogs popping like cannon half a mile off, piston-gates slamming, grazers bellowing up from the ground itself, ice cracking, drips in a root chamber echoing into a drum. You'll want to sleep through them, and not misjudge direction by sound.
- Respirator mask and eye protection. Turbine-stalks fling spores in white plumes, sideways rain stings, black pools are revolting. Don't inhale plumes downwind of the clickers.
- Camp stove, canister kept warm. No firewood — everything is wet and alive, and open flame near bubbling fermentation bogs (cold methane and other gas lifting the mat) is a very bad idea. Likewise, no toilet pits in a bog. Pack out waste; this compost is ancient and doesn't forgive.
- First aid for blisters, trench foot, minor cuts from old stones, and rewarming: chemical heat packs, dry base layer in a zip bag, low-reading thermometer. Hypothermia wrap first, worrying later.

Leave a check-in plan and a hard return window with someone in your home world. Because the terrain moves — channels cut in a day, old ones choke shut — your exit point may not look the same on the way back. Mark your base camp with a physical, non-electronic beacon: flagged poles tied to rock, not mat.

### 4. Water, food, and the “industrial” ground

Do not drink from black pools, iron seeps, fermentation rivulets, or weeping cliffs. They are distilleries and smelters. Smell of loam and ozone in root tunnels, hot oil and copper near resin, sweet rot in distillery bogs — that's not seasoning.

Carry all water, or melt clean snow/ice only if you must and purify it aggressively. The bubbles in bogs are cold fermentation gas, not heat. Stepping on a swelling mat can drop you into a gas pocket that pops, sprays, and folds over. Keep distance from bogs that are visibly heaving, bubbling, or popping.

Eat for cold work. Wind and a sinking mat burn calories fast. Do not forage: sentinels put out mostly hollow seed, young grazers are fewer than old ones, the place is in a late, exhausted stage. Fruiting vines and bright mat plants are not groceries.

Stay off the electrified zones. When blue pulses are running, they travel in straight lines and spark at thin moss. You won't be electrocuted to death by them in insulated boots, but don't lie on wet mat to feel the piston rhythm “through your bones” for long, don't ground a metal tent pole in a pulse line, and don't touch spark junctions with bare hands. It's a warning: the ground is live and wet.

### 5. How to move when nothing repeats

Navigation is the trap. Mist never fully lifts. The horizon repeats — range upon vegetated range, weeping cliff pattern again and again, until distance looks like memory. Some lichen and smelter-root make straight seams, almost grids, across boulders. It's not a map, it's pareidolia stamped by industry.

Compass and altimeter help only partly: iron-rich seeps and electrified roots lie, and the pale sun behind milky sky is a poor reference. Use them, but verify every bearing against physical markers you leave, and take bearings *before* the mist closes. Travel short loops from camp the first day. Learn your hummocks, your sentinel, your root arch. Don't assume a channel you followed yesterday exists today.

The one reliable path is a grazer highway: trampled, re-greener mat where the herd sheared through. It's easier walking, but it may be churned black and slick after rain, and it puts you in the herd's way. If a band is coming — ground trembling, bellowing that seems to rise through your boots — get off the highway, get *up* onto a root terrace, a vine-swallowed boulder, or a dead trunk tunnel. They advance like weather; they will not stop for you, and they'll turn bog to churn in minutes.

Dead trunks become tunnels you must crawl through, smelling of loam and ozone, into drip-lit chambers where water falls in rhythm. Useful as emergency storm shelter, never as a campsite. Channels cut by a single hard rain will run through them.

Storms arrive in minutes: clouds drag their bellies across, rain sideways, then sunless snow, then sudden dripping and ringing. The whole plateau roars and goes quiet. When that happens, stop. Travel in this wind, with water sheeting sideways off cliffs, is how ankles roll on polished stone.

### 6. Reality, thin here — a multiverse traveler's specific warnings

This is where an ordinary human needs discipline more than courage.

- **Reflections lag.** A pool may show a warmer, sunnier day with a horizon that isn't in the sky, and your reflection may lag a breath behind you, looking older, younger, or frost-coated when you're not. Do not step toward it, do not wash your face close to the surface to “check,” do not trust a companion as seen only in water. Verify with voice and touch. Carry a personal anchor — a worn object, a phrase, a wristband from home — and recite it when you feel late for yourself.
- **Echoes arrive early.** You may hear a drip, then a pop, then the groan that caused both. A herd may pass in silence at noon and bellow at dusk from the opposite slope in empty air. Do not navigate by ear to water, herd, or danger. Trust eyes and poles.
- **Shadows misbehave.** Shadows pool under things that cast none. Don't use them for time or direction.
- **Water runs uphill** on strong wind days, confused by pressure and thirsty mat. Don't assume slope equals drainage.
- **The past is the floor.** Dead plant matter remembers being other plants and animals. In a thin-reality place, don't stay long staring at mat, pools, or boulder seams, trying to read what they remember. It thickens. Pay attention to now, to your pole test, to your partner's hand.

In all cases: stop, ground yourself physically, check your paper log, ask your partner what they saw, and move on at walking pace. The plateau is ancient, churned, and coming apart into green at the tempo of decay. You get tired just like its young things do. That shared exhaustion is contagious if you let it be.

### 7. When to turn back

Turn back when socks can't be kept damp-only, when you lose your hummocks in mist twice in an hour, when a bog has visibly risen under your tent spot, when grazer bands are frequent enough that you can't choose ground, or when reflections stop matching the person next to you.

You don't conquer this place. You visit lightly, very small, very late, across a world that is still industriously, turbulently coming apart into itself. Pack like you will be wet for a week, move like the ground may not be there tonight, and listen like sound may arrive before it happens.

If you do that, you'll get the best of it: the slow pulse coming up through the mat at night, the grid of lichen no one stamped, the long empty quarter-miles and the suddenly crowded ones, and the weird comfort of a landscape too old and too busy to be lonely — even when you are the only animal in it that's worrying about staying dry.
