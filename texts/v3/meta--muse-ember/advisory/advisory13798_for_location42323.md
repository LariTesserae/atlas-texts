It is below, and it is bright. That sentence is the whole preparation.

Almost everything you know about going down into a a valley, a cave, a forest, is inverted here. You will go *down* 50 to 80 metres and get hotter, brighter, and stiller. There will be no night, no season, no wind, no rain, no decay, and no change to watch for. You will be the only thing in it that changes - you will sweat, burn, get tired, get hungry, get disoriented - while a leaf stays folded in exactly the same crease for exactly as long as you are gone.

Prepare for that, not for a hike.

### 1. The Three Real Hazards

**1. Overhead white light + greenhouse heat, with no shade and no wind.**
The sun is not slanting. It's high, through a round hole far above the rim, half-closed by leaves. It bleaches everything to yellow-green glare. Your shadow is a small hard black patch under your feet and it does not move. That means no time of day to wait for, no cooler hour, no shadow to rest in.

On the high, thin parts - the raw mineral ridge in the middle, the stone terraces - the air is dry and hot and sweat dries white and papery leaves are pale. In the hollows a few steps away, the same air is damp and does not stir. You will sweat on the ridge and feel clammy in the hollow, and nothing will dry you properly in between because there is no wind at all. Your only wind will be what you bring.

**2. Stasis and disorientation.**
Nothing moves predictably. The buds do not open. The fallen leaves do not fall or rot - they are fresh and colour-bright underfoot forever. The pools do not flow. A single drop condenses on a cool leaf, falls, condenses again on the same leaf, falls again, to a ticking rhythm. Animals, if you ever see one, move so slowly they look like growth.

There are no bird calls, no insect chorus, no wind in the canopy. Sound is swallowed by the root mat. A step is a muffled thump. A brushed stem creaks once, the same way, and holds. After 20-30 minutes your ears will start inventing sounds, your breathing will feel too loud, and you will have no light-change, no bird, no drip-variation to tell you how long you've been there or which way is out.

The green also closes behind you. There is no path. The tunnels at head-height, the tangle at knee-height, the carpet at ankle-height - they all look the same from inside. From the ridge you can see the whole sea of plants, wall to wall, 2 kilometres across to the other side. From inside it, you can see about one leaf away.

**3. Footing that is alive.**
You do not walk on soil. There is no soil - no time for soil, no decay to make it. You walk on a spongy interlocked mat of roots, rhizomes, stems woven through stems, and fresh-looking litter pressed flat. It gives and springs back. It is warm. In places it's tacky with sap, in places fibrous like rope. Bare mineral only shows where the walls rise above the green line, and those walls are sharp-angled, newly split, door-sized facets with no patina, no moss, no rounded pebbles to cushion a fall.

### 2. Before You Descend

Plan a short visit. For an ordinary human, 2 to 4 hours total below is plenty - many people are done in 90 minutes. Do not plan to traverse the basin, do not plan to camp, do not expect to sleep there. There is no night to make sleeping useful, the woven domes on the drier rises are not shelters (one has already collapsed into a ring), and the reed platform over the deepest pool is not a dock.

Go in the morning of *your* world, well hydrated, well fed, and with a hard turnaround time written down somewhere your companion can see. Outside time may not feel like inside time. Bring two time sources - a watch and a phone/clock - and set alarms for water and for turning back. You will not feel duration correctly.

Mark the entry before you go down: the tear in the green overhead and the slant of warm stone. Take a GPS fix from the rim, take a photo looking back up, and note a bearing to a distinctive wall facet. Once you are down, the entry is not obvious from below. The warm stone will be warm on your hands - use gloves or a rope, not bare palms, for the descent/ascent.

Do not go alone if you can avoid it. Stay within voice distance. The plant closes behind you without a sound, and shouting does not carry far in that damp, still air. A companion is your clock, your mirror for sunburn, and your witness that you are, in fact, moving.

Tell someone outside: when you expect to come up, and by when they should call for help at that entry.

### 3. Gear - Pack Light, Cover Completely

Weight is heat. Carry only what solves the inverted cave problem.

**Eyes and skin:**
- Wrap-around Category 3-4 sunglasses. The glare is not just bright sun, it's reflected white light off pale stone and translucent leaves from all sides. Even in the leaf-tunnels your eyes will fatigue.
- Wide-brim hat with a neck flap. No baseball cap - your neck and ears will burn while your face is in shade that barely exists.
- UPF-rated long-sleeve shirt and long pants, light-coloured but not white (sap stains and doesn't hide dirt well, and will show salt). The shirt keeps sap, mites, and sun off you while still being as breathable as possible. Tuck pants into socks. You will be pushing through stems constantly.
- Sunscreen, lip balm, and reapply. Sweat will take it off on the ridges, damp air will make you think you're not burning in the hollows. You are.
- Light gloves. For pushing stems aside, for the warm stone, for not getting sap ground into your skin.

**Feet:**
- Closed, ankle-high boots with a stiff sole and good ankle support, and socks you can change. Thin shoes will feel the warm mat through the sole. Open sandals are a trap - sap, fibrous roots, mites, sharp mineral at the edges, and damp hollows you cannot see.
- Trekking poles. On a spongy, springy mat over warm pools, poles are balance, a probe for root-bridges, and a way to push without putting your full weight and tearing a stem that will never heal.

**Heat and water:**
- Water you bring, and more than you think. At least 1 litre per hour in still heat, plus electrolytes. The black, still, warm pockets in hollows and cupped leaves are *not* drinking water, and they are not for cooling off in. They are stagnant, warm, full of plant compounds and sap. Drinking them or swimming in them is an infection risk.
- Salty snacks. You will lose salt and see it dry white on your arms.
- A small battery fan, and/or a cooling towel. This is the only wind you will have. In the hollows, a fan is a medical device, not a luxury.
- Anti-fog wipes for glasses and camera lenses. Going from a hot dry ridge to a damp hollow will fog everything.

**Navigation and safety:**
- Bright, contrasting outer colour - orange, red, hi-vis yellow. In a yellow-green sea, a person in green or earth tones disappears. You want to be findable from the ridge.
- Compass, but do not trust shadow for direction or time. It will not move.
- Headlamp with a red mode for the darkest leaf tunnels. The contrast between the bleached top and the dark green below is extreme; your eyes will constantly be readjusting.
- Camera with a lens hood and a polarizing/ND filter if you have one. Everything overexposes to pale glare except the hard black shadow under your feet. Expect fogging.
- Whistle. Low shouting gets swallowed. A high whistle carries a bit further over the mat.
- Small first-aid kit: blister care, antiseptic for sap / mite scratches and any cut from stone, tweezers, antihistamine if you are sap-sensitive, and oral rehydration salts. The pale mites and seed-shell beetles you may not even see will ride out on your clothing - check and shake out when you come up.

**What not to bring:** Open flame, a camp stove, bait, anything to cut plants with to "make a path." A cut stem here does not heal, rot, or grow back. It stays cut, exactly as you left it.

### 4. Moving in There

Your first view should be from the low mineral spine. Go down, get to that ridge, sit for five minutes, let your eyes adjust, look across to the opposite wall, along the terraces, find your entry point with your eyes, and pick a small loop: ridge -> one damp hollow -> back to ridge. That is a complete visit.

Keep to the ridges and the drier rises as much as possible. They are hotter but you can see, you can be seen, and your feet are drier. The hollows are cooler-sounding but they are where air sits in your throat, pools are warm and black and mirror-perfect, and footing is spongiest and most disorienting. Do not step into a pool to test it. Do not step onto a root-bridge over a pool unless you have to, and test it with a pole first. The famous slow, mottled, low animal - if you see one at all - will be on such a bridge, moving at a growth-pace. Give it a wide berth. It is not startled, it will not move away, and it belongs to the equilibrium you are visiting, not visiting for.

Push stems gently and let them close. Force them and you will tear young tissue that will remain torn. Expect to be damp, tacky, and a little claustrophobic in the head-height vaults. If you start to feel the quiet pressing - ears ringing, breath too loud, a sense that the forest is unfinished and waiting - that is normal. Talk out loud, quietly. Name things. Count your breaths with your watch, not with the drip. The drip is a metronome that never changes tempo; it will lie to you about time.

Do not eat anything. Do not drink anything except what you brought. The plants feed whatever lives there, or prune it - the distinction is too fine and too slow to use. For a human stomach, crushed green and warm sap is poison until proven otherwise, and nothing here will prove otherwise quickly.

### 5. Mind and Body Limits

You will not acclimate. There is no cooler evening, no breeze to build up to, no shade to learn. Heat will accumulate in your body while the place stays at its perfect birth-instant. Headache, nausea, cramp, confusion, and irrational irritability are your signal to turn back *now*, not at the next terrace. Because the walls are stone and the air is still, heat exhaustion will come on quietly, without the usual cues of a hot day outside.

Likewise, your skin will keep burning even after you are in the tunnels. Check your companion's shoulders, back of neck, and nose every half hour.

Mentally, expect a strange unfinished feeling - scaffolding left open to the sky, a forest at germination enlarged to valley scale, trunks like pillars with fist-tight buds for crowns, ferns stopped mid-curl. It is not hostile. It is indifferent, and perfect. The urge to "finish" it, to mark it, to add a stone to the three-high cairn on the spine, to carve the parallel grooves on the smooth wall further, to leave a message - resist it, and know why:

### 6. Leave No Trace - Here It Is Literal

In most places, leave-no-trace is about recovery. Here recovery does not happen. Nothing erodes, nothing rots, nothing blows away, nothing washes clean. A plastic bottle, a piece of tape on a leaf, a cairn you add, a trail you cut, a campfire scar, a footprint in sap - it will be there, in exactly the same fold of light, for the next visitor, and the one after that, forever.

Pack out everything, including food scraps and sunscreen wipes. Do not add to the cairn. Do not move the balanced stones. Do not sit in the domes. Do not drag the reed platform. Do not touch the grooves and call them writing. They are marks, not messages, made by whoever or whatever left no other trace.

If you take photos, take them and leave. The place has been waiting at its instant of birth longer than you can measure. It does not need your improvement.

### 7. Coming Back Up

Start back well before you think you need to. The ascent is the slant of warm stone you came down, and you will be hotter, tireder, and saltier than when you went down. Sit on the ridge for two minutes before you climb. Rehydrate. Check for mites. Wiggle your toes in your boots.

When you emerge through the tear in the green, the relief you feel is not just from the air outside - it's from motion returning. Wind, birds, a slanting shadow, a leaf that actually falls. Notice it. That is what you were missing, and why you should not try to stay and become continuous with the place, as its builders may have.

Bring your water bottles up empty, your photos full, and nothing from the valley with you except the memory of a drop that was about to fall, and never quite fell anywhere else.

That memory is heavier than it sounds. The place is beautiful because it is complete and young and still. For an ordinary human body, complete and young and still is not a place to live. It is a place to visit briefly, with covered skin, with your own water and wind, with a clock you trust more than the light, and to leave exactly as unchanged as you found it.
