**Tinta**

If you pack for a "small mountain town at 2,600m," you will be miserable within two hours. Pack for a wet, living forest that has learned to build houses in itself, that happens to be at altitude and is always raining in a different way.

Tinta is a bowl 20 minutes around the edge, at maybe 2,600 meters where your breath shows at noon. Houses are woven onto living trees, roofs are planted, ground is ankle-deep felt of moss, liverwort, root and rot, lanes are terraces that become canals by 4pm, and the night frogs make conversation impossible. Nothing there is built to stay dry, stay dead, or stay in place. Every piece of gear and every expectation you bring has to survive that.

Here is how an ordinary human should actually prepare.

### 1. Your body first - altitude, cold, and damp

**Acclimate like it matters, because it does.** Thin, cold air will make carrying a basket up the spiral terraces feel like running uphill. Arrive at the pass slowly if you can, plan a full rest day before you try to be useful in the market, and don't treat the first afternoon rain as a setback - it's the daily weather event.

**Cold is wet cold.** The sun, when a shaft opens for an hour after midday, is hard and white at this height. The shade, the mist at eye-level, and the air after rain are cold enough to chill you through in damp clothes. Temperature drops fast at dusk. Hypothermia here won't look like an arctic storm; it'll be sitting still in a market your poncho has soaked through, listening to looms, getting colder for an hour.

Bring no cotton. No jeans, no cotton hoodie, no cotton socks, no down jacket or down sleeping bag - down dies permanently when it stays damp, and in Tinta *everything* stays damp. Even on the "dry-ish" days, dawn beads every surface: wool, wood, hair, your tent fly. You need wool and synthetics that insulate when wet, and you need to rotate.

Pack for four seasons in one day: misty cold dawn, grey-green cloud-filtered morning, blinding high sun at noon, hard cold rain by early afternoon, dripping for hours after, then frosty dark.

Practically:
- **3-4 pairs wool socks, 2 base layers.** One set is always drying, one is always damp. Wool doesn't stink and doesn't lose warmth.
- **A proper wool poncho or woven poncho like the locals wear, plus a breathable rain shell.** A poncho sheds sideways onto troughs and keeps your legs warm on the steps. A shell alone leaves you cold from below.
- **Warm hat, balaclava or buff, and gloves you can use wet.** You will be handling wet bamboo, wet stone, wet potatoes all day.
- **Sunscreen SPF 50, lip balm, sunglasses, wide-brim hat.** Altitude sun burns through cloud, and you will burn while you think you're in shade.
- **Broken-in waterproof leather boots, not sneakers, not trail runners.** The felt gives like a raft and rolls ankles, the woven-root steps are slick, the lanes fill with ankle-deep water and fish. Add gaiters to keep felt and mud out. Broken-in is non-negotiable - there is no shop in Tinta that sells your size. Bring spare laces and a second pair of insoles to dry out overnight.
- **Flip-flops or rubber sandals for the bathhouse/washhouse.** Communal bathing is central here, and you will want them for wet platforms and hot springs alternating with cold washes.
- **Trekking poles with rubber tips.** Not for the mountain, for the terraces. Slick steps + a basket-load coming down at you is the daily hazard.

**Feet and skin care:** Never go barefoot on the felt, in the drains, or under houses. The felt is alive with animal waste - under the living floors are pigs, guinea pigs, chickens. Wading through canals and settlement drains netted for fish is normal for locals, not for your immune system. Wash feet nightly, treat every cut immediately, bring antiseptic and blister kit. Small cuts in warm, wet, rotting ground go bad fast. Tetanus up to date, and talk to your doctor well in advance about altitude, waterborne illness, and routine vaccines for close animal contact. There are no engines and no wire in Tinta. There is no ambulance, no helicopter, no clinic with refrigeration. Evacuation is you, on foot, over the 2,600m pass, with a mule if you're lucky. Pack as if that's true.

### 2. What to bring, and what absolutely not to bring

Tinta runs on hand tech. What cannot be carried by a person, a mule, or a waterwheel does not come in. Your multiverse gear will not be repaired there, and most of it will be useless or unwanted.

**Bring an analog kit:**
- A mechanical or waterproof watch. Your phone will die of damp, there is no signal in the bowl, and there is nowhere to charge it. Solar chargers are a joke under the afternoon cloud. If you must carry a phone/camera, 3 dry bags inside a dry bag, silica, and assume it will fog. Ask before photographing people, houses, the bathhouse, and rituals - looms in doorways are workplaces, not sets.
- Paper map and laminated notes. Paper books will mildew in days. Laminate anything you need to keep.
- Good steel needle and thread, cord, a sharp folding knife, small repair kit, waterproof matches in a sealed tin, headlamp with red mode (moths crowd oil lamps and will crowd you).
- Water filter *plus* chemical backup. A filter alone will clog with moss sediment; pre-filter through cloth. You will be offered water that's been filtered through moss for a day from a eave trough. Thank them, admire the system, and drink your own purified water. Do not drink from aqueducts, drains, or settling pits.
- Dry bag for passport/documents/money, backpack cover, pack everything as if it will be submerged.
- Small, humble trade goods if you want to give something: steel needles, good cord, soap, salt, matches. Do NOT bring seeds, plants, soil, live animals, or fresh food from another world. Tinta is a closed watershed. One hitchhiking beetle, seed, or pathogen is an invasion. Leave drones, generators, plastic pipes, disposable anything, and loud speakers at home. Nothing is wasted in Tinta, but plastic is not rot - it can't become soil, so it becomes a problem they didn't create.

Pack light: 30-35 liters max. You will be climbing spiraling steps with it, and you will need your hands free to help carry potatoes, oca, squash, and greens down.

### 3. Noise, animals, and sleep

Tinta is louder than people. Waterwheel south, frogs north at dusk, bells, looms, bargaining, hummingbirds at the troughs, bats from granary eaves, shrews and mice in the thatch, dragonflies on the aqueducts, snails in silver lines on every wet wall, beetles under the houses, ducks herded down lanes after rain to eat mosquito larvae, fish netted by children before the afternoon rise.

You cannot get quiet. Bring:
- **Earplugs and an eye mask.** Frogs start at dusk and go until dawn. Rain on 100 thatched roofs is a roar. Walls are woven and breathe - there is no soundproofing, and everyone will hear you as clearly as you hear them.
- **A synthetic sleeping bag rated colder than you expect, plus a liner.** Houses are 2-3 storeys: animals and storage below in warm dark, people in the middle, roof garden above. The smell of pigs and smoke will be in the weave. Nights are cold and misty. Condensation is inevitable. Air everything in the noon sun shaft when it comes.
- Light clothing for sharing close sleeping platforms, and patience for moths around lamps.

Don't be surprised by the anthill energy. The market at noon - waterwheel, looms, pack bells, bargaining, birds, rain starting upslope - is a single roar you have to lean into. If constant noise, damp, and close quarters wear on you, plan a daily walk to the ridge. The bowl is small on purpose; the forest wall is the horizon.

### 4. Food and water - local rules

Eat what's there: tubers, potatoes, oca, squash, beans, greens, maize and tubers ground by the waterwheel, small cold-water fish from the drains, guinea pig and pork if offered. Cookfires are green wood, wet leaves, and dung - sweet, acrid, and low. If you are vegetarian, allergic to fish, or have animal-contact restrictions, say so early and plainly, in simple local language if you can learn it.

Do not try to cook alone on a fuel stove inside a woven house. Smoke, fire risk, and no fuel system for you. Eat with people, help carry, help sort.

Learn a few greetings and how to ask, not assume. Addresses are by flow - upper drain, lower drain, weeping rock - not street numbers. A lane that is a market stall this week is a pond next. The way to navigate is to ask for the bathhouse, the waterwheel, the north frogs, the granary on stone feet, and follow people. Wander at mist-level and you will get lost 200 meters from where you started, which is funny until dark.

### 5. Etiquette for a place that is always rebuilding

Tinta's turbulence is normal. A retaining weave sighs and gives way in night rain, ten people weave a new one by evening and frogs sing in the new hollow. A tree fruits too heavy and falls, becomes a bridge for a week. Rot is not failure; it's how soil is made.

Fit in by being useful, not by being a spectator:
- Help carry, help divert a silted channel, help carry a basket down the steps. Offer hands, hours, and your knife - not money to "fix" things. Repair happens where it lies, with green material worked in beside old. A roof is never replaced, it's patched, layered, replanted.
- Never cut, lean tools against, or climb a living pole. It's a tree holding a house. Never block a sluice, a drain, or an aqueduct joint. Water is not scenery; it's the urban plan.
- Give way to mules with bells, to duck herders, to children carrying fish scoops. Move off the terrace steps when a load is coming down.
- Take all non-organic trash out over the pass with you. Organic waste goes to the felt, bones to the pit, but *your* system is alien. Don't introduce compostable plastics either - they won't rot at the right rate.
- Ask before entering a house, before sitting on a platform, before taking a photo, before helping with a loom. Houses stack, walls breathe, privacy is managed by politeness, not doors.
- Modesty at the communal bathhouse, respect for granaries and settling pits. Wash before entering kitchens and food areas, even if no one asks.

Barter and bargaining take time and leaning-in. Don't rush it, don't flash expensive gear, don't treat weaving, waterwheel work, or terrace soil as "primitive." It's sophisticated watershed engineering that keeps a steep, wet, high bowl alive indefinitely. You are the visitor with the fragile technology.

### 6. Timing and daily plan

Rain 11 months a year, mostly as a convective cloud that builds early afternoon and unloads for an hour, then stops as if cut, with dripping for hours. Plan your day around it:
- **Dawn:** Mist, beaded everything. Shake off, eat, help carry.
- **Late morning:** Market roar, dealings, sun begins to break.
- **Midday shaft:** Dry clothes, charge nothing essential, take the photos you asked permission for, sun is dangerous.
- **Afternoon:** Stay off steep steps and out of lanes if you can. Lanes become canals. Fish appear. Sit under a woven canopy, listen, help indoors.
- **Evening:** Rain stops, mist settles, temperature plunges. Frogs take over sound. Ponchos tight. Smoke hangs low. Use your headlamp, watch for slick steps.

Stay at least 3-4 days. The first day you will be wet, loud, and altitude-sick. The second day you will learn the flow. The third day you will understand why a wall is also a garden and a terrace is also a path, a drain, and a frog habitat at once.

**Final checklist before the pass:**
- Wool/synthetic layers, no cotton, no down, 3 sock sets, boots broken in, gaiters, poles, poncho, warm hat/gloves, sunscreen, sunglasses
- Dry bags x3, headlamp, earplugs, eye mask, synthetic cold-weather sleep system + liner, flip-flops
- Filter + chemicals, antiseptic, blister kit, altitude plan, evacuation plan
- Needle/thread, cord, knife, waterproof matches, laminated notes, paper map
- No seeds, no plants, no drone, no generator, no disposables, minimal electronics, humble trade goods
- Phrases learned, patience packed, expectations lowered: everything will be damp, clothes, documents, mood, and the ground itself

Prepare to be changed by the churn. In Tinta you don't visit a place that stays still long enough to be photographed on a platform. You lend your hands to a town that is always carrying, always rebuilding, always diverting water and replanting. If you can stay warm, stay clean, stay humble, and keep moving with the flow, you'll be welcome - and wet, and happy, and listening to frogs explain why conversation is impossible after dusk.
