This is not a hike, a swim, or a tour of a ruin. Prepare to wade for hours, waist-deep, through a heated, nutrient-thick reservoir that happens to be a factory, a greenhouse, and a mountaintop all at once.

The Works is hamlet-sized — about 200 steps across — but packed wall-to-wall, terraced inward in a ring, with a stepped spire of tanks, chimneys, and greenhouse decks in the centre. The water is everywhere, held and planned. Bath-warm, body-warm, clear enough to cast sharp shadows onto the things breaking the surface, but thickened so ripples die a few inches from where they start. Above the ring wall there is no countryside: air, and far below, a slow sea of cloud. You are high, submerged, and inside a machine that is also overgrown with plants, with no animal life at all — no fish, no insects, no birds. Only water, leaf, machine, and your own slow movement.

Dollar for an ordinary human, the main effects are not exotic monsters. They are heat, wet, sharpness, electricity, disorientation, and slowness.

### 1. How to think about it before you go

Don't treat it as nature. There is no natural ground to stand on anywhere — not stone, not soil, not a bank. Everything you will touch is poured concrete, resin floor, welded steel, glazed tile, glass, rubber cable, enamelled machinery, or black ceramic granules in plastic trays. That means no safe places to sit down in mud, no drinking stream, no forage, no path worn by feet.

Don't treat it as industry either. The plants are not decoration. Leaf panels are wired into the grid and drink light for power. Some housings photosynthesize. Vines have climbed pipes in spirals, tank lids are garden beds, gutters are planters. The water is at once reservoir, coolant, nutrient solution, and mist. It is pumped uphill to the highest terraces and trickles back down in a slow artificial rain. Disturb a pipe and you are disturbing its plumbing, its water supply, and its garden.

And don't treat it as weather. The light is plain midday, white and hard, noon that stays noon. It is humid and warm long after a high place should be cold because the water and engines keep it so. The water level changes by centimeters in a *year*. Nothing will drain or rise while you are there. Nothing will clear up.

### 2. Clothes and footwear — cotton will defeat you

You will be waist-deep the moment you step off the entry platform, and probably waist-deep for most of any visit. Depth will vary between tanks and gutters, so assume it can be higher.

**Bring:**
- **Lightweight thigh or chest waders, not heavy neoprene.** The water is body-warm. You do not need insulation, you need abrasion protection, buoyancy, and quick drying. Thick waders will overheat you.
- **Synthetic, quick-dry layers only.** No jeans, no wool, no leather boots, no cotton socks, no canvas. Cotton once wet stays wet, heavy, and cold when you climb back onto the platform into the high air, where evaporative cooling will hit you fast.
- **A rash-guard or long-sleeve quick-dry top, and long trousers under the waders.** Concrete edges are sharp enough to catch a wrist. Welds are bright silver, enamel is new and glossy but edges are unrounded, leaf edges are crisp as cut plastic, gear teeth stand exposed and oily. Shorts and bare arms invite cuts you won't feel in warm water until later.
- **Sturdy, closed, high wading boots with thick, puncture-resistant soles and real ankle support.** Grooved resin and concrete under clear water is slippery and uneven. Catwalks are submerged and re-emerge without warning. Steps are submerged. Black ceramic granules in trays are grit that will get into a low shoe. Do not go barefoot, in sandals, or in soft trainers.
- **Cut-resistant wading gloves with grip.** You'll want to steady yourself on a tank or rail, and you want that to be safe and not slippery with nutrient film.
- **A wide hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen.** You are high over a cloud sea in unfiltered midday sun, with reflective water below and white work-lights wired under decks. You'll burn while feeling cool and wet, and sweat won't evaporate well in the damp air.
- **A waterproof jacket shell** for artificial rain, condensation dripping steadily from glass and steel, and splashes, even though it isn't "weather."
- **A dry bag for a complete change of clothes, warm dry layer, towel, and extra socks.** The highest decks of the central spire are dry and warm in the sun — that is your only rest stop and changing place besides the entry platform itself. Wet humans on a high shelf chill quickly once out of the water.

### 3. Packing for a slow, wet, silent place

Travel light. Wading in thick water is leg work, and a heavy pack will exhaust you.

- **Your own drinking water and food, in a dry bag.** The water is nutrient solution. Do not drink it, even if thirsty, even if it looks clear. Do not expect to catch fish, find fruit, or eat anything growing there — the lush growth is industrial, wired, fed on factory nutrients, and sterile.
- **Dry bags for everything electronic.** Condensation, mist, and splashes are constant. A phone or camera in a waterproof case, a headlamp for the dark undersides of decks and tanks (daylight is bright, but shadows under the packed machinery are deep), and a laminated map. Paper will be useless.
- **First aid, waterproofed:** antiseptic, waterproof plasters, bandage, tweezers, painkillers, antihistamine and asthma inhaler if you are sensitive to humidity, plant spores, ozone from electric motors, and greenhouse air. The biggest medical risk is a small cut from a sharp edge, a weld, or a leaf, then spending an hour with it in warm nutrient water.
- **A probing stick or trekking pole, short.** To feel for a submerged step, a drop between catwalks, a pipe snaking across the floor under clear water, or a deeper channel between vats. Clear water lies: sharp sunlight through it lets you see the bottom, but depth is deceptive.
- **A whistle and a visible companion if possible.** Sound is taken by the water and arrives muted and late. Ripples die quickly, so your splashing won't carry. You will feel oddly unheard and unseen. Going solo is not recommended for an ordinary traveler — there is a single entry platform, a continuous ring wall, and no other exit. Stay within sight and signal range of your partner.
- **Your own clock and alarm.** Light doesn't change, pumps thud about once a minute, a conveyor advances once an hour, bubbles take a long time to rise. Time will feel stretched and monotonous. Set a hard turn-back time. Do not rely on sunset, hunger, or fatigue cues.

### 4. Moving in the place

Expect to move at a fraction of normal walking speed. 200 steps across on dry land is trivial; waist-deep in thick water, threading between hundreds of identical tanks and columns, it is not.

- Step off the platform slowly and test. That first step is waist-deep and the deck below is grooved and slick. Keep weight on the rail until you are balanced.
- Walk feet-first, small steps, feel ahead. Watch for pipes running like vines along the ground, cables in festoons rooted in planters, coil-vines under the surface, and submerged catwalks that end abruptly. The most likely injury is an ankle roll, a trip over a pipe you can see but forget, or stepping off a catwalk into deep water between tanks.
- Do not climb on tank lids, trays stacked on tanks, or machine housings, even if they look like steps. They are gardens, lids, and live equipment.
- Do not touch machinery. Motors hum under housings, gear teeth are exposed, leaf panels are live electrical plant, some machines assemble and press at slow but relentless speed. The water itself conducts, and warm water plus submerged electrics is not a place to grab for balance.
- Route to orient yourself: walk the outer ring terrace first, then work inward toward the spire. Repetition is extreme — hundreds of identical units — and in uniform midday light you can lose your sense of direction very quickly. The spire is your landmark; the entry platform is your only way out.
- Stay out of the central industrial core except for the dry upper decks. Distillation columns, vats, assemblers, and chimneys are hot, pressurised, and active. A submerged valve releasing a slow bubble is not something to open or lean over.

The highest dry decks are warm in the sun and are the place to rest, eat, dry off, and check your feet. There is no shade elsewhere except under decks, where it is damp and dark.

### 5. Health and hygiene specifics

- **Heat, not cold, is the problem in the water.** Body-warm water prevents you cooling, and humid air prevents sweat evaporating. You can overheat while feeling pleasantly warm. Limit continuous immersion to 30-45 minutes, then get out, sit on the dry decks, drink your own water, and cool off. An ordinary human should plan a first visit as 1-3 hours total, not a day.
- **Skin.** Prolonged immersion will wrinkle and soften skin, and the nutrient film will make you slippery. Rinse as soon as you can at the platform with clean water, dry thoroughly, change out of wet layers, and inspect for cuts, scratches from crisp leaves, and scrapes from concrete and ceramic grit. If you have an open wound, a compromised immune system, or a skin infection, do not enter the water at all.
- **Eyes and lungs.** Don't splash the water into your eyes — it is not sterile in a human sense, it's a nutrient bath. Faint ozone, wet concrete, warm metal, and leaf smells are normal. If you wheeze, sneeze persistently, or feel tight-chested, leave.
- **Altitude.** You are perched far above a cloud sea. Even though it's warm, the air is thinner and the sun stronger than at low altitude. Exertion in waist-deep water will tire you faster. Go slow for the first 15 minutes, especially if you've come from a lower world.
- **No bites, no stings.** There are no insects, fish, or birds. That is a relief, and then it isn't. It means there are no natural sounds to warn you, no scavengers, and no wildlife to make the place feel alive. The silence becomes noticeable after a while — plant, machine, water only.

### 6. Psychological preparation

This is a jungle without a single animal motion. That is psychologically powerful. Ordinary travelers often report after an hour a sense of being invisible, of time dragging, of their own breathing being too loud and then, paradoxically, swallowed by the water. The light does not change. A flower on a vine around a pipe opens over a season.

Prepare for boredom and for a strange, meditative slowness. Bring patience, not entertainment. Don't expect dawn, dusk, weather, or wildlife to structure your visit. If you need stimulation, it's in the detail: shadows through clear water, feathery fronds waving in pump currents underwater, a soft ticking when a frond slides off a housing, leaf panels veined like leaves drinking light.

Don't panic if a sound seems delayed or a ripple doesn't reach the tank you expected. That's normal here.

### 7. Do's and don'ts for the Works itself

**Do:** Carry all trash out, including food wrappers. Nothing rots quickly here — floating paddle-leaves rot very slowly in nutrient water, and your litter would sit in its circulatory system.

**Do:** Keep soaps, sunscreen runoff, fuel, and chemicals out of the water as much as possible. It's a closed loop.

**Don't:** Pick leaves, unplug cables, move trays, open valves, or "help" a plant-module root. You cannot tell which green things are plants and which are power plants.

**Don't:** Feed or bait anything, because there is nothing to feed, and you will contaminate the nutrients.

**Don't:** Leave the entry platform without noting its exact position and confirming your return route. There is no second door.

### A sensible first visit

Arrive rested, hydrated, and acclimated. Step down, feel the warmth, accept you will be wet to the waist immediately. Spend 20 minutes on the outer ring getting your legs, balance, and eye accustomed to clear water over sharp shadows. Work slowly inward, resting on the dry upper spire decks every half hour. Eat and drink your own supplies there, dry your feet, check for cuts. Leave well before you are tired — the climb back onto the platform, wet and with tired legs, through wind at altitude, is the final hazard.

Rinse, change into dry clothes, check your skin and feet, log your time. You will have visited a small, high, entirely new garden of slow industry: fresh paint, sharp edges, lush sterility, warm water under ordinary light. It does not need you to improve it, survive it, or tame it. It needs you to be brief, careful, and clean — and to walk out the way you came in.
