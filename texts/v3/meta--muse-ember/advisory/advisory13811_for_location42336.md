If you think of Vessinlow as a city you can *tour*, prepare to be disappointed for the first few days. It is not a city you cross. You do not arrive, get your bearings, and leave. Going north you are still in Vessinlow after three days. Going down you are still in Vessinlow after you have passed what would have been bedrock. There is no horizon to settle your eyes on, no edge to aim for, no map that will still be true by evening.

It is better to prepare for it as you would for a very large, very warm, very wet *organism* that happens to have streets in it. "Nothing is stable, but everything is settled" is not poetry here. It is the operating principle. If you can accept that, and pack for the body and the mind rather than for sightseeing, you will be fine. If you try to conquer it, you will get lost, wet, sun-slick, and chewed on by midges while a roof-canid watches you from a laundry line.

### Before you go: adjust your expectations

**1. Give up on overview.** You will climb, or try to climb, to a roof, a gantry, a stacked bridge a hundred storeys up. Mostly you won't find an unoccupied climb at all. When you do, the light will be ordinary summer white-gold, casting sharp shadows through seams of sky, and beyond the seams will be only more of it, flat, low, breathing. That vertigo, that sense of distance turning into continuation, does not go away with familiarity. Plan to navigate by the near, the tactile, the local — a wall's wetness, a smell, a sound — not by landmarks in the far distance.

Maps, GPS coordinates, and compass bearings are worse than useless here. The city is grown and re-grown by sub-visible printers drawing matter out of air, water, and dust. A street can close for repair and open three meters to the left by evening because the plan beneath it has dissolved and re-printed. Photograph your corner, describe it in words, tattoo nothing permanent on anything. A wall can stiffen against a surge, soften to let a crowd pass another way, heal a crack before you notice it. Your destination has not moved. The *route* has.

Bring a local guide if you can afford it, even for only the first day. Not for history — for tempo. Locals do not think of "up" and "down" so much as as shifts, and they read the city the way you would read weather off a sky. You will learn to.

**2. Accept that you will never be dry.** The ground is a basin a few meters above the regional water table, tiled over with warm, dark, self-healing basalt polymer. It gives slightly underfoot, holds the day's heat, and weeps. Put your palm on almost any outward surface and it is wet. At dawn the whole mineral skin condenses and runs in slow lines down ceramic scales. By midday some of that water is recycled and exhaled as steam from a vent somewhere else. The cycle never stops.

You do not adapt to 30-degree, saturated air in a day. Give yourself three to five days of low exertion to acclimate. You will sweat while sitting still. Your skin will stay slick. You will lose salt even when you are not moving.

### What to pack, as an ordinary human

Pack for wet, heat, noise, and loss. In Vessinlow, Rule One is never stated but always enforced: *Nothing is wasted. Nothing is empty. Nothing is left alone.* That applies to you. Anything you set down unattended is not litter — it is food, material, or tempo for something else. Bring only what you can carry on your body, and expect to lose anything you can't.

**Clothing:** Quick-dry synthetics only. No cotton, no leather, no wool that stays wet. Loose, but not flapping — you will be brushed, bumped, and squeezed in a crowd that is not an event but a condition. Long sleeves and a high collar help against midge clouds over drains and compost vents and against warm, mineral-tasting rain that can fall in one district while it's clear three districts over. A light hat or head net for markets, where stack-birds ride updrafts snatching midges from the swarms over the food.

Bring at least three changes of socks and underlayers, and rotate them dry. You will get sweat rash, fungal rash, and chafing in places you did not know could chafe. Antifungal powder, barrier cream, and blister care are not optional.

**Footwear:** This is the most important item you own. The polymer is polished by traffic and slick as river stone when beaded with water; the ceramic scales can be sharp; there are ankle-deep service canyons where water is intentionally led, and shallow canals under pavement where fish swim past your boots. You need ankle-high, waterproof boots with aggressive tread, excellent drainage, and a toe that can take a toe-stub on a regrown ledge. Never go barefoot, never go in sandals. Your feet will be wet anyway — choose boots that dry, or that you can bail out.

**Gear:** Everything in sealed dry bags, even inside your pack. Condensation is a weather event, not a risk. Microfiber towel, headlamp (the canyons between high decks and the galleries below ground go genuinely dark), sunglasses for those sharp seams of sun, and a compact water filter or purification tablets.

Do not bring your good water container unsealed. Mineral rain and overhead troughs, wrist-deep with water running to where it's wanted, will fill it with whatever is being borrowed at that moment — fish eggs, fungal spores, runoff protein. Fish swim over your head in clear tubes built into bridges. That is normal. That does not make it drinking water.

Bring ear protection that *doesn't* seal you off. The baseline sound is deafening: water in a million pipes, thermal stacks, construction swarms, braided market cries, the low thrum of transit tubes. Voices do not carry more than a few steps. People speak by touch, by tight-beam, by vibration passed through a shared rail. You want soft plugs that take the roar out of your teeth but let you feel a rail buzz under your palm, and you want to keep your jaw relaxed — the noise will set your teeth on edge if you clench.

Bring power in a waterproof bank. The city powers itself in part off its own noise, vibration, and temperature differences. You will not find plugs, and your electronics will sweat, bead, and short if you leave them exposed at dawn. Keep them sealed. Do not expect a signal. The grown walls are not built for your prior world's towers.

Bring trading calories, not just credits. Stallholders negotiate in a bazaar-scream of overlapping offers. Small, sealed, useful things you can afford to give up — salt, electrolytes, dried protein, a decent filter cartridge — will travel further than a promise to pay later. A gift here is lent, not given; it will be used, and that is considered respect.

And do not bring seeds. Or plants. Or soil. No grass finds purchase on the polymer, no tree roots. A seed that germinates is noticed within hours and cleared, not out of malice but because an unoccupied crevice is waste. Old-world plants exist only in sealed atria as a living archive, tended with reverence and not allowed outside. Trying to plant anything outside will mark you instantly as someone who does not understand the place, and will cost you a fine you could have avoided by leaving your garden at home.

### Moving through it, day to day

**Keep moving, or step fully aside.** Density here is occupied in three dimensions. Housing ligaments braid around transit tubes. Markets overflow into airspace. Bridges are also shops that are also homes that are also water tanks. Up/down is a shift, not a direction. If you stop in a thoroughfare to look, you are not resting; you are blocking tempo. People will flow around you for a while, then they will move you. Learn the small niches at the edge of a flow — a pressure ledge, a market rim, a gantry side — and pause there, brief and ready to rejoin.

Do not try to find an empty roof to sit on. Roofs are streets to the lean, long-legged canids who travel the whole city without touching wet ground, crossing ledge to wire to laundry line while human couriers envy them. They are not pets, not strays, not to be fed. Feeding one changes its route, and it will remember you. Ask before you climb. Most climbs are occupied because they are useful.

**Read the walls, not the sky.** Vessinlow makes its own weather. It can be clear in the sliver you see and raining warm out of a cloud that has never left the city limits three districts over. Locals read how brightly the walls weep, how fast the fungal beds fruit, how loud the eels are in their tubes. After rain, mushrooms push up through cracks — soft, cap-like, smelling faintly of cooked bread and wet iron. They are harvested in baskets by people, and by other things. Do not pick them yourself unless a harvester invites you. Edible and cleaning fungi look alike to you, and a bread smell is not a guarantee. The continuous shallow world under the slabs — white and ochre and sulfur mats knitting the wet mineral together — is doing the city's digestion. It does not need your help, and it does not forgive trampling.

When the walls weep bright and the drain-midges thicken, rain is local and imminent. Cover your water, cover your head, step out from under an overhead trough. When fungal heads are up, the pavements will be slicker than usual and the spores thicker. If you are asthmatic, that is your cue to move up a level, toward a vent outflow where stack-birds nest, or to ask for a room near a sealed atrium rather than a flooded basement that is fished at night.

**Eat what the loop offers, and do not discard.** There is no plant green here in the wild sense. The living green equivalent is fungal, subterranean, opportunistic. Humans eat fungus, birds' eggs, conduit eel that flashes silver in the canals, and the opportunistic rest. A market stall may offer you a cake that smells of bread and iron, a skewered eel, a protein paste made from runoff. Be polite, be curious, be clear about limits. If you cannot eat something, decline before you take it. Do not take food and throw it away. That is the one sure way to offend. Leftovers go to the beds, to the salamanders, to something that is using something else.

Blind wall-salamanders, no larger than your hand, graze fungal film off vertical surfaces in a flicking rhythm that becomes a background pulse. Leave them alone; their tongues are not for petting. Midges will be in your nose and ears over any compost vent — net, sleeves, do not scratch. Eels in overhead tubes are not a show; do not tap the glass.

Drink only treated, sealed water bought from a vendor or purified yourself. The water here is borrowed, not consumed, everywhere else. Do not urinate or defecate outside marked channels. Waste is food, and food is tempo, and an unplanned deposit in a service canyon is a problem someone else will have to clean within the hour.

### People, sound, and the silence

Personal space does not exist. You will be pressed, touched, guided by a hand on your elbow or a tap on a rail. Children navigate by touch; do not grab them, do not block a child's path because you are looking at an eel. Learn a few touch-codes from your guide: a palm flat on a rail for pause, two short taps for "with me," a vibration through a handle to ask passage. Your voice is almost decorative beyond three steps. Tight-beam rentals are available in transit hubs for short-range, directional speech, but touch is faster, cheaper, and warmer.

And listen for the wrong thing. In Vessinlow, silence is not peace. Silence is a failure — a blockage, a place where something has stopped being repurposed. People move *toward* a sudden quiet to see what is wrong. If the roar drops out from under you on a gantry, or the water in the tubes overhead goes still, do not celebrate the rest. Move away from that pocket, follow a local, and let the swarm-tenders sort it out. Gas, a collapsed re-print, a burst cistern can all present as quiet first.

### Health, plainly

You are an ordinary human in a closed, turbulent loop that includes everything, including you. Expect:

- **Heat and salt loss.** Electrolytes daily, not just when you feel dizzy. By the time you feel dizzy here, you have been losing for hours.
- **Skin and lung irritation.** Warm mineral rain, fungal spores after rain, steam from vents. Rinse skin after a soaking, change socks before they stay wet, keep antifungal on standby. If you develop a persistent cough after fruiting, rest up a level and get your filter checked.
- **Waterborne illness.** Never drink from a canal, a trough, a weeping wall, or a flooded basement. Wash hands after touching rails, after handling harvest baskets, after touching your boots.
- **Noise fatigue.** Sleep will be in shifts, in braided housing, with water running past your feet and eels flashing overhead. Bring a sleep mask, plugs as above, and do not expect a quiet room. Ask your lodging if the ground floor is a deliberate fishery — it will be cooler, and it will be noisy and damp in ways that have nothing to do with complaint.

Keep a small first aid kit: plasters, antiseptic, antifungal, antihistamine, electrolyte sachets, any personal medication in a waterproof case with a three-day buffer. There is no pharmacy that will have your world's exact prescription on a shelf that may have re-printed itself that morning.

### A short checklist to leave on

- Quick-dry clothing, 3x socks, hat/net, sunglasses
- Sturdy waterproof drainage boots, broken in
- Dry bags for everything electronic and important
- Headlamp, microfiber towel, water filter
- Earplugs that let vibration through, jaw protector mindset
- Electrolytes, antifungal, barrier cream, personal meds
- Small tradable food/goods you can afford to lose
- No seeds, no plants, no soil
- A guide for Day 1, a notebook for tactile descriptions (not just photos)
- Patience for a place that will not reveal itself from above

Stand still for a moment — if you can find a stillness that lasts, on a warm ledge with water beading past your feet, salamanders grazing the wall beside you, the whole vast weeping expanse continuing without a break — and you will feel what locals feel: this is not a place that contains life. It is a life that has learned to contain places. You do not need to master it to visit it well. You need to be wet with it, move with its tempo, waste nothing, leave nothing empty, and let it use you, briefly and usefully, the way it uses everything else.
