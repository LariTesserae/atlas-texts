Tessik is not a mountain town, and it is not a lake town. That is the first thing to prepare for, because your body will keep expecting one or the other.

You climb expecting cold rock and wind, and you get that — thin air, short breath, sun white-gold on your shoulders even at noon while the air stays cool. Then the rock falls away and there is no shore at all. Just a bowl, eight hundred paces from rim to rim, filled with one to five meters of clear water held against cliff rock like a cupped hand. 360 degrees of water, and the rim is always visible. You never lose your bearings, and you never get land under your feet. Everything stands on, in, or through a living mat of roots and peat and reed-stock six feet thick, which floats and is grounded at once, yields when you press it, bubbles faintly, and holds water inside itself.

Prepare for damp at altitude, slow everything, and almost no solid ground.

### 1. Before the climb: altitude and pace

An ordinary human will feel this first. Clouds are still trapped below you in the valleys, which means you are genuinely high, with no lower town to walk back down to for a night of acclimatizing. Give yourself several days at a lower elevation if you can, climb early in the morning before the sun is hard, and go slower than you think you need to. The climb is not technical, but breathing will come short on the ridge and coming down into the basin will feel strange — the air is clean, thin, and quiet, and the sun is stronger than it feels.

Bring water and electrolytes for the climb, and a real lunch. Once you are in the basin there is no shopfront to stop at. Tessik is settlement-scale but sparse — dozens to hundreds of houses spread over a whole bitterly quiet sea. You can pole an hour and never pass another boat. You will eat as a guest, in households, not in markets.

### 2. What to wear: you will never be dry, and you will be warm and cold at once

This is the most important packing decision. Tessik is permanently damp. Nothing is allowed to dry out, roofs green with moss, thatch sags, piers are stained dark to the waterline, and your own clothes will not dry indoors. Bring synthetic or wool layers, nothing cotton as your main layer, and bring *more* of them than feels reasonable. Two or three changes of socks and underclothes in a waterproof dry bag, not a stuff sack.

Feet matter most. You will not walk on a beach or a road. You will step from a narrow dugout onto a pier, onto a stone hearth-hummock, onto a hummock of mat that rises a hand's breadth above the water and trembles with frogs. Wear boots or shoes that grip when wet, that drain fast, and that you do not mind getting permanently algae-green. They will get wet stepping in and out of boats. Bring a second pair of dry socks for evenings upstairs, and keep them in the dry bag no matter how tired you are. Damp feet, cool air, and no way to dry things is how an ordinary traveler gets a miserable fungal infection in a place that otherwise will not hurt you.

Layers because the sun lies: On a pier at noon you can sit in shirt sleeves and feel the sun warm your shoulders while a wind comes off the ridge and finds every seam. In shade, on water, after rain, you will be cold. Hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen for the glare off water and bright sky — you are high, the sun is white-gold, and midday is when you can see clearly down to the amber-green depths. Do not use midday clarity as a trick to swim. The water is snowmelt-cold, five meters deep in places, and the mat underneath is not a bottom you can stand on. It will give.

For sleeping: a warm, damp-tolerant bag or heavy blanket. Upstairs rooms are dry in the sense of being above the lily line, but they are not heated like a house on land. At night the lamps are rendered fat, the pots simmer on stone islands, and the fish drift past below. It is cozy and it is cold. An insulating pad matters, because the floor — even upstairs — is wood over water.

### 3. How you will move: boats, poles, and not walking

There is no road, though there is a memory of one. Under the water runs a broad causeway of flat, algae-smoothed stones from rim to rim. Boats are poled over it, and a local poler feels for it with the pole through the water. To you it will just feel like a faint resistance.

You do not walk the causeway. You do not try to wade between houses. You do not stand up in a dugout to look over the side. These boats are single logs hollowed by fire and adze — narrow, stable if you sit low and keep your weight centered, tippy if you are not. Learn to sit still, hand the pole back, let the boatman read the water. The drift is barely perceptible, from the snowmelt seep on the north rim to the south, through fish pens and lily meadows. It will feel like the town is barely moving, because it is.

Anything that looks like ground is not, except for two things: piers (learned, leaning, stable in their lean) and the hearth-hummocks — rock piled deliberately above water where pots simmer and smoke rises thin and straight. Those are the only places fire is allowed. Do not make a fire on a pier, on a raft garden, on a floating storehouse, or on the mat itself. Do not smoke on a boat. Do not camp on a hummock unless invited. If you step onto mat to get a frog photograph, you will press it, bubbles will rise, and it will yield under you toward deeper water. Stay on piers, boats, and stone.

It is easy to cross Tessik in an hour. It will take you days to get used to how slow it moves — houses sink inches in a decade and rise when the mat swells, a channel becomes a meadow and a new water-street is poled beside it, a flooded room becomes a fish nursery and later a planter. Don't try to speed it up by dragging a boat over a lily meadow or cutting through a reed bed. The reeds are beds, gardens, and walls.

### 4. Where you will stay and eat

You will most likely be offered the dry room upstairs in someone's woven house. The lowest room is always flooded, quiet and lit from above. Fish drift through the doorway. Old furniture stands under water for a generation. That is not a basement for storage, a bathroom, or an adventure. If you are invited down, go slow, step where you are shown, and do not disturb the fish. If you are not invited, do not peer in. At evening, fish drift past the windows of light — it is beautiful, and it is someone's lived-in ground floor.

Food is fish from pens, things from floating gardens anchored by root, ducks and eggs from raft gardens, and whatever simmers on the hearth-hummocks. There is no metal worth naming — clay pots, bone hooks, flint blades. Bring your own cold snacks for the climb, and expect to eat what is shared. Bring any personal medicine in the dry bag. Do not bring a great steel knife as a gift or a trade good expecting it to be treasure; in a hand-tool town built of living willow, coir, reed, and grown root pillars, metal is not the local measure of value, and a flint or bone tool is handled with a respect an ordinary traveler may underestimate.

Drink: the basin is fed by snowmelt seep, the water over the mat is clear at midday. You will still want to purify drinking water for your own stomach, especially when evening turns it dark green-brown and you can no longer see the mat below. The mat drips and trickles like distant rain inside the ground. It is alive and it is not sterile.

### 5. Weather, light, and sound — the two loud silences

Weather here is patient. Rain comes as a wall crossing the rim out of the cloud below, curtains the basin for an afternoon, and leaves. The water level does not surge; it breathes a finger's width in a season. You will not be flooded out, but you can be rained-in for an afternoon with no dry pavement to stroll. Bring a waterproof shell that fits over your layers, and plan to sit.

Light goes ordinary gold, then flat. Midday is for travel — see the pondweed waving like slow hair between hummocks, see the silver fish flashing under houses. Evening is for staying put. The water turns opaque, the rim reflects, the fires come up, and poling in the dark over an invisible causeway is a skill you don't have yet. Stay on your pier after dark.

Sound is the opposite of what you expect from water. Sound carries whole over water and the mat magnifies it. Frogs by the hundreds start at dusk and do not quite stop. Reeds hiss when wind comes off the ridge. Fish jump with a slap among the houses. Herons call from rooftops. Otters slide down piles and bark. Boats knock against piers, over and over, all night. Children shout across channels and arrive unsoftened, because there is nothing to absorb them. Bring light earplugs for sleep if you need them, but do not use them as an excuse to be unaware — you *are* the loud thing in a very listening place. Your voice will carry across open water to a house you cannot see. Speak as if the whole basin is listening, because it is.

### 6. Wildlife and other residents

Birds own the roofs. Swallows in the eaves, owls in hollow trees that serve as pillars, a heronry in a half-dead alder that is also somebody's wall. Do not use a roof as a lookout, do not shake a thatch for a view, and keep your distance from the heron tree especially. Ducks and grebes nest in the raft gardens — don't tie up against them.

Silver fish move through the streets, eels live in the drowned stonework, crayfish walk the causeway at night. Dragonflies stitch the air above the lilies. None of this is dangerous in the way a predator is, but all of it is vulnerable in the way a nursery is. Don't put your hand in a fish pen to feel the current, don't lift a stone off a hummock, don't step through a frog hummock in the dark. Your weight in the wrong place will do more harm than any animal will do to you.

If an otter slides down a pile near you at night, that is entertainment, not a problem to solve. If a heron startles off a roof with wet beating wings, you have gotten too close.

### 7. Manners that keep you safe

Tessik expects to be exactly as it is, only greener, for a very long time. That confidence is social as well as physical. Conversations will be unhurried. A root pillar takes a childhood to grow thick enough to bear a house — people here do not rush a guest, a meal, or a departure. Mirror that.

Ask before you board a boat, before you step onto a raft storehouse, before you go downstairs, before you sit on a pier. Great empty water between houses is not public parkland; it is meadow, garden, pen, and street at once. Don't anchor your own boat by pushing a pole into the mat and leaving it.

Leave electronics, drones, and anything that beeps or shines brightly in the dry bag. There is no electricity to charge them, no signal to use them, and light and noise carry strangely over water. Bring a paper map, an analogue watch, a notebook. The rim is your compass: north is the snowmelt seep, south is where the slow drift leaves. You will not get lost, but you can get turned around in reed beds at dusk.

### 8. A small checklist

- Dry bag for: clothes change, socks, medicine, sleep insulation, snacks, passport/papers, notebook
- Quick-dry layers + warm layer + waterproof shell, hat, sunglasses, sunscreen
- Grippy, draining footwear + dry evening socks
- Warm sleeping bag/blanket and pad
- Water purification, electrolytes, altitude time
- Light earplugs, headlamp with dim red (turn it off when listening to frogs)
- Patience and low center of gravity in boats

If you prepare for altitude, perpetual damp, boat-only travel, and a town that is loud at night and slow by day — and if you accept that you will be a guest in a living house built of water, root, and woven branch, high in the cold and utterly at home — then Tessik will take care of the rest. You will sit upstairs while fish pass below your lamp, listen to boats knock and frogs begin their chorus, watch fires rise straight in the cool air from their stone islands, and understand why no one here is in a hurry to change anything.
