**VARKESH - How To Prepare As An Ordinary Human**

Varkesh is not a destination you visit so much as a condition you move into. It's high, damp, grey, loud in a low way, and built on rock that is younger than the city on top of it. The mountain is still rising. The city is still accruing - layer over layer, rust over paint over moss over more moss - because nothing here erodes, washes away, or gets cleared.

If you prepare for a normal mountain town, you will be wrong in three important ways: you will be wetter than you expect, sharper than you expect, and slower than you expect. Prepare for those three and most of the rest follows.

### 1. The Mindset: Slowness Is The Weather

There is no rain in the ordinary sense, no road, no season that dries things out. The plateau sits in the cloud rim. Moisture condenses on cold stone, on iron, on wool, on your skin, on the inside of your glasses. Taps drip, roofs drip, the undersides of bridges drip a fine patient rain that does not soak you so much as settle on you until you are damp to the touch all day.

Light does not get brighter as you rise. It gets thinner and more even and more grey. The sun is a pale smear behind cloud, never quite a disc. Below you is a white sea that is not water, only cloud. Above you is the same cloud, closer. At night it is not very different, only darker, with the lamps more distinct.

Sound carries too far and arrives too thickly. Flywheels turn once a minute and you feel them through wet stone. Bells mark shift changes all over the plateau, out of time with each other, and echoes come back before the bells are struck again. Things happen twice, with a delay. Children learn to wait for the second sound before they answer, as politeness. Learn that first. Do not hurry to answer a shout, a bell, a boiler sigh. Wait.

Your ordinary human schedule will not survive here. Expect to walk an hour toward a tower that looks close enough to touch and have it withdraw. Expect a tree fern to look unchanged after a season. Expect to feel ancient and unfinished at the same time. That's not jet lag. That's Varkesh accruing around you.

### 2. Getting Up, Getting Around

There is no road up. You will ascend in a cable car or on a geared railway in slow, patient stages. The wire sings. Your breath shows. The air is cool against your teeth. Take that ascent seriously - it's your acclimatization. Don't rush luggage handling when you arrive. You'll be carrying everything on your back for the rest of the trip.

Once up, Varkesh is not *one* place. It's a region. One district can be miles from the next, separated by empty, luminous plateau of wet stone and moss meadows. Lamps stop, factories stop, and for two hundred meters there is only fog, wet rock, and the sound of your own boots - then another street begins, as if continued by a different hand.

You cannot walk this as a lowland city. Plan in days, not hours, and plan on cableways and the geared rails between districts where they exist. Walking is on hand-ropes stretched between lamps for fog, and on streets that are never quite level because the ground will not hold the same shape twice.

Bring a headlamp even for daytime. In grey, even light with no hard shadows, depth is a lie. Your own lamp gives you a shadow to judge edges by. At midday you will actually have *two* shadows - one from the dim sun above, one upward from the cloud sea below - and birds cast their shadows not on the plateau but on the clouds beneath you. Don't try to use shadows for direction or time. It will only confuse you more.

### 3. What To Wear: Damp Beats Cold

Cold here is cool, not extreme. Damp is merciless.

Forget cotton completely. It will wet out from condensation and stay wet. Build for persistent mist-drip:

* **Merino wool base layers, and a wool mid-layer.** Wool stays warm when its damp, which it will be.
* **An oilskin or waxed cape / hooded shell, not a rain jacket you expect to take off.** The drip from bridges and scaffolds will find the gap between jacket and trousers. A hood matters more than it seems - condensation settles on wool and skin.
* **Hat and ear covering.** Wind comes organized here, cable-wire wind, scaffold wind that makes chords you can hear from the next peak. Ears get cold fast and sound is constant.
* **Gloves for grip, not just warmth.** Hand-ropes, wet iron, copper wire, sharp wire and rails. Your hands will be wet all day, so grip is a safety issue.
* **Boots, not shoes, with ankle support, deep tread, and puncture resistance.** This is the most important piece. The rock has never been softened or rounded. Slabs meet at sharp, fresh angles. There is no soil to cushion you, no worn path to follow, and moss grows over everything in a thick, slippery lawn. A dropped bottle stays sharp and clean for years unless someone picks it up - so will a nail, a shard, a cutting edge, a step. You will trip on edges that lowland weather would have worn smooth a century ago.
* **Gaiters.** Ferns burst from drainpipes, moss meadows are inches high, and you will brush through wet vegetation constantly.
* **Spare socks, in a dry bag inside your pack, and a quick-dry towel.** Rotating dry socks is your main defense against blisters, fungal chafing, and cold feet. Carry a second pair of laces. Carry everything electronic, paper, and food in a waterproof pouch - not just against rain, but against the air itself. Lenses fog, paper won't dry, bread will dampen.

Leave at home: suede, canvas trainers, paper maps you love, loose metal jewelry. Ravens in Varkesh steal bearings while machines are turning. Mice fill grain stores. They will steal or chew anything loose, metal or edible, that you leave unattended.

Washing lines never quite dry. Lodging will be in riveted steel apartment blocks, ten or twenty stories, layered with new brick in front of old brick, scaffolding left in place because it's now a trellis and pigeon house. Your room will be damp. Hang things, use your body heat and the room's to dry socks over a day. Don't expect a dry room to restore you - expect to manage dampness in rotations.

### 4. Water, Food, Hygiene

You will be thirsty and you will not find a river. Varkesh lives on condensation. Copper nets comb mist into troughs, cisterns, and public taps where people fill bottles and carry them home. That water is drinkable at the tap, but it will taste of wet iron and ozone from the generators. Bring a filter or purification tablets, and carry a sturdy metal bottle you can fill at taps. Top it up at every district - there is no guarantee the next district is close.

Do not drink from the black, still pools in sheltered hollows, however inviting. They hold algae and frogs singing loud and wet at night, and blind pale fish circling in the condensation tanks themselves. Those fish mean the tanks are a living system, not a clean source.

Food: Canteens are your anchor. They cook heavy food, and the whole city smells of hot oil and canteen cooking and wet iron. You can also get small alpine apples from walled gardens tucked between casting sheds - ask, don't pick. Moths number in the thousands around every light, so at night the lamps have actual weather around them. Seal all food in an airtight metal tin. Mice, moths, ravens, and goats are all excellent foragers, and in a place where nothing erodes, a dropped lunch tin will be there, perfect, for years - either for you to retrieve or for them to find.

Bring a thermos, calorie-dense snacks, and patience for meals. Everything is slow here, including the looms weaving heavy canvas for condensation nets. Meals will feel like accrual too.

For hygiene, assume rust and mold are normal. Cuts from sharp basalt and granite, from rusty iron, from wire, will happen. Carry a proper first aid kit: antiseptic, bandages, tweezers, anti-fungal cream, blister care. Medical help is district-to-district at cable-car speed; you want to handle a bad cut yourself for hours. Don't touch metal in factory yards in the morning, even if it's covered in moss. Goats graze the yards cropping moss off *hot* metal - it looks innocuous and will burn you. The same applies to slag heaps and warm deep cracks in the stone where uplift hasn't finished cooling. Don't sit, sleep, or store gear in a warm crack.

### 5. Navigation When North Won't Sit Still

Compass needles near the foundries wander - not wildly, but they drift and settle and drift again, as if north is undecided. Don't carry a compass as your primary tool. If you carry one, recalibrate away from foundries and treat it as a suggestion.

People navigate in fog by rope handlines between lamps. Follow them. Sometimes the ropes meet a lamp that is not on any map, burning in empty space above a void, lighting nothing. Do not follow a light just because it's lit. Stay on the line that connects to other lamps and to voices and bells.

Trust locals over your eyes for distance. On a clear damp day a tower two kilometers off looks close enough to touch. Double your time estimate. If you are lost in an empty stretch, stay on the lamp line, listen for a flywheel or a bell - sound carries - and wait for the second sound. Someone will come along the rope.

Take waterproof notebook and graphite pencil, not ink. Get a local map if offered, but know newer lamps, new scaffolds, and new slabs rising a centimeter at night with a tiny deep click under your feet will not be on it. History layers here; a mistake in street alignment persists for decades with grass growing through it and people walking around it. Don't try to correct it.

### 6. Animals, Machines, and Not Falling

The fauna is louder and more numerous than the people, and they have seniority.

Swifts pour from vent stacks at dusk in black curtains, screaming. Long-eared bats flicker in lamp halos. Ravens nest inside active cranes, building twigs around machinery. A mountain fox will cross an empty district at noon as if it owned the street, which in a practical sense it does. Do not approach, feed, or block a fox. Give goats in factory yards a wide berth.

Cranes stand over rooftops not lifting, only holding, hooks swaying a fraction in the wind. Never walk under a hook, even if it's been stationary for years. Rails loop around districts at different levels, never quite level. Flywheels, winches, stamp falls, boilers that sigh - you will feel them in your feet. Stay off machinery, off conveyor bridges, off scaffolds that have become trellises. They are not yours to climb for a view.

And remember the fossil rule: new rock slowly forms around slag heaps, trapping a wrench, a boot, a dropped lunch tin in a thin skin of stone. If you put something down in a yard or on a slab and forget it, the mountain may keep it. Pack out everything, every time. Nothing erodes, so litter and lost objects are permanent. This is also a multiverse courtesy: clean your boots thoroughly before you return. Varkesh moss, fern spores, and condensation-tank organisms have never been cleared, storm-torn, or scoured - they are very good at accruing on things that leave.

### 7. Sleep and Senses

Night is not quiet and not dark. Districts become a constellation of points across a black wet plain, each haloed in fog. Flywheels keep turning. Frogs sing in pools, swifts scream at moths somewhere, a goat steps carefully between slabs that have never been rounded.

If you are a light sleeper, bring earplugs - but know you'll need to wake for shift bells. Bells across the plateau are out of time with each other. Don't set your watch by them. Bring an eye shade - lamps are always-on and moths swirl around them all night.

Your eyes will tire in grey light. Your ears will tire in thick sound. Your nose will adjust to wet iron, hot oil, ozone, and cooking. Give yourself a day of just being there before you try to do anything ambitious. Watch the children waiting for the second sound. Imitate them. It is good manners and good survival.

### Packing Checklist For An Ordinary Human

**On your person:** Headlamp + spare batteries, waterproof phone/electronics pouch, small dry bag for socks, gloves, hat, water bottle, filter, blister/first aid kit, waterproof notebook + pencil, earplugs, eye shade, metal food tin, thermos.

**In your pack:** Wool layers + oilskin shell, gaiters, spare socks and laces (2x what you think), quick-dry towel, anti-fungal cream, tweezers, cable-car/rail ticket in oilskin pouch.

**Leave behind:** Cotton, suede, paper books, loose metal trinkets, light-colored suede boots, expectations of a dry day, a sunny day, a short walk, or a quiet night.

Varkesh is not finished, and it is not falling down. It is accruing. Go prepared to accrue with it: slower, damper, sharper-shod, and listening twice.
