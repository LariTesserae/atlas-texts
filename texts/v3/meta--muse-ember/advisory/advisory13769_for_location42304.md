# Preparing to Visit the Town Under the Hummock

The most important preparation is not equipment. It is understanding what you are entering.

This is not a cave, a ruin, a temple, or a natural curiosity. It is a complete, inhabited town, compressed into a single room. It has houses, stores, gardens, waterworks, streets, bridges, and people, all built into the inside of a low dome of earth and ice. From outside, you will probably walk past it.

You do not go there to explore a wilderness. You go, quietly and briefly, into someone’s home — a home that has reached a state of perfect, precarious balance. A pot set down rather than dropped. A birch pruned as fast as it grows. A pool that neither rises nor falls. Berries ripe, green, and in flower on the same shrub. Everything there is old because nothing has been allowed to be new, sharp, or out of place for a very long time.

An ordinary human traveler, without extraordinary powers, immunity to disease, or a built-in translator, will be unusually conspicuous there: warm, noisy, mobile, chemically scented, and likely to leave traces. Prepare accordingly.

## 1. Decide whether you should go at all

There is no sign, gatehouse, ticket office, or visitor center. The entrance is a hanging curtain of moss and roots, two low rounded steps, and a stoop. If you have not been welcomed — by gesture, invitation, or some understanding arranged beforehand — do not part that curtain simply because you found it.

Do not bring gifts of metal, tools, coins, cloth of synthetic material, alcohol, tobacco, weapons, or any marvel of your own world. The inhabitants use stone, bone, wood, and fiber. A steel knife will not read as useful. It will read as bright, sharp, alien, and possibly dangerous. Their infrastructure is already complete: carved channels, cisterns, compost heat, woven sledge-mats, root bridges. They do not need your technology, and a single foreign object left on a mat can remain there, frost-dusted, for “season after season.”

Do not bring a companion animal, a noisy child who cannot yet be quiet, or a large group. The chamber is no larger than a village square, and already contains a whole town. One quiet visitor is an event. Five are a crowd pressing into someone’s streets.

If you are ill — a cold, a cough, a stomach bug, a skin infection — postpone the visit. They have flint and bone tools, no metal medicine, and no reason to have resistance to your germs. You could sneeze your way through a town. In reverse, moss, algae, compost, bird-nesting dust, and damp cold soil can make a visitor sick and leave seeds and spores on your boots. Multiverse travel requires biosecurity in both directions.

Also assess your body honestly. You must stoop at the entrance, descend two steps, pass through narrow gaps shoulder-to-shoulder between houses, walk on paths worn concave by feet, and possibly duck under root-bridges at chest height. Your shoulders, back, and knees will be used in ways that have never been designed for a laden outsider. If you cannot crouch, move slowly, and keep your hands visible, you are not ready.

## 2. Prepare for cold without warmth

Outside, the light lies. It is bright, hard, far-northern light that glares on snow and wet stone but gives no heat. The air tastes of metal and rain that never quite falls. Mist condenses on skin and low plants. The ground is cold and damp. You can get snow-blind and sunburned while freezing.

Dress for that plain first, and for the dome second.

Wear layers of pale, natural fiber — wool, linen, woven plant fiber. Avoid bright synthetic colors. Everything inside is saturated green, silvered wood, pale ice, and soft blue. A neon jacket will be visible to every person, bird, and frog in the room, and will mark you as a disturbance.

Bring UV protection for the approach: a wide hat, covering for your neck and face, and eye protection against glare. Remove the heavy outer layer before entering, if you can do so without a flurry of zippers. Inside is only a few degrees warmer than outside, warmed by compost beds and the enclosure, but your breath still fogs. Frost still survives year-round in the corners like white lace.

Fabrics will dampen and not dry. Bring a dry change of socks and a spare underlayer sealed in a waterproof bag to put on *after* you leave, not inside. Boots need thin, soft, quiet soles. No cleats, no squeaking rubber, no hard heels, no Velcro, no jingling buckles, no loose keys. The paths are polished cobbles set in earth; the ice wall is polished by centuries of hands. Victors’ boots will slip, squeak, and tear sledge-mats.

Leave your phone, camera, recorder, light, and radio off and deeply stored. Inside, there are no strong shadows. Light pours down filtered through the crown of the dome and bounces off the pool, living and diffuse, as if from within paper. A flashlight would be a harsh, wrong thing. A shutter click would be one of the loudest sounds in the room. A phone alarm, a buzzing backpack, a camera motor — all of these violate the silence that the place is built around. If you must make notes, do so *outside*, or use a small pencil notebook afterward. Paper will dampen; pencil will handle it better than ink.

Do not bring perfume, scented soap, sunscreen spray, insect repellent, or strongly flavored food. The air inside smells of cold clay, crushed green leaf, peat, wet stone, and moss. Your body will be an intruder in that smell, and everyone can notice it in the still air.

## 3. Learn to be quiet before you arrive

Inside, almost nothing makes the sound it would make elsewhere.

Flies hover without buzzing — too cold to hum. Frogs sit fat in the pool and do not croak. Birds watch from the birch crown and house eaves and do not call. Snails graze algae on the ice. Even the drip from the dome is caught in cups and channels before it can strike anything hard.

People compensate. They speak in breath, too quiet to carry, and mostly with their hands: quick, precise movements in the bright, shadowless air. Hammers are struck on moss pads. Pots are set down. Mats are let fall, not shut.

You, by contrast, will be loud merely by existing. Your stomach will growl. Your breathing will fog. Your coat will rustle. Your zipper will sing. Your shoes will thud on the moss that deadens everyone else’s steps.

Practice before you go. Spend an hour moving through your own home in socks, in silence, doing ordinary tasks slowly. Practice keeping your hands visible and relaxed, not in pockets. Practice chewing quietly and swallowing without a crinkle of packaging. Pack food in a cloth wrap, not foil. Put medicines in a padded, non-rattling container, not a blister pack that clicks.

Eat before you enter. Do not bring crumbly, crunchy, aromatic food inside. Do not eat cloudberries, even though they will be ripe, green, and blossoming beside one another. Foraging there — however tempting — is theft from a balanced garden. Do not pick saxifrage, moss, fern, birch leaves, or herbs hanging to dry. They are food, medicine, roofing, and building material.

And do not use your own water. The town has no river. Water arrives as condensation on the cold upper dome, beads, gathers, and rolls down carved channels to the central pool, gardens, and cisterns. It is drunk by roots and rises again. That closed cycle is their entire water supply. Do not pour your bottle into a channel. Do not wash your hands in the pool. Do not relieve yourself in the pool, a cistern, a compost bed, or a moss wall. Bring your own sealed drinking water and a pack-out bag for waste, and use them outside on the plain, well away from the hummock, if the hosts have not shown you a proper place.

## 4. Learn the language of hands

You will not be understood by voice, and shouting, even politely, will be a shock. The inhabitants watch hands first. Bright light with no shadows makes hands unusually legible.

Learn a few gestures beforehand if you can: *hello, please, wait, thank you, sorry, I do not understand, may I pass, may I sit, I will go.* Move slowly. Keep palms open and empty. Avoid pointing — a finger may mean something else, or nothing they use. Avoid thumbs-up, waving, beckoning, and other gestures from your world whose meaning may not travel.

Do not try to teach them your sign language in return. Speak, if you must, in a breathy whisper close to the person, and expect that they may not answer with voice. Above all, do not sing, chant, pray aloud, clap, or attempt to make music. The birds nest above you. They are watching with bright eyes. Silence is their courtesy, and it will be yours.

## 5. Find the place without damaging it

The plain will disorient you. It is flat, pale, evenly spread to the horizon, with no trees, no hills, no landmarks. The dome is only a low green swell, perhaps thirty paces wide, no higher than a person, covered in moss, low shrubs, and white saxifrage. The flowers may appear to tremble although there is no wind.

Walk gently on the plain and do not climb on top of the hummock to get a better look. Under the moss is packed earth and old clear ice — a roof. Your weight, your digging, your trampling could crack its outer skin. Approach from the low side where the moss and roots hang as a curtain. Do not tear it. Part it, pass through, and let it fall exactly behind you.

Mark your route out in your head before you go in. The light inside never changes, so you cannot tell time by it. Your watch may keep moving, but the town will not. Write down — on paper you will keep in your outer pocket, not inside — when you entered, and decide beforehand how long you will stay. It will feel both like five minutes and like forever. People who have left tools there find them unchanged, season after season. Do not let that feeling of stillness turn into an unplanned overnight stay.

Do not take a souvenir, even a small one: no moss, no ice chip, no driftwood, no flower, no berry, no root, no pottery shard, no feather. Bring nothing in, leave nothing. Count your belongings as you exit.

## 6. Inside: how to move through a town in one room

After the two steps, the world turns inside out. Instead of darkness, there is more light. The dome glows. Greens are luminous, wood is silvered, stone is rounded by touch. Everything is damp to the touch and nothing is wet enough to run.

This will feel uncanny. You can see an entire urban world at once — terraced houses built into the ice wall, three and four deep, root-bridges above, gardens clinging to the curve of the ceiling, cellars below, laundry and herbs drying overhead — yet you will never fully see it, because every surface is covered by another surface, another life. Do not stare into alcove houses, cellars, or root gardens as if into shop windows. These are homes. Ask by gesture before looking closely, and move on.

Stay on the worn paths: cobbled where feet fall, or woven sledge-mats laid to keep the earth from mud. Do not walk on moss carpets. They deaden steps, hold moisture, and protect the dome and floors. Do not step on sledge-mats except as a path. Do not step on the birch’s spreading roots, which run across the packed earth floor like a river delta to the central pool. The trunk is pale as bone, twisted, and its crown is held against the roof by pruning. Do not touch it, climb it, pick it, or lean your pack against it.

Go slowly. The streets are the gaps left between houses, no wider than a person. If someone is coming, stop, turn sideways, and let them pass. Do not squeeze between walls with your backpack scraping clay and turf. Keep your pack small — a basket-sized load, not a framed rucksack. Doorways are rounded where shoulders have passed for a hundred years. Overhead, laundry and weaving hang in still air; brushing them with your shoulder could knock herbs into dust or stir mold. Root-bridges are at a man’s chest height; use them only if invited, and do not assume every ledge is a public walkway.

The central pool is not a fountain. Frogs sit half-submerged. Tiny stickleback fish turn among the roots. Smaller pocket pools hold more of the same. If you drop something in, ask; do not wade in after it. The ice wall is not a climbing wall. It is polished, fluted, veined — meltwater channels that once ran and stopped. Do not touch the frost lace in the corners to see if it melts. Do not lean against houses to rest. Their walls are turf, woven mats, and thick clay daubing, lashed driftwood and root-fiber. Everything has slumped into its final place.

Expect to be watched constantly, from the terraces above, the bridges, the eaves, and the birch crown. You will be visible in that shadowless light, and your every hand movement will be read. That is not hostility. It is how a quiet, crowded town watches a stranger. Let them.

## 7. Mind and body inside the stillness

The light does not rise or fall. It is always the same bright, diffuse morning. There is no night. There is no season. A single shrub will be in blossom, half-ripe, and ripe at once. This will unsettle your sense of time, fatigue, and seasons for sleep. Do not expect to be shown a communal long-house bed, a private room, or a morning departure. If you are invited to stay, ask by gesture where visitors sleep, and do not lie on a mat, in a cellar, or on a terrace roof unless shown.

Your heat and breath add moisture to the air. A long stay literally changes their water cycle. Keep visits short. If you feel dizzy from the glowing dome, the layered town, or the close, damp air — from the compost warmth, the peat, the crushed leaf, the wet stone — sit where invited, breathe slowly, and leave.

Do not try to “complete” anything. Do not rearrange a mat, sweep moss from a path, prune the birch, replant a garden, or repair a channel. The town has been pruning, clearing, and re-clearing its paths to exactly the same shapes for a very long time. Moss will grow over a path and it will be cleared again. Your help will be an interruption.

## 8. Leaving

Reverse everything you did to enter, more slowly.

Part the moss curtain, let it fall, step up the two rounded steps, and stoop into the glaring cold. The plain will look exactly as it did before. The hummock will look like only a bump. That is the point. Do not linger on the roof.

Outside, check your boots and clothing for moss, seeds, snails, and damp soil. Brush them off on the plain away from the dome, seal waste, change into dry socks, and do not spread algae or soil spores to your next world. Wash your hands. Your clothes will remain damp; that is normal. The air tastes of metal again. Your own breathing, your stomach, your footsteps will seem shockingly loud in the absolute flatness.

Do not assume you were gone only a short time because nothing inside has changed. Check real time. Nothing inside changes — that is not a promise that you have not.

### A short checklist to carry in your head

- **Welcomed?** If not, admire the hummock from a distance and leave.
- **Healthy, quiet, small?** One or two people, no pets, no instruments, no fire, no smoking.
- **Clothed in pale natural layers, soft quiet boots, no metal, no scent, no electronics on.**
- **Hands visible, gestures slow, voice in breath or not at all.**
- **Pack small, food eaten outside, water sealed, waste packed out.**
- **On paths only. Off moss, off roots, off roofs, out of the pool, out of cellars unless invited.**
- **No touching, no picking, no souvenirs, no rearranging.**
- **Timed entry, short stay, curtain replaced, nothing left behind.**

Go in expecting less darkness and more light, less noise and more attention, less room and more town than you imagined. Be a person who can be silent, damp, cold, and still — and the place may let you remain a guest for a while, glowing green and quiet under its roof.
