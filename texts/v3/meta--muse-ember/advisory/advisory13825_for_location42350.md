If you are an ordinary human, The Sear Vault will not try to eat you. Everything in it is busy eating rock. That doesn't make it safe. It will try to dry you out, cook you if you wander, blind you a little, silence you completely, and make you lose track of time — all without ever touching you.

Think of it less as a cave to explore and more as a small, sealed, living furnace you are allowed to walk through briefly, on its terms. Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Before you go: get your body ready

**Altitude first, heat second.** The entrance is a narrow crack near a summit, where the air is already thin, fast, and cold. The Vault itself is warm. That double demand — low oxygen *and* heat — is what will undo you. Spend at least a couple of days acclimatizing to that altitude if you can. Do not arrive from sea level and go straight in.

**Hydrate outside, not inside.** This is the biggest trap. There has never been water in the Vault — no rain, no seep, no condensation. Any moisture you carry in evaporates in minutes. Worse, your own sweat evaporates so fast in the hot, dry air that you will *feel* like you're not sweating while you are quietly losing liters. Pre-hydrate thoroughly on the approach, carry a small sealed bottle for sips inside, and plan on rehydrating properly once you are out. Do not plan to find water, forage, or rest inside.

**Short visit only.** Almost nobody should sleep here, and an ordinary first visit should be 20-40 minutes in and out. Time perception collapses in that dark, quiet, heat-cycling room. Set a silent vibrating timer on your wrist for your turnaround time *before* you enter, and agree with your companion that the timer, not your feeling of "just a bit longer," is the boss.

Leave a beacon, marker, or filed itinerary at the entrance with your exit time. Rock swallows sound and signal. If something goes wrong, rescue is not going to be quick.

### 2. What to pack

You need to be sealed, insulated, and dim.

**Feet:** The most important thing you own in there. Thick-soled, heat-resistant, closed boots with the seams taped or gaiters pulled tight. The center floor is too hot for bare feet, and even on the cooler rim you will be stepping on a living pavement. The ash is flour-fine, unsorted by water, and gets *everywhere* — in boots, in teeth, on your tongue. Sealed boots keep it, and the pavers' hot enzymatic film, off your skin. No sandals, no open shoes, ever.

**Body:** Cover skin. A light long layer top and bottom, a scarf or balaclava you can pull over nose and mouth, goggles or close-fitting glasses. This is not for modesty — it slows evaporative water loss, shafts the fine ash off your eyes and lungs, and keeps your body heat signature a little less obvious. You will be cold on the throat on the way in and hot once you're inside, so layer accordingly.

**Breathing:** A simple P100 / high-particulate mask if you have one. The creatures breathe thin, sulphurous vapour rising from the floor, and the air is always full of that baked dust. Breathing through your nose and not talking too much helps. Don't eat inside — your mouth will be full of ash.

**Light:** A dim, red-filtered headlamp, with a low setting and a way to kill it instantly. The Vault's only natural light is a dull red seep from fissures, like embers under ash. It shows silhouettes at arm's length. A bright white light will destroy your night adaptation, startle nothing in particular (the animals don't have eyes), but make it much harder for *you* to see. Bring red, use it sparingly, prefer to let your eyes adapt.

**Water and waste:** A small sealed bottle, just for sips. No open cups. No food crumbs. And a small ziplock for waste. This is a perfect closed system — rock to paver to hearthback to ribbon to rock, nothing leaves, nothing is wasted. Do not add to it. No peeing, no spitting, no dropping a wrapper, no leaving a tissue. Everything you carry in, including your body's waste, comes out with you.

**Hands:** Light gloves are useful not because the walls are sharp — nothing here is sharp, everything is worn smooth — but to remind you *not to touch*. Hearthback domes are glass and hot, and fissures spit tiny glass beads when the stone ticks.

Do not bring plants, seeds, seeds on your clothes, pets, or anything green. There is no plant life here, and never has been. A dead "frost-fern" is just silica and iron dendrites. Leave living things outside.

### 3. How to move in there

Enter slowly down the throat. Let your eyes adjust from the bright grey summit light to the red dark. Let the heat adjust too — the first blast will feel worse than it is.

Once inside, **stay on the cooler rim along the wall**. Temperature is everything here, like water is elsewhere. Near walls and roof it's only uncomfortably warm; toward the center it's a shimmering bake. Never cut straight across the middle to save time. The long way around the edge is the safe way.

**Step between animals, not on them.** The floor is almost entirely pavers, palm-sized hexagons locked edge to edge. They *will* yield, but slowly and reluctantly, rippling in a circle around your boot and closing behind you. Do not stomp, run, or shuffle. Place your foot, wait for the ripple to settle, then step again. If you rush you'll get a foot caught as they close, or lose your balance.

**Go wide around hearthbacks.** Those metre-wide glass turtles move maybe once an hour and do not yield at all. Step around them with clearance. Don't touch their ticking domes — they're hot, and a warm, still human shape sitting on the floor is exactly the heat signature a ribbon is feeling for.

**Watch the ribbons, but don't fear them.** The long, translucent, legless hunters flow through cracks and withdraw with a soft sigh when they sense you. They hunt hearthbacks by heat, not people. The risk is a ribbon investigating a warm boot or a pack you've set down. Don't sit down on the floor. Don't place your pack down. Keep moving at a slow, steady pace, and give cracks distance.

**Stand still sometimes.** The place is profoundly quiet. The creatures communicate by warming a patch of shell a fraction of a degree and pressing the stone — felt in the floor, not heard. What you *will* hear if you stop is the dry shingle of pavers shifting, a soft hiss of gas from a wall pore, the dull knock of a ribbon's kill somewhere in the pavement, and, very distantly, summit wind through the far fissure. That silence is normal. Don't fill it by shouting; the basalt swallows it and it leaves you feeling more alone.

Light signals and touch taps work better than talking loudly.

### 4. The heat-tide, and your abort signals

Every few hours the heat-tide turns: fissures brighten, the center warms, the pavers ripple outward, hearthbacks inch toward the deep, ribbons rise. Then it dims and reverses. It feels like the whole floor exhaling under you.

Treat brightening and warming as your cue to head back, even if your timer hasn't gone off. Note the spots where the roof is low as a man's head — duck without touching the ceiling pavement, which will periodically rain down a dry, silent snowfall of shed plates. They never melt. Glints will swarm to eat them before they land. Just walk through it, mask on, eyes down.

There are only two mouths, and the chamber is bounded on all sides. You cannot get lost in the map sense. You can get disoriented, overheated, and dehydrated, which feels the same.

Turn back immediately — not at the next hearthback, now — for dizziness, nausea, headache, confusion, a racing heart that won't settle, or skin that feels suddenly hot and very dry. In thin air plus heat, those signs come fast and you won't sweat your way out of them.

On your way out, that far high fissure is not a shortcut. It's a thin leak of grey light that dies almost at once. Use the throat you came in by. You know its slope and its coolness.

### 5. Head and etiquette

Stay long enough and the Vault imposes its own rhythm, which is not yours. The red light pulses like a sleeping heart. Above you, upside down, a second pavement grazes stone that has never seen a sky. Nothing about it is fast, loud, green, or wet. Ordinary humans find that unsettling — the feeling of walking on something alive that is indifferent to you — and then, after a while, strangely boring. Both reactions are normal. Boredom is when people start touching things.

Don't.

Don't climb the seven waist-high nursery domes, cool and hollow in the pavement. Don't pry at the half-ring of huge slabs — too smooth and regular to be natural, too weathered to read, built by someone and now completely abandoned and paved over. The pavers have colonized its base. Admire it from where you stand. Don't collect a glass bead, a shed plate, a dendrite. In a closed world, one souvenir is an ecological removal and a contamination risk on the way out.

Don't bring Vault back with you, either. Brush off your boots and clothes *outside* the entrance, in the wind, and seal the ash powder in a bag if you can. Your gear will be coated anyway. That's fine. Just don't carry it to the next world.

### A simple checklist to tape to your pack

- Altitude-acclimatized, pre-hydrated, timer set, exit filed
- Sealed boots, covered skin, goggles, scarf/mask
- Dim red lamp, kill switch
- Small sealed water bottle, waste bag, no food
- Stay on cool wall route, step between pavers, go around hearthbacks
- Sit never, touch never, leave nothing
- Brightening heat = leave
- Rehydrate, cool, and de-dust outside

Go in quiet, stay short, move slow, come out the way you came. The Vault will still be there, breathing at its own pace, when you've gone — a small, hot, dark, full world that does not need you to stay alive.
