Pack for visiting a body, not for exploring a cave.

That room under the frozen ground is not a cave with plants in it. It's a plant that has hollowed out a room to live in, lit by its own roots, watered from above, crowded with animals that are half plant themselves. It is only twenty steps across, five or six feet high at the crown and sinking to a wet seam at the edges, but it is dense enough that moving is like pushing through a crowd that is also alive under your feet.

An ordinary human won't get hurt there by a fall or a predator. You'll get hurt by getting cold, wet, stuck, infected, and by the damage you do just by being too heavy, too hot, too smelly, too bright, and too loud for that small a space. Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Before you go down

**Go in with one person at a time, and ideally not at all unless you have a companion on the surface.** The chamber is already crowded with slugs, blind beetles under the rosettes, worms in the floor, frogs in the fingernail-deep pools, shrews darting through the mat. You are the extra animal. Two or three visiting humans would fill it and trample it.

Tell your surface companion: when you expect to come back, what to do if you don't. A timer outside is essential. Inside, with no sky, no path, drips drumming on leaves in a resonant room, time and direction go soft. Twenty steps can feel like an hour, and you can forget you've been lying in wet cold for twenty minutes.

**Check your body first.** This is not a place for an open cut, a bad cold, a recent allergy flare, or a weak ankle. The air is full of midges thick enough to breathe at the edge of your mouth, fungal spores from leaf-mold that composts in days, and water that is not drinking water - it's plant water, with sugars, salts, antifreeze compounds, and microbes. Cover any small cut with a waterproof dressing, however minor. If you know you react badly to mold, pollen, or crushed green plant smell, take your usual antihistamine *before* you go down, and carry medication with you - but keep the bulk of first aid up top, you don't have room for it below.

Eat lightly, hydrate, and empty your bladder beforehand. There is nothing inside to eat, drink, or relieve yourself on without poisoning the felt. Food smell, crumbs, sweat, lotion, perfume, sunscreen, and especially insect repellent are foreign substances to this place. DEET and similar chemicals will damage the plants and animals you came to see. Don't wear them. Use clothing and a veil instead.

Practice the squeeze at home. The way down is not a path. It's a fissure no wider than shoulders where frozen peat and silt have parted and roots form a curtain. You go through sideways, exhaling, with no pack on your back. Anything bulky - a backpack, a camera rig, a thick coat, a portal device that hums, heats, or projects light - will widen the fissure, tear roots, and get you stuck.

### 2. What to wear, what to carry, what to leave above

Think wet cold, not winter cold. The cold there is a pressure against your skin, not wind. Your breath fogs instantly, frost forms on your lashes, every leaf is beaded with slush. Everything you bring will get wet from condensation and drips, and stay wet.

**Clothing:**
* **No cotton at all.** No cotton socks, jeans, or t-shirt. When wet, cotton kills warmth. Use merino wool base layers and synthetic fleece mid-layer, then a soft, flexible waterproof shell that doesn't crinkle loudly. The room amplifies sound - a stiff rain jacket will drum like a sail.
* **A thin beanie and a balaclava or buff** you can pull over mouth and nose as a mesh veil against midges. Your head will brush the low vaulted ceiling and hanging tendrils. No tall hat, no hood up if you can avoid it.
* **Thin gloves, not ski gloves.** You need to feel the floor to avoid crushing it, and to brace without grabbing vines. Thin merino or nitrile liners are enough. Thick gloves make you clumsy and forceful.
* **Soft, flexible, waterproof footwear, not hiking boots.** Rigid boots concentrate your weight on your heels and crush the living floor - inches to a foot of interwoven roots, rhizomes, moss, liverwort and pale rosettes over cold silt. You want your weight spread, your foot able to feel spongy resistance and slippery water film, and your toes protected from the one bare place: the sharp grey siltstone fracture in the corner that is still weeping and unsettled. Thick wool socks inside soft water shoes or neoprene booties with a flexible sole are a good compromise. Clean them thoroughly before you enter - as a multiverse traveler, your treads carry seeds and bacteria from other worlds that this place has no defense against.

**Carry in (in pockets, nothing dangling):**
* A dim headlamp on its lowest green setting, well-shielded. The chamber's own light is phosphorescent lichen, iridescent leaf sheen, and slow pulses of electrical glow in roots. A white flash blinds them and you, and is a physical disturbance. Better: turn it off completely for the first five minutes and let your eyes adapt to the greenish dimness.
* A thin breathable veil, spare lip balm, and your medication.
* Nothing else. No snacks, no water bottle, no notebook - paper will be soaked in seconds and electronics will fog and short. Memorize what you need to remember. No hand warmers inside: your body heat is already enough to make a nearby leaf turn toward you. Extra heat will draw growth and can burn tissue.

**Leave above:** backpack, food, water, bright light, repellent, scented items, loud devices, tools, collection jars. Everything made here is grown and immediately reused. There are no artifacts. If you leave one, it will be eaten - badly.

### 3. The descent

Go sideways, shoulders first, exhaling, hands close to your body. Don't push against the root curtain with your hands - you'll tear it and crush whatever lives in it. Let the cold meet you. It won't get better inside.

Don't rush. The floor is spongy, then resistant, then slippery. Step with your whole foot, weight shifting slowly from one to the other, testing with your toe before committing. Avoid heel strikes. Avoid the rosettes with translucent windows, the shallow pools no deeper than a fingernail, and especially that fresh fracture in the corner - it's sharp, unstable, misty with sediment, and it's the only place the plant hasn't claimed yet. The white root hairs tasting it will be damaged by a boot.

Move at the pace of the place. A fern unrolls a thumb's length in a year. A ceiling tendril advances a hand's breadth before the next winter above. If you move like a tourist, you'll be a storm. If you move like a slug, the animals will part around you.

### 4. Inside: twenty steps

Your ceiling is low at the edges. Stay near the crown where it's five or six feet, duck only when you must, and keep your head from brushing the hanging strands. Some are already reaching the floor and joining it. Don't push them aside - they are becoming walls.

Listen more than you talk. The room is an instrument. Drips hit broad leaves and drum onward. Beetles click on the far wall and you feel it under your feet through the root mat. Shout or even speak loudly and you'll fill the whole oval instantly. Whisper, move cloth slowly, keep zippers covered.

Breathe through the veil. The midges are not a cloud you pass through, they're a persistent thin presence. So is the mist ankle-high that never rises, and the smell of wet iron, crushed green, and sweet rot being composted in days. Don't touch your eyes inside. Don't drink from pools, don't splash your face, don't wash your hands there.

Don't touch to test. A leaf will turn almost imperceptibly toward the warmth of your hand. A slug will climb onto a stem and stay, becoming architecture. A beetle carries a leaf growing from its shell; a slug carries moss on its flank. This is normal. This is not an attack and not an invitation. Keep your hands still, your breath slow, and let them ignore you. If a slug climbs onto you, stay still and let it move off. Don't flick it - you'll tear it and yourself.

If you stay still long enough, the pools will show bubbles of plant exhalation popping in sequence. The rosettes will open and close with the water table, not with light. Roots will pulse faintly from one side of the chamber to the other over minutes. You will start to feel crowded in every direction, as if you're inside a body. That's accurate. Don't panic and thrash. The way back is the way you came: turn sideways, breathe out, back up through the fissure slowly.

Limit your first visit. Even though it's small, cold and wet accumulate. Twenty to thirty minutes is plenty for an ordinary human to get chilled through, especially with wet socks and gloves. Set your outside timer to warn you - you won't feel time inside.

### 5. What not to do - the short list

* Don't widen the fissure, pull vines aside, snap tendrils, or step on rosettes.
* Don't use white light, flash photography, or humming devices.
* Don't eat, drink, urinate, defecate, spit, or use repellent inside.
* Don't take leaves, water, animals, or "samples" home. Nothing here is waste, and nothing here evolved to survive being carried to another world. Neither did your Earth bacteria evolve to survive here harmlessly - they will alter it.
* Don't mark the walls. There is no path, no tool, no artifact. Keeping it that way is the whole point.
* Don't bring a second person down to watch you. One visitor in the center is already a continent-sized disturbance.

### 6. Leaving and coming home clean

Back out the same way you came in, slowly, and don't strip off wet clothes until you're above and sheltered. Then do it immediately. Hypothermia in this environment comes from wet cold and stillness, not wind. Get into dry layers, drink something warm, and check yourself carefully:

* Skin folds, sock lines, hairline, and inside the veil for midges, tiny blind beetles, slug trails, seeds, or root hairs.
* Hands and feet for micro-cuts from silt or sharp plant tissue.
* Any plant material stuck to you - do not release it at home. Bag it separately for disposal according to multiverse quarantine rules, and wash your gear away from soil and waterways.

Shower and dry your boots and clothes fully before you travel again. Watch for the next 48 hours for coughing, itchy eyes, rash, or itching where you were wet. It will usually be just irritation from spores and cold, but ordinary human immune systems have never met this place's fungi.

Above all, prepare mentally to be a guest, not a conqueror. You are not standing in a cave that has plants in it. You are standing inside a living thing that is still being born, that recycles a dead leaf in days and repairs torn tissue in an afternoon, that talks in slow electrical pulses and shares harvested light down through glowing roots. It does not need you to do anything here except notice, tread lightly, and leave it exactly as dense and unfinished as you found it.

If you can do that - go in small, go slow, go quiet, go dry, and come back out promptly - you'll be ready.
