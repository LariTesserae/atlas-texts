Do not walk into this place. That is the first and most important preparation.

It isn't a plain, a swamp, or a cold beach. It is a fully submerged, ice-roofed, hand-built basin — essentially walking the bottom of a very large, very cold, very old pond, at night, with no shore anywhere. There is nowhere to stand dry, nowhere to breathe, nowhere to sit down and rest, and no natural landmark to get your bearings by. Water takes your whole body at once, near freezing, and stays that way the whole time. The ice above you is a dull grey-white ceiling, not sky, and it will never brighten to day.

If you just step through a portal in ordinary clothes, you are dead in minutes from cold shock and drowning. An ordinary human visits this place by diving it, briefly, from a marked entry point, and then getting out.

### What you are actually doing

Think of it less as tourism and more as a silent archaeological survey dive in a blackwater quarry under ice.

You will be on the bottom — never more than a few metres down — walking, shuffling, and feeling your way. Visibility is 10 to 15 steps *if* you stir nothing up. One kick, one careless kneel, and it falls to half a metre of brown-green silt-snow and stays there.

Everything underfoot is man-made and soft: puddled clay platforms, low dikes, stone courses laid without mortar, reed mats, timber stakes lashed and now soft as fruit in the cold anoxic mud. It is all covered in algae felt, eelgrass-like ribbons, moss cushions, and filamentous mats that roof over the canals. It is lush because nothing eats it. There are no fish, no crabs, no snails, no birds, no insects. Nothing will bite you. Nothing will warn you, either.

The pattern — platform, canal, low ridge, mat — repeats for as far as you can see and far beyond. You will not reach an edge. You will not find a natural bank. "West a wall is a little higher" is not a destination you can walk to. Accept a tiny sample.

### 1. Get trained and get a buddy, or don't go in person

Ordinary means: you need at least cold-water, under-ice diving training, a buddy in the water with you at all times, and a watcher at the entry. This is not a solo site. Under ice there is no emergency ascent — you cannot just drop your gear and surface, you will hit ice. If you get lost in a uniform grid of identical dikes, no one outside can see you, hear you, or find you.

Send a camera or a small ROV first if you can. It will tell you depth at your entry, exact temperature, how thick the entry hole/portal-mouth is, and whether the silt that day is clear or thick. Water patches drift overhead over days, so a site clear on one visit can be blind the next.

Your watcher stays on your side of the portal, on solid ice or platform, with a timer, spare plan, and your planned return time. Radio, GPS, and phone do not work here. Sound does not travel. If you need help, it will be rope pulls and light signals, not a voice.

### 2. Thermal protection is life support

Near-freezing fresh water strips heat ~25 times faster than air. The cold will come up through your boot soles from the made earth even while you are working. Your face, your hands, and your feet will go first.

Minimum kit:

* A drysuit rated for 0-2°C, with thick merino or synthetic thermal underlayers. A heated vest/undersuit with battery capacity for *two* dives, tested before transit. Argon suit inflation is far better than air, if you are trained for it.
* Thick boot soles — not soft fins alone — to stand on clay without sinking and to protect against a broken stake. Stake-holes are spaced an arm's length apart for a reason, and old timber is soft, black, and splintered.
* Heavy neoprene drysuit gloves over liners. You need dexterity to work a reel, a slate, and a light, but you also need not to tear skin on stone and timber. Cold, algae-coated water and a cut is an infection waiting to happen.
* A full-face or well-sealed mask with anti-fog and a double strap. Algae mats will brush you constantly; you don't want silt in your eyes.

Do a full seal check *after* you transit, not before. Portals snag, twist seals, dump argon, switch off heaters, and kink hoses. Move every joint, wiggle fingers, check suit pressure, check battery lights, before your head goes under.

Once in, you have a hard clock that is not your air. For most ordinary people, first dive in this water is 30-45 minutes of real work, even if your cylinder says you have longer. Hands lose dexterity, then feeling. That is your abort signal, not the air gauge.

### 3. Breathing, buoyancy, and lights

You are walking, not swimming in open water. Aim for ever-so-slightly negative buoyancy on the bottom, neutral enough to lift off without kicking.

Open-circuit scuba is simpler and more familiar; bubbles will rise to the ice and patter away, and your regulator will be the loudest thing in the world. A rebreather is quieter and longer-lasting, gives you no bubbles to distract you, and is better in silt — but only if you are already trained on it. Do not learn a rebreather for this trip.

Bring three lights minimum: primary, backup, and buddy's. Use low, diffuse light aimed down at the floor, not a tight spotlight into the silt. A direct beam will find the wall, the brick, the thumbprint under algae — and then, if you move, turn the water above it to snow. Flash photography is almost useless; it will light up particles and show you nothing. If you must photograph, get close, use a tripod or brace on a stone, slow shutter, and expect disappointment.

Bring: wrist compass, depth gauge, timer with vibrating alarm (you cannot hear a beep), a waterproof slate, and a 60-100m line reel. The line is not optional. Lay it from the entry, always behind you, never crossing it. In a place where every canal looks like the last canal, your own line is the only geography you have.

### 4. How to move without ruining the visit for yourself

Speed is your enemy. The silt is still falling and almost never arrives — until you kick it up, and then it hangs for a long time.

* Descend at the entry slowly. No big exhalations, no fin-kicking down.
* Walk like you are on ice in socks: step, pause, let the little cloud settle, step. Hands out a few centimetres in front of you.
* Kneel or brace on a flat stone slab, not on soft mudbrick. Mudbrick yields under a thumb — a footprint will hold for a season. You can destroy a piece of work by standing on it all dive.
* Never pull yourself up by timber stakes or lashings. They are rotten-soft and will snap off in your hand, stir silt, and possibly puncture your glove.
* Watch for mats roofed over canals. A canal a pace wide can be bridged by filamentous threads, root to root. Don't tear through it — you'll bring a veil of weed down on your head, lose your light, and stir silt. Go around, or duck low and slow.
* Resist the urge to dig. Under algae felt and slump there is another course, another floor, another layer of packed earth. An archaeologist would call that information. For you it means anoxic mud, zero visibility, and a hole you cannot get out of cleanly.

If visibility goes to zero, stop. Conserve air. Do not swim in a random direction through the grid hoping to hit the entry. Stay on your line, use your compass, and backtrack.

### 5. Mind preparation

This is the part people underestimate. There is no weather, no light change, no animal, no sound except settling pebbles, your own breathing, and the occasional distant crack of ice far above. Time does not pass in a useful way here. A mat torn will regrow in your lifetime, not your dive. A footprint will fill in next season.

It is boring in a profound, sensory-depriving way. The repetition — platform, canal, ridge, mat — will make you loop, backtrack over your own steps without noticing, and feel a strange urge to hurry to *find* something. There is nothing to find. There are no lights in the water, no voices, no things that should not be. The wonder is how ordinary, how finished, how settled it is.

Give yourself one small task before you go in: document one platform, measure one canal junction, photograph one stretch of stone course. Otherwise you will wander until you are cold, lost, and out of air. Set a turnaround time well before you feel cold, and honor it.

Practice in a cold, dark, silty quarry first. Practice doing simple tasks — writing on a slate with thick gloves, paying out line, signaling with a light — while bored and cold. That is the skill.

### 6. Entry, exit, and aftercare

Never leave your entry unguided. Under ice, a hole can skin over, snow can cover it, and the portal mouth can shift. Mark it, light it from below, keep a watcher on it, keep it open.

There is no shore to climb out onto. Your exit is a controlled return to the exact entry point, up your line, in a planned ascent that does not hit the ice. Decompression is trivial at this depth; cold and disorientation are not.

After you are out:

* Rewarm slowly with hot shower and warm drinks *on your side*, not with site water. Check hands and feet for numbness, cuts, algae in glove seals.
* Rinse all gear thoroughly. Do not let algae, silt, and pond organisms dry in your suit seals, regulator, and boots, and do not carry them back through to your home universe. For a multiverse traveler, decontamination is not politeness — you could seed another world, or bring something back that does not belong.
* Do not drink the site water. Do not eat there. Bring your own water on the entry side.
* Debrief while it's fresh. In this dark you will forget which canal was which within an hour. Sketch the grid from your slate.

### Quick checklist

Drysuit + heated underlayer + argon | Thick boots/gloves | Rebreather or scuba + spare | 3 lights | Compass, depth, vibrating timer | Line reel + bright markers (retrieve them) | Slate, pencil | Buddy + ice watcher + hard turnaround time | Warm-up food/water and hot shower on entry side

If any part of that list is missing because you wanted to travel light, travel to the edge, look at a live feed from a probe, and stay dry. This place rewards patience and punishes haste, and it does not care that you have come a long way to see it. It will be exactly as cold, dark, repetitive, and beautiful — in its settled, primordial way — whether you hurry or not.
