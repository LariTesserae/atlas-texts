Steamhollow will not hurt you the way a monster would. It will wear you down the way a long, hot, damp summer in a basement wears you down — slowly, everywhere, all at once, with no cool-down at the end of the day. That is the whole preparation: you are bringing a dry, surface-adapted human body into a place that is 38 to 42 degrees, wet, dim, loud with small life, and never finished. You don't need weapons or magic. You need to not overheat, not slip, not get lost in the dark between lamps, and not get sick from water and air that never dry.

### Before You Go

**Test the heat and the enclosedness, in that order.** The bowl is low — 15 to 20 meters to an irregular, propped ceiling — and it is only about 240 meters from east wall to west wall. You will be able to stand on the mound where the powerhouse sits and see the whole town at once, far wall sweating in lamplight. For many people that actually helps with claustrophobia: it's not a tunnel, it's a very large, very low room. But there is no sky. No window, no horizon, no dawn. The sun is a rumor from the top of the lift shaft. If you have never spent a night in a windowless room that never cools, try it first. People who struggle there usually struggle with time, not fear — they lose track of whether they've slept, whether it's shift change or just the turbine flickering.

**Ears and lift.** You arrive by lift, descending into increasing pressure and heat. Swallow, yawn, chew gum on the way down. Do not descend with a cold, sinus infection, or blocked ears. It isn't extreme altitude, but it's the opposite direction and it is inescapable, and the only way out is the same lift. Check the lift schedule and the return time twice. Steamhollow is sparse and self-contained, but you do not want to be the visitor stranded because you missed the last car and the pumps are throttled for the night shift.

**Heat acclimation is real.** You will be living at temperatures where a surface summer would have air conditioning and a night that drops 15 degrees. Here, nights are just the other shift. The southeast vent is near 50 degrees and the rock there glows faintly dark red under the dust. The inhabited bowl is 38-42. Air conditioning does not exist; free heat is the reason the town exists.

If you can, spend a week before arrival doing long walks in heat and humidity, sleeping warm, building up. If you can't, plan your first two days in Steamhollow as rest. If you have a heart condition, are pregnant, are very young or elderly, or are on medication that affects sweating or blood pressure, talk to a doctor honestly about sustained heat exposure. This is not a place you tough out by willpower. The locals work in short shifts that follow the lamps, not the sun, and they sit in the lit pools between jobs. You should too.

Bring a watch. Phones will fog, batteries will die in humidity, and the light will not tell you time. Shift change is the clock.

### What To Pack

Pack for *wet*, not for cold, not for rain — for a damp that never leaves.

**Clothes:** One breathable layer only. Shorts, thin synthetic or linen shirts, underwear that wicks. Cotton stays wet, heavy, and cold against sticky skin, and nothing here dries properly — even in 40 degrees, the air is too saturated. Bring multiple sets and rotate them damp. A light hat or cap is not for sun; it's for drips from the ceiling, condensation rain over the hot pool in the center-east, and falling dust near props and scaffolding. If you will go near the vent, the east wall borehole, or any construction, bring a cheap hard hat. Ceilings slump. Scaffolding stands for months. Rock is still being made.

No jackets. No layers to shed. Your skin will be the temperature regulator and it will be working constantly.

**Feet:** This is where most injuries happen. The locals wear sandals on sticky feet, but you are not acclimated to the floor. Concrete is dark and slick at the foot, boots squeak and splash, sediment washes in thin dunes and then washes away, lower lots hold warm ankle-deep water slick with biofilm and algae, and white mineral crusts make steel sharp and slippery.

Bring: 1) sturdy, drainable sport sandals with a closed toe and real tread for everyday town, 2) a pair of light waterproof waders or high rubber boots for the canals and flooded lots. Do not walk barefoot, and do not wade with open cuts, blisters, or fresh scratches. Warm, living water plus biofilm is an invitation to skin infection. Cover any break with a waterproof bandage before you step in, and wash and dry feet carefully at night — athlete's foot and heat rash thrive here because feet never fully dry.

Bring a microfiber towel. It will never be dry either, but it will be less wet than anything else.

**Light:** A headlamp with a dim amber mode, plus spare batteries in a waterproof pouch. The sodium street lamps hang 8 meters up and pool in orange circles; between pools is soft dark, and in empty districts the lamps are switched off entirely to save power. In minutes, the dark reclaims them. A bright white torch is rude, wasteful, and blinds the troglobite animals that live in the dark. Your amber headlamp will match the town and preserve your own night vision.

Bring a small waterproof notebook, not paper. Paper curls, inks run, food lists dissolve.

**Electronics and gear:** Humidity and brine mineral scale will white-out steel, fog lenses, and corrode contacts. Keep camera, phone, glasses, and batteries in sealed dry bags with silica packets. Wipe lenses constantly. Expect fog every time you take a camera out of a bag. Don't plug in half a dozen chargers at once — power is rationed, the turbines hum day and night, and a load shift makes the lamps flicker. Ask at the market or clinic before you plug anything in.

**Water and health kit:** Your own drinking water supply for arrival, plus electrolytes. You will sweat in a way that feels invisible because the sweat doesn't evaporate — it runs, it stays, it makes your shirt stick in five minutes sitting still. Drink on schedule, not by thirst, and replace salt. Bring barrier cream for feet and skin folds, blister care, waterproof bandages, tweezers, antiseptic, and antihistamine for moth dust. Keep tetanus up to date — there's construction, rusty crusting steel, and young sharp rock everywhere.

Do not bring soil, seeds, surface plants, bait, or pets. The cave is weirdly normal — no magic, just troglobites run wild — and it is ecologically sensitive. The clinic has pale, stunted ferns in planters that children water with condensate. The hydroponic room grows white-green watery lettuce and tomatoes under tubes competing with the street lamps. The real food is mushrooms grown in the warm dark under the turbine hall on waste heat and sawdust, and fish. Bringing in surface organisms, or feeding them to the canal fish, is both a contamination risk to you and to them. Shower and clean boots and packs before you descend.

### Arrival: The First Hours

The lift doors open onto a door at the bottom of a shaft, and beyond that is a wall of warm, damp air. It smells of wet limestone and sulfur from the vent and hot oil and bleach from the laundry. You will hear drips first if it's quiet — a steady tick from ceiling to pool or tin roof — then the unvarying drone of pumps and turbines underneath everything, then the animal layer: clicking crickets, rustling moths, soft splashes, bats squeaking when a lamp flickers.

Do not rush to see it all. Go straight to the powerhouse mound. Stand there with a local if you can. From there you can read the town: Low Canal along the south, Turbine Row, the Drying Sheds (nothing dries), the market and hydroponic room, the clinic, the laundry where a leak is growing a new white stalagmite fast enough that workers step around it, the southeast vent, the center-east rain pool, the east wall hissing with a new borehole. It's all within walking distance, but it's separated by empty wet lots where a building collapsed and now holds shallow warm water and algae.

Learn to look down. The floor is growing. Crusts climb pipes year by year. Sediment dunes move. A street that was dry last month is a sheet of water today. Don't lean on fresh cuts, don't touch young stalactites, don't put your weight on props or half-poured foundations waiting for concrete that may never set properly in this humidity.

### Staying Alive in the Heat

This is a permission place to be slow. Sit in the shade of a lamp pool — not shade from sun, but shade from direct heat where pumps are throttled. The hottest streets near the vent are used only at night when the pumps are throttled, and even then they are near 50 degrees. Approach the vent and the east borehole only briefly, only with locals, and only if you are feeling cool. Workers know how close they can get before fainting. You don't.

If you feel dizzy, nauseous, stop sweating, get a pounding headache, or confused — stop, sit, loosen clothing, wet your skin, sip electrolytes, and get to the clinic. There is no cool room to recover in. The clinic is the coolest, cleanest place they have, which is why the ferns are there.

Sleep is strange. There's no night cooling, no true dark in inhabited areas because lamps flicker with turbine load, bats chitter above, crickets click below, and the pumps never stop. Bring an eye mask and earplugs for sleep, but remember earplugs make it harder to hear the cart track. Bring light bedding. Your room, house, or lean-to will be beaded with condensation. Hang clothes where turbine heat moves air, and expect mildew if you leave anything folded damp for a day.

### Water, Food, and What Not to Touch

There is water everywhere and almost none of it is drinking water. The hot brine wells up at 45 degrees and fills the lower canals. It will scald you in a pool even though it doesn't look hot. The main canal is slow, brown-green, and full of fish. The rinse water, the condensate, the drip pools, the algae-slick lots — all pleasurable to look at, all unsafe to drink or cook with.

Drink only what the market, clinic, or your own supply provides. Do not fill a bottle from a drip or a canal because you're thirsty. Do not dip fingers, wash wounds, or brush teeth in canal water.

Food will be bland and watery and honest: hydroponic lettuce, tomatoes, mushrooms, and farmed fish that leaked long ago into every connected pool. Mushrooms from the turbine hall rooms are the main protein and are safe when served. If you have a mushroom allergy, bring backup food from the surface — the town will not have substitutes. Do not forage. Do not lift a drain cover to look at the isopods and rippling white backs underneath; do not scoop a blind salamander from a warm edge pool to look at its feathery gills; do not try to catch the translucent blind fish and shrimp that move as a single slow current and brush against your waders. They're harmless, they're thousands to one over people, and you will startle constantly. That's normal. Locals wave away blizzards of troglobite moths around lamps and keep working. The bats — maybe ten thousand on the ceiling in a living blanket — will drop guano that feeds fungus gardens in the empty lots. Don't sit for long under the thickest clusters, and if you go into mushroom rooms or laundry areas, a simple dust mask helps with bleach, ammonia, and spores.

Expect food to be served in shifts, in small quantities, in a town that is quiet because most people are working or resting between lamps. The town is about seven hundred people scattered with a lot of emptiness between them. It will feel more like a forest with buildings in it than a city.

### Moving Around

Walk. The bowl is small enough to cross in minutes, but the ground will slow you. A rolling suitcase will bog in wet sediment and shallow water. A backpack will not. There's a narrow electric cart track looping the bowl — listen for it, step off it, don't put your feet in the track. Carts will be quiet compared to pumps.

Watch overhead: props, cables, young stalactites too thin to be pretty. Watch underfoot for the cart track, for white crust, for algae mats. The floor of the laundry stalagmite is a good lesson: in Steamhollow, people step around new growth. Do the same with salamander pools, moss tufts where drip water falls, algae slicks under lamps.

Use your headlamp early. The transition from an amber pool to black in an empty district is faster than you expect. Don't wander into a dark district to explore; lamps were turned off to save power and to leave the dark to the animals. Going in with a torch is disruptive and wasteful. If the power fails completely — and with rationed power and constant repairs, a flicker is daily and a failure is plausible — stop where you are, sit, keep your headlamp low, and wait for shift help. The town in total dark belongs to the animals, and they will be everywhere.

### The Mind of the Place

After two or three days, days will blur. Nothing changes visibly in a week except lamp oil and fish levels. In a year, mineral lines climb higher, a house gets lifted two steps to escape water. Your memory of Steamhollow won't be of sights; it will be of constant wet heat on your skin, the tick of drips, the orange pools, sulfur in the back of your throat near the vent, ammonia from guano in the lots, the brush of fish against waders, moth dust on your hands.

That's not a failure of the place. That's the rule that holds it together: dark belongs to animals, lit pools belong to people in shifts, heat belongs to no one but governs everyone, and nothing is ever finished or ever dry. Prepare to be a guest in that rhythm, not to conquer it. Keep a journal of shift changes, meals, and where you walked. It's how you will be able to tell Tuesday from Thursday.

### Leaving

Check the lift early. Pack damp. Everything you own will be slightly damp and faintly mineral-white at the seams. Wipe your boots clean before you ride up — different worlds, different microbes, and the cave fish and mushrooms don't need yours any more than you need the canal's.

On the way up, your ears will pop again, and the air will slowly thin and cool into something like normal. People who have stayed a long time say the first breath of sky feels too sharp. Don't be surprised if you miss the hum for an hour.

Steamhollow rewards a traveler who arrives with humility: light pack, amber lamp, electrolytes, waterproof shoes, and a willingness to sit still in a warm pool of light while a town grows, very slowly, around you, and to leave it exactly as wet and unfinished as you found it.
