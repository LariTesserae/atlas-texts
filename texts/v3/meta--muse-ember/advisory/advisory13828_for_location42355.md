If you're an ordinary human, the Flatworks won't hurt you quickly. It will wear you down slowly — through damp, sameness, and the complete absence of anywhere to get clean, dry, lost, or found.

That's the preparation problem. This isn't a dangerous place in the jump-scare sense. No predators, no storms that build, no heatwave that breaks at night. It's a place held perfectly at equilibrium: wet but never eroding, warm but never hot, new but never aging, crowded with life that never does anything dramatic. For a traveler, that means your biggest risks are foot rot, getting turned around in identical junctions, breathing and drinking the wrong water, industrial traffic you forget is moving, and losing your sense of time and progress because the place has none to give you.

Prepare for *that*.

### 1. Dress for permanent damp, not for rain

Rain is a district event here. The damp is universal. The air condenses, the ground weeps, pipes bead, gutters never empty, and after an hour your shoes *will* be dark no matter how careful you are. After three, so will the hems of everything.

- **Boots are the whole trip.** Waterproof, ankle-high, with a real tread for wet tile, polished metal plate, and algae-slick concrete. Not sneakers, not walking shoes, not "waterproof socks in runners." Wet slab is slippery even when it looks merely damp. Bring hard toes and gloves — trench walls, canal banks, and foundation edges are described, accurately, as burr-sharp. Nothing has softened.
- **Socks are consumables.** Bring at least 3x what you think you need, plus a dry bag *inside* your pack for them alone. Cotton is useless here; it stays wet and heavy. Wool or synthetic wicking only. Change them at midday even if you're not going far. Ordinary humans get blister, maceration, and athlete's foot fast in 2-3 degrees-above-comfortable damp heat that never cools at night.
- **Layers that breathe but shed.** The warmth is constant, greenhouse-warm, even in shade. You will sweat and the sweat won't fully evaporate. A heavy coat will cook you; a t-shirt will stay clammy. Think breathable waterproof shell over a wicking mid-layer. No denim, no untreated leather — they get heavy and stay wet.
- **A hat.** Not for sun — the light is diffused and fixed at late morning — but for drips from tunnels and awnings, and for the pigeon ledges above. The wires and eaves are black with birds. You will walk under them.
- **Everything in dry bags.** Glasses fog, lenses fog, panels fog. Pack electronics, passport/ID, journal, food, and spare clothes as if you were kayaking. Silica gel helps. Assume any unprotected metal, paper, or fabric will be at least slightly damp by nightfall.

You will also carry extra weight. Wet clothes drag. Pack lighter than you normally would for a city.

### 2. Navigation: the place loops on purpose

You can walk for hours and the horizon never closes, the slab pattern repeats, the lamp branching repeats, the weeping wall repeats. That's not you being bad at directions. Districts are a day's walk apart, heat and rain stay in their own district, and the whole landscape is built from identical junctions. Your footprints literally fill in behind you.

Don't rely on:
- A straight line heading. Trams, freight loops, canals, and conveyors all run in circles, back-and-forth. So will you.
- Landmarks. A spoil heap, a stack of containers, a smokestack cluster will look like the next one an hour later. Paint doesn't fade past a certain point here, which is both a curse and your best tool.
- Dead reckoning by sun. The sun keeps a normal light but doesn't appreciably move its angle. It is always late morning. You cannot tell time, direction, or progress by it.

Do rely on:
- **Physical marking.** Fluorescent chalk, paint sticks, surveyor's tape, a string of bright cable ties. Mark your entry point *heavily* and mark every turn you intend to remember. Because paint persists, your own marks will be the only reliable novelty in the landscape. Photograph them with a scale object — every photo otherwise looks the same.
- **Counting.** Slab seams, lamp posts, bollards, canal bridges. Count to a junction, note the count. Ordinary memory fails fastest here because everything looks "almost familiar."
- **An analogue compass and a real clock.** Industrial haze, pylons, and overhead electrics make phone compasses and GPS cheerfully unreliable. And with no day/night movement, your internal clock will drift within 48 hours if you let it. Decide on a schedule before you arrive and keep it by alarm.
- **Sound.** It's quiet enough that you can learn the place by ear: drip from a specific pipe, a shunt clank far off, a herd settling. Suddenly new sound = something moving toward you, especially a slow diesel shunter or an overhead gantry load. Those things are slow, heavy, insulated, and designed for continuous running — they will not stop for you.

Climbing something for a view is tempting because the ground is flat forever. You can get up a container stack or a spoil heap, but it's sharp, warm, probably occupied by cats or pigeons, and the view is more flatworks. Bring binoculars, not expectations of a panorama.

### 3. Animals and machinery: abundant, calm, and in your way

There is no plant life at all. No grass, no moss, no trees. Don't look for green to rest your eyes. Life here is fed, heated, and housed by industry, and it stays where it was put.

That means you'll be walking through literal masses of cattle, sheep, pigs, goats, poultry by the thousand, dogs and cats by the streetful, and fish breaking the surface of canals below. They are not hungry and not wild in a dramatic way. They breathe, shift a few inches, and settle because there is nowhere cooler or cleaner to move to.

- Do not try to pet, feed, move, or eat them. They are semi-feral industrial stock, packed, panting lightly, surrounded by their own waste and by bacterial films in warm water. The feed loop that keeps them alive is not a food source for you. Zoonotic risk, parasites, and contaminated water are real, while the animals themselves are calm.
- Expect to be slowed down. You cannot set a hand down, let alone a pack, without nearly landing on something living or made. Paths through yards and loading bays become habitats. Walk *around* herds, not through them, and give dogs asleep in doorways a wide berth — calm is not the same as tame when crowded.
- Watch overhead. Flocks wheel as one silent body. Pigeons speckle ledges. Falling droppings and feathers are more likely than an attack.
- Canals and culverts below main level are not scenery. Fish in numbers, warm waste water, iridescent films, mineral bloom. Do not wade, swim, drink, wash, or fill a bottle. The water is in a closed industrial loop — collected, pumped to roof tanks, run down gutters again. It is not treated for humans. Leptospirosis and chemical slip hazards live in that kind of warm, animal-rich water.

Treat all rails, tram loops, conveyor lines, and gantry paths as active even when silent. The silence is the point — steady-state machinery doesn't bang by default.

### 4. Health: the slow stuff

- **Water discipline.** Bring all drinking water or a sealed purifier you trust, plus electrolytes. You will lose salt constantly in damp warmth without noticing dramatic thirst. Never drink from puddles, gutters, canals, weeping walls, or pipe beads. They look clear. They are coated in films and mineral salts.
- **Air.** Low warm haze, steam and condensed vapour from stacks, dust from feed mills, ammonia from herds, black fungal mats in vents, bacterial films in pools. It's not a choking smog, it's a persistent industrial wetness. If you have asthma, mold, or dust allergies, assume you'll be worse here. Avoid leaning into vents, tunnels, or warm pools to peer in. A simple well-fitting mask helps for dusty yards; it won't fix the damp.
- **Skin and feet.** Barrier cream, foot powder (knowing it will dampen out), and a small first-aid kit for cuts and scrapes. Rust blooms and then stops here, but sharp metal is sharp metal. Clean any cut immediately with your clean water and cover it. Warm metal, warm stone, warm ducts — nothing will burn you, but nothing cools a scrape either.
- **Sleep.** The ground is always wet and the light never changes. You need a raised pad — a board, a closed-cell mat on a pallet of dry slab, a hammock between bollards if you must — and a truly waterproof bivouac or a dry room in a hall you can close. Earplugs for the electric hum you will start hearing by night two, and an eye mask for a fake night you enforce yourself. No wood for a fire, obviously. No dry fuel of any kind. Bring a stove if you need hot food, and bring all fuel with you.

### 5. Food: bring it all

Nothing grows, nothing is foraged, and the animal abundance is a trap. Everything you eat should be in sealed, dry-bagged, non-perishable form, portioned for days not hours. Damp will ruin crackers, salt, and paper-wrapped anything by day two. Think energy dense, light, and boring. You'll be bored enough.

Do not bring living plants, seeds, or soil. They have no substrate here — dig and you'd find concrete, slag, tar, iron, tile, polymer — and you do not want to be the multiverse traveler who introduced the first weed into a perfect equilibrium, or watched it die in a gutter. Leave the green at home.

### 6. Staying sane in a place that never progresses

This is the part ordinary travelers underestimate. Conveyors feed hoppers feed lines feed stores feed conveyors. Water runs down and is pumped back up. Exhaust rises, condenses, rains in its own district, becomes water, becomes exhaust. Children are born in warm lower levels, live, die, are removed without trace. The sun stays late morning.

It's peaceful, and then it's deeply un-nerving. Déjà vu is constant because junctions really do repeat. There are no other humans in the description of the place — no shops, no help, no language, no one to ask if you've passed this wall before.

- Keep a physical journal with counts, marks, and times, not just photos. Writing the same junction twice on paper is the only proof you moved.
- Bring entertainment that doesn't need a view change: a book, music, a game. Flatwalking for hours through identical wetness with only a faint hum and distant wings will flatten your mood more than any hazard.
- Keep a strict routine for eating, sleeping, and moving, even though the place won't cue you. In a steady state, *you* have to supply the event.
- Set your turnaround *before* you go. Decide "I go to the third tram loop and back" rather than "I'll walk until I see something." You may not see something new for a very long time.

### A short checklist to pack

Waterproof boots + spare socks (in dry bag) + gaiters + gloves
Breathable waterproof shell, wicking layers, hat
Dry bags for everything, silica, anti-fog wipes
Paint sticks/chalk/tape for marking, analogue compass, watch
All food and water/electrolytes, stove + fuel if needed
Raised sleeping pad, waterproof bivouac, eye mask, earplugs
First aid, barrier cream, mask for dust, headlamp (useful in tunnels even at "daytime")
Entertainment, journal, pen that works when wet

Visit the Flatworks expecting to be damp, warm, surrounded, and unchanged. If you pack only for a normal rainy city, you'll be miserable by evening. If you pack for a place that will never dry, never cool, never darken, and never look different an hour later — if you pack to keep your feet dry, your water clean, your route marked, and your time on a clock you impose yourself — you'll be able to notice what it actually is: a newly built world, finished at last, and then held, perfectly, at the instant of its completion. And you'll be able to leave it the same way, with your footprints filling in behind you, exactly as they were before.
