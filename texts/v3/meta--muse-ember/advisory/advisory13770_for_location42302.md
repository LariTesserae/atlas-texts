This is not a hike, a park, or a wilderness visit. It's a work hamlet built on top of its own weather, and you prepare for it like you'd prepare for a very small, very wet factory that has grown over itself — not like a mountain.

You're going to be high up, warm, wet, and off the ground. Everything else follows from that.

### 1. Before you arrive: mental adjustment

Expect disorientation at first. You will come up through a sea of cloud with no horizon, no soil, and no view of where you're standing until the fog clears off your glasses, your sleeves, and the handrail. The light will be high, clear, and flat — that thin high-country quality — but the air will not feel thin or cold.

It's a warm pocket. Sun on stone and metal, heat rising from the engines below, wet green holding moisture — it makes a kettle at altitude. You will sweat, not shiver. Your ears will pop on the way up for real, so do the usual swallow / yawn / equalize on ascent, and give yourself 10 minutes of slow walking at the start before you try to cross it. Even though you *can* cross platform to parapet in 20 minutes, don't. You'll be going up and down catwalks, through 3-meter lanes narrowing to 1 meter, around crews, gantries, and lifts. Plan 2-3 hours, not a forced march.

And prepare for a strange quiet. A place this lush and wet would normally be loud with insects and birds. Here there are almost no insects by design — they are treated as contaminants in the nutrient loop — and birdsong is absent. What you hear is water dripping, ticking, rushing in runnels, pumps clucking through felt and rubber sound shells. It is not eerie once you settle into it, but the first silence after green, green, green will feel uncanny. Take that as a cue to lower your own voice.

As a multiverse traveler, this matters doubly: do not bring seeds, soil, plants, pets, or uncleaned boots. This is a closed loop. The phosphorescent moss in the pipe-vents has already escaped a little and no one wants a second escape. Arrive with clean footwear and no organic souvenirs — and leave the same way.

### 2. What to wear and pack

Everything will get wet, all the time, even if it's not raining. It arrives as intercepted fog on fins, condensate on pipes, spray cycles every 40 minutes, drips from every gutter and sweating joint, and the weep from a 3-week-old curing wall. "Waterproof" is not about a storm, it's about constant contact.

**Clothing:**
* **No cotton, no leather, no jeans.** They stay wet, get heavy, and cold against skin when you pause. Wear synthetics or merino that can dry while you move: quick-dry trousers, a breathable rain shell with a hood (for spray cycles, not rain), a light warm layer underneath for when you stop on the parapet and wind comes across the flat plateau.
* **A hat, and sun protection despite the haze.** The light is diffuse but high and strong. Your face will be wet and you will burn without noticing. Sunglasses are optional — it's flat light, not glare — but a brim helps with drips from above. A small towel to wipe your glasses and face is more useful than sunglasses.
* **Footwear is the whole trip.** Closed-toe, ankle-high, waterproof, with aggressive tread you trust on wet metal, wet turf modules, wet concrete, and algae-slick edges. Think waterproof hiking boots or sturdy rubber boots, not running shoes. You'll be in ankle-deep warm water in the rice troughs and sumps whether you mean to or not. Bring spare socks and a spare pair of insoles — your feet will macerate in constant damp if you stay in one pair. Powder / barrier cream for toes helps.

**Hands and eyes:**
Thin work gloves. You will touch wet handrails, cable-laced trunks, rebar, bolt holes, corrugated edges, and the lattice scaffolding that the ferns are already exploring. Small cuts in nutrient water are how you get a skin infection here.
Anti-fog wipes or a glasses strap. Condensation forms on *you* and on everything you touch. Your phone, camera, and notebook need a waterproof pouch — not just against spray, but against sweat from pipes and the constant drip. Everything metal will rust or short faster here.

**Other essentials:**
Your own drinking water. Do not drink from gutters, runnels, sumps, or troughs. They are warm, green with algae, and full of fertilizer and nutrient loop water. They are not potable, even if they look clear.
Hand sanitizer. No insects does not mean clean — sumps are stirred to keep them from stagnating, but they are still biological.
A headlamp for the underwalk, where the escaped phosphor-moss glows faintly at dusk. It is romantic, and it is also a shadowed, narrow passage under catwalks where you will trip if you rely on it alone.

Bring snacks. You might be offered fruit from the espaliered trees or rice from the moving troughs — always ask first. That food is part of a managed system, tended by hand because there are no pollinators to do it. Don't pick, pull, or taste without a crew member saying yes.

### 3. Getting around safely

There is no ground to fall back on. A "hill" is a stack of ribbed shells bolted together and turfed over. A "ridge" is a factory roof covered in moss and sedge. Tap it and it is hollow. A "valley" is a catwalk.

* **Stay on the lanes, watch above as much as below.** Crews work vertically: one in yellow slicks carrying cuttings at your level, another on a gantry 2 meters above bolting a water tank, a third below your feet in the press shop you can feel through the deck. Drones bump gently off cables. Cranes — small, articulated, very careful because nothing is allowed to fall off the edge — move modules overhead in the north end. If you hear a spray-bar sigh, a lift overhead, or see a conveyor carrying matting roof to roof, stop and let it pass.
* **The north is a construction site, the south is a garden, and the border moves.** The south terraces are old, overgrown, vines padding steel struts. The north is rebar thickets, raw aggregate slopes staked with polythene, chalk survey marks fading in minutes. Do not wander into the north unescorted. You will not be dressed for it, and a lane that was a lane last month can be a wall of beans this month. A printed map will be out of date. Ask the pairs of workers — almost everyone works in pairs, at walking pace — where the current closures are.
* **The parapet is not a viewpoint.** It's a greenhouse frame and cable fence at a straight drop into cloud. The ferns lean out over it and the handrail is wet. No leaning, no sitting, no climbing for a photo. Wind picks up on the exposed plateau and the whole place must keep moving or pools rot — don't stand still in a lane for long, and don't block a crew.
* **Respect the spray cycle.** Roughly every 40 minutes the fans turn and a fine warm mist goes over the gardens. It will fog you instantly and soak your pack. Either keep moving through it or step under a pipe-vent / gantry and wait it out. Cover electronics before it starts if you can hear the fans change pitch.

### 4. Health — the specific hazards here

* **Constant wetness:** Trench foot, fungal rash, and slipped-on metal are your main risks, not altitude sickness. Dry your feet at every stop, change socks, cover cuts *before* you go out. If you have broken skin, don't wade in the sumps.
* **Warm nutrient water:** Treat any standing water as contaminated. Wash after contact. Don't submerge a backpack, don't let a child splash in the paddled sumps.
* **Heat + humidity:** You are high but warm. You can get heat-exhausted while feeling wet and cool. Hydrate, rest in shade under catwalks (where the moss glows), and don't carry a heavy cold-weather parka "just in case" — you won't need it and you'll overheat.
* **Humidity and spores:** If you are sensitive to mold, algae, or dust from compost shredding, a light mask helps. The air is thick and condensing; breathing is easy due to all the tended green, but it's heavy.
* **Ears and pressure:** Already handled on the way up. On the way down through the cloud sea, equalize again.

There are almost no animals to worry about. A pair of clean white birds nests in the warm vent of the high tower and circles once in the morning — don't approach the nest. A few pale, translucent amphibians live in the lower pipes on algae; seeing one is noted to the next shift, so don't disturb them. Leave them be.

### 5. Etiquette that will make your visit pass

This is a hamlet of workshops, dormitories, vats, and gardens. People live and work there in shifts, and everyone knows everyone else's shift. That is why they speak low — shouting carries across the flat plateau.

Walk at their pace. Don't shout across a lane to a companion. Don't touch trees in their bubbling tanks, don't open a tank, don't pull water hyacinth from a runnel, don't pick a leaf off a turf module wall just to see what it's made of. The whole rule of the place is: nothing is allowed to be natural, and nothing is allowed to be finished. Natural would rot; finished would stop. Your job as a visitor is not to finish or naturalize anything — just to pass through without adding stillness, waste, or foreign life.

Don't leave trash. In a closed loop, your trash becomes someone's contaminant. Carry everything back down.

If you arrive at dusk, the seams of phosphor-moss will glow under the underwalk and the light will flatten even more. It's beautiful, but it's also when night-shift drainage crews are working wet lanes with low visibility. Keep your lamp low, stay off active work areas, and wrap up early.

### A one-page checklist

- Waterproof boots + spare dry socks + towel
- Quick-dry clothes, rain shell, hat, sun cream
- Thin gloves, anti-fog solution, glasses strap
- Waterproof pouches for phone / notebook
- Own water bottle, snacks, hand sanitizer
- Headlamp
- Clean boots, no seeds/soil/plants
- Cover any cuts, equalize ears on ascent/descent
- Ask locally about lane closures; don't enter north construction without escort
- Plan for spray every ~40 min; keep electronics covered

Arrive expecting to be damp within five minutes and to stay damp, to hear water before you see people, and to walk on roofs that feel like hills. If you prepare for that — light, wet, quiet, and always in motion — you'll be fine. The place is designed to stay alive by being tended by hand, in pairs, at walking pace. The best way to visit it is to join that pace for a while, listen to the tick-tick of the gutters, and leave it still moving after you go.
