**The Unfinished Ocean in the Sky: How to Prepare as an Ordinary Human**

This is not a tourist place, and it is not a place you conquer. It's a small, bright, deafening pocket inside a storm cloud, high above a mountain ridge, where the air has become liquid and the water has become air. You will not drown in it, exactly — you will *breathe* it — but an ordinary body will get cold, disoriented, and lonely very quickly if you go in as if it were a scenic swim.

You don't need to be a superhuman to visit. You need to be prepared for three things most travelers are not: constant wetness without a surface, constant sound without speech, and a landscape that un-happens while you look at it.

Think of a visit as a short, tethered crossing, not an expedition. The pocket is only a few minutes of swimming from one rain wall to the other in a straight line. Hour-long stays are possible, but they are where most of the danger lives.

### 1. Before You Go: Training Your Body and Mind

**Cold-water swimming, not ocean swimming.** The medium is described as cool, west of cool — deep spring, high mountain air — and it never gets warmer. You are wet the way a thought is wet in a dream: there is no dry side to get back to. That strips heat much faster than air, and slower but more completely than a warm river. In training, spend time in 10-15°C water learning to swim slowly, to use short fins, to keep your hands big and relaxed to feel invisible shear currents before your body hits them. Learn to test a current with an outstretched hand or probe stick, not with your face.

Practice equalizing your ears as if diving, even though you are not deep. Water fills your ears and sinuses for the whole visit. The roar lives in your molars and chest because sound travels fast and close in this medium. You will not be able to talk normally. Anything you say will have to be said slowly, mostly to yourself. So train a quiet, internal pace. Practice thinking in short, clear sentences while buffeted by noise — whitewater, wind tunnels, a shower running cold.

**Breathing mist.** Novices panic because they try to inhale the medium like air and trigger a cough/gag reflex. Train in steam, waterfall mist, and white-water spray to exhale as a small, warm secondary mist and inhale through the nose with a relaxed throat. With a full-face mask this is easier. Without one, go only with a guide who has watched you breathe saturated air for ten minutes without choking.

**Vertigo management.** The light is perfectly normal, white-gold mid-afternoon, from everywhere and nowhere. No sun to orient by, shadows blunt and brief. Then, in a thin place in the floor, you see the ridge and glacier and forest far below. The vertigo comes and goes like a current. Train on high places and in swimming pools looking down through clear water. Your rule inside: look at the bright middle, not down for more than a second. A tether will keep a glance from becoming a fall.

**Loneliness tolerance.** The pocket is mostly empty, visually. You can cross it and meet nothing at all. Half the time you meet something that was not there a minute ago, because it has just finished coming into being. An ordinary traveler expects a glider, a filament, an artifact. Expect instead long stretches of empty bright water with droplets passing like slow snow in reverse. Bring a task for that emptiness — count breaths, watch for the soft pop where a droplet is born, note the difference between a fast ridge and a slow valley of water by feeling it on your gloves. If you go in needing constant stimulation or company, the pocket will feel hostile. It's not. It's just unfinished.

**Medical screening.** Asthma, chronic sinusitis, Raynaud's, uncontrolled anxiety, and heart conditions that make cold immersion dangerous are reasons to watch from the mountain below, not to enter. You will inhale mineral dust and algae traces and the faint iron-green smell of the filaments. That should be clean, but your lungs will have no rest from moisture. Plan a post-visit warm shower, hot drink, and 24 hours of watching for rattly breathing or ear pain.

And as a multiverse traveler: rest at altitude before you enter. Don't stack travel disorientation on top of immersion disorientation. Decontaminate your suit and gear between worlds — this pocket supports almost no life, and each organism appears to be alone by rule. Don't bring in seeds, soil, spores, or loose organic matter on boots or in pack pockets. Wash everything, seal it, and assume any alien microbes you carry are unwelcome here.

### 2. Gear: Assume Everything Gets Wet

Forget cotton, forget paper, forget zips you trust. Nothing stays dry unless it is hermetically sealed, and even then, plan as if it will leak after 20 minutes.

**Body:** A neoprene or synthetic immersion suit, not a heavy SCUBA drysuit unless you are experienced with its buoyancy in a mist-water mix. In this medium you may be near-neutral, hesitating, not quite floating and not quite sinking. A heavy tank will pull you down into the heavier droplets that gather below; a thick suit may pop you up into the newborn droplets above. Test your buoyancy *in the pocket* on a short first crossing with a guide. Wear gloves (you will use them to feel currents), fin socks with short bus fins (long ocean fins overcorrect in turbulence), and a balaclava that protects ears and neck but doesn't trap water.

Bring chemical heat packs in sealed pouches against your core, and high-calorie food in sealed, chewable form. You won't be able to unzip a bag elegantly in the roar. You will be wet, your jaw will be tired from noise, and your calories will dissolve unless sealed. Dehydration still happens while breathing mist. Drink before you enter, sip after.

**Breathing and face:** A full-face mask with anti-fog, a rigid face shield for the rain wall, and either a filtered air supply or a small self-contained warm-air regulator in a backpack for first-timers. A snorkel will flood. A demand regulator will fill with droplets. Practice with your chosen mask in a shower before you ever enter the cloud. Bring a spare mask strap; a torn strap in a shear current is how a crossing becomes a drowning thought come true.

Do not bring open flames, smoke, or anything that might burn. Fire has no business here — the only smoke is the ghost-smell on an old piece of hide.

**Ears, eyes, and communication:** Use -15 dB musician-style plugs, not solid foam. You want to take the edge off the roar that lives in your chest without losing the pressure-change "hearing" of a glider coming. Bone-conduction or tether-tug communication is essential. Touch codes from diving work well: tap the shoulder once, a tug on the tether, a squeeze of the hand. Carry a small grease-pencil slate. Paper will dissolve in minutes. For buddy work, use rope signals: one sharp tug = OK, two = wait, three = I need to exit now. Keep an outside team on the mountain below in radio contact with your entry point above the cloud — you cannot shout through the rain wall.

**Navigation and safety:** No map will work. Crusts of mineral dust and ice crystal knit together and dissolve before they can be called stone. A cairn you stack, a raft you *think* you see — the place un-happens. Objects hesitate in the water-air for long, weird moments before they fall or rise. So don't trust landmarks, and don't make new ones you intend to keep.

Carry: 
- A harness and elastic tether to your buddy or to a main line at the entry wall. Walls breathe — ten paces become two, become twenty. A fixed line will go slack then jerk. Elastic absorbs that.
- Floating, timed strobe markers you will *retrieve* on exit. They may drift, get eaten by light, or be reabsorbed. That is data, not failure.
- A vibrating timer watch. Time inside will stretch because sound arrives before sight and sometimes seems to precede its source. Set a hard turn-around: 20 minutes for a first crossing, 45 minutes maximum in an unheated suit, and leave ten minutes of that to exit through the hammering rain wall.
- A personal locator and an emergency parachute system or personal flight belt if your multiverse kit normally carries one. This is not optional thinking: if the pocket decides to collapse into a downpour that will soak the ridge below for an hour, you will be in that rain, falling a mile. If it expands into a cloud that drifts for days, you will be adrift with it. Your outside team must be watching the storm for swell, for sudden cracking, for bundles of droplets falling up.

**Camera and notebook:** Waterproof camera, no flash (the glitter will blind you). Sound is your best record — crackling means a crust is forming before you see it; a pressure change means a glider before you see it. Record audio if you can. For artifacts — the half-woven reed raft, the clay cup, the knapped tool, the patched hide — photograph in place, note depth relative to current, and *do not collect, touch, or tidy*. Their lashings loosen, their edges soften. They are as unfinished as everything else, and they are the only proof the pocket has had a past. Taking one home would remove the only context it has, and it would likely dissolve into fiber and water before you reached the mountain anyway. Do not stack a cairn, carve a name, or leave a marker you can't retrieve. Anything you leave may be knitted into a crust, eaten by a filament, or incorporated into whatever the pocket is trying to become next.

### 3. Inside: How to Move, Breathe, and Be

Enter through the rain wall at a point your outside team has marked as a brighter hesitation in the storm. You will pass *through* the roar — it changes timbre, droplets strike droplets as they are born, your face shield takes hammering. Equalize, exhale slowly, and you are inside. Behind you is now a curtain you can sometimes see.

Move slowly. Ride the breathing rather than fighting it. When the pocket contracts, heavy droplets below are lifted in bundles and rise, silently, against expectation, then fall and the roar swells. Let yourself be lifted and dropped a little. Fight it and you will tumble. Extend a hand. The water will tell you where the fast ridge is before your eyes do.

Breathe circularly and notice it, don't panic about it. You inhale mist; downstream that same mist may be exhaled by the pocket as a finer rain that never reaches ground. If you go in a group larger than two or three, you will noticeably change the pocket's air. Keep groups tiny, keep visits short, and get out before your own exhalations become someone else's late visit.

Distance is untrustworthy. Two things that look close may be separated by a shear you can't see; two things that look far may be brought together in one swell. Cross differently each time, even though the pocket is small. That's normal. Don't chase a shape that seems near. Let the current bring it or not.

Life, when it comes, will be spare. The filaments hang on the light side, green-black, swaying in current, tasting of iron and rain. Where one hangs, the water smells faintly clean and green. Where there is none — most places — there is no smell at all. Don't touch, don't harvest, don't eat. You're allergic to almost everything in an alien sky, and a filament is a thread, not a plant.

The glider — if you are lucky enough to see the only kind of animal the pocket seems to admit — will cross on slow, shallow wing-strokes, translucent at the edges, trailing disturbed droplets. It does not avoid you. It does not notice you. Watch it cross to the far rain wall and be gone, or dissolve back into emergence. Do not chase it, do not try to follow its wake. Its indifference is the point. Rarity here is not scarcity of food; it's a rule. Nothing lives here in numbers.

Artifacts will startle you because the pocket is empty. Enjoy that startle. Photograph, don't touch. Smell of smoke on a hide, sinew on a raft, clay holding a palmful of sky-water that is not quite water — these are visitors or mountain people who left things to the cloud. You will never know which. Sit with not-knowing. The pocket has no fixed shape to protect, and it will not give you a story that finishes.

**Abort signals — leave immediately if:**
- Walls approach to two paces and don't recede
- Cracking under the roar becomes continuous and close
- You hear an echo before you see its source, repeatedly — coherence is breaking
- A bundle of droplets falls up and stays up, or the roar swells for no swell you can feel
- Your buddy's tether tugs go unclear, your core feels cold in a way heat packs can't fix, or you realize you've been looking down at the ridge for more than a few seconds and can't stop

These are not dramatic failures. They are the pocket breathing differently. Exit through the nearest rain wall, not the one you entered, if it's closer. Don't try to be thorough.

### 4. After: Rewarm, Dry, Debrief

The exit will be abrupt: hammering rain, then normal mountain air, thin and cold, and a silence that may hurt. Your ears will pop. You will be soaked through despite your suit, your skin pruned, your sinuses full.

Rewarm actively at the mountain shelter: hot shower, dry wool or synthetic layers, hot sweet drink, food. Check your ears, lungs, and skin. Log everything while it's fresh — but know your log will be wrong in places. Sound arrived before sight. Distance lied. A raft that was "to the left" may have been nowhere, then somewhere else. That's not faulty memory; that's the place.

Rest before any further multiverse travel. You have been in a landscape that creates itself by destroying itself. Your body and mind will keep expecting it to dissolve and reform for a day. Talk through the loneliness with your buddy. You were alone together in a vast, bright, unfinished room. That feeling is not a failure of the visit. It is the visit.

**The short checklist to tape inside your dry bag:**

1. Short visit, hard timer, outside team watching storm.
2. Full-face mask, face shield, filtered/warm air, ear plugs not full isolation.
3. Harness, elastic buddy tether, strobes you will retrieve.
4. No cotton, no paper, no fire, no collections, no leaving things.
5. Sealed calories, heat packs, grease slate, touch-code comms.
6. Move slow, feel currents with hands, ride the breathing, don't look down.
7. Photograph artifacts and filaments, don't touch glider, don't chase anything.
8. If cracks rise, walls close, or echo precedes source — exit now.

Go in quietly, stay briefly, leave no mark that the pocket must then spend itself un-happening. You are not there to finish it. You are there, for a few minutes of swimming through sky-water, to be present while it is being born around you — and to come back down to the ridge below with wet socks, ringing ears, and a clearer sense of how rare it is for anything to be finished at all.
