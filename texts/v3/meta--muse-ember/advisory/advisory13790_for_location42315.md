If you are an ordinary human — no teleporting into a room, no breathing volcanic air, no walking on a crowd without consequence — then preparing for this place is mostly preparing to accept that you cannot really *go* there in the way you can go to a market or a cave.

This pocket is defined by not having a door. It is a sealed gas vesicle in cooling basalt, 2-3 km up, with no entrance larger than the crack between two stones. What lives in it got in as air and as animals smaller than that crack, and has been sealed in ever since. The moment you make a door for yourself, you stop having a sealed, rainless, dark, hot, undisturbed factory of rock-eating beetles, and start having a drilled hole, a flood of cold plateau air, a beam of light, and a set of human germs.

That paradox should shape everything you bring.

### 1. The entry problem — decide how you will get in before anything else

There is no trail, no crawl, no squeeze. An ordinary traveler has only three honest options, in order of safety:

**a) Don't go in person. Send a probe.**
This is what everyone who has studied extreme sealed habitats actually does, and it's what you should do first. A bean-sized, heat-tolerant crawler with a thermal camera, an IR illumination system, and a microphone can sit in the crack traffic for weeks. It will not crush the beetle layer, it will not shed skin, sweat, or CO2, and it will not need to be rescued.

For a multiverse traveler this also matters the other way: you do not want pocket dust, mites, and gut bacteria in the seams of your suit to come home with you to another timeline.

**b) Drill down to it and lower a sealed capsule.**
You would need a geophysical survey first — magnetics, resistivity, micro-gravity — to find a 15 x 8 metre void you cannot see from the surface, then a slim drill hole from the plateau down to its roof. The capsule would descend on a cable and open *inside* the pocket, end-on, displacing as little air as possible.

Even then, you will not "step onto the floor." There is no floor. There is a shingled layer of beetles on a floor of basalt. An 80kg human standing up crushes hundreds of thumb-sized bellows beetles instantly, and the sudden weight, heat, and vibration reads to the chamber as an earthquake and a predator falling out of the ceiling at once.

If you must be in there physically, you stay **suspended**. A chest harness on a winch, feet dangling 5-10 cm above the beetle layer, letting you hover, pivot, and be walked under by clinker-striders. You crawl, if you crawl at all, on forearms with knee and elbow pads and distributed weight — and you accept you are still doing damage.

**c) Wait for the mountain to open it.**
The eastern fissure is opening by fractions of a millimetre a decade. You will not live to see it. Unacceptable.

Do not plan on shifting/walking through rock. Being off by centimetres means materializing in solid basalt and dying there.

Any drilling also needs a volcanologist, not just a guide. This is still-warm rock in a still-cooling mountain. The seam glows, the pressure outside changes it, slabs are still settling. You need seismic and gas monitoring before, during, and after, and an abort trigger. A fresh slab splitting overhead in a low, hot room with no exit is a crush hazard.

### 2. The body problem — heat, dryness, dust, and altitude

Outside, you will be at 2-3,000 metres on a bare, bright, windy plateau. Thin air, hard UV, cold wind. You need days of altitude acclimation if you are not used to it, sun protection, windproof layers — all of which you will then strip off to enter a >50°C oven.

Inside, the problems flip:

**Heat:** Floor level is well over 50°C, the seam hotter, the ceiling a few degrees cooler. Dry heat is more survivable than humid, but you will still raise your core temperature in minutes, especially in a suit. Ordinary humans do not *work* at 50°C+ for an hour.

Pre-heat-acclimate for 10-14 days with exercise in heat, learn your personal limit, and plan on **10-20 minutes inside, not an hour.** Take multiple short entries rather than one long stay. Hydrate heavily *before* — water, electrolytes — then do not drink inside. You cannot eat, drink, urinate, or sweat freely in there. Any liquid you release is a flood, a nutrient bonanza, and a microbial invasion in a world with no water cycle. Use a sealed waste bladder, and empty it before entry.

Bring active cooling: a chilled-air supplied respirator or a liquid cooling vest under your suit is not luxury here, it is what keeps you conscious. Ice packs melt into water, which you must not spill.

**Dryness and dust:** It is oven-dry. Lips, eyes, nose, and lungs will crack and bleed. The dust is iron-grey, yellowed with powdered sulfur, partly magnetic, and it hangs instead of settling quickly. It will coat teeth, eyes, lungs, and every seam in your kit, and it will cling to camera lenses and compasses. Inhaling it for an afternoon would leave you coughing sulfur and metal for days; chronically, it is a pneumoconiosis risk.

You need supplied, cooled, filtered air — not a paper mask — with a full face seal. No contact lenses (dust gets under them), no exposed skin. Wrap everything: a heat-resistant, dust-proof cover suit over your cooling vest, double gloves, a hard hat for the low, sharp basalt ceiling (you will have to stoop; it is low enough to hit your head on crockery-sharp slabs), padded knees and elbows, and boots with puncture-proof soles that can be decontaminated.

Your skin will be cut if you touch anything. The rock is fresh, unweathered, glassy, and interlocked like broken pottery. Every slab edge cuts. Gloves are not for warmth — they are to keep your blood, which is also water and food, off the beetles, and the basalt out of you.

**Noise and vibration:** It is described as a working mill, and it will not stop. Grinding, clinking, bellows-wheezing, shell squeals, constant shushing. Through your boots you will feel every shift of the layers. You need custom-molded earplugs *plus* a headset with a radio, because shouting to a partner 3 metres away will be impossible. Expect your ears to ring afterwards even with protection. Expect also to be deafened mentally — you will not be able to think clearly narrative thoughts under mechanical noise for long.

**Air quality:** Assume the pocket air is not breathable as-is. It is hot, dusty, sulfur-bearing, moved only by beetle bellows, and likely low in oxygen and high in CO2 from all that metabolism. Do not test it by breathing it.

### 3. Light, sight, and what to bring / not bring

Beyond 2 metres you have almost nothing. A dull cherry-red glow from the fissure, phosphorescent seam-glow on shells, and red eyeshine moving across surfaces. Your eyes need 20 minutes to dark-adapt, then your own headlamp will ruin it for everyone.

Bring *low* light, in red/IR, and prefer thermal imaging and a microphone over a bright white torch. A camera flash will blind animals whose eyes are only for detecting glow and shine, and will startle the whole floor into a defensive shift.

Bring magnetism-tolerant equipment. That dust is magnetic in patches. It will mess with compass needles, stick to motors, and coat everything. Bring sealed sample jars if you are permitted to sample at all — sterile, double-sealed, opened only inside — and a protocol for *not* sampling. Most of the value here is in watching it work, not in taking it apart.

**Do not bring:** open food, open water, plants, pets, loose fabric, insecticide (you think you are protecting yourself from mites; you are poisoning the cleaners), bright lights, loud power tools inside, or anything you cannot account for on the way out. Everything you shed — hair, skin flakes, microbes — is invasive.

**Do bring:** radio, harness and winch rated for the capsule, spare gloves, eye protection that seals, supplied-air regulator, cooling unit, sealed waste system, thermal camera, quiet tools, a notebook that works with gloves (you will not remember the small weather later if you don't write it down), and a second person monitoring you from the capsule with a retrieval line. No solo entry. No entry without a way out that doesn't depend on you being able to walk.

### 4. Behaviour inside — how to be a slow, quiet thing

Think like the animals, not like a tourist.

Move as slowly as they do. A jerky human movement is, to them, a slab falling. Enter, descend, become still, and let the striders investigate you and then ignore you. They will clatter over your boots, reposition pellets around you, test you with antennae. The mites in the seams of shells will probably climb you if your suit is not perfectly sealed — they clean films of oil and waste, and you are full of both.

Do not stand on the beetle layer. Do not sit. Sit = crush. Hover, or lie spread-eagled to distribute weight if your harness fails, knowing you are still killing.

Do not touch, feed, or try to communicate. A broad, many-legged grub at the cool end takes weakened beetles and mites. To a bellows beetle, your serrated, iron-hard fingernail is nothing; your glove is a boulder that just landed.

Do not speak loudly or sing or play music to fill the silence. There is no silence. There is industry. Your job is to learn its waves: one zone grinding hard while another rests, a shimmer as striders cross the ceiling, the seam brightening a fraction when a distant storm drops plateau pressure. That takes 20 minutes of just hovering and listening, not an hour of exploring. The room will be recognizably the same tomorrow and next year. That is the point.

Expect claustrophobia, boredom, and sensory confusion to hit at the same time: hot and dry, stone on stone in your ears, eyeshine everywhere, no horizon, no day-night, a ceiling you can touch, a floor that breathes. Train for confined space in heat beforehand. Have a mantra, a breathing rhythm, and a time-keeper outside who will pull you out on schedule whether you feel fine or not. Heat impairs judgment before you notice it.

### 5. Coming home — decontamination is part of the visit

You do not just climb back up. You seal yourself in bags inside the capsule, and your suit exterior goes into a sealed decontamination bag before it passes up the drill hole. Cold plateau air rushing down the hole after you, dust and mites clinging to your boots, and your own microbes going back up — all of that is damage in both directions.

Afterwards: shower, eye and lung check, rest your hearing, rehydrate with electrolytes, sleep. It will take a day to stop hearing the grinding. That is normal.

And then, the multiverse-traveler part: log exactly what you brought in, what you brought out, and what you left. Date, location, duration, suit integrity, any contact with the layer. If you are carrying samples, they are quarantined until cleared. A pocket like this exists in one timeline, maybe, and bringing its mites to a timeline with different beetles is how you create a problem that outlived the mountain.

### The ordinary human summary

Prepare to spend 95% of your effort on getting in without destroying it, cooling and sealing your body, and getting out clean — and 5% on actually seeing.

Your toolkit, in short:

- Altitude and heat acclimation, confined-space training, medical clearance (no asthma, heart issues, heat sensitivity)
- Supplied cooled air, sealed heat/dust suit, cooling vest, hard hat, double gloves, pads, molded hearing protection + radio
- Harness suspension, retrieval line, thermal/IR camera, low red light, sealed waste and sample system
- A surface team, volcanic monitoring, abort plan, strict time limit, decontamination protocol

And prepare, most of all, for the disappointment of a human timescale. You will not witness evolution, or a beetle finishing its gram of stone, or the chamber deciding its final shape. You will witness a hot, loud, crowded room where stone becomes animal becomes pellet becomes stone again, at a pace that makes your 15 minutes feel like a blink — and if you are quiet, hover, and do not crush anyone, the beetles will go on grinding as if you had never fallen through their ceiling at all.

That is, in the end, the respectful way to visit: be a brief, sealed, cold-blooded visitor who leaves no water, no light debt, and no footprints.
