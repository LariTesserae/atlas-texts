If you remember nothing else: **you do not get to choose how you enter The First Water.**

You don't walk in from a shore. There is no shore. It arrives through your ribs, and you're already in it - shoulders deep, deafening, warm, high up, with sharp new rock under you and no sky above you. You won't have time to dress, find your mask, or think in whole sentences.

So you prepare the way you prepare for a sudden immersion you cannot prevent: everything vital is *already on your body* before you transit, already in muscle memory, and already simple enough to do in fragments between booms.

### What you're actually going to

Don't pack for a lake. Pack for a small, vertical, newborn bowl at the top of the world that is trying to become a sea and hasn't decided how.

- **Altitude, but wet and warm.** You're above a cloud-sea, in thin air, but the bowl itself is a trapped storm. The air and water are bath-warm, steamy, mineral-slick and gritty. You will sweat, prune, hyperventilate, get tired fast, and still risk a chill the second you get out because the real outside air is thin and windy. Warm does not mean safe.
- **Sound as a physical force.** The roar isn't background. It presses your chest, rattles your teeth, makes up feel tilted toward whichever eddy is loudest, and *is* how the water moves, sediment settles, and animals navigate. You will lose words, lose balance, and over time lose a little hearing if you don't protect it. But you also need to feel it - that's your navigation system.
- **New rock.** Black, glassy, freshly split, never worn smooth. Cubes and razors and splinters that shear off and clatter down every few minutes. No beach, no silt bank, no safe place to put your feet. Every cut you get in that water will be jagged, mineral-filled, and hard to rinse clean.
- **Water that won't hold still.** Upwellings, whirlpools that eat themselves, level rising and falling by handbreadths, domes of boiled water collapsing, sediment veils that never clear. You can't swim straight across a bowl that's only a few minutes wide, and you shouldn't try to anchor to anything - ground, debris, and stones move.
- **Almost nothing alive, almost nothing built.** Pale algae films that tear and regrow, hair-thin white strands in rain, a few blind translucent swimmers who ride the booms, one sessile ear-flap near the deep point. A knee-high ring of slabs, a snapped half-wall, stepping stones to the middle that stop. No metal, no glass, no plastic, no tools, no lights from whoever made them. Don't expect help, guidance, or a friendly native. If you're hurt, you are the rescue service.
- **Frayed reality, only a little at a time.** Up drifts toward noise. Air and water are a thick zone, not a line. A reflection can arrive a fraction before you. Distance is measured in echo-seconds, not meters, an echo can come before its rockfall, the same bubble can pass you three times. It's worst at the center, deepest, loudest part. Stay off-center if you can.

This is a 20 to 40 minute place for an ordinary human on a first visit. An hour in, you may have seen almost nothing except emptiness and your own exhaustion - and that is exactly when people get careless.

### Who should not go

Ordinary means ordinary here. Don't go - or at least not without a full medical clearance and a plan to stage at altitude first - if you have asthma, a recent ear infection or perforated eardrum, vertigo, panic in water, poor swimming fitness, open wounds, or you haven't been to altitude in months. A jump from sea level straight to a mountaintop pool is hypoxia + exertion + submersion + noise. That combination drowns people who are strong swimmers at sea level.

And don't go solo. Not because someone can carry you out - no one can get to you in that bowl - but because the roar will separate you, disorient you, and make a simple signal impossible to send. A tethered partner is an extra set of ears, hands, and memory.

### Training that actually helps

You do not need to be an elite diver. You need to be boringly reliable when you can't think.

1.  **Altitude acclimatization first.** Spend days to weeks at a similar height *before* the jump. Arrive with a little reserve. In thin air, panting is a reflex - practice slow breathing through a snorkel/regulator while floating.
2.  **Chaotic water, eyes closed.** Practice water entries blind. Practice finding your mask seal, mouthpiece, tether clip, and beacon pull-cord without looking, with gloves on, while someone slaps the water and shouts over you. Pruned fingers are clumsy. Use big, gloved toggles, not fiddly buckles.
3.  **Task under noise.** Put your whole plan on one waterproof wrist card in large print: *Float. Breathe. Signal? Stay near wall. No center. 20 min, check. Beacon.* You will read it in fragments. Memorize the order.
4.  **Vertigo drills.** Practice fixing your eyes on a fixed dark point on the rim, not on bubbles or surface chop, when "up" tries to tilt toward noise. Debris drifting *up* is normal here - ignore it.
5.  **Short surface swims in cold, turbulent water** for fitness, even though The First Water is warm. You need the endurance of cold-water swimming without the cold-water panic to trust your PFD and your breathing.

Get tetanus up to date. Your biggest likelihood of injury is not the animals. It's a fresh edge.

### Packing - on your body, not in a bag

Assume anything not clipped to you is gone. It will float up, get dragged into a fissure, or be sheared off with a splinter.

**Worn before transit:**
- **High-collar PFD / buoyancy harness,** fitted tight. You arrive submerged to the shoulders. You need your airway up immediately, but you also need to be able to dip and turn. A low, yacht-style vest will do nothing against domes. Practice in it.
- **Full-face mask with a regulator or a short, stiff snorkel you can clear in one blow,** plus secondary goggles as backup. The water is gritty, mineral, turbid, and the breath zone is thick - bubbles don't break, rain doesn't fall so much as exist. You will taste water, inhale spray, and get grit in your nose if your face is open. Keep mouth closed unless you're on the regulator. Do not drink the lake, do not rinse your mouth in it.
- **Ear protection that leaves bone conduction.** Custom molded plugs or a vented band, *plus* a bone-conduction headset or simple throat mic if you use comms. You must dampen the boom enough to protect your eardrums, but you need to feel pressure changes and hear your partner's tap signals. Pure silence is dangerous here; total exposure is also dangerous.
- **Thin abrasion suit, 2mm neoprene or equivalent, head to ankle, with hard-soled water shoes that strap tight.** It keeps razor rock off your skin, warms you after you leave, and protects you if an upwelling is hotter than bath-warm. No cotton, no loose hems, no dangling straps. Anything loose snags on shards and gets pulled by whirlpools. Hard soles for the stepping stones, but expect them to be slick, sharp, and to end in deep water. Never trust them as a path.
- **Helmet, low profile, chin-strapped.** Rockfall is announced by echo, sometimes *after* the sound. Splinters clatter down constantly. You also need protection if you get tossed against the wall by a dome.
- **Neoprene gloves with real grip.** You'll lose feeling quickly to pruning. Plan to manipulate things big.
- **Bite / splash hood or balaclava** under the helmet to keep rain and grit out of ears and neck. The suspended rain will soak you through no matter what.

**Clipped / tethered to you:**
- **Self-tether (6-8 ft, floating, low-stretch) between partners,** not to rock. You were told not to anchor. Anchor to each other. Mark it with a bright whipping that you can *feel* in murky water.
- **Dimensional recall beacon / emergency pull,** on a chest strap with a big, gloved pull ring. That's your exit if your adrenaline tells you to leave. Don't leave it on a slab or a stone - the water will not keep it where you left it.
- **Small dry pouch *on* the PFD, not on your back,** with: drinking water in a soft flask with a bite valve you can use under the mask, a waterproof slate + grease pencil, a chem light (note: your light will disappear in turbidity - bring it for signaling, not for seeing), a whistle you probably won't be able to use over the roar but still carry, and a simple pressure dressing and wound packets. You can't rinse a cut with lake water.
- **Mechanical or simple analog timer on your wrist.** Phones, GPS, compasses will lie. Up is unreliable, distance is echo, and time stutters in eddies. Your wrist timer and your partner's tap-count are what you have.

**Leave behind:** metal tools, glass, food, perfume/scented anything, loose rope, backpacks, and anything you would mourn if it floated up and hung trembling in a loud current. The place has no trace of those materials for a reason, and you don't want your biology seeding a newborn algae film. No urinating, defecating, eating, or spitting in the bowl. No touching the slab rings, the half-wall, or the ear-flap. They hum in sympathy, they may be resonant, and the animals are listening. Touching them gives you nothing and makes you part of the experiment.

### The first minute

You will arrive disoriented. The roar will be in your ribs. Your reflection may show your mouth open before you open it. Don't fight it.

1. Exhale, find regulator/mask, take one slow breath. Don't try to stand. There is no stand.
2. Find your partner's tether by feel. Two taps = I'm here. Three taps = are you okay?
3. Eyes on the dark rim, not the surface. Breathe in fragments. Read your wrist card. Put your timer on.
4. Get to *floating rest*, on your back, ears mostly submerged to dampen the boom, face above the thick zone. Resting here is work. You will be tired within minutes because of altitude, noise, and trying to swim against a spiral that changes its mind.

### Moving in the bowl

- **Stay near the wall, off the center.** The fraying is loudest where it's deepest. The eastern wall with the slab ring is a little calmer, but the slabs are half-silted and sharp - use them as a visual reference, not a handhold.
- **Don't swim through whirlpools.** Go around, or wait for them to eat themselves. Time stutters in them, and a bubble passing you three times is your hint you're looping.
- **Don't chase echoes or animals.** A boom that sounds close may be far. The blind swimmers and the ear-flap aren't dangerous in themselves, but following them takes you to the center. Watch, don't pursue.
- **Communicate tactilely.** Shouting is useless and may, in a place where sound is mechanism, actually stir the water. Use slate words: BACK, STOP, UP, OK, OUT. Use tether pulls. Use bone conduction clicks.
- **Don't estimate distance by volume.** Count echo seconds to the far wall if you must, and expect to be wrong.
- **Watch the level.** It rises and falls visibly. A low notch that was free can overtop; a rock that was glistening can vanish. Your exit rock is not guaranteed to still be there in ten minutes.

Use your abort criteria *before* you need them: timer hits your planned limit, ringing in ears doesn't fade between booms, nausea/vertigo rising, skin cut, regulator flooding repeatedly, partner signal lost, or you feel that pull of wanting to follow the loudest eddy because "up must be that way." That last one is the place talking.

### After you leave

Get out of the wind. You're wet, at altitude, in thin air - you will chill fast even though the water was warm. Dry, windproof layer immediately. Rinse skin, ears, and mask with your clean water, *not* lake water. Check for cuts, treat them, and watch them - mineral water in a fresh shard cut is a infection story waiting.

Expect a phantom roar in your ribs and teeth for hours, tinnitus, and a strange reluctance to trust "up." Decontaminate everything that touched the water before you bring it back into your travel system. Log what you saw, but don't trust your distance or time notes too much - write "echo ~3 sec to far wall" not "10 meters."

And next time, stage at altitude again. The place doesn't care if you're experienced somewhere else. It's still trying out shapes, one loud minute at a time, and it will try one on you the second you arrive.

Go light, go tethered, go brief, and let the water keep its first shape.
