This is not a place you walk into for a stroll. Treat it like what it was: a working industrial room that kept running after the workers left, and now it's hot, damp, dark, and breathing on its own. As an ordinary human with ordinary lungs, skin, and a body that needs to climb a ladder to get out, your preparation is mostly about not letting the place tax you more than you budgeted for.

### The mindset shift first

You don't descend through a cave. You drop down a concrete shaft — ladder, dead cage lift — and the air gets heavier before your feet do. Chamber Three is one wide room, about 60 meters long, but you can't see it all at once. The ceiling is 12 meters up and mostly black. The floor is tilted, sharp, fresh black basalt plates that have not been smoothed by time. They will cut boots and skin like broken crockery.

The heat is not weather. It's geothermal rock, pipes, a turbine turning slowly, and a furnace mouth glowing dull red. It hits you immediately, sweats you before you've moved, fogs glasses, and makes your shirt stick. The damp is not a stream. It's a loop: steam from a crack in the south floor condenses on cold pipes, drips, collects in warm black pockets, and bursts again as fine mechanical rain when a pipe sighs. There is no drinkable water here, no animals at all, and no sunlight ever. Light is borrowed — tired orange sodium standby tubes, a blinking green panel, furnace glow — and darkness pools in between.

The green is engineered to live on heat, sulphur, nitrates, and condensation. It grows thick under every weeping joint and hangs upside down under walkways toward warm joints. It's beautiful and lush, and it will rub against you. It is not food, not medicine, and not safe to touch casually.

Plan for a short, slow visit, not exploration for hours. Most humans do best with 45-90 minutes below on a first trip, with plenty of reserve left to climb out.

### Before you drop down

1. **You need a topside partner.** The shaft is the only exit. Someone stays up top, in cooler air, with a phone/radio check-in, water, and a plan if you don't come back up on time. No cage lift. If you can't climb a ladder carrying your pack, you can't go yet.

2. **Acclimate like it's a sauna you have to work in.** Sleep, eat, hydrate well the night and morning before. Don't arrive hungover, sleep-deprived, or already overheated from travel between worlds — your body clock will already be confused because down there there is *no* day/night cycle to trust. Heat builds in you slowly, eases only fractionally. Bring your own timer and set hard alarms for turnaround; you will lose an intuitive sense of time in that silence.

3. **Do a gear check at the collar.** Test your headlamp, your gas detector, and your water *before* you commit to the descent. Everything is harder to fix once you're sweltering and fogged up.

### What to wear — the awkward compromise

You need protection *and* you need to shed heat. Synthetic fleece and waterproof rain gear will cook you. Thin shorts and t-shirt will be cut by basalt and burned by a steam sigh.

Aim for loose, light-colored, heat-tolerant layers:

- **Footwear:** Sturdy, ankle-support boots with thick, puncture-resistant soles and a toe guard. This floor is tilted, wet with condensation, and sharp. Sneakers or light trail shoes will not last. Knees, too — you will kneel on hot rock to look at things.
- **Legs and arms:** Loose long pants and a light long-sleeve shirt, ideally cotton or aramid-blend that won't melt. They protect against sharp edges, hot splashes, leaf rubs, and biofilm, while still letting sweat evaporate. Roll sleeves only if you need to.
- **Hands:** Cut-resistant work gloves. Touch the wall and it gives heat back, not cool — don't test it bare-handed. The rock is still cooling from a recent wound.
- **Eyes:** Safety glasses, not regular glasses if you can avoid it. They'll fog anyway. Get anti-fog if you have it, expect to take them off to wipe, and keep a microfiber in a pocket. Steam and spore drift are real in still, hot air.
- **Headlamp, not handheld torch.** Both hands free for ladder and railings. Bring a second light source as backup — when your main dies, the standby orange in that room will not be enough to find the ladder safely. A red mode helps you preserve night adaptation in the black pools, but white is best for footing.

Leave perfume, heavy insect repellent, and open flame behind. You don't want to add volatiles to hot, low-ventilation air, and there are no insects to repel.

### What to pack

**Must-haves:**
- Personal CO2 / O2 / H2S detector, clipped where you can see it. The place smells of iron and faint sulphur, the plants live on waste gases, and CO2 is heavier in low pits. Headache, nausea, sudden fatigue, or feeling "off" down there is not just heat — treat it as air until proven otherwise and get out. A headache that came on fast is an abort signal, not something to push through.
- Water, more than you think. 500ml to 1L per person for a short visit, plus electrolytes. You will sweat in the thick air even standing still. Sip, don't gulp. You cannot refill from the pools — that black, still water is warm condensate mixed with coolant and minerals. Don't drink it, don't splash it on your face, and don't get it in cuts.
- Small battery fan on your belt or pack strap. It won't cool the room, but moving hot air across sweaty skin helps enormously when the air is saturated. Note: heat drains batteries faster and humidity condenses on cold electronics when you return to the surface — put phones/cameras in a ziplock.
- P100 or well-fitted N95 respirator. For ash and grit, drifting spores from the engineered ferns, and biofilm dust from the waterlines. In saturated air a mask gets damp fast; bring a spare.
- Flagging tape or chalk sparingly for marking your own route back to the shaft if the room occludes, and a small notebook. Cell signal and compass will be unreliable — basalt and steel confuse both.
- First aid basics: blister care, burn gel, tweezers for basalt splinters, and hand wipes. Wash hands before eating anything topside.

**Leave behind / don't rely on:**
- The plant's own lights, the pump house panel, the control hut. That green blink is standby, not a promise. The tank gauge is frozen at a fake "productive" middle. The conveyor still creeps a centimeter an hour and the pruner arm wakes once a day — don't lean on machinery or put hands into a hopper to "check." The silence is uninhabited, not dead; things move slowly and can surprise you.
- Any living thing from another world. No pets, no seeds, no soil. The symbiosis in there is finished and closed: plants, machines, rock. Introducing an animal, a plant from home, or even multiversal microbes/spores on your shoes could be fatal to it and to whatever you take home. This is especially important for a traveler — decontaminate.

### Moving inside Chamber Three

Go slow, slower than you think. That is the rule of the place: turbine turns once per breath, a drip takes four minutes to gather, moss creeps. Rushing will only overheat you and make you miss the steam sighs that come from a different place every time.

- **Watch your footing more than the ceiling.** The basalt plates are tilted and slick with condensation sheets. The steel grating walkway over the shallow pit will be slippery and its railings are bent. Test each plate.
- **Stay back from the southern steam crack and from pipe joints.** Steam here is invisible until it condenses. A burst darkens a circle of stone, then it's gone — but if you're leaning there, it's a burn. Keep a full arm's length from any sighing pipe.
- **Don't touch the green to your face.** Those fronds and broad leaves are waxy above, pale below, and engineered for waste gases. They are not toxic in a dramatic way, just not meant for human skin, eyes, or lungs over time. Admire, don't pick. Don't sit in moss patches — they're wet with coolant loop water.
- **Expect your own sounds to feel foreign.** No insects, no worms, no birds. Just a drip echoing once, metal ticking as it expands, a low hum felt in your teeth. Your footstep will sound loud. This is psychologically tiring. Talking, a quiet radio, or a checklist helps. If claustrophobia or that waiting-machine feeling grows, that's information — go up.
- **Keep a turnaround budget.** Decide on the surface: "We go down, we look at the pump house, tank, and the fern wall, and at minute X we turn regardless of what we haven't seen." Because the room is one room, the temptation is to "just check the far clearing." Don't. Save your legs for the ladder climb out, which will be in heat, carrying a pack, in thicker air. Exhaustion at the bottom is how people get stuck.

### Getting out and multiversal hygiene

Climb steadily, rest if you need to on the ladder rungs. When you emerge into the basalt plain and weather, you will feel suddenly cold — your sweat will evaporate fast and your clothes will be damp. Change into dry layers topside if you have them.

Then, decontaminate properly before you jump again:

- Shower and wash hair. Spores and biofilm ride on skin, hair, and clothing.
- Bag your Chamber Three clothes separately to wash hot. Don't shake them out in a clean world.
- Inspect boots for seeds, filaments, and black water. Scrub them. If you brought plants back on you inadvertently, destroy them — do not compost them elsewhere, do not release them.
- Rehydrate with electrolytes, eat, and give yourself a full cool-down before descending again. Heat stress is cumulative.

### Abort criteria — leave immediately if:

- Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or unusually rapid fatigue
- Detector alarms, or air smells *more* sulphurous / sweet / stale than when you arrived
- Heart pounding that doesn't settle after 2-3 minutes of sitting still
- Steam burn, cut that won't stop bleeding on sharp basalt, or you lose a light
- You realize you're too tired to climb with reserve

Don't wait for it to get better down there. The heat only builds. The exit is up.

Chamber Three rewards patience: look for the stalactite straws just beginning, the leaves glowing orange in front of a heating duct, the belt carrying fallen leaves an inch toward becoming soil. It's a place where heat is the source, water is recirculated, light is stolen, and nothing with a heartbeat was invited to stay — so you are the exception. Prepare like a respectful, temporary guest: bring your own light, air, water, and exit plan, stay cool, stay brief, touch almost nothing, and leave it waiting exactly as you found it.
