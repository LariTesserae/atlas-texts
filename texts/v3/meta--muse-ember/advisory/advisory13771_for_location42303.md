Prepare to be wet. Not wet as an accident, wet as the condition of the place. Everything else follows from that.

This is not a destination you traverse, climb, or explore ruins in. It is a low, warm, saturated plain that has finished becoming. An ordinary human will not stay dry here, will not build shelter from the land, will not find food or a path, and will not see the landscape change in a day's walk. You will wade, you will be bitten, you will listen, and you will learn to move slowly through repetition. Pack for *that*, not for adventure.

### 1. Your body first

You are not adapted to permanent damp. Their skin never dries out. Yours will complain in a few days, and that is your main medical risk - not a predator.

**Get current before you go:** Tetanus booster (essential for any cut you get in warm, biologically rich mud), routine vaccinations, and a talk with your doctor about immersion foot, fungal infection, and insect-borne illness in still freshwater. If you are prone to athlete's foot, eczema, or asthma triggered by mold and midges, be honest about it. The air never becomes truly dry, even at midday.

**Fitness is for mud, not miles.** You can see for kilometres, but you will not walk them quickly. Ankle-deep to knee-deep sheet water, soft peat, slick clay, and swells of only a metre or two that you feel in your ankles more than you see will tire you out of proportion to distance. A day's walk here is a day of lifting your knees through water while watching the horizon not change. Do short, slow loops from a basecamp. Do not plan a crossing.

**Skin discipline:** Any cut, blister, or bite you dip in that green-sheened water can infect. Cover cuts with a waterproof dressing *before* you wade. Keep nails short. Every evening, dry your feet thoroughly, change into a completely dry pair of sleep socks you never wade in, inspect between toes, and treat redness early with antifungal powder. Prolonged warm wetness gives you the same problems as cold trench foot, just slower.

### 2. Clothes and footwear - the whole trip lives here

Cotton is useless. Leather is useless. Sandals are dangerous - crabs work the flats, clay cracks hide ankle-twisting edges, and mudfish and terrapins are in the channels you will step in.

You want:

**Footwear:** High, waterproof, broken-in boots with stiff ankle support and a deep tread that can clear mud, worn with gaiters. Or, if the season is wet, chest or waist waders over boots - water is sometimes only toe-deep, sometimes knee-deep, and you will not know which until you step in. Two trekking poles are almost mandatory. They test peat depth, steady you on algae-slick mud (the green biofilm is invisible and very slippery), and help you sound the lazy braided sloughs, perhaps 20cm deep, that join and divide across the pan without any obvious bank. A pole that sinks deep is a warning.

Bring *three* changes of synthetic or merino socks, in separate dry bags, and a dedicated evening ritual to dry them. In this vapour your sweat does not evaporate. Overheating happens even when it feels mild. Pace is slow, shade is almost non-existent between far-apart gnarled trees, and water reflects the filtered white-yellow sun.

**Clothing system:** A quick-drying base layer that tolerates damp, a breathable sun shirt with long sleeves, trousers that dry fast and can be rolled or unrolled, and a lightweight waterproof shell that lives in your pack and goes on for the frequent, non-violent afternoon rain. Do not wear the shell all day - you will cook inside it in the close air. A wide-brim hat with a chin strap, sunglasses with a retainer strap for glare off shallow water, and a midday rest in whatever little shade you can make yourself.

Everything textile goes in dry bags inside your pack liner. Assume the outside of the bag will be damp all the time. Paper maps, notebook, phone, camera lenses - all in waterproof cases with desiccant. Humidity fogs lenses at dawn, mist rolls in, and electronics corrode quickly.

**Insects:** Dragonflies will patrol at arm's length. For every one, there are two biting midges around you. This is not hyperbole, it is the ratio you will live in. A head net for dawn and dusk, long sleeves and trousers, and a repellent you will reapply after sweat and mist, are not optional. Gloves help when you need to handle wet gear. The frogs start singly at first light and within minutes the whole basin joins them - you'll hear the hour of worst biting coming. Sleep with earplugs if you're sound-sensitive; the hum never fully stops.

### 3. Shelter - you cannot build it

There is no building, no tool, no wood that stays hard. Dead wood falls, softens, and becomes black peat. Trees are solitary, hollow, and far apart. You cannot rely on finding a dry tree to hang a hammock from, and you cannot make a fire that does not smolder in damp detritus and leave a foreign scar.

Bring a self-supporting tent with a separate footprint, and camp *raised*. Even a few centimetres matters when water rises an inch in a season and an afternoon's rain turns the plain into a skin. Choose a subtle swell, or the edge of a sedge clump where the ground lifts a little - the only places plants become dense. Do not camp on the southern low stone outcrops: they weep at the sides and are the only reliably dry tops in the south, which is exactly why turtles haul themselves onto them to warm. You would displace them from their basking place and be sleeping on their latrine.

Stakes do not hold well in wet clay or peat. Use wide, low guylines and dead weights. Use a camp cot or a thick inflated mat if you can carry it; sleeping directly on the ground means sleeping on cold, perpetually damp earth that will soak up through a thin foam pad within a day. Cook on a windproof stove with a sealed canister, not on a fire.

Toileting: The water table is at the surface. You cannot dig a pit that will not flood. Use a portable waste bag system and pack it out. This place recycles everything through algae and mud very efficiently, which is also why you must not add waste, soap, or food scraps to that loop.

### 4. Food and water - abundance is a trap

Water is everywhere and drinkable nowhere. The warm shallows are full of algae, shrimp clouds, tadpole-darkened pools, fish, ducks and geese, and the bacteria that come with them. The west lagoons are permanent, the north peat hollows are dark and still, the east pans are bare - all of it needs full treatment.

Carry a fine filter (0.1 micron) *plus* chemical purification, a collapsible container you can fill and let settle, and more calories than you think. There is nothing to forage. The soil is leached and nutrients wash out; plants are sparse salt-tolerant shrubs with waxy leaves, sedges and rushes. Do not eat anything. Dead wood, roots, and peat are not food, and the few animals are spread thin and essential to the system. You are not a predator here, you are a visitor.

Plan simple, no-smell, no-cook meals for many hours. The midges will find an open stew quickly.

### 5. Navigation and safety in the open

Openness here is disorienting, not helpful. The horizon is a distance, not a line, shadows are soft and brief, and after an hour every pan, slough, and tuft looks like the last. At dawn a low mist settles in the hollows and erases landmarks until mid-morning. After rain, the clay cracks close, the mud softens, and the whole surface is saturated for days.

You will not follow a path - none hold their shape. You will navigate by *zones* and backtrack on your own disturbance:

- **West:** Chain of warm, shallow lagoons linked by films. Most permanent water, most ducks and geese, deepest wading.
- **East:** Bare clay pans, patterned with old ripples and faint animal trails, hexagonal cracks when dry, slick sheets when wet. Easiest walking when dry, hardest to read.
- **North:** Peat hollows, darkest still water, floating plants. Softest, most springy ground. Your footprint here can soften, fill at the edges, and persist long enough to fossilize. Tread as little as possible, and never alone.
- **South:** Low stone outcrops, rounded and healed by water. Only dry rock. Observe turtles from a distance.

Use compass and GPS daily, note the position of the few solitary trees as vertical references, and take a photo of your camp from 20 paces out each morning. Do not try to blaze a new route each day. Use the same wading line in and out to minimize footprints in peat and disturbance to algae mats. Carry a personal locator beacon. No one with local knowledge will find you quickly, and sound carries far - if you shout, you will flush wide-spaced wading birds that are alone in their stretch of pan by design.

Flash floods are not the risk; slow inundation, ankle rolls, and hypothermia-by-damp when you finally stop moving are. Always have a complete dry change sealed away from everything else, even if you never use it.

### 6. Wildlife manners and multiverse protocol

There are no people here to offend, which makes it more important, not less, that you act like the first human who has ever stepped here - because in this timeline, you are.

Keep your voice down. The plain is loud: frogs layering at dawn, birds honking and cackling, insects humming, water dripping off sedge heads, rain drumming. That loudness is the sound of many small lives making themselves known. Shouting carries and disturbs all of it.

Do not approach: wading birds far out on the pans (they are spaced widely for a reason), terrapins paddling in procession along the channels (let them pass, don't pick them up), turtles basking, or crab burrows in the bank (watch where you step). Do not collect the pleasingly rounded pebbles, do not trample reed clumps where shrimp flicker, do not step on algae sheens if you can step in open water beside them.

As a multiverse traveler, your boots are the risk. Sterilize all footwear, waders, poles, and pack frames *before* arrival and again after departure. No seeds, no live bait, no pets, no soil from a previous world. Leave no artifact behind - no tent peg, no wrapper, no gear stash. This place has never had a tool. A lost carabiner will be a geological anomaly. Pack out everything, double-check the mud around camp when you leave.

Photograph, but don't use flash on close birds or frogs. Red-light headlamps at night preserve your dark adaptation - the night sky over a flat, wet plain with no buildings will be extraordinary, and your eyes will need time.

### 7. Mental preparation

This is the hardest part for most ordinary humans.

The dynamic is patient and weak-gradient. Water migrates a pan over years. A channel takes a thousand years to shift its width. You could walk for a day and the world would not climb or change its character, only repeat with subtle difference - another pan, another slough, another tuft. That repetition is the point. It has settled into its final form.

If you arrive expecting vistas, ruins, a summit, you will be bored and frustrated and walk too fast, slip, and get lost in monotony. Arrive expecting to *stay*. Schedule nothing in the afternoon except rain-watching and drying socks. Learn the dawn chorus. Watch dragonflies. Notice the hexagonal cracks closing. Measure the world in ankle-muscles, not in kilometres.

It feels primordial not because anything is strange, but because nothing is new. The light is normal, the animals are ordinary frogs and fish and birds, the rain is just rain. That normality, held in warm dampness that softens all edges, is what will reset your pace. Let it. The place is ignoring you, not welcoming you. That is not hostility. It is age.

**Your 3-5 day checklist, in one dry bag:**

High waterproof boots + waders + gaiters, 3x socks, poles, quick-dry layers, waterproof shell, wide-brim hat, head net, repellent, sunglasses, 3-5 days food, filter + purifier, stove + canister, raised tent + footprint + cot/mat, headlamp (red), compass/GPS, locator beacon, first aid with waterproof dressings and antifungal, waste pack-out kit, dry bags for everything, notebook in waterproof case, earplugs, camera in waterproof case.

There is no way to keep everything dry. The goal is to be wet, bitten a little, muddy to the knee, deafened pleasantly by frogs at dawn, and still warm, fed, and able to dry out at night - and to leave footprints that are no deeper than they have to be.
