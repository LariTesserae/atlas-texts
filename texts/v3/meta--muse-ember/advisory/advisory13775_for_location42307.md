If you want to visit this place, the first preparation is to unlearn what visiting means. You don't walk there, you don't swim there, and you don't look at it from a distance. You sink into it, slowly, in a machine, and it stays essentially the same while you do.

This is the abyssal plain — 4 to 5 kilometers under the sea, on ancient oceanic crust paved over with ferromanganese nodules. An ordinary human body has no business there at all, and preparing for it is mostly preparing for that fact.

### 1. The Hard Truth: Your Body Cannot Go

At this depth you are under 400-500 atmospheres of pressure. That's roughly 50 kilograms of force on every square centimeter of skin, the weight of the entire ocean above you pressing in. The water is 2-4°C, there is no light to make photosynthetic oxygen, and there is no air to breathe. Even a perfect dive suit, a spacesuit, or a rebreather will crush, freeze, or flood in seconds.

You do not travel there. A pressure hull travels there *with* you in it.

That means your multiverse travel method — portal, jump, transit bubble, whatever you have — cannot be just a hole you step through. An open hole to another Earth at this depth is a catastrophic flood. The ocean will try to come through it at the speed of sound for the entire time it's open.

For an ordinary traveler, you need:

**A rigid, pressure-rated vehicle or habitat landed in advance,** or a portal locked to its airlock. Think a deep-sea submersible like Alvin or a benthic lander, not a capsule you wear. It needs to be rated to at least 600 bar with a safety margin, have its own life support for 72+ hours if you can't ascend, and have a way to equalize or not equalize on return. If you have to teleport *out* while still pressurized, you will experience rapid decompression. Test that on land first.

**Wide landing feet.** The floor is not rock, it's flour-fine mud, centimeters to meters thick, over basalt. A narrow vehicle, a foot, even a tripod will sink and slump back around itself. The description is literal: marks close up almost imperceptibly. Your lander needs to distribute its weight like a snowshoe, and it needs to land *between* nodule fields if you can, not on them.

**External propulsion and navigation.** The bottom current runs steadily at 20-30 cm/s, braided and turbulent at centimeter scale. It will push a light vehicle, lift marine snow, and move mud ripples like slow dunes. You cannot orient yourself by sight — vision is a few meters in murk, and beyond that the nodule field dissolves into black. You need Doppler velocity log, inertial navigation, acoustic beacons to your mothership above, and sonar. There are no landmarks. The "hills" are hundreds of kilometers across and rounded like sleeping animals. To human eyes, mile after mile looks identical.

**Tether, or autonomy for loss of tether.** Sound is your only link to the surface, and it's slow, flattened, and easily lost. If you go alone with a short-range portal back, you have one failure point. Go with a crewed expedition or a remotely piloted support vehicle. For an ordinary traveler, alone is not brave here, it's unrecoverable.

Do not attempt this in a wetsuit, drysuit, or a "pressure suit" repurposed from space. Space suits are designed to keep pressure *in*. Down here, you need a hull designed to keep pressure *out* and not buckle under it from outside. That's a completely different engineering problem.

### 2. What Your Senses Will Actually Get

Prepare yourself for a mismatch between what looks crowded and what feels empty.

**Darkness:** It's not night. It's functional black. Bring your own lights, but understand the cost: your headlights will be the brightest thing for kilometers and will blind and disturb the rare animals that use bioluminescence. The tripod fish, rattail eel, sea pig, brittle stars, and glass sponges you might see use nodules as their only hard substrate in a world of mud. They are isolated by hundreds of meters because food is a dilute rain of dead plankton from far above. A brief flash is a startle. Use low-intensity, shielded lights, turn them down when you're just traveling, and expect long stretches of nothing but snow.

**Marine snow:** Every liter of water is full of it. It will coat your viewport in minutes. You need wipers, and you need to expect that "visibility" means you can see particles drifting past, turning in the current, not a clear blue. It will clog any intake, coat any camera, and settle on every upward-facing surface as a pale dust. That's not dirt — that's your ecosystem. It's food, falling for a month from the sunlight. Don't try to filter the water clean around you.

**Sound and silence:** Underwater sound travels far, so you might hear a whale through the deep sound channel 100 km away, flattened and distant. Close up, almost nothing makes noise. The current sighs through nodules. The turbulence is silent. Bring a hydrophone if you want to *hear* the place; your ears inside a hull will mostly hear the vehicle's own pumps, CO2 scrubbers, and your own breathing. The silence is not absence — it's absence of resonance. Water swallows impact. Nothing breaks.

**Cold and pressure inside:** Your hull will be warm, but the cold will seep through the hull structure and condense on everything. Expect condensation, expect to run heaters constantly, expect gloves and tools to be stiff. Your interior is comfortable; touch something outside without a heated manipulator and it's instant cold injury. The pressure outside will make any tiny leak into a jet. Check seals before descent, not after landing.

### 3. Psychological Preparation

This is probably the hardest part for an ordinary traveler.

You will be simultaneously claustrophobic and agoraphobic. Claustrophobic because you're in a small steel sphere, hundreds of meters from the nearest other human, with no way to open a window. Agoraphobic because outside the porthole, the plain extends past any horizon in every direction with no edge, no valley wall, no sky. It's regional, endless continuation.

And it is *slow*. Everything here operates on geological time. The black potatoes under your feet grew a millimeter at a time over millions of years. They began when human ancestors were still apes. A flake of snow you watch fall will feed a worm weeks from now. A sea pig track you make will persist for days, then be erased.

If you come expecting a dynamic landscape, you will be bored, then unsettled, then — if you stay long enough — weirdly calm. The turbulence at centimeter scale never repeats; the landscape at kilometer scale never changes. Bring books, bring a journal, bring something to do while you watch snow. The visit is not measured in sights per hour.

Do not go if you need constant novelty or constant contact. Communication to the surface is acoustic and delayed in meaning, not just time. You are alone in the way a person at the bottom of a well is alone, but the well is wider than the sky.

### 4. Practical Kit List for an Ordinary Traveler

1.  **Certified deep-submersible ride** — with a trained pilot, not a DIY portal sled.
2.  **Pressure-tested, heated suit for emergencies only** — not for walking. Assume you will never use it.
3.  **Redundant life support and CO2 scrubbing** — 72 hours minimum.
4.  **Hydrophone, low-light camera with wipers, and a soft, low-intensity light you can dim.**
5.  **Acoustic modem and a physical tether line** to the ascent vehicle.
6.  **Manipulator or sample scoop — but don't use it casually.** Nodules are irreplaceable habitat. A sponge grow as slowly as the nodule it sits on. Taking a fist-sized nodule is taking millions of years of growth and the only rock for miles. Photograph, don't collect, unless you are on a permitted scientific mission.
7.  **Navigation package: DVL, sonar, compass, and depth sensor.** Trust instruments over eyes.
8.  **Personal layering for the hull interior** — you will get cold spots, and after ascent you will be chilled in a way that surprises you.
9.  **Decompression plan for the return.** Even if your cabin stays at 1 atmosphere, your portal exit, oxygen stores, and medical kit need to account for a rapid return to the surface world. The bends are a risk if any part of your system pressurizes you.
10. **Emergency ascent ballast and locator beacon.** If the vehicle fails, your only chance is to be found, not to swim up. There is no up for a human here.

### 5. What Not To Do

**Don't walk on the nodules.** Even in a hard suit, your weight will crush them and the animals curled around them. The rule here is: if it looks like a rock, it's alive, or it's a million years old, or both.

**Don't trigger extra bioluminescence for entertainment.** A flash is a distress signal for the shrimp and fish that make it. We are visitors in a black room.

**Don't expect color.** At the bottom, what color there is — rust-black nodules, grey-white bacterial mats at cold methane seeps, pale fish — is seen only by your lights. The mats are sparse felt, not gardens. Don't try to stir them up.

**Don't leave a mark and think you've left nothing.** A track in mud slowly slumps back, but a crushed nodule, a dropped tool that sinks and is buried, a chemical leak from a battery — the currents won't clean it. This plain is undisturbed precisely because nothing made has ever been made here except by accretion. Keep it that way.

**Don't plan a short hop.** Descent to 4-5 km takes hours. Bottom time is limited by life support, battery, and human endurance. Ascent takes hours. A "quick look" is an all-day commitment, and if something goes wrong, a multi-day one.

### A Final Note

An ordinary traveler should go as a guest of an expedition, not as a solo explorer, and should arrive already humbled by the ride down. You will watch snow fall upward in the current eddies, watch a single pale fish sit tripod-like facing the rain, watch mud migrate like a dune under a wind that never stops and never hurries.

You will leave no footprint that lasts, see almost nothing move, hear almost nothing, and understand that you have briefly sunk into a place that was old before the surface world had mountains, and will continue, packed with particles, packed with nodules, packed with bacteria in mud that looks empty, long after you have ascended back into light and noise.

Prepare for the machinery, prepare for the cold, but mostly prepare to be small, slow, and silent in a place that rewards exactly that.
