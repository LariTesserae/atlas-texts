If you are an ordinary human - no augmented lungs, no heat-proof skin, no way to see in the dark the way the locals do - you do not "visit" this place for long. You enter it, prepared, for a short, very controlled immersion, and you leave before it finishes settling on you.

It is not night there, and it is not day. There is no sun to rise, no sky to look up to. The world is dark on top and faintly lit from below, by fungus, by veins in leaves, by algae films, by the bellies of half-buried grubs. The air is low, warm, and pressed down like a lid on a bowl. It is almost always at the point where sweat forms and does not dry. That one fact dictates almost everything about how to prepare.

### 1. Get your body ready before you go

You will be hot, wet, loud, and disoriented within minutes, and you will stay that way.

Train for heat and humidity, not for cold. Do 1-2 weeks of walking/working in near-100% humidity at 30-35C if you can. Your ordinary home sweat system is built for evaporation. Here evaporation almost stops. You will sweat constantly and cool very poorly. Heart rate stays high. You will lose salt fast.

Train for low light and for sound. Practice walking at night using only dim, downward light, letting your eyes dark-adapt for 20-30 minutes. Practice doing tasks - tying, filtering, eating, treating a cut - by touch and by whisper, because your voice will be taken by the noise almost as soon as you make it. This place is loud the way a crowd is loud: thousands of distinct voices, constant, layered, with no silence to rest in.

Get medical prep for wet, warm, living ground: tetanus up to date, wound care, antifungal cream, antihistamines, pain relief, electrolyte salts. Warm, humid wounds infect fast here. Blisters, chafing, and macerated skin are not minor annoyances; in this moisture they become portals. Bring moleskin, zinc oxide, and a habit of drying feet and changing socks before they break down. Bring a full course for parasites and fungal infection and do not be proud about using them. You *will* pick up mites, ticks of some kind, and spores.

Bring fitted, custom ear protection. Not total deafening - you need to hear a vent sigh, a tree start to tick, a swarm lift. Filtered plugs that knock 15-20 decibels off the chorus will save your hearing and your sanity on hour three. Also bring a mesh face veil and sealable goggles. The danger is not just biting insects. It's midges in eyes, spores in the nose when a bloom opens on a thousand plants at once, and a sheet of condensation rain shaken loose when a swarm passes overhead.

### 2. What to wear - cotton and leather will betray you

Wear one layer. Synthetic, quick-dry, light-colored so you can see leeches and ticks, with a weave tight enough to deter mites but open enough to dump heat. Permethrin-treat socks, trouser hems, and boot gaiters before you leave. A brimmed hat with a drip edge. A vented rain cape, not a poncho - a poncho will cook you.

Your feet are the mission. Forget walking shoes. You need high, waterproof boots with thick, puncture-resistant soles and real ankle support, with tall gaiters to keep basalt grit and pocket-water out. The ground is newborn: one step spongy mat of roots, the next hard ringing glass, the next black basalt shards fresh enough to slice boot leather, the next sulfur-yellow crust and polygonal mud that softens again in minutes. It heaves, it cracks open, it steams. Every ridge is a broken tooth.

Gloves, always. Not for warmth - for glass, for thorns, for vine hairs, for grabbing a sharp rock without opening your hand. A helmet or hard hat is not overkill. Trees rot through at the base and simply give up, vines as thick as wrists fall, condensation drops in sheets, and the ground shrugs stones off without warning. In a place where everything is falling apart and growing at the same time, things fall on you.

No perfume, no scented lotion, no bright colors. Everything here finds food by smell, heat, and light.

### 3. What to carry - and what not to

Bring your own water and food, sealed against humidity, and treat all local water as poisonous. There are no rivers. There are warm, soupy, knee-deep pocket-waters covered in a glowing film of floating plants, with frogs sitting up to their eyes. They evaporate and refill in the same place. They are not drinkable. They are a bacterial and parasitical soup, and the air over them can carry rot and sulfur gases. Do not drink them, do not filter them for drinking, do not wade in them if you can avoid it. If you are forced to cross one, move fast, dry off immediately, and disinfect any cut.

Bring enough water for your full stay plus a reserve, in a soft bladder and hard bottle, and drink in small sips with electrolytes. You will be wet everywhere and still dehydrate. Food should be high-calorie, low-odor when opened, impossible for flies to reach: sealed bars, paste tubes, dried rations eaten under a veil with food elevated off the ground. Do not eat local fruit, fungi, leaves, or frogs. Beautiful, glowing, and either toxic, hallucinogenic, or infested - and even if it isn't, you have no way to know.

Light: Bring it, but bring it wrong on purpose. A single dim, warm, downward-pointing lantern, shielded so it makes a small pool at your feet, plus a spare sealed in a dry bag. Do not use a white headlamp pointed forward. You will blind yourself for 20 minutes every time you look up, you will blind-adapted fauna, you will attract every eye in the dark - amber, green, curious, and hungry ones - and you will advertise yourself to the tool-using makers. Your eyes need that low glow from below. Let them learn it. Shield your phone, your watch, your travel device. Any bright screen undoes an hour of adaptation.

Electronics, paper, and leather will fog, swell, and rot in hours. Seal everything in dry bags, keep batteries warm and dry, keep your multiverse beacon / exit device on your body, charged, and protected from steam. Paper maps are a joke here - they will disintegrate and the ground will rearrange the landmarks anyway. Use a waterproof slate, a spoken countdown, and a physical tether rope if you travel with others. People get separated here by a swarm front, by a wall of webs, by three meters of vine. You will not hear each other call.

Do not bring seeds, tools of cooking, or anything you intend to leave. This is a closed, newborn system. Anything you drop will be colonized in days, and when you return home you must decontaminate boots, packs, and device thoroughly. Spores you cannot see will be in your pack.

### 4. How to move

Move slowly, test every step, and never trust a path. There is no path. What looks like a trail this morning is overgrown by tomorrow; a gully that was clear is now a fresh crack venting steam. Spider sheets webbed across glades will snag mist and you - push through with a stick, not your face.

Listen for steam before you see it. Vents sigh, hiss, fill a hollow with white mist, and give off a smell of hot stone and rot. The whole basin can hiss when the rock cools and ticks like knuckles. Do not step into cracks, do not lean over vents, do not camp in a hollow where vapor collects. If a vent puffs, step back, cover your nose, wait for insects to scatter.

When a swarm passes - a front that darkens the glow, shakes branches, and rains condensation - stop. Crouch low, shield your face with the veil, turn your light down or off, and let it pass. Do not run. Running in basalt and vines is how ankles break and hands get cut. The bigger herds/flocks that walk through a glade in a continuous body will block it for minutes. Get out of their line, get against a rock, let them go. Their eyes will catch your light. That is not an invitation.

Never touch the glow to see what makes it. Fungus shelves are lights for other things. Pale vines and translucent leaves are rafts for mites, perches for frogs, homes for chewers. Everything overlaps. If you break it, you break someone else's shelter, and something will be chewing on you in return.

### 5. Where and how to sleep - if you must

Do not sleep on the ground. The ground moves, heaves, steams, and is carpeted with worm, ant, and beetle pulses. Elevate yourself in a short hammock or on a raised platform slung between living trunks, but not so high you are in the drip line. You will not sleep well anyway: the chorus never stops, the light rises from below onto your face, condensation rains in bursts.

Use earplugs, a light shade that still allows air flow, and a schedule you impose yourself. There is no night to cue sleep. Set a timer for rest, hydrate, change socks, check skin, and eat sitting up with food covered. Do not build a shelter. Nothing built here persists. Woven nests dissolve in a week. Your camp will be tunneled, climbed, and reclaimed while you are in it. Stay light and ready to lift camp because the ground shifted, because a vine reached your shoulder, because the swarm you were standing in decided to move.

Feces and waste: pack it out. The litter decomposes fast, but it does not decompose safely with your gut flora in it, and the makers and other locals do not need your microbes. Do not bury it in pocket-water mud.

### 6. The makers

You may find them: a scatter of smooth heel-sized stones on a flat slab, piles of cracked shells in a meticulous repeated pattern. The makers themselves are glimpsed - small, quick, hand-like, face turned toward the glow. Leave the stones exactly where they are. Do not pick them up, do not stack them, do not clear the slab. Do not point your light directly at them. Do not follow them. Observe from a distance, briefly, without crowding. They have learned to use a tool and have not learned to build a place to keep it. That is their architecture. Respect it by not making yours next to it.

### 7. Know when to leave

Ordinary humans deteriorate here on a curve. Hour one: wonder. Hour three: sweat-blind, ear-tired, foot-sore, every surface damp. Hour six: fungal itch, chafing wounds, salt loss, disorientation because sound echoes off ridges and returns as a second voice. Your path back may not exist. New shoots will have crossed it. A tree will have fallen. A crack will have opened.

Set a hard exit time well before you feel you need it. Keep your exit beacon on you, not in a pack. Travel with a partner on a tether, with hand signals agreed in advance. If you are injured, if a vent burns you, if a cut looks hot and red, if you cannot stop coughing from spores - leave immediately. Infections spread in this heat and wet faster than at home, and flies arrive before the wound has stopped steaming.

On return, the work is not done: dry everything for 48 hours, disinfect boots and soles, wash thoroughly, check joints and groin and scalp for bites, launder hot, and watch for fever, rash, or lung irritation for a week.

Prepare, above all, to be inside the place, not looking at it. You will not get a view. You will get layers - near-touching presences, hotter and darker and wetter and louder than expected, reshaping themselves in minutes. The light is made, laboriously, by living things. The water is borrowed from air and earth. The soil is newborn and unstable. If you go in lightly, well-covered, well-hydrated, low-lit, tethered, and humble about how briefly you belong there, you can come back out with your skin intact and your ears ringing a little less.

If you go in as a tourist - cotton shirt, bright torch, sandals, bottle filled from a pocket-pool, sleeping on the moss - the basin will simply continue growing around you, and it will not notice you are gone.
