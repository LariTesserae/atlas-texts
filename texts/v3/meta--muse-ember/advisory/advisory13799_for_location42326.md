# Preparing for the Mat-World Plateau

This is not a hike so much as a temporary stay inside a living medium. The plateau is plant-built, perpetually wet, high, dim, muffled, and so slowly changing that a human visit is only a glimpse. Prepare accordingly: the goal is not to conquer distance or admire a view, but to move gently through a place that gives no horizon, no dry ground, and no fire—and then leave without carrying it home with you.

## First, reset your expectations

Do not go expecting the usual tools of travel to work.

- **No view, no map.** The horizon dissolves into mist. Distances must be judged by time, touch, and local landmarks—not by sighting a peak or following a valley. A printed map of the plateau is mostly decoration. Mark your own route with small, retrievable markers and always plan a direct backtrack.
- **The ground will not hold shape.** You walk on root mat, peat, moss, and rotting trunks that give, spring back, and slowly creep. A path may function for a while, but plants migrate, root terraces shift, fern patches move, and your footprints can fill with green if you sit still. There is no permanent trail to rely on.
- **You will be wet.** Everything drips, condenses, or seeps. Rain may fall from a single branch under a dry sky. Storms can form inside the plateau and wander for days. There is no normal “dry out” moment. Plan to live wet rather than fight it.
- **Fire is not an option.** Matches, lighters, stoves, and “survival” fires are useless. Anything burnable is saturated. Eat cold food, use chemical light sparingly, and do not plan on boiling water.
- **Sound and light are swallowed.** Shouting carries poorly. Your own voice may seem strange and close. Whistles and calls should be short-range only. At noon, the understory can feel like evening. Headlamps are essential, but bright white light is alien here.
- **Much of the life is tiny and hidden.** Frogs in bromeliads, slugs, fungi, tunneling mammals, transparent moths, birds in the canopy. The main risks are not predators; they are disorientation, sprains, infection, exhaustion, and falls—especially near the deceptive edge, where the mat thins into roots and water veils over cloud.

## Before you go

**Body preparation**
- Acclimate to altitude if you can. Thin air + saturated humidity + slow exertion is a hard combination. Walking should feel deliberately slow from the start.
- Train for humid heat: sweat will not evaporate well, so overheating can happen even without direct sun. Conditioning matters more than strength.
- Trim nails, check skin folds, and get medical clearance if you have asthma, allergies, recurrent skin infections, ear problems, or circulation issues.
- Keep the visit short. For an ordinary human, **3 to 10 days** is a sensible window; longer stays increase fungal skin problems, respiratory irritation, joint strain, and the chance of being disoriented by monotony.

**Mind preparation**
- Expect time to feel thick. The sun is filtered, the stars are invisible, and the sounds repeat: dripping, faint croaks, insects. Without routine, days blur.
- Bring a simple ritual: check your watch, mark your position, note direction, eat, rest, record a short voice memo. Boredom and the urge to “explore farther” are real hazards here.
- Practice being still without sitting on the mat. Rest in a hammock or on a raised root or rare boulder, briefly, then move on. Long stillness invites the place to begin reclaiming you.

## What to bring—and what to leave

**Footwear and clothing**
- Ankle-high, closed, broad-soled boots with good toe protection and tread for wet slime, not sharp cleats. Metal spikes can rust, slip on wet stone, and puncture the mat.
- Synthetic quick-dry layers. No cotton. No wool as a primary layer. Bring multiple synthetic socks, but understand that “dry” socks are only for sleeping and for the exit.
- A light waterproof shell for dripping canopy water, but expect condensation inside it. A wide-brim hat helps with drips and eye protection.
- Clear safety glasses or a light cap brim are more useful than sunglasses; the light is already dim. Glasses fog, so bring soft cloths and spare lenses if you use them.
- A bright-colored outer layer or bands for companions: in muted green gloom, hi-vis color is a practical safety tool.

**Navigation and light**
- Compass, watch/altimeter if available, waterproof notebook, pencil, and a few biodegradable or retrievable trail markers. Bring every marker back out.
- Headlamp with a low setting and, ideally, a dim red or amber mode. Use the minimum light. Let your eyes adapt. Do not shine light constantly into phosphorescent moss; observe it dark-adapted and close the moss gently afterward.
- Camera or phone in a dry bag, with anti-fog cloths. Landscape photography is mostly pointless; macro, sound, and texture are the real subjects. Electronics will fog and corrode in humidity—seal them, power them off when not in use, and do not expect satellite signal under dense canopy.

**Camp, food, water**
- Hammock with integrated bug net and a light rain fly, plus an insulating pad. Sleep **above** the mat, not on it. The mat may be man-high in places, and the ground is wet, creeping, and full of animal passages.
- Pack one hard dry container for transit papers, medication, and electronics. Everything else should be assumed to get damp eventually.
- Cold, shelf-stable food; no cooking required. Salt and electrolytes are important because sweat loss is constant and hard to notice.
- Water filter or purification tablets. Water is everywhere but not clean: it circulates through roots, animal tunnels, and detritus. Carry a day’s supply and refill from clean seepage or drips; do not drink from bromeliad cups—they are living habitats.
- Waste bag. Pack out all toilet paper and sanitary waste. Do not bury anything in the mat; the water table is essentially at the surface.

**Health kit**
- Waterproof wound dressings, antiseptic, blister care, antifungal cream, antihistamine, electrolyte packets, any prescribed inhaler or medication, tweezers, and a small mirror for skin checks. Any cut that stays wet and dirty can become infected quickly.
- A simple dust/spore mask can help for transit and for dusty gear handling, but saturated masks clog; bring spares.

**Leave behind**
- Matches, lighters, fuel stoves, fireworks, candles.
- Cotton clothing, canvas tents, paper maps you cannot afford to lose, knives for “cutting trail,” glass souvenirs, perfume, scented toiletries, and anything you would not mind becoming part of the substrate. The old stone places contain no metal or glass; do not introduce your own.
- Pets, seeds, soil, or untreated gear from other worlds. This is a quarantine-sensitive environment for multiverse travel.

## Movement rules

- **Slow down.** Half to one kilometer per hour is a reasonable pace in dense mat. Test each step; the surface may hold, then give. Avoid sudden weight on vaulted root sections.
- **Stay close.** Companions should remain within sight and easy voice range—often only a few meters. A light tether can help in the worst fog, but use it carefully so it does not snag epiphytes or pull you off balance.
- **Mark turns.** Anything that looks like a path may be a fallen trunk, a root terrace, or a drying water line. Do not trust it alone. Mark every significant turn and always keep a return route shorter than your outward exploration.
- **Do not approach the edge casually.** The “waterfall” is a fringe of roots pouring into mist above cloud. There is no dramatic cliff to warn you—just thinning mat and hidden air. Stay well back unless you are on a known exit route.
- **Refuse shortcuts through living structures.** Do not cut vines, pull epiphytes apart, or tear moss to make a passage. If you cannot pass without tearing, detour or turn back.

## Living there, briefly

- Eat and drink on schedule. Cold food and filtered water, every few hours. Dehydration here is insidious because you are surrounded by water.
- Keep your hammock camp tidy: food sealed, gear hung, waste packed out. Small animals are everywhere, and scented food invites trouble.
- Wash hands before eating and after touching plants, fungi, or stones. Unknown sap can irritate skin and eyes.
- Do not sit for long on the mat, leave gear on the ground overnight, or let the pause become permanent. If your boot prints begin to green, move.
- At night, reduce light and noise. Listen. You will hear dripping, croaks, insects, and the slow movement of mist. That is the environment; do not try to fill it.

## Etiquette for a place that absorbs things

- **Stone ruins:** photograph from a distance, note them, and leave them exactly as found. Do not clear moss, move stones, make rubbings, or take souvenirs. These structures are already being remade into substrate; your job is not to “restore” them.
- **Phosphorescence:** do not repeatedly pull moss aside for tours. Brief, gentle observation is enough.
- **Fruit and flowers:** do not eat unknown fruit, even if it looks translucent and inviting. Do not pick waxy flowers. Many things here are already on their way to becoming floor.
- **Water habitats:** do not drink from bromeliads, dip hands into frog cups, or drain small ponds. The place is full of micro-worlds that look empty to an impatient eye.
- **Silence:** no music, no loud calls, no banging on hollow stems. Sound is scarce and precious here; the mat muffles it for a reason.

## Emergencies

The most likely emergency is not an attack; it is being lost, sprained, ill, or exhausted. If lost:
1. Stop wandering. Wandering creates false trails.
2. Mark your position and time.
3. Sit or lie in the hammock or on a raised root/stone, not on the mat.
4. Signal with bright cloth and short whistle bursts, but do not exhaust yourself; sound carries poorly.
5. Activate your transit anchor or emergency beacon if you have one. Do not assume rescue can see you.
6. Conserve energy, keep drinking, and stay warm and as dry as possible in your sleep system.

Turn back immediately for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fever, confusion, inability to move at a safe pace, or any wound that is swelling, red, or leaking. In this environment, “pushing through” rarely ends well.

## Leaving and decontamination

Before transit out:
- Brush boots and gear thoroughly. Inspect every seam, fold, pocket, and strap for seeds, moss, slugs, frog eggs, fungal clumps, and bromeliad water.
- Remove all trail markers and waste.
- Shower and dry thoroughly on arrival. Check feet, groin, scalp, ears, and skin folds. Wash clothing and gear as hot as possible; quarantine damp equipment until inspected.
- Do not release any collected plants, water, or “souvenirs” in another world. A single frog in a leaf cup or a spore load in a boot can matter more than a dramatic predator.

The plateau rewards patience and punishes haste. Go in prepared to be cool, dim, damp, and nearly invisible; move slowly; keep your route reversible; touch as little as possible; and leave having added nothing foreign to a place that is already complete.
