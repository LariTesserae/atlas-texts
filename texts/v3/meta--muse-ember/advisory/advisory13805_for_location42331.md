### Preparing for Kersmill: A Traveller's Guide

Kersmill will not overwhelm you with altitude, gravity, poison, or any of the usual multiverse extremes. It will overwhelm you with damp, sound, smell, and slowness — and with how ordinary and close everything is.

You can see the whole place from the middle of it. A quarter of a mile, gate to gate. Ten minutes of slow walking. And yet you will never be dry, never be quiet, and never be far from anyone else's window, kettle, laundry, or work. Preparation for Kersmill is mostly preparation to accept that condition without fighting it, and to keep your body, feet, ears, and belongings from being slowly worn down by it.

#### 1. The mindset to bring

Do not come expecting wilderness, silence, or scenery. Kersmill is a working factory hamlet built entirely out of the marsh it stands in. The plants are not decoration — they are walls, roofs, roads, fences, filters, and fuel. The animals are almost absent. You can spend a week there and see nothing larger than a moth, a gecko on a warm wall at dusk, an eel below the sluice. A heron will pass high over, not landing.

That absence is part of it. So is the sound. The engine house in the center never stops. It is a slow thump you feel through the raft floors, a whine from the electric motors, clatter from the beaters, hiss of steam, constant dripping. At night the people quiet down and the machines do not. The town hums in its sleep.

And everything moves slowly. Not because people are lazy, but because the peat will not bear hurry and nothing heavy or impermeable can be built. A house that leans a degree to the south is not ruined — it's in balance for another decade. Change is measured in years. Come prepared to slow down to walking pace, to wait while a boardwalk is re-woven or a root is grafted into a pile, and to not try to fix, hurry, or improve anything yourself.

You will leave smelling of hot oil, wet peat, fermenting green matter, and worked fiber. That smell gets into hair and clothes. It's normal. Plan for it.

#### 2. Packing philosophy: light, soft, and damp-proof

This is the most important practical rule: **bring almost nothing heavy, hard, and wheeled.**

There are no roads for carts in town — streets are canals. Movement is causeway, boardwalk, veranda, and pole-boat. The causeway itself is a woven mat of root, palm, and packed fiber on piles that gives slightly under your boots. Boardwalks link house to house at second- and third-story level. Floors are rafts that float a little with the water table. A heavy, rigid suitcase on wheels will be useless, will snag on vines and low wires, and will load one spot on the peat badly.

Bring a soft, well-fitted backpack, 35-45 liters at most, that sits close to your back. Everything you own should be in dry bags, zip-sealed plastic, or at least a waterproof stuff sack inside it. The air is condensing. Steam beads on walls. Iron roofs drip for a full day after rain has stopped. Paper money, paper maps, books, leather, and unprotected electronics will warp, mold, and spot within a day or two.

Useful specifics:

* **Dry bag with a neck strap / belt pouch** for phone, wallet, passport-equivalents, and fare money. If you drop something off the causeway or a boardwalk, it's into black water between root mats. Tether it.
* **Waterproof notebook and pencil**, not pen. Paper still works when wet, pencil doesn't smear.
* **Small headlamp.** Morning white damp hangs low and lifts slowly. Afternoon is a sweat on the neck. Night is machinery-noise with very little light and wet boards underfoot. You will be glad of hands-free light on boardwalks and when stepping into a pole-boat.
* **Compact dry sack for dirty/wet clothes** — separate from your clean kit, because everything will pick up that sour fiber smell.

What to leave behind: wheeled luggage, heavy boots, cotton jeans and cotton shirts, leather shoes, a big umbrella (you will hit low wires, pipe-bridges, and vines, and poke people in a place this dense), a selfie stick, a tripod, a bicycle, a drone, a generator, plastic sheeting to lay down. Nothing impermeable can be laid here for a reason. Water stands; it does not run off. A tarp just makes a pool.

#### 3. Clothing: you need to breathe, dry, and not snag

It is warm, humid, and grey. Diffuse daylight turned a little grey by humidity, but you will still sweat and still need sun/heat protection — you just won't notice it as quickly because the air is already wet.

Cotton is your enemy. It absorbs, stays cold and clammy, and takes days to dry in air where laundry hangs over canals and still doesn't quite dry. Pack synthetics, merino, or light linen that dries fast.

A good kit:

* **2-3 quick-dry shirts, long-sleeved and light-colored.** Long sleeves protect against thorn fences, bamboo splinters, rough rattan, and brushing against the living, sedge-rooted thatch on roofs. You'll rinse them out; they should dry in a room, not just outside.
* **Quick-dry trousers, ankle-length.** The levee weeps, the water table is a spade-depth down, canals splash. Trousers should tolerate getting wet to the shin and dry by morning. No cuffs that drag in puddles.
* **A packable, breathable rain shell, not a heavy waterproof jacket.** A heavy jacket will cook you. You want something you can pull on when a roof-line starts dripping in earnest, and take off five minutes later. A wide-brim hat is better than an umbrella here.
* **Light work or grip gloves** for if you ride a pole-boat, handle rough root mat, or steady yourself on piles. They also protect against hot pipes and greasy belts — though you should not touch either.

Accessories: hat with a brim, light sunscreen (the grey light is misleading), insect repellent for *outside* the levee — inside, the noise keeps most insects out, but in the reeds and bamboo thickets beyond, there will be some. Keep it light.

#### 4. Feet: the most critical thing

Your feet will determine your visit. The ground is peat, mud, pressed fiber, ash, and living root, trodden into a buoyant substrate that has sunk a hand's breadth here and been jacked up there. Houses sit on driven piles, floors float, towns rise and fall a handspan between wet and dry seasons.

You need:

* **Flexible, ankle-high shoes or light boots with excellent grip on wet wood, root mat, and reed.** Not rigid mountaineering boots, not running shoes with smooth soles, not flip-flops, not heels. Something that can flex with a giving boardwalk, grip a slick board, and support your ankle on an uneven pile.
* **Something that drains and dries.** Leather and suede will soak and stay soaked. Low, breathable trail shoes with a good sole are usually better than heavy waterproof boots, unless rain is heavy — then a light rubber boot you can shake out is fine.
* **At least three pairs of synthetic or merino socks, in a dry bag.** Plus a small ziplock for the wet pair. Constant damp causes maceration, blisters, and fungal foot problems very quickly. Change socks at midday even if you think you don't need to. Dry your feet properly at night. Bring blister plasters, foot powder or anti-fungal cream, and antiseptic.
* **No bare feet, ever,** on causeways, boardwalks, or canal edges. Piles rot at the waterline, iron rusts and blooms orange then darkens, bamboo splinters, canal water is warm, green-black, and full of mill wash-water. Keep skin covered.

Walk like the people do: slow, testing boards with your toe, not jumping onto a raft floor, not running across a pipe-bridge. A boat wake can make a house rock slightly. The engine's thump is vibration, not an earthquake. Don't be alarmed, don't be careless.

#### 5. Ears, sleep, and lungs

The sound is continuous and it is the governing fact after wetness. There is no quiet corner, only muffled corners. If you are staying more than a day, pack:

* **Foam earplugs (and a spare pair)** for sleeping. Machinery does not stop at night. A sleep mask helps too — diffuse light by day, but workshop light and low wires at night.
* **Light earmuffs or filtered plugs** for time in or near the engine house. The flywheel, beaters, and motors are loud enough to cause fatigue over a day, and the thump is felt through the floor as much as heard.
* **A lightweight dust mask / bandana** and any allergy or asthma medication you normally need. Reed dust, fiber dust, mold and moss on north-side roofs, hot oil, woodsmoke, steam — the air is workable, but it is not clean in the way a forest is clean. If you have hay fever or reactive airways, assume you will need your inhaler/antihistamine.
* **Lip balm and electrolyte drink mix.** You will dehydrate in humidity without feeling properly thirsty, because sweat doesn't evaporate well. Water everywhere does not mean water you can drink. Canal water holds rust and green, wash-water is led through cattail filter beds before release — that is treatment, not drinking water. Do not drink from canals, ring canal, or wash-houses. Bring your own bottle and refill where locals are drinking, or carry a filter. Wash hands after handling fiber, mud, or boat.

Bring a basic first-aid kit: antiseptic, bandages, tweezers for splinters, anti-fungal cream. Your tetanus status should be up to date — rusted iron, rotting piles, and peat mud are everywhere. There is no exotic disease described, and no animals to fear, but stagnant warm water and fermenting matter mean any open cut should be kept covered and washed, and you should not wade or swim, even where an eel turns under a sluice.

#### 6. Moving around a tiny, vertical town

Orientation is easy and tricky at once. Easy because you can count roofs from the center and the engine house is always in the middle, humming. You can walk gate to gate in ten minutes.

Tricky because the town is layered. Water level: canals and moored pole-boats. Causeway level: workshops, market stalls, wash-houses. Above that: dwellings with verandas linked house to house. Overhead: pipes, wires, vines, drying lines. You can walk the length of town without touching the ground — which also means you can easily walk *into* someone's veranda, or onto a roof garden, without realizing it's private.

Practical rules:

* **Stay on boardwalks and the causeway.** Do not step into nursery beds where new root-mat is grown, drying racks of thatch, fuel piles, or filter beds. Do not sit on canal banks — they are woven root, renewed as they rot, and will give way. Do not pull at living roofs, vines trained to shade pipe-bridges, or living fences.
* **Ask before entering a house, workshop, or veranda**, before photographing people at windows (windows look onto other windows close enough to hear a kettle), and before riding a pole-boat. Boats move at walking pace; sit low, keep your hands in, and step with the boatman's timing. A boat loaded with fiber will not hurry for you.
* **Duck under low wires and pipe-bridges.** Electric motors hum off the same shaft; wires are strung low between roofs. Do not touch wires, belts, steam joints, or pipes. The belts creep thick and greasy — slow, but they will pull. Steam hisses at joints and condenses on walls.
* **Expect low visibility in the morning.** The white damp lifts slowly. The causeway over black water is raised a hand's breadth on piles; the edges are not obvious. Go slower until it burns off.
* **Expect dripping long after rain.** Don't stand under eaves if you can avoid it. Paths along iron roofs are drip-lines.

#### 7. Social and environmental ground rules (unspoken but real)

Kersmill is dense to the point of intimacy. Nothing is far from anything else. That means:

* Keep your voice down. You will be heard through a window, on a veranda, over a canal.
* Don't block a canal, a boardwalk, or a pipe-bridge. People carry baskets, poles, and fiber at the same unhurried pace. Work around them, not in front of them.
* Don't litter, don't dig, don't drive in a stake, don't lay a mat, don't add fertilizer or seeds. The economy is closed in a literal sense: marsh feeds mill, mill waste is burned for steam, roofs feed houses. Anything you leave becomes peat. Pack out everything, including food scraps.
* Don't help with repairs unless invited. A leaning house, a sagging walkway, a slumping canal bank — locals judge when to re-weave, graft a new root, or jack a floor. Your well-intentioned weight or tool can unbalance it.
* Don't expect privacy, quiet, or wildlife. You will hear the engine through the floor of your room. You will see plants, not birds. The gecko is a treat. Leave it alone.
* Fire caution: thatch roofs with living sedges, fuel piles, hot oil, woodsmoke, steam. No smoking indoors or near racks and piles, no open flame in workshops unless invited.

#### 8. Timing

If you have a choice:

* **Dry season** is easier: water a handspan lower, boardwalks a little more stable, less weeping from the levee. Wet season everything is higher, wetter, and more fragrant with fermentation.
* **Mid-morning** is the sweet spot: fog lifting, before afternoon heat settles like a sweat on your neck. Arrive on the causeway then if you can.
* **At night,** plan to be in and settled. You will navigate by sound and dim light, boards will be slick with condensation, and the hum will be the only guide.

A day visit is entirely possible. An overnight is fine if you have earplugs and dry socks. A week is fine if you are willing to live damp and hear the flywheel in your feet.

#### Quick checklist

- Soft backpack under 45L, everything in dry bags
- 2-3 quick-dry long-sleeve shirts, quick-dry trousers, packable rain shell, wide-brim hat
- Flexible grippy ankle shoes/boots that drain, 3+ pairs dry socks in dry bag
- Earplugs, eye mask, light earmuffs
- Headlamp, waterproof notebook + pencil, phone in tethered dry pouch
- Bandana/dust mask, allergy/asthma meds, electrolyte mix
- First aid: antiseptic, bandages, blister plasters, anti-fungal cream, tweezers
- Light gloves, insect repellent, sunscreen
- Your own drinking water / filter, portable snacks
- Patience

Come light, come slow, come prepared to be damp and to hear a small town whole, all the time. Kersmill does not need you to conquer it. It needs you to walk gently on a floating ground, duck under its wires, respect its living roofs and filter beds, and let its slow, loud, warm continuity go on around you.
