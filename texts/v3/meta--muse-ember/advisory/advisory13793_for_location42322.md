This is not a place you tour. It is a place you briefly enter, and then leave exactly as you found it. Prepare accordingly.

The mound on the white mineral flat is knee-high from outside, a low, flattened dome of bleached, woven material with one narrow slit for a mouth. Inside is a sealed pocket about six steps long, just wide enough to touch both walls with your arms out, and less than a man's height at the top. You cannot stand up in it. You enter on your knees, you move on your knees, and you back out on your knees.

Everything inside - floor, walls, ceiling, the thick hidden body of the mound itself - is the same thing: thousands of small, hollow, finger-length loops, slightly curved, tapered at the ends, each end tucked under its neighbour, fused together with pale mineral skin. It is at once plant and stone. Newer loops toward the surface are buff and springy. Older ones are bone-white, pearl-grey, faintly blue, papery-hard as thin ceramic, and they click and hold when you press them. There is no soil, no rock, no wood separate from it. If you put your hand down, you cover a dozen loops. The surface is terrain at the scale of a hand: little cliffs where a layer settled on another, little saddles, little basins of ground-down dust that was once loops and is now cemented again.

That density, that cold, and that sound are the whole experience. There are no inhabitants to meet, no weather to wait for, no seasons, no variation between visits. It is a closed, finished equilibrium. It will be identical when you arrive, identical an hour later, identical next year. Do not plan to wait for something to happen.

### How it will feel

**Sound, first and loudest.** You hear it before you see the mound. Outside it is a steady, wide hum braided with thousands of small ticks, pops, and dry creaks, repeating without variation. Inside, it is louder and unmistakably from the walls themselves. The low, cool outside current is filtered through millions of hollow loops acting as reeds, and the pale mineral skin answers bright light with endless micro-settling - a loop contracting a fraction, resin sighing, a brittle end seating against its neighbour.

It is not alive. There are no insects, no animals scratching, no birds. The ticks do not stop, speed up, or change pattern. Ordinary speech inside is absurd - you will be talking into a sealed room full of bells that never shout and never go quiet. When you shift your weight, the floor gives a hollow boom under your knee, the whole chamber resonating through its woven thickness. Move slowly, as if you were walking on a drum.

**Light, bright but not warm.** Outside is glare off white mineral. Inside, paradoxically, is if anything brighter. Light enters not in beams but in hundreds of pinpoints through the tiny gaps between loops - the weave does not quite seal - and then it reflects wall to wall until shadow has almost nowhere to settle. It will wash out a phone camera, tire your eyes quickly, and make you want sunglasses even though there is no sunbeam to face. There is no day-night change in there to use.

**Cold, dry, clean air.** Inside the air changes at the slit: still, mineral, cold enough to feel on your teeth. The woven mass drinks heat and holds cold, so your palm and knees will come away chilled fast. Your breath will not fog. There is no dampness anywhere, no condensation, no smell of rot, wet earth, or decay - just dry dust, old resin, and a faint clean mineral tang. Your eyes, lips, sinuses, and skin will dry out faster than you expect for such a cool place.

**Touch and crampedness.** Nothing is sharp; every edge has been rounded by age. Everything is dry, cool, and brittle at the edges. You cannot widen the space. The walls answer everywhere. Even if you are not normally claustrophobic, the fact that every surface is the same repeated presence, turned in every direction, with light coming through pinholes like stars, can disorient you. You have no shadow to orient by, no soft surface to rest your elbows.

**The green.** There are perhaps five living shoots in the whole chamber. Pale green-grey, no thicker than a root hair, they grow a hand's length out of a crack, stiffen, and fasten with a curling tendril to a neighboring loop. One may be half-mineralized along its length, living green at the tip grading into bone-white rigidity behind it. They do not sway, do not visibly grow while you watch, and are the only obviously living things there. They feed on mineral dust, salts, light, and dry resin chemistry - not water, because there is none. Do not touch them. They are the whole living margin of the place.

Outside, the ecology is just as closed: a low mat of climbing stems on a bright, cool flat, each generation hooking, dying, stiffening, becoming scaffold for the next, until the living center shrank to this pocket and the dead outer mass became the room. No animal eats or burrows. No fungi soften it to loam. No rain washes it. Dust becomes resin becomes fibre becomes dust, in place, forever. That is why nothing is scarred, fresh, or changing.

### What to bring

Travel light, dry, and small enough to fit through the slit.

**For your body:**
* Knee pads and elbow pads, and thin leather or work gloves. You will be kneeling on the loop floor for the entire visit, and the mineralised dust and resin will coat bare skin. Thin gloves so you can still feel how brittle and springy the loops are.
* Warm layers for contact, even if the outside looks bright. A warm base layer, warm socks inside sturdy shoes. You will lose heat through your knees and palms.
* Sunglasses for the flat *and* for inside. The glare outside is snow-like; the trapped light inside is nearly as tiring.
* A simple dust mask or buff for nose and mouth, lip balm, and saline eye drops. The dust is dry, mineral, fine as powder, and there is no moisture to settle it.
* All your own water, with salts. There is no water inside, on top, or nearby to find, drink, splash, or be offered. The air is cool, still, and dry. Do not bring water to pour, spray, or moisten anything - moisture is foreign to this system.

**For your senses and gear:**
* Foam earplugs or low-profile hearing protection. The hum does not get louder in a way you can predict and does not habituate because of the unending ticks. A 15-20 minute visit is plenty; an hour of that texture will leave you fatigued and strangely deaf to normal sound.
* No flashlight needed. The interior is already bright. A flashlight will only create glare on reflective walls. If you carry a camera, expect overexposure and white-balance problems; do not use flash.
* A small, soft-sided pack that can go *in front* of you when you crawl, not on your back. A bulky backpack will not fit the slit and will catch on loop ends. Tie back loose sleeves, tuck jewelry, zip pockets. The loop tips are not sharp, but they are interlocked and brittle.
* A notepad and pencil if you want to communicate inside. You will not be heard, and tapping to communicate will boom through the chamber. If you go with a partner, one person waits outside at the slit. Agree on a time limit beforehand - voices do not carry out.
* A timer. Time feels held in there because nothing varies. Set a phone alarm, vibrate only, to remind yourself to leave before monotony becomes discomfort.

**Do not bring:**
Food, scented products, plants, soil, water bottles you might spill, insect repellent, or anything to leave behind. Nothing here decomposes, nothing scavenges, nothing washes away. Trash, crumbs, bacteria, or moisture you introduce will remain visible in the equilibrium forever. Clean your boots and gloves on the flat outside before entering.

### How to visit

1. **Listen from outside first.** Walk the flat carefully - white mineral, sparse pale shoots a hand's breadth apart, no shade, no landmarks, easy to get disoriented. Do not crush the outside shoots. When you hear the hum get steady and braided, you are close.
2. **Look at the mound as a mound.** It is easy to mistake it for a collapsed thicket. Knee high, irregular, bleached straw and bone in color. Find the low, dark slit between two woven bulges. That is the only entrance.
3. **Enter slowly, on your knees, one person at a time.** Knees down first. Move in small increments, distribute your weight, do not stamp or drag. Expect the floor to boom softly under you and the ticks to continue unchanged. Do not pull yourself up by the walls - the bands you see are older surfaces fused into the wall, and the surface loops can click, settle, and powder.
4. **Sit still for a few minutes and let the monotony register.** Notice the pinpoints of light, the cold palm, the mineral tang, the green tendril mineralized halfway. That is the whole of it. There is no inner room, no view, no wildlife, no second chamber beyond the shallow bowl at the far end where the weave pinches nearly shut and opens again.
5. **Leave backwards, just as slowly.** The glare outside will hit harder after the trapped brightness, and the hum will lose its resonance but not stop. Do not pat the mound, take a loop as a souvenir, or try to widen the slit. The place is stable because weight is shared in all directions and because nothing is added or removed. Your weight, your moisture, your noise are the only disturbances it will ever get.

### Who should not go in

If you have bad knees or back, cannot crouch for several minutes, are claustrophobic in small bright spaces, have respiratory sensitivity to mineral dust, or are easily overwhelmed by constant broadband sound, observe from outside. That is a complete visit in itself - hearing the single unchanging note from the mound in the cool air is what most travelers remember.

Do not expect help, signs, paths, trade, language, or a change in conditions if you wait. The mound does not respond to visitors, and in the multiverse it does not fork into a variant with rain, or a living animal, or a different season. That refusal to change is not a waiting state. It is the finished state.

Go in briefly, quietly, dry, warm, and empty-handed, and let it go on sounding after you.
