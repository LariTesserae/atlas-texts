### Preparing for The Elder Table

You will not know when you have arrived. There is no cliff to climb, no gate, no change in glow. The ground simply keeps going, higher than the weather that makes it, under an ordinary white-gold sun. Mentally, that is the first thing to prepare for: The Table is not a dramatic otherworld. It is a finished, worn-down, cold place that has been going on for so long after its ending that going on has become its natural state. The strangeness is not in the light. It is in the silence, the repetition, and the very slow way everything here revises itself.

As an ordinary human — no roots, no ability to stand still for a month, no tolerance for moss as food — you need to be entirely self-sufficient, patient, and a little stubborn about not trusting your senses in the usual ways.

#### 1. The Body: Cold, Sun, and Thin Air at the Same Time

Expect to be sunburned and frostbitten in the same hour, while dehydrated and only mildly altitude-sick.

The cold lives in the stone itself. You can stand with your back cooking in the sun while your shaded side goes numb in minutes. Your breath will be visible at midday. Wet stone steams faintly in the sun but stays cold through to the damp inside it. A metal tool left out overnight will be stiff with frost by dawn.

Pack for asymmetry, not for a season:

* **Layers you can rotate.** Wool or synthetic base layers, a windproof outer cloak. The rootfolk use woven moss-fibre and grass; that is a sensible model. You want something that works when one side is in full sun and the other is in a wind that has circled the whole Table and come back damp and smelling of peat.
* **Sun protection as serious as cold protection.** High, thin air, sharp noon shadows, bleached lichen. Hat with ear and neck coverage, sunglasses, sunscreen, lip balm. You will burn while feeling cold.
* **Insulation from the ground.** Do not sleep on a rock that feels warm on top. It is cold inside. Bring a thick pad, not just a blanket. Four-season bag rated well below what the temperature suggests — nights can drop below the usual cold and hold there.
* **Boots, gaiters, and poles.** The Table is not mountaineering. Cliffs are rounded to old bone, there is nothing to climb. It is walking: endless low swells, stone that ticks like a knuckle cracking as it cools, pavements heaved a hair's breadth by frost, slabs dished in the middle by ten thousand feet. Then, for no reason, you step off stone onto moss as deep as a mattress. Your step hushes, lands soft, and leaves a footprint for a week. That transition is where ankles turn. Poles and waterproof boots help on both stone and spongy peat.
* **Keep metal warm.** Knives, awls, stove parts, water bottle. Sleep with them in your bag or boots, wrap handles in wood or cloth. Iron will be unreliable in the morning.
* **Fitness for distance without landmarks.** You may walk six days over bare stone seeing nothing taller than a boot, then cross a fern-clump in an hour, then six more days to the first town. It may be the fifth day or the seventh, depending on which way the weather moved you. Carry food for at least 14 days between settlements, and be prepared to go longer.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects over the flowers, no fish in the pools, no worms in the peat, no dogs or chickens or flies in town, no meat. That is not a figure of speech. You cannot fish, hunt, forage in any normal sense, or rely on anything to eat your waste. What falls — leaves, and by extension anything you drop — lies pale and recognizable for decades. Pack out waste in sealed bags. Bring a small trowel, but know that peat is thick, slow to decay, and will remember your footprint.

Bring bug spray and fishing gear and leave them at home. Bring calories instead: dry, dense, vegetarian rations — grains, dried fruit, nuts, tea, salt — that will not stink or rot quickly in a place that doesn't really rot things.

#### 2. Water: Always Arriving, Never Abundant

There are no lakes or broad rivers. Water arrives as condensation ticking off stones at dawn, as an eleven-minute sideways mist that beads on your cloak and hisses on warm stone, as springs in cracks that never change shape and may shrink in three days to a damp stain with a collar of green, and as a few still, black, shallow pools ringed with frost and moss.

You can drink from the pools and springs. It tastes of stone and lichen and is always slightly out of date. That last part matters: rain that fell on Wednesday may still be falling in a cistern. Do not assume water is fresh in the normal sense.

Practical system:

* Carry 2-3 days' capacity, not more. Weight is your enemy.
* Filter and boil when you can, even though there are no animals to contaminate it. The risk here is age and stagnation, not beasts.
* In town, roofs drain by carved channels to cisterns. Ask before taking from a cistern — it may be looping old rain.
* Catch condensation deliberately. A shallow bowl, a tilted slab, a small canvas left out overnight will collect more than you expect. The Table waters itself stone by stone.

Do not count on a spring being the same size tomorrow. It is fixed in place, but it can diminish.

#### 3. Weather and Navigation: Measure in Storms, Not Miles

People here do not measure distance in miles. They measure in weather: one storm away, two thaws, a forgotten season of cloud.

Clouds are born here in vertical stacks, fatten, split, and die within sight. You can watch a storm assemble a hundred miles away for a day while the sun shines directly on you, and a smaller, forgotten storm can rain itself out between two ridges right overhead. A wind can circle the whole Table and return to you changed.

So: watch overhead stacks as much as the horizon. Watch the horizon all day for a dark smear that is growing — it may be your weather tomorrow. Do not assume a distant storm will pass over you, or that clear sky above you means clear sky a mile ahead.

Navigation is the real preparation:

* **Maps will betray you, gently.** Town maps are redrawn, sometimes in ink that smells of damp and is still wet in the morning. A storehouse shown to you in the morning may have an extra, well-built room by evening. A street entered from the south can deposit you facing north across ground you haven't crossed.
* **Use a compass for absolute direction, not for the map.** North may still be north, but the town may not be where the map insists it is.
* **Shadows lie at noon.** For about ten minutes a day a shadow may point exactly opposite to where the sun requires it. The rootfolk's shadows are sometimes cast by trees that are not there. Do not navigate by shadow. Stop, eat, wait.
* **Echoes arrive early.** Your echo off a low swell may come a fraction before you shout. A bell in a distant town is rung before the hammer that forges it is struck. Do not shout for help across distance, and do not use sound to judge distance or order. If you hear a bell, the hammering hasn't happened yet — it is about to.
* **Paths move if they are not walked, and reappear worn if they are.** Footprints in peat and frost will last a week; on stone there are no tracks at all except the old ones in a town's mud, which are not animal tracks. Retracing your own peat footprints is often your best way back to camp. A new path you make today may be gone tomorrow unless you keep walking it.
* **Landmarks repeat.** The same lichen pattern — yellow crust here, dull green smear there, ochre and iron-red where damp sits longest — can appear a mile away and then again frosted on a window. A flower appears in two hollows at once because the Table remembers the plural. Do not panic that you are lost when you see a familiar pattern. You may be in the Table's second copy of it. Note colors: ochre-red stone means damp has sat there longest, and green pools are likely near.

Write in pencil, not ink, and write twice. Keep a plain journal and expect entries to feel revised, out of order, or déjà vu. That is normal here. Dates are less reliable than weather signs.

Bring a compass, a notebook, several pencils, and a willingness to ask inhabitants rather than trust paper.

#### 4. Shelter, Fire, and the Towns

You will not find a cave or a dramatic shelter. The land is too polished for that. Windbreaks are made: walled courtyards, low narrow doorways against the wind, roofs of woven grass, turf, and hand-hammered copper sheet.

In town, keep your belongings low and on the ground. A basket on an upper floor may be found, carefully, on the ground by afternoon — that is not theft, that is the town losing a storey and someone taking the basket down. Walls themselves may straighten for an hour on a very cold night, proud and impossible, then sigh back into their slump. Do not sleep wedged against a wall. Do not leave a tool in a crack — the crack may seal with a soft ticking around it.

Fire is scarce. The kiln is cold now. Low birches and tallow-trees are stunted, wrapped in hanging moss, and slow to grow. Peat is deep and wet. Bring a small, efficient portable stove and conserved fuel. An open fire is an emergency measure in a stone hollow, not a routine. Metal stoves will be stiff with frost; keep them warm.

Towns are built in layers: pavement slabs over older pavement over cobbles. Houses are megalithic, repaired by hand forever by laying the same stone back in the same place until the place remembers it. Nothing is new, nothing is quite broken. If you bring new, shiny gear, expect it to look older and more worn after a night or two. That is not necessarily damage. The Table revises new things until they fit.

That is why repair kit matters more than luxury: needle and thread, copper awl, simple knife, spare laces, patches, a stone hammer if you can carry one. The people here value maintenance over novelty.

#### 5. The Rootfolk: Etiquette for People Who Are Gardens

They are not animals, and they do not think that strange. They stand rooted politely in deep planted beds along walls, dark taproots visible where soil has washed away. Their hands are jointed and woody, bark split over the knuckles, moving with great patience over looms and stonework. They grow a finger's length in warm weather, stand perfectly still in cold snaps, and shake moss off their shoulders in a slow green rain.

They speak very little, in low dry clicks that carry almost nowhere even in this great quiet. To speak with them you must get close and be quiet yourself. Do not shout — your shout may arrive before it leaves.

Practical etiquette:

* **Do not step in the beds.** The planted beds along walls are their footing, their floor, their place. Walk around them. Wash your feet before entering a courtyard if you can.
* **Do not handle them as if they are trees or people exactly.** Do not pull at roots, do not pluck moss from shoulders unless invited. Do not rush them. Their pace is part of the place.
* **Do not eat meat in town.** There is no meat here. There are granaries with stone doors, looms in open workshops, racks of tools. Offer help with mending, weaving, laying a stone back, tending soil — that is their language.
* **Accept revision without argument.** If they add a room to their map, or redraw it, or the map redraws itself, they will not be dismayed. Do not insist on your version. Ask, in the moment, where the storehouse is today.
* **Trade in skills and small durable things:** thread, needles, copper wire, pencils, salt, dried food, a spare knife. Not pets, not livestock, not live seeds — there is no animal life to be part of the terms here, and imported life will be revised or will linger uneaten for decades.

Their shadows may be cast by trees that are not there. Do not use that to judge who is present.

#### 6. The Backward Erosion and How Not to Panic

At night, especially when temperature drops below its usual cold and holds, the Table erodes backward. A step worn hollow in the morning is shallower at dawn. A crack seals. A fern that unfolded yesterday curls to a bud and opens again from the bud, as if yesterday was practice. A shadow points the wrong way for ten minutes. A town gains and loses a storey.

This is not malice. It is a place that reached its oldest stable state — the shapes of its first hills, its first rivers, its first towns — and must keep repeating them to stay stable. New things that do not fit are gently revised until they do.

For a traveler, that means:

* Do not leave critical gear in a crack, under a stone, or hanging on a wall that may move.
* Do not mark your trail with a fern, a fragile cairn, or a chalk line. It may unfold, refold, or vanish. Use your own footprints and weather memory.
* If your echo beats you, if a bell comes before its hammer, if a lichen pattern repeats from a mile away to a window — note it, sit down, have tea. Staying present is safer than trying to correct it.
* Injuries, tools, and supplies will not behave in a simple timeline. Keep first aid close, not stored high. Frostbite, blister, sprain, sunburn, and dehydration can all happen without dramatic cause.

The stone ticking of its own accord is not something moving. The moss pools — knife-ferns taller than you, reed-thick grass with grey-blue bells that open only in the warmest minute of the day — are not to be picked for markers. They will refold and reopen elsewhere.

#### 7. Mind: The Longest Part of the Pack

This may be the hardest preparation. Days of bare stone, no birdsong, no insects, no animal tracks, no sound except your steps and the wind finding the tiniest edge to complain against, and stars sharp and severe at night. The horizon is only weather, moving out to meet you.

Ordinary human minds expect a bird, a cry, a wing. When none comes, for a long time, you will feel the absence in your body. Bring something to fill the mind without filling the silence too aggressively: a book, a harmonica, a string of prayer beads, a memorized song. Read aloud sometimes, knowing your echo may precede you — it is unsettling the first time, then simply part of the place.

Expect disappointment at first with the ordinariness of the sun. That is correct. The Table does not glow. Its wonder is that everything lush pools in hollows three feet deep after hours of nothing, that a town behind you does not fade but becomes a pattern in lichen under your boots at a certain distance, that everything is still in motion — frost heaving, peat thickening, clouds born and unborn — while looking utterly final.

Do not go looking for an edge. The Table continues. Far edges are lost in plain blue distance. A wind that circles it will bring back old weather, damp and peat-smelling. If you smell peat an hour after a dry wind, you are not necessarily near a pool; the weather has been around the world of the Table and come back.

#### A Minimal Pack List to Start From

* Layered windproof clothing, spare socks, insulated boots, gaiters, sun hat, two weights of gloves
* Thick sleeping pad, cold-weather bag, bivy or windproof cloak
* Poles, compass, pencil notebook (multiple pencils), headlamp
* Portable stove + fuel, water filter, 2-3L capacity, collapsible bowl for condensation
* 14+ days dry vegetarian food, salt, tea
* Repair kit: needle, thread, awl, knife, patches, cord
* First aid for frostbite, sunburn, blisters, sprains, altitude
* Sunscreen, sunglasses, lip balm
* Sealed waste bags, small trowel
* Small trade/help items: thread, needles, copper wire, pencils, dried food
* Something for the mind: book, music, beads

Leave at home: climbing gear, fishing and hunting gear, insect nets, pets or livestock, fresh meat, valuables you plan to leave unattended, and any expectation that a map, a shadow, an echo, or a clock will tell you where and when you are in the way you are used to.

Go slowly. Walk the same line if you want it to stay a path. Keep your tools warm, your water filtered, your feet dry, and your maps in pencil. And when the town behind you becomes a pattern in the lichen, do not try to watch it fade. It hasn't faded. It has simply finished being a town at that distance, and gone back to being part of the Table — which is, and has been for a very long time, practicing how to go on.
