If you go in expecting a cave, you'll be wrong in the most important ways. This isn't a natural hot spring and it isn't an old ruin.

It's a brand-new, man-made bath chamber, built underground out of brick and lime, and it works like a machine. That changes almost everything about how you prepare.

Think: swimming in a very bright, very loud, very warm laundry room that someone has just finished tiling. No earth, no cold, no animals, no hiding places. Just one room, 3/4 full of clear hot water, with the ceiling low, the walls white, and the water always moving.

Here is how an ordinary, unaugmented human should get ready for it:

### 1. The Mindset

**It will feel bigger than it is, and hotter than you think.** The vault throws sound back at you. A drip sounds like a stone in a well, the wooden sluice banging on its frame sounds like a door slamming, the inflow pipe roars in your ears. You will have to shout across a room you could throw a ball across. Sound is the main disorienter here - it makes a 20-pace pocket feel cavernous and claustrophobic at the same time.

**Depth is lying to you.** The water is that impossible turquoise-green, but the floor under it is white-washed brick, bleached almost white by the sun through the vents. You can see the far wall through the water as clearly as the near one, which means you get *no* normal depth cues. The entrance steps are waist-deep, the channel along the far wall is chest-deep, and the deep corner by the spring is up past your chin, with only a curved lung of hot air left above it under the arch. Where you are depends entirely on where you are standing, not what you see.

**It is empty on purpose.** No fish, no snails, no insects, no mud bank to crawl out on. The water is too warm, too lime-rich, too fast and too regularly scoured for anything to live in it except algae films, bacteria skins on the slack water, and a few tufts of salt-grass and a fern holding on in the mortar above the waterline. You don't need to prepare for bites, stings, or predators. You *do* need to prepare for the uncanny feeling of swimming over a perfectly paved, perfectly empty floor while steam thickens around you.

### 2. What to Wear

Leave the hiking kit at the door.

**Swim clothes, not hiking clothes.** Cotton, wool, jeans, and boots will get waterlogged, heavy, and cold-stealing on the way out - and there is no cold in here to warm up from. Wear a normal swimsuit or quick-dry synthetic trunks/shorts and a top that doesn't trap heat. Remember this is a workplace by day - workers come down to soak wool, hides, and laundry - so don't treat it like a private natural spring.

**Foot protection, but grippy.** The brick is still sharp. Not rounded, not softened. That cracked brick at shoulder-height, the thumbprints in the mortar, the chisel marks - under water that sharpness will cut feet that are numb from heat. Bare feet will grip the algae-slick white brick better than smooth soles, but they risk cuts.

Best compromise for an ordinary traveler: thick-soled aqua socks or water shoes with real tread, not flat pool slippers. Test each step. The submerged bricks are warm to the touch, so you won't get a cold warning before a slip.

**No hat, no heavy pack.** The tunnel comes in low and dog-legged. You have to turn sideways around a jutting sluice-beam, and the ceiling in the deep corner is almost at water level. Anything on your head, anything tall on your back, is a liability. Bring in only what you can carry in your hands or a small dry bag at your waist.

### 3. What to Bring (and Leave)

**Bring:**
* **Drinking water for before and after.** 30-something degrees, steam-thick air, and immersion in hot water even up to your waist will dehydrate you faster than you feel because the water is holding you up. One bottle to drink *outside* on the steps, one to sip on the dry ledge. Do not drink the chamber water - it's mineral, lime-rich, has a bacterial skin in the slack corners, and it's been cycling through a work drain.
* **Earplugs, plus agreed hand signals.** Not for volume damage alone, but because you can't have a normal conversation in there. If you're going with a buddy, agree beforehand: palm up = I'm hot/dizzy, thumb toward entrance = I'm leaving.
* **A towel, soap, and a rinse bottle for after.** Lime and hot mineral water leave skin dry and eyes stinging. Rinse your eyes and ears when you get out. Don't submerge your face if you can avoid it.
* **A small plaster kit.** Sharp new brick + wet lime mortar = scrapes. The stone hammer sitting half-submerged in the shallows and the legs of that loom/hide-stretcher frame poking up through the water are also trip hazards you will *not* see clearly because of the glare.
* **Sunglasses if you plan to float on your back.** The three dinner-plate vents in the crown are not skylights, they're suns. Float for a minute and the bright brick ceiling becomes sky. It's beautiful and completely disorienting, and the rippling white-gold glare will leave you with aching eyes.

**Leave:**
* Electronics, paper notebooks, food, anything you care about getting wet or steamed. There's no signal underground through brick, no clock you can hear over the sluice, and everything will fog and drip.
* Bug spray, tent, cold-weather gear, fishing kit. There is literally nothing here for them to do.

If you are a multiverse traveler specifically: don't anchor a portal, tent, or exit point below the dry ledge. The water level rises a hand's breadth in a rush every few minutes when the counterweight sluice drops, then falls again through the overflow by the entrance. Anything you leave in the shallows - including an exit - will be waist-deep, then chin-deep, then back again.

### 4. Your Body in There

**Heat is cumulative and sneaky.** Brick is hot above the water, hot below the water. There is no cold surface to cool against. You will feel buoyant and relaxed long after your core temperature has started to climb. For a first visit, plan on 15-25 minutes in the water, maximum. Eat light beforehand, hydrate like you're about to do a light workout, and get out at the first sign of dizziness, headache, or that sudden need to sit down.

Pregnant travelers, anyone with heart, blood pressure, or fainting issues, and anyone with open cuts should skip the soak entirely. This is bath-hot, moving water, not a lukewarm pool.

**Respect the cycle.** The sluice is on a stone-and-rope timer. When it drops with a bang, the roar changes pitch and the level slaps up the bricks. Don't be standing on the second step with your towel when it does. Don't hang on the rope or the counterweight - it's wood, copper pins, and rope, not built for a person's weight, and dropping it will flood or drain the room unexpectedly.

The inflow pipe at the deep corner is mouth-sized and pushes constantly. Don't put your body over it, don't try to swim into that corner alone. That corner has almost no air to spare. The safe swimming is actually toward the entrance - the current is always trying to get to the overflow channel there. If you get tired, floating toward the entrance is floating toward the exit.

**Never pull the plug.** The heavy wooden plug sealed with clay in the drain by the entrance will empty the whole chamber in minutes through the tunnel. It sounds like a fun experiment. It will leave the bricks steaming, ruin the workers' cycle, and create a sudden sucking current in a low, sideways tunnel you have to crawl out of wet. Same rule: don't move the paddles, the jars bobbing in a line, or the rope coil. They are tools left on the dry ledge for a reason.

### 5. Getting In and Out

Go in the morning if you can. Midday sun through those vents is blinding and makes the room hottest and steamiest, and that's when workers are most likely to be using it.

Come in sideways, slowly, one hand on the wall to feel the jutting beam and the sharp brick. No earth to break your fall, just pavement continuing straight down.

On the way out, you will be wet, overheated, and the tunnel bricks will be slick with condensation. Sit on the narrow dry ledge for a minute before you try to stand and re-enter the dog-leg. Let the drip echoing off the vault stop sounding like it's inside your head before you move.

You don't need courage for this place, and you don't need special powers. You need a swimsuit, good footwear, water to drink, earplugs, a towel, and the discipline to treat it like what it is: a new, hot, loud, working machine that happens to have a beautiful, empty pool inside it.
