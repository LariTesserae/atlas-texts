If you're an ordinary human — no heat-proof skin, no way to photosynthesize, no ability to drink fog — this place will try to kill you with three things at once, and you'll be tempted to prepare for only one of them.

Cold. Brightness. Damp.

You need to prepare for all three at the same time, because that's how they exist here. It's a young, high, torn-up plateau. The rock is still sharp as broken crockery, the soil is thin pockets of dark iron-rich dirt between ridges, and the moss has come in absurdly fast and thick. You'll be sunburned and frostbitten and wet without ever being rained on. Your boots will sink 30cm into saturated moss that closes over your footprint. Your lungs will be dry at altitude while your sleeves are wet from air that never quite becomes rain.

No town, no resupply, no trail system, no birds to follow, no stream to follow. The scattered industrial sheds are not a town and will not rescue you. Workers, if any, come in the bright months and then leave for a long, slow season. Design everything for self-rescue.

### 1. Before You Go: The Body

**Altitude is real, even if the air looks clear.** The sky is pale blue because the air is too thin to hold color. You will get winded on a ridge that looks like a gentle climb, you will dehydrate without noticing because cold masks thirst, and you will burn — under constant, bouncing light from white pumice, silver-bleached moss, and mirror-bright corrugated roofs — without feeling the sun's heat. Cold does not mean the UV is low. It means you won't notice the burn until it's serious.

Spend 2-3 days acclimatizing low if you can. Ascend slowly. No alcohol or sedatives. If you have a headache that won't quit, get down. There is no clinic in the valley.

**Hypothermia here doesn't look like hypothermia.** You won't be in snow. You'll be damp from fog, dew, and brushing through wet leaves, standing in freezing shade under a dense green canopy while sun 2 meters away is blinding. Cotton will kill you — it holds that damp against you. Metal will burn-freeze you. A handrail, a water bottle, a shed door handle, even a tent pole left in shade can stick to skin.

Prepare mentally for paradox: you must be waterproof *and* insulated *and* sun-protected *and* vented, all at once.

### 2. Pack For Cold-Bright-Wet

**Feet and legs:**
- High, stiff, waterproof boots with puncture-proof soles and ankle support. The scree is angular and fresh, not worn rounded. Smooth soles will skate and cut.
- Gaiters. Moss, seeds, and sharp grit get in everything.
- **Three pairs of socks minimum, in separate dry bags.** You will be wet daily. Rotating dry socks is more important than a second shirt.
- Trekking poles. You cannot see the ground in the understory, the moss is a springy mattress with hollows underneath, and gullies are still widening cracks. Use poles to test depth and stability before you commit your weight.
- Knee pads or thick pants. You'll be pushing through a living wall of fern and shrub, kneeling on sharp rock to look under a leaf.

Consider bog shoes / snowshoe-like platforms for long moss crossings if your pack allows. Sinking to the ankle all day is exhausting and cold.

**Layers:**
- Synthetic or wool base layer, never cotton next to skin.
- Insulated mid-layer that *still insulates when damp* — synthetic insulation, not down, unless you can guarantee it stays in a vapor-proof bag at all times.
- Waterproof-breathable shell, not a heavy rain suit. You need to dump sweat in sun, then close up instantly in black shade.
- Warm hat that covers ears (frostbite targets ears, nose, fingers), plus a sun hat layer. You need both. A balaclava or neck gaiter you can pull up at night.
- Insulated gloves *plus* thin liner gloves for tasks. You will never touch bare metal, bare rock in shade, or cold water with bare hands.
- Spare sleep clothing kept in its own waterproof bag, never worn during the day.

**Eyes and skin:**
- Wraparound glacier glasses, Category 3-4, with a strap. The light is not just overhead — it bounces up from pumice sand, silver leaves, and metal roofs. Do not rely on sunglasses with open sides.
- SPF 50, lip balm with SPF, applied to nose, ears, under chin, and the backs of hands. Reapply after sweating or brushing wet foliage. The cold will hide the burn.
- Anti-fog wipes and a microfiber lens cloth in a pocket, not in your pack. Everything fogs: glasses, camera, binoculars. The air is wet even when it's not raining.
- Face shield or sunglasses *plus* a light buff for dust/mites. There are almost no insects, but there are mites finer than dust in the moss, and mold spores in damp plant matter. A simple mask helps if you enter a press house where moss insulation is stacked.

### 3. Shelter, Water, Food: What Doesn't Exist

**Shelter:** Assume freezing nights, even in sun. Shade is still freezing. Your sleeping bag should be synthetic, rated well below freezing, and used with a vapor barrier liner because damp air destroys loft. Pitch on a rock slab, a small gravel shelf, or bare pumice in a clearing, *not* on the thick moss mattress — it's saturated and will soak through a pad. Don't camp in a gully (they shift, and fog ponds there) and don't camp on a high ridge (wind is broken in the valley by design, but exposed ridges are another matter).

A tent with a full waterproof fly, not just mesh. Dew will drip from trees, fog will roll in horizontal bands in the morning and shred on ridges, and that brief monthly rain — 3-4 minutes, rattling on metal — will find every seam.

Keep your tomorrow clothes, boots, and electronics in dry bags inside the tent, and dry your day socks on your body, not hoped-for sun. Sun-warmed stone is the only reliable dryer here, and it's a pleasure, not a system.

**Water:** Do not plan to drink from the landscape. This is the crucial difference. Plants drink the air. There is no stream system. The tarn in the lowest basin is a sheet a few centimeters deep over pumice that appears in a wet week and disappears in a dry one, leaving a pale ring. It will be silty, warm in sun, cold in shade, and full of mineral and biological matter.

Carry 3-4 liters per day, plus a filter/purifier for emergencies. You *can* collect dew and fog with a fine mesh net rigged between poles overnight — that's literally the local industry — but do not drink from industrial condensation gutters in the press houses. That's the only place water pools, it's painted metal, it may have hot oil residue, and it's home to the valley's one visible animal, a pale translucent newt that lives its entire life in about two meters of channel. Leave it alone.

Bring a canister stove. There is no dry wood to burn, the trees are low, gnarled, and wet, and you should not burn living moss. Hot food and tea are morale and rewarming tools, not luxuries.

**Food:** High-calorie, no-cook backup. Cold plus altitude plus wading through dense green burns energy fast. Keep bars in an inside pocket — a pack in the moss will get damp and freeze.

### 4. Moving Safely Through a Young World

Navigation is hard in a new way. There are no smooth pebbles, no patient valleys carved over millions of years, no birdsong to orient by. Ridges look alike in bright haze. Under the canopy, light goes black and instant. At eye level, leaves touch leaves. You can push through a living wall and step into a clearing the size of a room that is also full.

- Compass (not just phone GPS — cold drains batteries, damp fogs screens, and there is no cell signal to expect) and a paper map in a waterproof case. Mark your camp. Moss footprints close behind you; you will lose your own trail.
- Arrive and move in mid-morning to afternoon if you can. Mornings are foggy and coldest. Light comes over the ridge all at once, hard and bleached, and then shadows are black. A headlamp is essential under trees even at noon.
- Stay out of narrow gorgelets and fresh cracks. They are widening a finger's width a year and scree shifts grain by grain when it rains.
- Do not shout for help expecting an answer. The silence is absolute. Wind is broken, moss and saturated leaves absorb sound, there is no insect hum, and machines run at a crawl. Your own breathing will seem obscenely loud. A whistle and a personal locator beacon are worth more than your voice. Leave a detailed itinerary with someone off-plateau, because no one in the valley is likely to hear or come.

If you have a multiverse return anchor, portal, or tech: test it in cold damp before you need it. Keep batteries warm against your body, keep contacts dry, keep a physical backup plan (walk out) that does not depend on it. Excess heat from a portal will attract plants — they lean toward warm cracks and heat leaking from buried pipes — and living moss will happily adopt cables and conduct poorly through living tissue.

### 5. At the Industrial Nodes: Respect the Slow Machines

What looks abandoned is tended. What looks tended may be dormant for months and then move for an afternoon.

You will find single sheds, silos half-sunk in moss, relay poles with blue glass insulators, pipes warm under moss, generators bolted to pads, condensation nets draped in moss, conveyor belts running straight through fern rows you would otherwise swear were wild.

Rules:
- **Stay off their systems.** Do not touch nets, don't hang laundry on them, don't climb silos or tanks, don't drink from gutters, don't enter sheds. Doors sealed by a vine curtain are closed. A door that still swings on oiled hinges is still private/active.
- Assume anything with a wire or pipe is live or about to move, however silent. The loom moves a centimeter and stops. A press comes down over the course of an afternoon. A conveyor glides. If you stand where it will be after that afternoon, you will learn why the cycle is slow.
- Do not walk through straight fern rows. They are a cultivated crop on a seven-year cycle, hidden under the wild understory until you see the conveyor. If you find yourself in obvious rows, back out the way you came.
- Do not harvest moss, strip lichen, or collect specimens. Moss insulation is harvested on a cycle of years and stacked under tarps. The lichens growing in straight lines along panel seams are not decoration. The white albino flowers that don't close for days are not just pretty. Everything is in its first draft. Take photos, leave it.
- Keep gloves on around rusted metal. Tetanus vaccination up to date — rust creeps a millimeter a year here, but cuts from sharp scree and iron-rich soil are constant. Clean and bandage any cut immediately; damp warmth under a bandage is infection heaven.

If you meet workers, speak quietly — you'll find yourself doing it anyway — and ask before you use anything. They will be as rare as everything else that walks.

### 6. The Psychological Preparation

This is an ordinary-human warning, not a mystical one: the silence and slowness get to people.

There are no birds. No insect hum. Your footsteps are swallowed. Drips fall once a minute from a leaf tip. Metal ticks as it cools. A slug crosses a shed floor once a season. The world moves on plant time: a tree adds a ring and calls it a decade, moss advances across a machine at fingernail pace. Time will feel both stretched and first-year fragile, as if you're walking through a world that hasn't learned to hold water, make noise, or grow animals yet.

Bring something to do in camp that doesn't need a screen: a book, cards, a notebook. Expect boredom, expect to talk quieter without deciding to, expect to feel very alone despite bright light everywhere. That's normal here. Don't each day's plan demand speed. This place punishes rushing — in scree, in wet moss, around slow machinery.

### Final Checklist for an Ordinary Traveler

Dry bags for: sleep clothes, socks, map, electronics
Glacier glasses + strap, SPF, hat + warm hat
Stiff waterproof boots, gaiters, poles, gloves (insulated + liner)
Synthetic insulated sleeping system with vapor barrier, waterproof tent
3-4L water/day + filter, stove + fuel, 1 day emergency food
Compass, paper map, headlamp, whistle, PLB, first aid + tetanus
Lens cloths / anti-fog, mask for dusty sheds
Notebook/camera — but no collection

Bring your own warmth, your own water, your own light, and your own way back. The plateau will provide light, damp, cold, and silence in abundance, and green. It will not provide anything else, and for a while, that will be enough to make you feel like a rare second animal, an intruder in a first draft, carefully walking on a mattress of moss that closes behind you.
