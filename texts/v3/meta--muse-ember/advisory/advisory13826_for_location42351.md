For an ordinary human, the Turbine Garden is not one place. It's two places that contradict each other, and you have to prepare for both.

The first is the summit shelf: black basalt, pale feldspar, white salt-crusts, no water at all, sky gone blackish at midday and air so thin it will not carry a breath. No birds, no insects, no tracks. A few missed industrial things — a cable stapled to rock, a concrete anchor block, a hut with the door gone, a valve wheel half-buried. Nothing to eat, nothing to drink, nowhere to shelter. Everything is dry, mineral, and vastly larger than anything humans built.

The second is the pocket behind the highest face, in a jam of boulders: a room-sized turbine casing, weathered to the point where rust bloomed and then *stopped*, painted to a settled grey, rivets flush. Darling, it does not look like a door. It looks like part of the rock. You find it by the seam where it warped, wide enough to slip through sideways, breathing out air cool as stone.

Inside: dim grey-green twilight that never changes, a faint constant amber glow from a dead panel, and a sound that is loud and moves nothing — a low fan rumble, a high belt whine, a dull clank in an exact loop, felt in your ribs and the soles of your feet. And inside that, saturation. Dust-ferns shingled across the ceiling, soot-fern and copper-moss up the curved iron, grease-vine thick as cables braided with real cables across the floor. No animals. No water. No rot. No growth. Leaves that are papery and dusty to the touch, and an underlayer mineralized to stone-leaves. Plants that don't grow to light — there is no directional light — they grew, once, to vibration, leaning into the loudest joints, slatting into louvers and spikes. Now growth and fall have stopped at the same rate.

You will not be welcomed and you will not be attacked. You will simply be a new vibration in a place that has already reached its final form. That is the hardest part to prepare for.

### 1. Your body is the problem, not the mountain

You evolved for dense air, water on tap, seasons, and other animals. Here you get none of that feedback, and your senses will start inventing it.

**Altitude first.** This is not a high hill. "Air will not carry a breath," blackish midday sky, pressure pressing at everything — think well above the altitude where an unacclimatized sea-level human can work, and then higher. You cannot power through it. Stage your ascent over days, sleep progressively higher, and plan on at least one rest day before you even look for the casing. If you have any cardiac, respiratory, or inner-ear issues, get checked before a multiverse hop puts you here. The summit has no water to rehydrate you, and dehydration accelerates altitude sickness. You carry all of it.

Plan for cold, for fierce UV off black rock despite the cold, for wind on an exposed shelf, and for dryness that will crack lips, nose, eyes, and skin within minutes. Inside the casing it is cold, dry, metallic, and dusty, with dust hanging in still strata that will coat a mask — or your lungs if you breathe hard through your mouth.

Limit your first entry. 15 to 30 minutes is a full visit for an ordinary human. The clank never varies, no shadow ever moves, no leaf ever trembles. After five minutes it looks the same as after an hour. Your brain will get bored, anxious, nauseated, and then fixate on predicting the clank. Infrasound-like pressure in the chest makes people feel panic they mistake for fear. If you feel ringing, chest tightness, dizziness, or a strong urge to *listen harder* to find the variation, step out. The exit is the only seam; disorientation inside a shadowless, packed box is real.

Do not go in alone the first time, and do not go in without someone outside who knows the time. Inside, a shout won't carry over the note, and hand contact is almost impossible because there is no empty space to turn around in.

### 2. Pack like two expeditions

Split your gear into Summit Pack (stays outside) and Entry Kit (what actually goes through the seam).

**Summit Pack — leave behind:**
- A large, bright pack, loose straps, scarves, dangling cords. The seam is sideways-only. Anything loose will jam — and an ordinary human jammed sideways in a warping metal seam at altitude is an emergency no one can reach well.
- Food and open water bottles. Eat and drink *outside*, sealed and resealed. Inside, the ecology is sealed and has never spent water. Your crumbs, your spit, your sweat, your breath condensation are the first foreign organic matter and first free water this system has met in... a very long time. They could rust iron that stopped rusting, rot a papery leaf that stopped rotting. Be the guest who leaves no moisture.
- Bright headlamps on full. You will need light, but use it dim and low. A torch will throw the first sharp shadows this place has ever had, ruin your dark adaptation, and make depth perception *worse* in a place that was built to be seen without shadows.
- A bright cord or tape to mark the outside of the seam, and a watch with a vibrating alarm set *before* you enter. You'll lose time inside. Remove the marker when you leave.

**Entry Kit — wear, don't carry:**
- Sturdy boots with thick, soft soles. They damp the vibration you feel through your feet, grip the mineralized stone-leaf litter and grease-vine mat (which is slick and brittle), and protect you from bolt heads and cables braided through the floor. Boot covers outside to keep summit salt-dust off; knee pads, because you will be low, sideways, and brushing everything.
- Sealed, close-fitting coveralls, gloves, and a dust mask with a filter. You will touch everything whether you mean to or not — there is no clear path. Gloves protect the plants from your skin oils and protect you from soot, grease, coppery salts, and sharp cooling fins and gear teeth that leaves have grown *into*. No loose sleeves.
- Molded earplugs *plus* over-ear muffs in your pocket. Plugs alone won't stop the rumble you feel in your ribs — that's bone conduction and floor vibration — but the high whine will damage hearing over a long stay. Put plugs in *before* the seam, so transition isn't a blast. You may want to take one plug out for the last few seconds before exiting to hear the outside silence return.
- Glacier glasses or dark lenses for the summit, and clear lenses on a strap for inside. Transition both ways is blinding.
- A small dim light — a phone on lowest amber, a clipped low lamp — and a camera if you must, with the understanding it will record mostly unchanging green gloom and a constant, oppressive note. No one watching it later will believe you stayed that long, because nothing in it will move.

Forbidden inside: open flame, smoking, eating, drinking, urinating, spitting, opening a water bottle, collecting a leaf (they are mineralized stone or papery husks; taking one breaks the density law and they'll crumble), trying to read or touch the amber panel, trying to turn the outside valve, or trying to use the cable line or the doorless hut as shelter. The hut will not protect you from altitude or cold, and the cable is not a handrail.

### 3. Approach and entry

Go in summit midday, when contrast is least cruel and temperature is highest. Use the missed landmarks — cable, block, valve — only as rough reference. Look for the boulder jam behind the highest face, for paint smoothed to mineral, for a faint hum under your boots before you see anything, and for cold air at a seam.

Face the seam, photograph it from outside, note its position relative to the boulders. Inhale, drink, use the toilet outside, put plugs in, slip sideways. Keep your profile narrow. Your partner *stays outside*, watch in hand, hand on your tether if you use one — but keep the tether short and visible, because anything trailing will braid into the vine.

Inside, do not try to find a room. You are embedded in a pocket. Move slow, feet placed on iron ribs where you can see them, not stomping the mat. There is nowhere to sit. There is nowhere to lean without pressing a leaf flat against a fin. Keep your elbows in, your hands gloved and near your body. Breathe through the mask, shallow and slow; deep altitude breathing will dry you faster and stir dust that does not drift away.

Orient by touch and by the amber glow, not by shadows. Count steps from the seam to the vine mat, but expect to lose the seam visually — everything is green-black and shingled. Before you move deeper, turn back and look at the grey-green light coming through the seam. That is your exit, and it will be the only directional cue you have.

Listen with your body, not just your ears. Expect the note to continue unchanged. Do not try to sync to the clank, do not hum, do not talk loudly. Your voice is a new vibration source, and the plants' whole architecture is an answer to vibration. They are saturated and cannot grow — but they may orient to you, and a human whispering in a still, loud place feels obscene anyway.

### 4. What to expect psychologically

This is not abandonment. Abandoned places decay, shift, fill with animal life. This place is *finished*. Rust stopped. Growth stopped. Rot stopped. The machine runs at exactly the speed it ran when the last leaf filled the last square. Grease-vine, dust-fern, copper-moss, fan, belt, clank — one metabolism, no animals to interrupt it, pollen shaken free by the casing itself.

That means you will feel ignored in a way that is almost physical. Other wild places respond to you — a bird flushes, a plant turns to light, a shadow moves. Here, nothing will. An hour of watching will show you nothing changing. Travelers often report a flat, held feeling on exit, as if they'd been listened to by thousands of leaf-slats and then let go, and a ringing that follows them back to the summit.

That is normal for this pocket. It was long-existed before you, and it will be exactly as you left it long after. Stepping out, the sound drops to a faint hum in the rock under your boots. The summit is again dry, black, silent, above the clouds. No water. No animal crossing. That silence will feel huge, and also thin — because the air is thin.

Aftercare matters: drink and eat once you're clear of the seam, wash hands and face (soot, grease, salt, copper dust), check ears, check for numb or aching ribs, remove dust-caked mask. Note the time. People overestimate how long they were inside and underestimate how dehydrated and cold they are.

Do not camp, sleep, meditate for hours, or bring children or pets into the casing. They do not have the lung capacity, the hearing protection discipline, or the ability to slip sideways and stay still — and the place has no animal life for a reason. It cannot accommodate yours.

Prepare, finally, to be unimpressed by movement and overwhelmed by density. Go up a silent mountain, find a modest grey box a first-timer would walk past, slip sideways into twilight that vibrates forever and moves never, stay briefly, touch nothing with bare skin, leave no water behind, and step back into mineral silence.

If you can do all of that, you will have visited the Turbine Garden as it allows itself to be visited: briefly, dryly, quietly, and unchanged.
