For an ordinary human, the main fact about this place is that it is not built for you. It's built — barely, recently, by an accident — for a lying-down body, and even then only just. Everything about preparing for it is about making yourself smaller, slower, quieter, and less likely to be fooled by your own senses once you're in there.

Don't imagine a cave dive. Imagine lying flat in a cold, flooded cupboard that is already full of living things.

### 1. Accept in advance that you will be the disturbance

The pocket is young. The roof is a slab of pale blue-grey mudstone that has only just torn off the bank and slumped into the basin. The faces are still fresh, banded, and knife-edged. Dust from the fall is still settling, at a pace too slow to notice. The crack in the slab is still widening a hair at a time. The plants inside are older than the room.

That means:

- It will cut you. Not metaphorically. Fresh mudstone flakes and splinters will slice neoprene and skin if you brace a hand or knee against them, and the splinters are unrounded, still bright where they broke. You will not find a clean handhold. Floor, ceiling, and walls are all furred over with algae ribbons, leafy weed, braids, ivory sponge cushions, and hydroid plumes. Reach and you displace a curtain that closes behind you more slowly than you opened it. Reach again and it's there, rearranged.

- It is not stable. A slab that recently fell can settle again. A new splinter can part from the roof while you're under it. That is the single most serious risk, more than depth — the basin outside is low, flat, cold and silted, but not deep in an oceanic sense.

- It is fragile and already has a history. At the back, in the darkest silt, there is a flat stone scored with five shallow, regular notches, a cupped fragment of dull brick-red fired clay half-sunk to its rim, and three uncut stones in a rough crescent around darker silt. Whether someone laid them or the sea slumped them that way, they are the only built things there. A multiverse traveller's job is to not become a fourth. No taking, no touching to "check," no new marks, no dropped gear, no lights left on, no food, no coins, no breathing apparatus abandoned under the roof. The place, as described, knows only water and rock and growth. Keep it that way.

If the slab is still shedding fresh dust, or you see a new silt plume or hear a tick from outside — remember sounds get displaced inside, so you won't judge direction reliably — wait. Come back in a season when the edges have blunted a little. There is nothing in there that will reward haste.

### 2. Train the body you actually have

You have no gills, and your interdimensional gear will not give you air, warmth, or a way to turn around. Prepare as a cold-water freediver and confined-space diver combined, and only if you are already certified and fit for cold water. This is not a first dive.

**Cold and clumsiness:** The water is cool throughout, the kind that finds its way through cloth and settles in the hands until they go clumsy. The pressure is gentle but constant — it fills the ears and presses at the joints — and there is no dry place to put a foot, no surface to break toward. You can't stand up inside.

Warm up before, eat normally, sleep. Practice equalizing ears until it's boring. Practice lying completely still on a mat in a dark room for two minutes at a time, arms at your sides, not grabbing for a wall. Practice slow CO2-tolerant breathing in a shallow pool, eyes open then closed, on a tether. Your hands *will* get clumsy; plan not to need them for fine work inside.

**Claustrophobia and disorientation:** Inside, up and down are negotiated away. Algae growing down meets algae growing up and braids in midwater. Bubbles don't rise straight — they lean sideways, flatten against nothing, and are drawn toward the walls as if the ceiling weren't up. A flake shed from the roof will hang, turning end over end, and after what feels like minutes will have moved a finger's width. Your shadow falls two ways at once, thinly, from the entrance and from a reflection with no source. Sounds arrive displaced: a sound from the far wall at your ear, a sound from the entrance behind your head.

Rehearse an orientation ritual *before*: one hand on the tether at all times, a count of breaths, a physical check — stretch one arm back toward the entrance, one forward toward the stone, and know that both finding a surface at once is normal here, not a sign the room is shrinking. If you rely on bubbles, voices, or memory of where the entrance was, you will exit into the far wall. The entrance you saw from outside will, inside, appear behind you and also impossibly beyond the far wall, with the outside light in the wrong place.

**Patience:** It is slow in every register. The sponge pulses so slowly you feel it before you hear it, a faint sigh against the skin like something breathing in its sleep. The double-thump of the surface comes through the slab at intervals too long to count. The weed adds a fraction of itself in a season. A shrimp may or may not come to the curtain — one pale shrimp in the whole visit is a lot — and it will not enter. Do not stay gripping, watching, waiting for a flake to fall or an animal to perform. Boredom and quiet are the visit.

### 3. Dress slim, bring almost nothing

The pocket is only as long as you are lying down and barely wider than your shoulders. In a thick wetsuit, with a hood, a tank, a camera rig and a torch, an ordinary human literally will not fit, and will get stuck where a rescue would be impossible. No turning is possible inside.

What fits the place:

- **Thin, warm, layered:** A slim 3mm wetsuit over a warm synthetic top, thin hood, no cotton anywhere. You need core warmth more than armour. Empty all pockets, remove watch, jewelry, knife — a knife tempts you to cut algae; never cut algae, you'll tear the curtain that marks the entrance and the community with it.
- **Thin cut-resistant dive gloves**, or bare hands with eyes before touch. Thick gloves protect you and make you too wide and too clumsy; bare hands give you feeling and no protection. Gloves are the compromise, plus a rule: don't touch stone at all. Tether only.
- **Low-volume mask.** That's it for face gear if freediving.
- **Air, if you use it, from outside:** You cannot take a scuba cylinder in. Either a very short, trained freedive — think under a minute of still, lying time, not a swim — or a surface-supplied umbilical with just a regulator, fed by a buddy outside. A long hose will snag on the algae curtain, which closes slowly around it. If you can't do a calm, short freedive with a buddy watching, don't enter the pocket at all. Peer in from the curtain.
- **No fins inside.** Fins are wide, they kick up silt, they tear weed, and there is no room to kick. Leave fins outside the slab with your buddy, or tuck them and frog-kick only in the open basin.
- **One small, low torch** clipped to your chest, angled down, on its lowest setting, used in short bursts. It's not a machine to bring light to a dark place — it's to see the sharp edge before you brush it. Remember the ivory sponge is pale from lack of light; don't wash it with a beam. Expect it to make two shadows and confuse you more.
- **A 4-5 metre tether / guide line**, clipped at your waist. This is not optional. Inside, the water holds a straight, glassy edge against the murk outside — clear dark inside, cloudier green outside, same water, same temperature, line unmoving. You can see that line. Keep your hand or waist on the line that crosses it. That line is your up, your exit, your rescue handle.

A buddy outside is more important than any piece of kit. They stay in the basin, watch the slab for settling, watch for fresh dust, hold the other end of the tether with steady tension, and can pull you straight back out. Agree tugs in advance because voices won't work and sounds are displaced: one tug check-in, two tugs turn back, three tugs pull me out. No solo entry. An ordinary human in a body-sized flooded pocket with a sharp roof is a rescue problem no one can solve from inside.

### 4. Approach like you're looking for something that doesn't want a footprint

Outside, you could cross the basin a dozen times and never know it was there. The algae curtain has a parting no more than a handspan wide under the slab — a shadow where strands have rubbed thin. Search slow, low, and without kicking silt. Fins stir the fresh dust that is still settling. The pale shrimp on the outside of the rock is your sign you're in the right neighborhood; it will not go in, and neither should you chase it.

Drop your reference *outside* the slab: your buddy, your fins, a small beacon on the basin floor away from the pocket — not inside. After you leave, the water will close its clear edge behind you in a moment. You will need an external mark to find it again, and to prove to yourself which way is out.

### 5. Entering — decide before you go in

Decide lying on a mat at home: feet-first or head-first. Don't decide in the water.

Feet-first is generally safer for a human this size. Slide in slow, flat, face toward the back of the pocket, knees long, arms tight to your body to save width. Your head stays nearest the entrance as long as possible, your regulator/mask nearest fresh observation, and a pull on the tether brings you out the way you came. Head-first puts your eyes deepest soonest, but puts you furthest from abort.

Move as if the water were glass — because visually it is. A fast movement smudges the water-within-water edge, stirs silt over the lowest algae, and displaces curtains that will close slowly. Every hand placement is a tear in a braid. Hover, don't plant. If you must steady yourself, use the back of a gloved hand, once, and let go.

### 6. Inside: what to do with your attention

- Don't try to make an empty space. There isn't one. The room is mostly living matter.
- Don't follow the hydroid plumes. They sway though there is no current, in opposing directions above and below, as if answering a tide the other way. It's not current, and it's not a compass.
- Don't chase bubbles. Yours will lean to the walls and may gather under the roof in a stray pocket. Exhale small and slow, away from the roof face.
- Don't interpret the silence. Far above is the dull double-thump of the surface. Nearer is the sponge sigh you feel before you hear. That's the whole soundscape. No fish crosses. No school turns. The one shrimp staying outside is normal, not a rejection.
- Don't touch the marks. Look. Photograph from the curtain if you must, no flash, no measuring with your fingers. The five notches, the clay cup, the crescent of stones — document by memory and a slate *outside* afterwards.
- Keep your visit short and still. A minute or two of lying in the green seep — that bruised green light caught and lost and caught again by foliage — is a complete visit. The dark becomes almost total within a body length, and your eyes will not improve it.

### 7. Leaving

Turn by reversing, not turning. You cannot turn. Back out along the tether, feet or head leading the way you came, hand over hand on the line, not on the weed. The algae curtain will close behind you more slowly than you opened it; don't yank it. Once your hips are clear of the glassy edge, the pocket will reseal itself. Check your gloves, skin, and suit for nicks immediately — fresh mudstone cuts in cold, silty water are the infection risk you carry back to other worlds.

### 8. After

Warm up actively, dry, inspect every cut, rinse and disinfect gear thoroughly before you travel on. Don't carry basin microbes, silt, or algae to the next universe on a damp suit, and don't carry your universe's microbes in. Log the location, the marks, the shrimp, the sponge pulse — by all means — and leave no physical trace.

And be honest in the log: for an ordinary human, the right preparation is often to realize the pocket is a place to visit with your eyes and your tether hand at the curtain, not to occupy. It is a small, fresh, flooded room at the bottom of a flat cold basin, breathing at sponge-pace, holding its water inside its water, waiting years for erosion to make it less sharp and less new. You can be a brief, careful guest in that first sharp stage — slim, slow, tethered, and out again before the curtain closes — or you can wait, and let it become an old place first, and visit then.
