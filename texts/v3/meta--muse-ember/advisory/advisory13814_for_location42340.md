If you prepare for Selan like a normal hot desert, or like a normal ruined city, you will be unprepared in slightly interesting ways.

It is not hot *because* it is bright. It is not wet *because* it rains. It is not empty because nothing is happening. And the ground, the water, the buildings, and the distances all behave about half a step out of phase with what you expect. For an ordinary human body, the preparation is mostly about respecting those half-steps.

### 1. What kind of trip this actually is

Treat Selan as a series of separate, self-sufficient day-expeditions, not as a city you can wander through and find a hotel, a shop, or a guide.

There are at least eleven solid clusters, town-sized, separated by one to three hours of bare plain. Between them there is almost nothing: a tilted plate, a black-glass seam, a lone tree, a perfect oval pool, a single standing wall. Each cluster is lush and urban up close — layered ceramic towers, vines the size of your hand, trees growing out of roofs and plazas, glow-flowers that never close — and utterly bare the moment you leave it.

There is almost no fauna to forage, no river to follow, no rain to collect, and no established trail. The one human figure you might see in a day is a gardener far off in a vine wall, moving very slowly, who may simply be swallowed by mist when you look away. Do not plan on local help, directions, or rescue. If you get hurt, your own gear and your own itinerary are the rescue.

The city also does not stay put the way a map wants it to. The solid clusters are your only reliable landmarks. The second, mist-built city appears at dawn when everything condenses, holds your weight if you lean on it while wet, and thins to nothing by dry midday, leaving no shadow. A week later it may be standing a little to one side, as if it walked in its sleep. A map of the mist-buildings is wrong in a day. A reflection in a pocket pool can show a mist-building as solid while your eyes still see only fog. At dusk you may see a whole second horizon of buildings that will only exist tomorrow. Do not navigate toward either of those. Navigate toward solid towers only.

### 2. Feet first: the ground is new and sharp

This is the single most important piece of preparation.

The basin is pressed flat, but the plates are tilted a degree or two against each other, their edges razor-sharp, their seams fresh black glass with no weathering, no rounded pebbles, no dust to soften them. The soil where there is soil is black, crumbly, and smells of warm stone and unfinished chemistry. The plain is still lifting a few millimetres a year and occasionally settles with a dry tick.

That means:
- Sturdy, ankle-supporting boots with thick, puncture-resistant soles. Not trail shoes, not sandals, not bare feet, ever. Black glass will cut a thin sole, and a tilted plate will twist an unprotected ankle over three hours of supposedly flat walking.
- Gaiters or tall socks to keep sharp grit out, and gloves for steadying yourself on ceramic and stone.
- A proper first-aid kit with wound cleaning, pressure dressings, and blister care. Scratches here are not dirty in the usual sense, but they are sharp, and the black soil is not something you want in an open cut.
- No assumption that a path will develop. Because nothing erodes and repair swarms close fractures and scratches as you watch, your footprints will not mark a trail for anyone else, including you on the way back. Leave discrete, retrievable markers on the plain if you need to, and place any transit anchor or return beacon in a solid cluster, never in the outline of a mist-building and never by a lone pocket.
- Test every plate before you commit your weight. Under a cluster, plates bear weight a fraction differently and there are hollow, doorless chambers below. Do not go down fresh-cut openings, do not jump from a tower fringe — the edges feather out into particles that catch light and let go — and do not climb the lone standing walls to get a view. You can already see for miles at ground level.

Camp on an open plate at the edge of a cluster, not in the greenery. Trees here can grow a hand’s breadth overnight and pull back into spirals in the dry hours. Vines root into wall seams and sweat upward through ceramic capillaries. If you pitch in the plants, you will wake up overgrown, damp, and moved.

### 3. Heat without light, damp without rain

The sun is a pale disc that never gets brighter than late evening elsewhere and never sets properly. It pales, the haze thickens, the light goes amber-grey, and it pales again. Your skin in the meantime stays warm because the ground holds the heat. Do not use brightness as a heat gauge. You can get heat-exhausted at midday in what looks like dusk.

At the same time, there is no rain as a weather event. There is a daily cycle: at night every vertical surface sweats, by dawn towers, leaves, and plates are beaded and cold, pockets fill, and the mist-buildings solidify. Then the dim sun dries the air, beads shrink, mist-buildings fade, and leaves give back what they drank as vapour. By midday the basin can smell like a shut greenhouse — warm wet stone, crushed leaf, something sweet. In late afternoon the plants and tower skins exhale again and a fine condensation settles on your shoulders like a sighing rain.

Prepare for both:
- Quick-drying, non-cotton layers in a warm, dim palette. You will be damp at dawn and sweaty at midday. Bring a completely dry sleep layer in a waterproof bag, and a light shell or wide-brim hat for the late-afternoon condensation. You do not need a heavy rain system.
- An insulated sleeping pad even though it is warm. The plates are cold at dawn and will condense straight through an unprotected bivvy.
- A fine-dust mask and eye covering. The air is full of dust so fine it never settles — the same dust that dims the sun — and it takes sound apart as it goes. It will irritate eyes, throat, and lungs over hours, especially in the morning mist. A bandana is not enough.
- Lip balm, moisturiser, and extra socks. You will be wet, dry, wet again in one day.
- A headlamp with a low, warm setting. It never gets properly dark, but your sense of time will drift because the sun never sets properly. At dusk, when the light is most amber and distorted, you want to already be stopped.

### 4. Water: there is almost none

There are no rivers. The only free water is the pockets: perfect room-sized ovals set into stone, unnaturally still, reflecting the grey sky so exactly they look like holes in the world. They are clear, mineral-cold, shallow, and smell faintly green.

Do not treat them as water supply. Do not drink them untreated, do not wade or swim in them, and do not try to collect condensation off leaves or tower skins to drink — that water is moving up through plant capillaries and ceramic in ways you do not understand. The air will feel damp for long stretches; that is not hydration.

For an inter-cluster crossing, carry water for the *longer*, distorted version of the trip, plus reserve. For most people that means 3-4 litres, electrolytes, and a water filter if you might be delayed to a second day. Test your pack loaded at home on a three-hour flat walk. Distance in Selan is deceptive: a tower that looks 200 metres away can take twenty minutes to reach, and the plain ahead feels longer than the plain behind you. Plan by hours, not by what your eyes tell you, and start crossings early enough that even a 50% overrun still puts you in a solid cluster before dusk.

Bring all food. There is nothing reliable to eat — the vines drink too fast for the dim light, the flowers are for the one iridescent beetle that matters, and everything growing here is part of a system you do not want to ingest. Do not forage, do not pick glow-flowers, do not tear vines for rope. Use beetle-safe, minimal repellent if you need it at all, and wash your hands after handling wet leaves.

### 5. Sound, sight, and mind

Selan is very quiet in a way that is physically active. Your footsteps ping once on the plates and stop. Plant growth clicks like fingernails at night. Condensation drips from a roof for an hour, each drop a separate weather. Underneath is a low hum you feel more in your teeth than your ears — the mist-buildings changing phase, repair swarms working, the ground lifting.

You cannot shout for help across a plain here. Sound is taken apart by the air. You *can* be seen for a very long way. Prepare visual signalling instead: bright panel, signal mirror for the pale disc, agreed hand signals with a companion, and a two-way radio used line-of-sight only. If you travel solo, leave a detailed itinerary with someone off-basin by *elapsed clock time*, not by sun position, and overdue check-ins.

Expect small perceptual wrongness, and do not argue with it while walking:
- Shadows linger a heartbeat after you step out of them.
- Pools show mist-buildings more solidly than the air does.
- At dusk a second horizon appears.
- Distances stretch.

None of this is dangerous by itself. It becomes dangerous when you are tired, dehydrated, and trying to correct a course by eye at dusk. The rule for an ordinary traveler is simple: stop before dusk, sit in a solid cluster, and let the dawn re-solidify the mist city for looking, not for travelling far.

The quiet itself wears on people. Nothing rushes and nothing rests; trees grow and retract, towers re-weave their fringes, ground cracks and is closed again. Bring a journal, a way to mark time, and patience for long stretches with nothing moving except a leaf or a building appearing. If you are prone to anxiety, vertigo, or tooth sensitivity, do a short first trip — one cluster, out and back in a few hours — before attempting a three-hour crossing. Note the teeth-hum; if it becomes pain, sit down, hydrate, and stop pushing.

### 6. How to behave around the living city

- **Buildings:** They precipitate out of the air and repair themselves. If you scratch a solid wall, a faint silver film will spread and close it without heat or sound. Do not scratch one to test it. Do not lean for long against a mist-building in mid-morning; it holds you while wet and can fade under you. Do not sleep in a mist-building outline. Do not expect your gear to be repaired — the swarms edit buildings and stone, not your boots, tent, or electronics. Bring spares and waterproof cases for anything electronic; condensation, fine dust, and warm damp will kill what a fall would not.
- **Plants:** They are the lungs of the clusters. Roots go into seams, leaves sweat *upward*, canopies unfold when you look away. Step around them, do not anchor tent guylines to vines, and do not use a tree that is spiralled tight at midday as a reliable shade or landmark — it will be different tomorrow.
- **Animals:** You may walk all morning and see nothing that moves except leaf and building. Then a tall, pale, almost translucent animal with a too-long neck may drink at a pocket, look at you with a polished-bead eye, and step silently back into the green. Birds are slow motes crossing once a day. Keep your distance, do not block its path to a cluster, do not approach a pocket where one is drinking, and do not try to attract the beetles. Do not kill or collect anything. With so little fauna, every animal is a large proportion of the local population.
- **Gardener figures:** If you see one, far off, tending a vine wall, do not follow, call out, or wait for them to guide you. They move at Selan’s pace and the mist may simply thicken between you. They are not a service and not, necessarily, in your time.

And because the city will repair your traces away, do not use that as permission to leave them. Do not carve ceramic, do not chip stone for souvenirs, do not dig in the black soil. The fact that a scratch closes while you watch does not mean nothing happened; it means something unseen did the work.

### 7. A sensible rhythm for a day

**Dawn:** Dampest, most beautiful, mist-buildings most solid. Plates cold and slippery when beaded. Good for a short local walk near a cluster to see the second city inhale into being. Bad for a long crossing.

**Morning, after first light is usable:** Best window to start an inter-cluster walk. Pockets are full, heat has not yet built in the ground, and you have the full pale day ahead.

**Midday:** Driest, clearest, mist-buildings gone. Heat is highest despite the dim sun. The greenhouse smell is strongest. If you are still crossing, you are working hardest now — drink on schedule, not by thirst.

**Late afternoon:** Exhalation begins. Mist rises from plants and towers, fine condensation settles on shoulders and leaves. Arrive, bed down, dry your feet.

**Dusk:** Most amber, most distorted, second horizon, shadows lagging. Do not be caught mid-plain trying to walk toward a building that only exists tomorrow.

### 8. Minimum checklist

- Ankle-supporting, puncture-proof boots; gaiters; gloves
- 3-4L water + electrolytes + filter; all food for at least one extra day
- Quick-dry layers, dry sleep layer in waterproof bag, insulated pad, light shell/hat
- Dust mask, eye covering, headlamp (low/warm), watch/clock you trust
- Physical compass and a dated sketch map of solid clusters only; bright marker panel / signal mirror
- Wound care for glass cuts, blister kit, tweezers, pain relief
- Waterproof cases for electronics; spare batteries in dry bags
- Journal, pen, and itinerary left off-basin with overdue times
- No foraging kit, no climbing kit, no expectation of shelter. Self-sufficient means self-sufficient.

Selan rewards a traveler who moves slowly, carries their own water, respects sharp new stone, walks between solid things only, and is willing to be quiet for a long time while everything around them is quietly busy. You will leave it the way you came — stepping over unweathered plates that bear almost no record of you — and behind you the basin will go on condensing itself into being, one breath at a time.
