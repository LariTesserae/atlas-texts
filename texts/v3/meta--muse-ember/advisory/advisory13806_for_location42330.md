You don't need a suit, a seal, a filter, or any multiverse equipment at all for the Undermade. That's part of the preparation. It's a perfectly ordinary, entirely human place — just an ordinary human place compressed, heated, made louder and smaller than any hamlet has a right to be, and sunk underneath its own roof.

An ordinary body will do fine there. An ordinary traveler with a big pack, a hard shell, bad knees, a sensitive nose, or no tolerance for being touched by strangeness will not. Prepare for density, heat, sound, and for being known.

### 1. Finding it, and going down

From above there is almost nothing: a low, long roof of warm clay tiles and lime plaster, sun-baked, with thin grass nobody planted running through it. The only opening is a single brick stair-hole with a heavy wooden door on bone hinges.

Don't expect signage, a path, a landing pad, or a discreet portal marker. Mark it on your own maps and keep it that way — the place is low-profile in a way that feels deliberate. Don't pin it, geotag it, or advertise it.

When you get there, step carefully in the grass and on the hot tiles. The top is for walking as little as possible — sun there is vertical and personal, and the tiles spit heat back down at night long after the hearths die.

Then you need your hands free for the descent. The door is heavy and the hinges are bone, not steel. Don't lean your pack against it, don't let it bang in a shaft-draft, and don't try to prop it open. Announce yourself as you go down if there's no one to greet you at the top. Sound travels up the stair long before you do — they will hear you, smell you, and know a stranger is coming before you see them. People shout greetings up two terraces here. Expect words before faces.

Bring nothing you can't carry in one awkward hug in front of you. There is no road, no cart that will take it — the hand-carts with solid wheels only travel the twenty paces that matter — and there is no corridor wide enough for a hard, square, swinging rucksack.

### 2. The body you need to bring

**Size down, physically and in baggage.**
From the stair to the farthest wall is sixty paces. A hundred people live in a stepped bowl of three terraced rings, every inch used: shops under houses, lofts over shops, pens under both. You will not walk *past* people; you will negotiate past them, sideways, elbows in, sometimes ducking laundry hung at head-height, sometimes brushing gourds, coils of rope, herbs, caged birds, spare loom shuttles hung close enough to touch your shoulder.

A tall, broad traveler *is* the obstacle. Shorten your stride. Keep your bag flat against your chest, not on your back. Nothing dangling — a strap will catch a drying rack, a pulley rope, a bunch of thyme, or the goat's tether. Tell yourself in advance: I will be in someone's doorway, under someone's balcony, above someone's cooking pot, for the entire visit. The loos-thump, the hammering, the children chanting lessons, the bath splash, the bell, the drip bowls, the goat bellowing — everything overlaps and nothing is far away. If you need personal space, this is the wrong universe.

**Knees, back, and center of gravity.**
The floors are beaten clay, plastered and re-plastered until they're concave in the middle where feet have passed for generations. Steps are hollowed in the center. Doors are rounded by shoulders. You will crouch under lofts and duck under walkways all day, though you will not think you're doing it. Walk slowly for the first hour. Watch your feet more than your head — rows of earthen jars on every shelf, shallow bowls permanently set under condensation lines, basins under rain drips, a polished-by-hooves hollow in the central court, chickens dust-bathing in the middle terrace dust.

**Stamina for heat, not for distance.**
It won't be oven heat, but it's the accumulated warmth of many bodies in a volume only a little larger than they need, plus sun in the shafts, plus hearths, plus a forge and kiln at the lowest level making perhaps one new iron nail a day. The top is dry and dusty; the bottom is moist, close, steamy from the great blackened pot and the communal bath tub. Your clothes will go from dry to clammy without you moving far.

Acclimatize on day one. Don't plan a packed midday itinerary — midday is the loudest, hottest, busiest hour, when every trapped sound bounces off plaster and low ceilings and conversation has to rise. Late afternoon, about four, is when it's most itself: sun in a shaft, goat pulling at its rope, chickens under the basins, wet clay and boiled grain and dung and thyme in the air. Go then if you can. Rest in a sun-patch that isn't someone's drying place, and drink more water than you think you need before you go down.

### 3. What to wear

Natural fibers, breathable, close-fitting, sacrificial.

Cotton or linen that can take brick dust, soot, flour dust, and dung-smoke and be washed in the communal rotation. No synthetics near the forge or kiln — the bellows boy is working the fire in time with the potter's wife singing, and sparks and hot iron do not mix with meltable cloth or loose sleeves.

Long sleeves and long trousers are not prudishness here, they're brushing armor. You will brush walls, rafters, cats sleeping on warm wall-caps, rope, woven reed mats, and bundles of herbs. A short-sleeved shirt will come away pink and covered in fine brick grit.

No hat with a brim. You will knock it off under a balcony, and it will knock something else. A soft cap for the few minutes on the roof is enough; below, the light is in neat rectangles from the shafts and you want to see up and down.

Shoes: closed-toe, soft but grippy, thick enough for warm floors and polished clay, malleable enough to step sideways. Do not arrive in muddy boots — you will track the outside onto floors that are swept and re-plastered as a rhythm of life. Brush off thoroughly at the top. Slip-overs are better than laced boots you have to bend for in a throng. Leave the flip-flops for the bath hollow, and even there, think twice; shared wet clay and warm damp are good for athlete's foot.

Cut your nails. Keep your hands moisturized. Plaster is lime, brick is abrasive, timber is adzed and fire-hardened, and you will steady yourself on walls without thinking. Cuts here seal quickly because everyone is always repairing, including scrapes.

### 4. Pack like you're entering a house, not a country

Leave almost everything behind. Bring only what fits in a soft, low bag:

**Water and salt.** You will see condensation carefully gathered, bowl after bowl, into jars with cloths over their mouths, and a cistern in the central court that catches the rain. That does not mean you drink it. Those are supplies, measured in sips. The one puddle that almost never dries, with frogs and green algae on its rim, is a living thing they will not pave over. Don't wash in it, don't dip a bottle, don't put your feet in it. Bring your own bottle, wrapped in cloth so it doesn't clang against the jars, and a little salt for the heat.

**A cloth mask and earplugs.** Not for disease — for dust, flour, soot, bird dust from the pigeons under the eaves, moldy damp in the bottom corner, reed dust — and for noise. It will never be silent: at night crickets, then the owl in the forge rafters, then the drip, then a pot lid. The drip bowls are a high perpetual counterpoint all day. If you're staying over, earplugs are hospitality to yourself. A face cloth is less conspicuous than a respirator, and less likely to be handed to a child to play with.

**A headlamp, rarely used.** On a clear day it can be walked in ordinary daylight — the surprise of the place. On a cloudy day, oil and fat lamps are lit in niches, and the bottom court lives in cool green-tinted shade from the vine strings. You don't need torchlight, and you certainly don't need to shine light up the chimneys where bats hang by day. A small lamp for the stair-hole shadow and for night is convenient, not necessary.

**Small first aid:** plasters, antiseptic, tweezers for splinters, something for insect bites and for a burn. You will be around a forge, a kiln hood, hearths, hot tiles, and a great bubbling pot. And around flies over the kitchen, beetles in grain jars, crickets in every warm crack, mice in the brick pores.

**One useful gift, not wealth.** Iron here is slow and precious — one nail a day. Do not flash a steel knife, a multitool, good scissors, or any bright manufactured thing; it will be wanted, borrowed, admired, and maybe lost into a repair. If you bring a gift, bring something consumable and low: extra cloth, needles, salt, good soap, oil for lamps. Do not bring seeds, soil, plants, or potted cuttings. A wild seed that blows down a shaft and sprouts in a crack is transplanted into a box or removed here because an unmade thing staying on its own is a failure. Your soil microbiome, your breadcrumbs, your hitchhiking grain beetles are the same problem in reverse. Shake out your bag, your cuffs, and your pockets before you leave — mice live in the walls, insects live in the jars, and at night the cats hunt what you might accidentally import.

**No plastic trash promise.** When a crack opens to raw earth it is filled the same day. If you dropped a stone here it would be ground into mortar in a week. There is nowhere for your wrapper to go that isn't someone's floor, someone's jar, someone's chicken run. Carry it all back up.

### 5. Ears, nose, eyes, and lungs — recalibrate

Expect to be greeted by sound first, and to carry a new soundscape home for days. The looms thump and snap in a dozen doorways — weavers sing to keep time with the shuttle. Potters talk while they shape. The bell is rung by rope not because anyone needs the hour but because by the time it's climbed the shafts it's part of the air. A door not quite shut bangs in every draft. A wind comes down a shaft and lifts laundry and sends a ripple of voices across the court.

Perfume, scented soap, deodorant — no. In this trapped, warm volume, you will be a weather event. Plain, clean, a little dusty is normal.

Your eyes will adjust constantly: sharp, normal sun rectangles moving across terraces, dust and midges turning like snow in the beams, flat grey light and lamp niches on a cloudy day, your own shadow in the bottom court. People plan their work around those sun-patches without thinking: drying here, fermenting there, sitting to weave where the light falls. Don't sit in a sun-patch and block it.

### 6. Animals are neighbors, not attractions

This is a layered house: bats in the chimney inlets, swallows and sparrows in clay cups built a hundred years ago and repaired every spring, pigeons on their own ledges, cats on the wall tops, mice in the pores, chickens and a single pig and goats in the court. Fish eating algae and larvae in earthen tanks by the bath. Earthworms in the compost boxes. A tame owl in the forge, fed, taking moths.

Don't chase, feed, pet, or photograph them at close range without asking. Don't step into the chickens' dust, don't move a sleeping cat from a wall-cap, don't stand under a pulley load of grain or water, don't lean on the coop. The goats are alarm systems and waste processors; their bells and bleats are information about where you are. The pig will patrol the center. The chickens will be where your feet want to go.

And wash your hands. Dung is fuel, dried into bricks. Bird dust gathers on ledges. Bats, pigeons, rodents, insects, livestock — all in one small, warm air volume. Routine tetanus and rabies cover is prudent for any barnyard world. If you are sick with anything, even a cold from another universe, do not go down. A hundred people, a few dozen families counted as one household, in one bowl, will trade it within the day.

### 7. Rain, water, and dirt — learn the rules

Rain here performs. It pours down the light shafts in bright curtains, hisses on hot tiles, drums for about twenty minutes in the cistern and basins, then drips for hours, tap tap, from eaves into bowls. Floors are slick, the cistern is at the low point, and the permanent puddle is a protected commons for frogs.

Stay out of the centers of shafts in rain, watch your footing on polished clay, and don't dry yourself by a hearth in the way of someone else's late meal.

Water is intermittent and local and then held. That is the ethic. Don't waste a drip to wash your hands if you can use your own bottle. Don't open a fermentation jar in the kitchen thinking it's storage. Don't drink from a bowl under a drip line unless it's offered — it may be set for the northern wall's condensation, set for a plant, set for a cat, or simply set for tomorrow.

Bathing is rotation in a sunken clay tub by the kitchen. It's communal, modest in practice, and not a tourist service. If you are invited, bring a towel and a wrap, accept the order, bring your own sandals, and do not expect privacy as you know it. Ask about toilet arrangements before you arrive; do not improvise on a made floor. Excuse yourself to ask — no one expects you to know.

Food is boiled grain, bitter-leaf tea, herbs from wall pockets watered from drip bowls, sprouting grain tasted rather than harvested. Accept what's offered. Don't expect a meal to be large, private, or on your schedule — the bell marks it, and the great pot is communal.

### 8. Social preparation

The Undermade has been smoothed by hands. Steps, doors, tool grips, beams darkened even with smoke — nothing looks new because repair is not an event but a rhythm. Don't test the walls. Don't scratch plaster to show twenty layers underneath. Don't lean on fresh replaster. Don't sit on a drying rack. Don't move a jug "just for a photo."

Everyone is working, all the time, with early tools kept very busy. Potters, weavers, a forge boy, someone always re-tying thatch, sweeping ash into a pile to be made into new bricks. You are not there to watch them work as a spectacle. If you can, offer idle hands for a small useful thing — sweeping, holding a string, carrying a bucket *carefully* — and accept that you will mostly be in the way and they will politely work around you. Children will find you fascinating. The extended household will decide quickly whether you are a guest or a burden.

Most of all, meet the fact that there is no wild ground here. Moss grows only on made surfaces, in mortar lines on the northern wall, fine bright lines after rain. The vine has learned the strings up to real sun. The swallows occupy rather than build. Children born here think of a stone as something fired in a kiln. If you have a deep need to touch unworked earth, to sit with your back against a tree, to be alone in quiet, prepare to feel a little homesick down there. It's not a flaw in you — it's the defining completeness of the place. It feels built *instead of* the earth, and remade a little every day so it can go on being exactly what it is.

### 9. Leaving

Climb back up in daylight if you can. Your shoes will be dusty, your shirt will smell of dung and thyme and wet clay and hot oil, your ears will ring taken off it, and your bag will need a shake-out on the roof before you close that heavy wooden door behind you. Check for beetles, mice-things, seeds. Carry out every scrap.

Rehearse your exit in your mind when you arrive. There's one stair-hole. If there's a fire, a kiln flare, a panic, smoke will gather in the bowl and travel up a single shaft. Stay low, keep your bag light, know which way the sun-patches and the bell rope are if you get turned around in the terraces. You'll probably never need it — they are very practiced at living close — but a traveler in a strange made bowl should know the up.

Do not leave a mark. Do not call it a ruin, a relic, a primitive marvel. It's a home that is holding itself together by touch, hour by hour. The best preparation is the simplest: come small, come clean, come quiet enough to hear the drip, and be ready to be remade a little by the place while you're in it.

**A pocket checklist:**

- [ ] Soft, small, front-carry bag; nothing hard, dangly, or valuable
- [ ] Breathable natural fibers, long sleeves, closed grippy shoes, no brimmed hat
- [ ] Own water, salt, cloth mask, earplugs, small lamp, first aid
- [ ] Hands washed, nails short, routine vaccines current, no colds
- [ ] No perfume, no seeds, no soil, no camera flash up chimneys, no geotags
- [ ] Knees ready, patience ready, sideways-walking ready
- [ ] Gift ready: cloth, needles, salt, soap — small and consumable
