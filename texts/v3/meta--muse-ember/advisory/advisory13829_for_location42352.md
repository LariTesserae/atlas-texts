The High Pavement is not a climb, a hike, or a wilderness in the ordinary sense. It is a logistics and physiology problem disguised as a landscape.

You will arrive above the weather, on a regional stone table perhaps forty kilometres across, with nowhere to sleep, nothing to drink, no fuel to burn, no path to follow, and no silence in which to think. The place does not change its mind. It does not offer a better season, a calmer morning, or a sheltered valley three hours ahead. If you are prepared, it will be austere but survivable for a limited time. If you are unprepared, it will be cold, breathless, sunburned, deafened, and disoriented at the same time.

Do not treat it as a destination for an unacclimatized ordinary human to wander into casually.

### 1. Decide whether you are fit to go at all

**Height comes first.** You will be above a cloud-sea, under a black-blue zenith, breathing air that is permanently cold, thin, and dry. People native to the Pavement, and the animals filmed across it, are adapted to that air. You are not. A portal or multiverse transit that drops you from sea level directly onto the table is dangerous, however convenient it is.

An ordinary traveller should have:

- A medical assessment specifically for high altitude, heart and lung conditions, and recent respiratory illness.
- Progressive acclimatization over days at altitude, ideally sleeping at gradually increasing heights before you spend a night on the upper table.
- Supplemental oxygen on arrival if a clinician advises it, especially for the first hours.
- A planned, prompt descent route back through cloud to lower, softer air.

Do not rely on “getting used to it” once you are there. The table is too exposed, too featureless, and too far from shelter for a first experiment. If you cannot spend time acclimatizing below the Pavement, a short visit with oxygen and an immediate retreat is safer than a long stay.

Also decide now that this is a short, self-contained visit. There are no huts, no caches left by others, no water to find, and no fuel to gather. You carry in everything you need, and you leave before your margins run out. The Pavement will look exactly the same when you leave as when you arrived; that is not a sign that you have misjudged the time, only that its equilibrium is complete.

### 2. The four hazards acting together

Prepare for all four simultaneously, because that is how they arrive.

**Thin, cold, dry air.** It will scrape your lungs, dry your eyes and lips, and make your breath mist even under full sun. You will lose moisture just by breathing, and thirst will lag behind the loss because you feel cold. Breathe through a buff or balaclava when moving, sip water on a schedule, and do not shout to be heard. Shouting is futile and wastes breath.

**Violent light.** The sun is described as 2.8 on any scale you invent, bouncing off pale quartzite, schist, slate, and granite. Shadows are luminous. One side of a limb can burn while the other stays wintry. Wear Category 4 glacier glasses or close-fitting goggles with side protection, a wide brim or legionnaire, sunscreen on face, ears, neck, underside of the chin, backs of hands, and nose. Reapply. Do not use contact lenses without backup glasses; the dry wind will make them miserable. The hard light and featureless stone are also a navigation trap: do not use shadows to judge temperature or time.

**Unending sound.** The pavement itself is an organ. Wind forced through hand-wide fissures makes a constant chord, and over it the cliff colonies of small long-winged birds make a chorus that never rests. This is not background noise that ebbs; it is a wall. It will drown speech, music, a whistle, and any hope of hearing a companion call.

Bring high-quality ear protection, but plan communication without voice. Use agreed hand signals, a small write-on slate, or notes. Travel in sight of one another. Do not separate to “explore” a basin or scree fan; in a landscape where you may pass three plants in an hour, you can lose a companion in minutes and never hear them shout.

**Open, old, polished stone.** The ground is mostly interlocking plates, smoothed by glaciers long past and wind since. Boulders do not roll, scree fans sit in balance, and the whole surface feels ancient rather than fresh. That stability is useful, but polished stone is still slippery. Add brief drizzle whose beads run off instead of soaking in, and you have wet, wind-polished plates.

Wear stiff, ankle-supporting boots with soft, high-friction soles. Use trekking poles; tapping ahead also helps you feel fissures instead of trusting your eyes in the glare. Avoid taking shortcuts across biological crust, cushion plants, or damp pan margins. The pavement’s life is a thin film and tiny domes; a careless kneel can crush what took years to grow. Rest and study plants standing or seated on bare stone, not pressed into cushions.

### 3. Clothing for sun and winter at once

Dress for the wind first. Constant wind will find every loose flap, every gap at a wrist, every mesh panel.

Use a windproof outer shell over insulating layers, with no dangling straps. Keep your head, neck, hands, and face covered even when the sun is blazing. The cook’s rule is simple: if stone is hot enough to burn one side of you, the wind is still cold enough to chill the other.

Rotate your body relative to the light when you stop, and check your skin periodically. Numb fingertips, white patches, or a suddenly hot ear are as likely here as frostbite. Gloves need to stay on for most tasks; choose dexterous ones and practise opening zips and eating while wearing them before you go.

### 4. Shelter without building anything

Nothing on the Pavement builds, and you should not start. There are no walls, paths, or permanent markers, and a footprint in basin dust disappears quickly. That means you cannot navigate by your own earlier tracks, and you cannot improve a campsite.

Bring a low-profile, windproof bivouac or tent that can be pitched on bare stone without stakes: use deadweight anchors, wide loops, and a low shape that will not sail. Pitch on a flat plate away from fissures; a crack is a resonator, not a windbreak. Use the lee of a large boulder if one is handy, but do not move stones to make a shelter. Under plates that can be lifted there are snow fleas, mites, and springtails; leave plates and boulders exactly as found.

Sleep on a high-insulation pad. Cold stone conducts heat straight out of your body, and the air will not warm it. Add a warm layer beneath you, not only over you. Use eye mask and earplugs; the Pavement does not dim at night, and the bird chorus does not stop for darkness.

Fire is not a plan. Air too thin, fuel too sparse, wind too constant. Do not carry firewood, and do not rely on a stove except as a backup in a fully shielded, tested setup. Eat mostly cold or tepid food kept in an insulated container. Chemical heat packs are useful for hands and core, kept away from lichens and crust. Practise a no-fire meal routine below before you rely on it above.

### 5. Food and water: bring it all

There is no river, lake, or stream. Water exists as condensation films in clay pans, perhaps a centimetre deep, perfectly still except for the tremor of the wind-sound. It does not deepen or spread. Rain is a fine drizzle that beads and runs off within minutes. That is hydrology, not a water supply.

Bring all drinking water. Cache extra on a lower table if your route allows, but assume nothing can be replenished on the upper surface. Do not drink from pans; they are habitat, mirror, and microbial film, not a well. A small cup to catch rain beads is a curiosity, not a plan.

Food should be compact, calorie-dense, pre-portioned, and edible with gloves on. Appetite often drops at altitude while energy needs rise in cold wind. Eat small amounts regularly. Keep bottles and food from becoming cold bricks: carry water inside your clothing or an insulated sleeve. Batteries, cameras, and satellite devices also lose performance in cold; keep them warm and tethered. Tools dropped on the Pavement become indistinguishable from wind-sorted stone within a day.

### 6. Navigation and communication in a place without paths

The table extends in every direction, then breaks to another table, then a ridge like a broken spine, and then air. Other summits rise from the cloud-sea like islands, and the horizon shows the beginning of curve. It is grand and dangerously easy to misread.

Use a compass, altimeter, GPS where available, and a paper map. Mark your route with bright, retrievable markers only as needed, and collect every one. Do not build cairns; they are not permitted by the place’s own logic and would mislead others as much as you. A personal locator beacon and satellite messenger are necessary, but remember that rescue, if possible at all, is not close.

The wind has a principal direction forced up a tilted slope and through fissures, and the sun gives a harsh reference, but local basins, troughs, and nunatak ribs interrupt both. Treat every “obvious” landmark—two house-sized stones, a shallow basin, a distant summit—as a bearing to check, not a promise.

Bird colonies concentrate in cliff bands and crevices; keep a respectful distance and do not flush thousands of birds for a photograph. Solitary goat-like grazers hold huge territories, one family per valley of stone; give them wide room. The great eagle-analogue circles for hours without flapping; do not leave small gear or food where it might attract attention. Midges and snow-insects drift up on the updraft; cover your face, and do not mistake their fallout for food or shelter.

### 7. Mind and pacing

The Pavement’s most insidious effect is monotony. The light repeats, the pockets stay the same size, the colonies scream at the same pitch, grazers move but do not increase, deaths are precisely replaced. Cushion plants look hard as stone and do not visibly move. Lichens may smell of rain that has not fallen for weeks. Pans reflect sound as well as sky, so the chord may seem to rise out of black water.

Bring ritual with you: a watch, a notebook, a route plan, fixed meal and rest times, a way to record bearings and symptoms. Boredom here is not laziness; it is a predictable response to a place with no feedback. Keep exertion deliberately slow. Breathlessness, headache, poor sleep, nausea, or unusual fatigue are altitude signals, not character flaws. Stop, rest, hydrate, and descend if they worsen.

Take photographs sparingly and do not kneel for long staring at plants that will not change. Cold stills you faster than movement. If you see something beautiful, note the bearing and move on; the Pavement is not an exception-seeking place.

### A compact checklist

- Medical clearance and acclimatization plan; oxygen if advised; scheduled descent.
- At least two, preferably three, travellers; never solo.
- Windproof, sunproof layers; glacier glasses; balaclava; gloves; sunscreen.
- Ear protection and silent communication plan: signals, slate, notes.
- Stiff boots, poles, low-profile stone-pitchable shelter, high-insulation sleep pad.
- All water and food; cold meals; insulated bottles; chemical warmers. No fire plan.
- Compass, altimeter, GPS, paper map, satellite messenger, retrievable markers.
- Waste bags; no crutches, cairns, digging, lifting plates, or collecting tools, shells, lichens, or stones.
- Warm storage for electronics; tethers for everything important.
- A firm turnaround time and route, written down before arrival.

Leave the way you came: down through cloud, into softer light and softer sound. Behind you the High Pavement will continue without noticing you. That is the final preparation. You are not there to conquer it, improve it, or be changed by it in any dramatic way. You are there to pass through a finished equilibrium briefly, do as little damage as possible, and return to a world where weather moves, water runs, and silence is allowed to exist.
