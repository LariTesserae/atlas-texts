If you prepare for this place as if it were an exotic world, you will be over-prepared in exactly the wrong ways. It is not exotic.

No suit, no respirator, no tent, no water filter, no bug spray, no climbing gear, no weapon, no magical or scientific shielding. It is an ordinary warm limestone hollow, 20 or 30 paces across, 5 or 6 metres below a flat dry plain. Everything anomalous about it is how completely *ordinary* it is, and how completely it has stopped changing.

Your preparation problem is not survival. It is heat and thirst on the way there, light and time and quiet once you are in it, and the fact that you will be the first thing in a very long time to change it.

### What it is not

There is nothing inside to hurt you, feed you, or hide from you.

No animals at all - no insects in the air, no lizards on the warm stones, no spiders, no birds coming down. No tracks, webs, droppings, bones, or burrows. You do not need a net, repellent, or bite kit.

No water at all - no pool, drip, seep, damp stone, or mud. You cannot refill, wash, or forage inside. There is nothing edible: half a dozen dormant plants in the whole hollow, moss cushions grey and crisp, lichen crusts, a tuft of straw grass, a leathery-leaved shrub that has probably not flowered here. No fruit, no berries, no leaf fall to speak of. Do not try to eat or drink anything there.

No unstable geology to speak of. The walls are settled, scalloped, softened. No fresh slabs, no new cracks, no active runnels, no frost-shattering. It is not going to drop a rock on you. You do not need a helmet or a rope.

No temporal or physical anomaly. Light is dim because the sun never reaches the floor directly, not because light is being stopped. Warmth is steady because still air has pooled below the rim and stone holds heat, not because of a field. Your watch, compass, phone, and body will work normally. The strangeness is equilibrium: erosion finished, growth at a standstill, dust unwashed for longer than anything remembers.

### The real discipline: what you bring in will stay

Because there is no water to wash the dust, no wind to move it, no rot to break anything down, and no animal to disturb it, this place does not clean up after visitors. That is the central rule for a traveler, especially a multiverse traveler.

**Go to the toilet and empty your pockets on the plain, before you step over the lip.** Anything dropped, left, or buried inside will lie there essentially as long as the hollow has already existed. No leaf rots here. A tissue, a wrapper, a coin, a cairn of stones, a boot print, would all persist.

**Come in clean.** Wash boots, brush out daypack, take food out. You are the first animal life this hollow has had, as far as its dust can tell. Do not bring seeds, soil, crumbs, or hitchhiking insects from another world, another farm, another gutter. The plants are hanging on by mineral moisture deep in fissures. They have no defense against introduction because they have never needed one.

**Walk as if your feet are writing.** A step on the fine calcareous silt makes that soft, almost soundless crunch, then settles. It will not be erased by wind or rain or time in any human sense. Choose one line in, one line out, near the middle if you can, and do not wander to fill the room. Do not move the half-sunk flint nodules to see under them, do not stack stones, do not scratch the wall, do not leave a marker. The temptation will be strong - in a place that feels timeless, you want to prove you were there. Resist it. That is the whole etiquette here.

No fires, no camping, no shelter. There is no wood, no fuel, and smoke would simply pool in the still air and stay with you. You could, in theory, strike a flake off the soft limestone. Don't. Take nothing, leave nothing. What you take home should be photos, notes, and dust on your shoes that you brush off outside.

### What to actually bring

A very small, ordinary daypack, mostly water.

1. **Water, and a hat, for the plain.** You will be thirsty inside, and there is none there. The inside feels mild because there is no direct sun, but you will have walked across pale, flat, sun-exposed ground to get there and you will climb back out into it. 1 litre is comfortable for a short visit; bring 1.5-2 if the plain is hot. Sip before you go down. Sun screen for the approach only - you will not need it on the floor.

2. **Sturdy, closed shoes you don't mind dusting.** The silt is fine, matte grey, and will show on everything. It packs in places, gives in others. No sandals - the lip is rounded stone, the floor has weathered flint, and you want ankle support for a clumsy 5-metre step down you will make while your eyes are still adjusting. A small brush to clean them at the rim afterward is a luxury.

3. **A way to know time that isn't the place.** The defining experience inside is that time stops being readable. Light does not change with the hour. Warmth does not rise or fall. Plants do not grow through a "season." Silence does not thin. After 20 minutes you will genuinely not know if it is 10 minutes or an hour. Bring a watch - wrist is better than a phone you keep putting away - and set a silent alarm for when you intend to leave. Many people doze in that thick, warm, uniform air. If you are prone to that, set the alarm before you descend.

4. **Sun glasses for outside, to be taken off gradually.** Inside light is diffused, grey-green, up through a small disc of sky. Colours mute quickly. There are no hard shadows because there is no hard light. Your eyes will need 10-15 minutes to separate wall from floor from stone from plant. A harsh white torch or camera flash used on entry will ruin that, and is unnecessary - you can see all the boundaries from anywhere, it is that small and bounded. If you want a light at all, bring a low, warm one, and use it sparingly. For most people, none is best.

5. **A phone or beacon, left conceptually outside.** Assume you will be out of contact on the floor. Stone and the rim will weaken signal, and more importantly, you will be quietly out of sight for 30 to 60 minutes. Tell someone on the plain - or your ship, or your contact in this universe - that you are going down into the hollow, when you expect to be back on the rim, and that silence from inside is expected, not an emergency. Leave any interdimensional beacon, anchor, or return marker on the rim where the sky is open. Not inside. You do not want to be the thing that changes the equilibrium, and you do not need a lifeline in a 6-metre dip you can climb out of at any moment.

6. **A notebook, if you want to remember it.** The place photographs poorly without effort - muted greys, no contrast - and it does not lend itself to dramatic description later. A few lines about the tick of a cooling mineral on a wall, your own breathing sounding loud and then absorbed, the uniformity of warmth at ankle and head, will be worth more than 50 identical grey pictures.

Leave at home: tent, stove, sleeping bag, filters, insect spray, climbing harness, helmet, food beyond a mouthful for the walk, alcohol, and a book or speaker to break the silence. The silence is the point, and it has a texture. Music would just sit in it.

### Entering

Step over the rounded lip and down, rather than climb up into it. There is no path. Test a foot on the silt - it may be packed or loose. Once down, stop in the middle. From there you can see the whole room of air and stone. That complete visibility is a gift; in a larger cave you would feel corners. Here, claustrophobia, if it comes, will come from closeness and stillness, not from unknownness. Breathe slowly. Let your eyes adjust. Let the grey-green separate.

Do not rush across to the shrub, the moss, the lichen. Walk at half speed. The dust is dry and still, moving slowly will keep it from rising, though it is not dusty in the sense of a road. If you have asthma or a dust sensitivity, a simple cloth over nose and mouth for the entry only is enough; once still, the air is still.

The warmth will surprise you by not changing. It is not the heat of the plain, not cool like a cave. It is indoor-closure warm, the same against the wall as in the centre, the same at head as at the ankle. It does not circulate. On the plain you regulated with breeze and shade. Here there is neither. If you stay long, you will warm slowly without noticing you are sweating because nothing feels harsh. Sip water even if you are not yet thirsty. Do not plan to stay more than 45 minutes to an hour on a first visit. The place will not change in that time, and neither, measurably, will you - except your comfort will decline.

### Inside conduct

Keep your voice low, not because anything is listening, but because sound behaves oddly. Your steps crunch, then settle. Your breath is the loudest event. A small stone ticking as it cools on the wall will be the most dramatic thing that happens in an hour. That is not a failure of the visit. That is the visit.

Do not touch the plants to test if they are alive. They are dormant, leathery, grey-undersided, crisp. They persist on vapour and deep stone moisture. Touching, picking, or photographing from far off with a flash will do more harm than help, and they cannot be replaced. Look at the lichen on the shaded faces of stones - green-grey, ochre, almost white. It is growing, at a pace too slow to notice in a lifetime of visits. That slowness is instructive.

Do not try to make the place do something - stack a cairn, clear a circle, build a windbreak, leave something for the next traveler. There has never been a next traveler in the dust. Be the first to leave no sign instead of the first to leave one.

Psychologically, expect anticlimax and unease in roughly equal measure. Some people find the completeness of the silence and the sameness of the light briefly soothing, then boring, then faintly anxious. Some get the urge to move, to rearrange, to make a sound on purpose. Some feel watched in an empty room. None of that means something is wrong with you or with the place. It means you are a warm, noisy, moving animal in a place tuned to almost exactly the opposite. The way out is always visible: a small dim disc of sky, and the rounded lip below it. You can leave at any moment without tools. Reminding yourself of that every few minutes helps.

Children and claustrophobic travelers: go in pairs for the first time, stay near the centre, keep the visit short, and keep the exit in sight. No one needs to "tough it out" in a hollow.

### Leaving

Climb back over the lip the way you came down, into flat, bright plain light. Your eyes will be dazzled for a minute. The wind, which never reached you inside, will be back. Brush dust from your shoes outside, not at the lip where it could fall back in.

Check your watch. You will probably be surprised. The hollow will be closed behind you, unchanged, as it was before anyone recorded a footprint in it, and as it will remain after your footprints have, inevitably, become part of its long record.

Preparation, in one sentence: bring water for the desert, glasses for the plain, a watch for the timelessness, clean boots for the dust, and the restraint to leave it exactly as you found it - because once you have been there, you are part of its history in a way no animal, plant, or stone here has ever been.
