This is not a place an ordinary human visits in the normal sense. You are not a tourist walking through a town - you are a weather event in it.

The hollow is, from the outside, a hand's-width crack under a freshly tipped plate of frost-lifted sod at the edge of a cold marsh. From the inside, ceiling to floor is less than your chest-high, and everything is within arm's reach. And yet it is a planned, inhabited, working town: canals, nursery terraces, broad traffic clearings, lanes following the weeping walls, houses woven onto living root-columns, storehouses on stilts, bridges trained from living roots, all of it built very carefully with chewed stems, flint, bone needles, sinew, and scavenged copper wire.

A human knee is the size of a district. A human footfall is an earthquake. A human breath, warm and wet in that cold still air, will literally become rain on the root-vault above you. Your body heat will melt the skin of ice on the canals.

Prepare accordingly, or don't go in at all. The most responsible preparation is to accept that you may only look in from the seam, and only for a few minutes at a time.

### Before you go

**1. Treat it as a living, inhabited habitat - not a cave.**
Everything here is substrate. The floor is packed living moss, root-hair, lichen and sedge rhizome. The walls are the same felt hung vertically. The ceiling is a braided dome of living roots. A house is also a plant. A road is also food for the grazers who maintain it by eating its edges. A dead animal is a brick. A living animal may be thatch.

Do not peel back moss to look underneath. Do not pick up a stone-shard, a stem, a patch, a tool carving. Do not collect a souvenir, a specimen, a photo with flash. Do not leave food, crumbs, tobacco, or anything edible - here a crumb is a famine or a plague for the things that live at millimeter scale.

Clean yourself and everything you wear the way you would for a surgical theater, then for a wilderness area: shower, no perfume, no sunscreen, no insect repellent, no scented deodorant. Boots and waders scrubbed free of mud from this world, let alone another. No seeds, soil, or water from your home marsh on your clothes. You are the invasive species.

For the same reason: no pets, no familiars, no drones, no metal detectors, no digging tools, no tent pegs, no trekking pole, no camp stove, no headlamp on bright white. No walking stick. Anything with a point, a cleat, or a bang will do disproportionate damage.

**2. Physical preparation for a human body in a cold, wet microclimate.**
The marsh outside is low, cold, and water lies just under the skin of the earth - black and cold within a finger's depth. The hollow inside is colder, darker, and perpetually condensing.

Bring for outside *and* inside, in layers:
- Knee-high soft-soled waders or waterproof boots. No crampons, no aggressive tread. You need insulation more than grip; the moss is springy, gives slowly, and will swallow sound so you will think you are lighter than you are.
- Wool socks, double-bagged, plus a complete dry change waiting outside on a dry hummock. You will get wet from drips even if it is not raining.
- Insulated knee and elbow pads, soft cloth, not shed-foam. You will spend the whole visit on the ground. The plant felt is insulated but cold will come through a knee in minutes.
- Thin work gloves to protect against fresh, sharp shards of frozen sod and broken root-wood jutting from the walls, and against cold. Thin fleece over them if you need dexterity.
- A soft cap, not a hard helmet. The root vault is low. A helmet will scrape and tear it.
- A small, soft, silent pack - or nothing. Anything rigid on your back will hit the ceiling and the walls behind you.
- Anti-fog for glasses if you wear them. Your breath will fog everything, then fall back as rain.
- Inhaler if you have asthma. Cold wet air, mould, moss spores, and the stress of crouching in darkness will provoke a cough, and a cough in there is a storm of warm wet air. If you are coughing uncontrollably, you must leave immediately.
- Tetanus up to date. You will be kneeling in black marsh water, among copper wire, bone, sharp ice-locked sod, and animals you cannot see.

Time and group: Go alone inside, one human at a time, for a planned 10-15 minutes maximum. No children, no one who cannot control panic or coughing. A partner waits outside, low and quiet, not blocking the seam. Their job is to watch the plate above - this hollow is *recent*. The fracture lines are still sharp, the ice still locked in the cracks, the plate still bright and unweathered. Frost heave made it; frost heave can settle it. Cracking sounds, a sudden increase in drips, water rising in the canals, animals fleeing in a silver patter - those are abort signals. Leave at once, backwards, without turning.

**3. Mental preparation.**
Once inside there is no horizon. Everything is in arm's reach, and it still feels like it has outskirts and distance. That is because the districts are real - cold seep quarter, light-seam quarter, isolated huts out toward the edges - but they are compressed into a pocket. Do not shout to see if someone answers. Sound does not carry; moss swallows it. You will hear drips, a soft tick as a root grows a hair's breadth, a faint click-chorus from the far wall, and then quiet again.

It is dark, but not night. The only true light is a hand's-width grey seam where the plate is propped open. The rest is underwater translucence through thick blue-green leaves and hundreds of points of eyeshine blinking independently on the walls. Your eyes will take 10-15 minutes to adjust. That is the point. Bringing bright light destroys that adaptation, blinds the residents, and makes your exit into flat marsh daylight brutal - even overcast light will feel like a blow when you come out. Close your eyes and shade them on the way out.

Expect to feel watched, crowded, claustrophobic, and simultaneously lost in a large place. All of those are honest responses to scale. Do not try to map it by feeling your way along the walls. The walls are wet, alive, and lined with froglets flattened against them and salamanders nosing through the canals.

### Entering and moving

Approach the marsh as marsh: step carefully on firm sedge, never dig, never stand on the tipped plate itself. A human's weight on that freshly lifted edge can settle the whole dome.

Get low at the seam, to the knee, wet. Enter feet-first, facing *out* toward the light for the entire visit. You will leave backwards. Never turn around inside. Turning means sweeping your body across houses, canals, and terraces you cannot see.

Move only on the worn clearings and broad lanes. The nursery terraces - low moss shelves with young plants in straight rows - look like flat places to kneel. They are farms. Do not step on them. Step *over* the black mirror canals, a few millimeters deep, never through them. Do not test a root bridge. If someone built it for a creature a tenth your weight, it is not for you. Go around, through the water at a clearing if you must, but do not put your weight on a bridge, a lashing, a stilt leg, or a double-skinned wall.

Do not stand still. Moss under a still foot sinks, suffocates, and does not spring back quickly. Shift, move slowly, keep your weight distributed on knees and forearms. Keep your head down - your standing height is higher than the vault.

Touch almost nothing. The walls weep, the shards are skinned in translucent green film, the water sheets in slow tears. Your hand will leave a warm imprint that alters growth, melts ice, brings predators. If you drop something small, leave it unless you can retrieve it without searching and crawling. A dropped object is less damage than a frantic search through a terrace.

Use, at most, one dim, warm, downward-aimed light, only when you cannot see the floor in front of your knees. One brief, low light is better than a bright beam sweeping the eyeshine. Do not use a camera flash, a phone screen, or a magnifier - focused light and heat are weapons at this scale.

Breathe slowly and through your nose if you can. You will fog the air. That is unavoidable; staying longer makes it worse.

### What will happen to you in there

You will get cold in your fingers, toes, and knees despite the waders. The plants are cold, the water is black and cold, the air condenses on you. When you cannot feel your fingers, you cannot feel the floor, and you will crush something. That is your cue to leave, not to push on.

You will get wet from above. The vault drips perpetually. Your breath joins it.

You will be bitten, in the broad sense, by everything you cannot see: springtails, mites, hair-thin worms in the felt, blind salamanders, skaters on the water. Do not touch the animals. Do not lift a moss cushion. Do not put a hand in the canal to steady yourself. The water is clear at the coldest seep and it is still not drinking water for you. It carries bacteria, fungal spores, and whatever lives in a town that uses dead animals as bricks and living animals as roofs. Do not drink, do not wash your face inside.

You will attract attention. Eyes will blink on. A hunt may go through the canal in a coordinated silver wave. That is not a show. Stay still, low, and let it pass. Your stillness is the only polite language you have in common.

### Leaving

Leave backwards, low, through the same seam, without trying to widen it. Do not pull roots, do not break ice to make a smoother path, do not brace your feet against the opposite wall to push yourself out - you will lever the hollow.

Outside, sit down immediately on something dry you brought. Rewarm slowly. Check hands and feet. Shake out your clothes into a sealed bag - mites, springtails, wormlets, and seeds hitchhike very well on wool. Do not wash your gear in the marsh water; wash it at home after it has dried thoroughly for several days. Note what you saw from memory later, outside, in daylight. Sketching inside with a light creates heat and shadow and duration you do not have.

Do not mark the place. No beacon, no string, no cut root, no GPS pin shared with other travelers. A pocket like this survives because almost no one knows it is there. From above it is only a tipped root-plate, already mossed over, easy to step over. That is how it should stay.

If you can see your own knee-print in the moss when you look back, you stayed too long and pressed too hard. The town will repair it, as it repairs everything - eaten, frozen, grown over, rebuilt in the dark - but it should not have to repair you.

Go in cold, clean, quiet, low, and briefly, face the light the whole time, touch nothing that is alive, and leave the way you came. If you cannot do all of that, watch from the seam and let the town keep its horizon, even though it is only an arm's length away.
