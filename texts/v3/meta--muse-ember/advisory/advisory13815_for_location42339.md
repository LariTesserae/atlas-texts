It is smaller than you expect, and that is the first trap.

Twenty minutes on foot across the summit bowl. No castles, no forests, no rivers to resupply from. But you will have climbed and climbed to get to a room that size, through cloud and through air so thin your lungs have to work like bellows. A portal does not acclimatize you. You arrive as an ordinary human, with ordinary lungs, skin, and eyes, at the top of the world. Dress for the summit *before* you step through, bring everything you need *with* you, and plan on the climb down being as serious as the climb up. The bowl is small. The way out of it is not.

Here is how to prepare for the place as it actually is:

### 1. Your body first: altitude is the whole trip

You will be breathless doing things that are trivial at sea level. Walking the rim in twenty minutes could take an hour with rests. That is normal at this height, and it is not a failure.

* **Do not portal straight to the top if you can avoid it.** If you have the choice, set your base camp / return portal lower, spend days ascending, sleep as low as you can for as long as you can. If you must arrive at the top, assume the first day is for sitting, breathing slowly, drinking, and doing almost nothing. Any headache, nausea, dizziness, or shortness of breath at rest is your cue to go back down. Ordinary human physiology at this height can deteriorate fast, especially once you are trapped above cloud - there is no ground below to just walk away to.
* Talk to a doctor before you go about altitude. Do not self-medicate or try to tough it out. Skip alcohol and sedatives up there - they depress the breathing you need most.
* Pace like a tortoise. Your boots will crunch and squeak on frozen cinder, your breath will be too loud in the quiet, and both will get louder the faster you try to go. Stop before you are gasping. The summit will still be there in five minutes.
* You will dehydrate without sweating. The air is so dry and thin that moisture is pulled straight from your breath and skin - sublimation, the same thing that makes the frost gardens vanish at sunrise. Drink on a schedule, not just when thirsty. Carry 3-4 liters at minimum, with electrolytes. Bring it as liquid, in wide-mouth bottles you can keep inside your pack or against your body.
    Do not count on the place to provide it. The melt pans are saucer-sized and frozen over at noon. The ice lens under a stone is inaccessible. The bead of condensation on a north-facing block will sit all day, but you cannot drink it. A cloud passing over for five minutes will damp everything, then the sun will burn it off in an hour. Water here never runs. It freezes, it evaporates, it stays put.

A fuel stove, if you need one, is the only fire you will ever make. There is no wood, no dung, no plant to burn. Water also boils cooler here, so cooking takes longer and needs more fuel than you expect.

### 2. Eyes and skin: light from above and below

At noon the sky is a hard, dark blue, almost black at the zenith, because there is so little air to scatter light. The sun itself is white, vertical, and merciless. Snow and ice that never melts throw it back up at you. Everything is bleached to greys and whites, shadows are the only color, and they are blue-black and knife-sharp.

You will squint even with a shaded hand. You will water, burn, and snow-blind if you are careless.

* **Glacier glasses, Category 4, with full side shields** are not optional. Bring a second pair, and a pair of dark goggles for wind and for when a cloud passes over the rim and drives saltation - low clouds of snow and ice dust a few centimeters off the ground. That dust in your eyes is sandpaper.
* A hat with a brim, a balaclava or buff you can pull up, and a neck gaiter. Sunburn here comes from below as well as above: under your nose, under your chin, inside your ears. Cover everything.
* SPF 50+ sunscreen, applied twice, and lip balm with SPF, on a tether. Reapply. UV will bleach your pack straps, your tent fly, and your skin in weeks, faster than anywhere you have been. Bring gear you can afford to lose to the sun, and keep toiletries and batteries in an inner pocket near your body - cold and UV kill them fast.
* Carry something bright - orange mittens, a bright pack cover, bright tape on your poles. In a landscape of white stone, white snow, and white grit, a dropped black glove or a grey water bottle disappears instantly. And because things do not rot here, what you drop will be bleached and sandblasted in place for a very long time. Mark everything, tether everything small.

### 3. Warmth: cold in the sun, colder in shadow

It is cold even at midday. Your breath clouds and stings your nose. Your fingers go numb despite the sun. A black glass shard under your palm might feel almost warm at noon, and the one step of shadow beside it feels unnaturally cold. At dusk the whole surface ticks and pings as stones contract, ice blades snap like tiny bells, and the temperature falls far below freezing. The rim keeps its shadow circle complete long after the center has gone dark.

That diurnal swing is the rule. Frost heave, freeze-thaw, sublimation - the place is busy just not biologically.

* No cotton, ever. Merino or synthetic base layers, a warm insulating mid-layer, and a windproof outer shell. Wind comes over the rim not as a howl but as a hiss, driving that low saltation and scouring the windward side. It will find every gap.
* Mittens over liner gloves. Your hands will be doing the work: catching yourself on angular blocks, on black volcanic glass that looks wet and cuts like a knife, on frost-shattered rock. Leather work gloves are better for the rim than soft ski gloves. Bare skin will stick to ice blades.
* Frostbite will take your nose, ears, and fingers before you feel it because sun is on your back. Check each other, and yourself, on a timer. Keep food from freezing solid by carrying it near your torso. Keep your water bottle from freezing in your pack by the same trick, and do not rely on a drinking tube - it will freeze solid.
* If you must spend the night, the night is not a suggestion. Ground is stone, cinder, and ice - not soft, and it conducts cold through anything thin. A high R-value pad, a serious sleeping bag, low-profile tent anchored to rock not to snow - because the surface is always arriving and departing, stripped from the crest and laid down in the bowl. Do not trust the old windbreaks as shelter. They are shoulder-high, low, semi-circular walls of uncut slabs, tops slumped like old backs, open to the sky, with grit filling the hollow. Good for crouching out of the wind at noon for five minutes. Not a hut. The one blackened hearth suggests a fire was lit there only a few times in a very long history. There is no fuel to repeat it.

Best time to be in the bowl is late morning, after the dawn hoarfrost has sublimated but before the glare is at its most vertical and the afternoon wind picks up. At dawn every windward edge is furred with needle-fine hoarfrost, crystal gardens a finger's length long in sheltered cracks. Beautiful, fragile, and slippery. At dusk the ice lenses glow from inside and the light goes long and blue - beautiful, and a warning to be off the rim before dark.

### 4. Feet and footing: crowded, layered, deceptive

The ground is crowded. You cannot take a step without stepping over, around, or between things: fist-size angular blocks, smooth pale rhyolite plates, loose cinder that crunches, fine sifting grit, sudden slabs of clear ice frozen into a hollow, drifts of snow a hand's breadth deep over crust over ice over stone.

* Stiff, ankle-high mountaineering boots, broken in, with microspikes or light crampons. Ice lenses set into rock like eyes will be hidden under a dusting of snow and will put you down without warning.
* Trekking poles. Use them to probe. What looks like solid ground may be a drift over a tongue of ice. What looks flat may tilt a fraction more every season from frost heave.
* Watch your distance. In this glare, a white stone ten meters away and a white stone two meters away look identical. Footprints fill with rime as you turn around - you cannot retrace your own track reliably. Use a compass - even with that strange dark sky - and a GPS if you have one, and pick a rim landmark *before* you need it. Keep an eye on the rim's shadow; it is your only clock and your only wall.
* Be careful with the cairns. Three or four stones high, spaced close across the white, they were probably meant to be followed in whiteout, but the mineral world has heaved walls outward and no one alive can tell you which way was forward. Do not add to them, do not knock them down, and do not navigate by them unless you have no better option.

### 5. Silence, sound, and the things that grow without living

The thin air carries sound poorly. Mostly you will hear yourself: your boots, your breath, the dry squeak on frozen cinder. Then intermittently: a sharp *ping* as a stone splits a hairline wider in the evening chill, a faint chime when an ice blade snaps, the wind's hiss stopping as suddenly as it came.

Prepare mentally for that. It is disorienting. It is not empty - frost gardens competing for shadow, salts blooming as pale crust, ice creeping in pockets, wind balancing wind - but it is not alive in any way you can eat, follow, or talk to. Nothing green. Nothing that burrows, flies, or crawls. A dead insect carried up from the cloud sea below will lie unrotted until the sun turns it white.

Do not step on the ice rosettes and efflorescence if you can help it. They will regrow, but they are the closest thing this place has to vegetation, and a boot through them is exactly the kind of damage that will be visible for years in a place where things bleach and crack instead of rotting. Step *between* things, not on them. Hands off the pecked lines on the flat boulder too - weathered almost past reading, they deserve to finish weathering on their own terms.

Nothing rots here. That means your trash does not either. Pack out absolutely everything: food wrappers, tape, a broken pole tip, a lost screw. A footprint will fill with rime. A plastic bottle will be sandblasted white and remain for a century.

### 6. Leaving

The bowl felt like an island taken out of the sky, ringed by cloud, because below the rim there is no ground at all. Your exit is a climb down through that cloud sea, on rock that has been frost-wedged for centuries. Start down early, in daylight, with air and water to spare, and with the understanding that weather can close the rim without warning.

If you are using a portal, secure it against saltation and drifting grit - the same wind that deposits snow in drifts on the lee side will fill and bury a low opening. Keep batteries warm, keep the threshold clear of ice, and do not let the portal be your only plan if it fails in wind or cold. Bring a satellite communicator if you have one; do not count on any signal from a bowl this high.

---

In short: come small, come warm, come covered, come slow, and come self-sufficient. This is not a day hike to a pretty view. It is a visit to a place where the living has been subtracted and stone, ice, and light carry on alone, dense and quiet and busy, in a village-sized room at the top of the world. Treat your body accordingly, and it will let you walk its whole extent before your breath settles - which, if you have prepared well, it eventually will.
