You don't prepare for this place like a mountain, or a desert, or a dangerous new world in the usual sense. There is nothing to climb, nothing to fight, nothing to outrun. It is a basin only a few hummocks lower than the land around it, and you will be inside it from the first second.

The danger is slower and wetter than that. Cold that is not sharp but permanent. Ground that is saturated to a finger's depth everywhere. Disorientation that is not a maze but a recurrence — you pass a grove, a pool, a white-stoned mound, and an hour later you pass them again, changed just enough that you cannot be certain if you have looped or arrived somewhere new. Sound that gets into your teeth and sternum. Time that pools in shallow places and is deeper than it should be.

It has no predators, no cliffs, no sharp rocks. Everything has been negotiated smooth by water over an age. It will not try to kill you. It will simply keep breathing, condensing, lifting its mist ten feet and raining it out again somewhere else, conducting signals root to root across miles in a second — and if you are not dressed and paced for that kind of relentlessness, you will get cold, stuck, lost, and exhausted in a place that never offered you a dry place to sit down.

Prepare for that.

### 1. Your mind first

Go in expecting to give up mastery.

There is no high ground to see it from. There will be no vista, no map you can make, no photograph that flattens it correctly. The horizon is a thickening — grey, white, green, grey again — mist in strata, reed tops fading before their bases. Ordinary navigation habits will fail you here in specific ways, and it's better to decide in advance not to fight them:

* Reflections lag. A pool may show you your back, or your face a minute ago, or reeds that are not behind you.
* Sound arrives ahead of its source. You may hear a drip a second before it falls.
* A straight line does not take you straight. Paths do not accumulate. Footprints disappear in minutes. A stick you push into the ground will be found lying flat an hour later.
* Time does not run in days. There is no day or night, only the mist tide: ground mist builds until the reeds are suggestions, then without wind it lifts into a second ground at about ten feet, rains in fine threads past the rising layer, and settles again.

If you treat recurrence as failure, you will panic. Treat it as the weather. Your job is not to conquer distance, it is to move at the basin's pace — the grazer's pace, the moss wave's pace — and to keep your return tether to the multiverse, not to landmarks. Do not try to blaze a trail, move a stone to mark a turn, or build a cairn. It will be unmade, gently and completely, and you will only have wasted energy and damaged a place that heals itself without waste.

Stay within sight and voice of your companions if you have them. When the mist thickens, separation is easy, and a reunion in "the same" grove may be a different grove. Agree beforehand: if separated, do not wander in expanding searches. Stop, stay still, listen for that one non-water note — the long, low, bowed-string sound that sometimes hangs over the reeds — and wait for the mist to lift. That lift *is* your clock.

### 2. Clothing: you will never be dry

Condensation is a permanent condition. It forms on eyelashes, on the hairs of your arms, on the undersides of leaves, and does not fall. The temperature never rises enough to dry anything. Even the warmest part of the cycle leaves chill in the stone under your hand and the water up to your ankle.

Cotton, wool, canvas, and leather are mistakes here. They will saturate, grow cold against you, and never dry. Dress like the dwarf shrubs: thick, waxy, cold to the touch, with a surface that water simply will not accept. Modern synthetics, a smooth waterproof shell, hood up most of the time because the drip chorus is millions of separate impacts, leaf to leaf to stone to pool, without rain.

Bring more layers than you think and accept that the inside of your pack will be damp no matter how many zip bags you use. Silica packets are a joke here. Keep anything critical — multiverse beacon, documents, medication, change of socks — in a hard, sealed case. Expect that case to weep on the outside.

Cold here is not frostbite cold. It's wet hypothermia cold over hours. You will not shiver dramatically; you will slow down. Bring high-calorie food you can eat cold on a hummock, and a sealed thermos. There is no fire. Everything is too wet to burn, and you should not try — the moss is a functioning circuit, and a flame would be a wound it has to heal, waste in a place that makes none.

Expect a material to be both dry and wet at once. A sleeve may feel dry while its core is saturated and give up its water later onto your hands. Don't trust touch alone.

### 3. Feet and movement

Step on a hummock and the hollow beside it fills instantly, black and still. A reed mat gives like a lung underfoot and sighs. The ground is braided sheet water, no more than a hand deep, moving toward a level it remembers, sometimes a puddle holding at a height that makes no topographic sense, sometimes draining upward into mist.

Wear soft, wide, flexible waterproof boots or shoes, not stiff mountaineering boots. You will push through ordered layers rather than fight them: root and moss mat at the bottom, a water film with duckweed, translucent sedges, dwarf shrubs, occasional dwarf trees. A stiff sole will sink, punch through, and tear that continuous mat soundlessly. That mat is the basin's nervous system. Go from hummock to hummock, slowly, and lift your feet clear.

Trenched, macerated feet are your most likely injury. Bring at least three changes of socks, in the hard case, and change them whenever you get a dry-ish hummock, even if your shell is still on. Keep any cut covered with a waterproof dressing. The eating here is done cleanly by water and fungus, molecularly, without smell of carrion. A leaf that is torn closes without a scar, re-fusing at the molecular level. You do not want your broken skin to be read as material to recycle. Don't test the moss by tearing a leaf. Don't eat anything. Lush here does not mean edible; nothing is showy, leaves are thick and waxy and cold, and everything is busy with chemistry you do not understand.

Tether everything. Glasses, hat, tools, loose straps. Gravity is present but shallow and negotiable. A dropped leaf may hover, turn slowly, and only settle when the moss signal reaches it. In the mist tide, small things lift to ten feet and rain elsewhere. A water sheet will peel up at its edges, drift, and lay itself down dry. If it's not tied to you, the basin may keep it.

Do not use tent pegs, tripods, or markers. They will be found flat, their shadows pooling underneath them in a way that does not match the dim light.

### 4. Eyes, light, and breath

There is no sun. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, flat and high, never hardening into shadow. Most colour comes from *below* — teal fluorescence in algae pools, moss pulses, dew lattices flaring in triangles and branching hexagons for seconds at a time. Your face will be lit from beneath while the sky is a featureless plate. Shadows are softer and brighter, pale and cool, as if emitting.

Bring a headlamp, but use it sparingly. Let your eyes adapt. A strong beam will bounce up off wet ground, bend upward out of pools, light the undersides of leaves, and ruin your night — your no-night — vision for the moss signal that is the real light source. Anti-fog measures for glasses and eyelashes matter as much as brightness. When mist thickens until reeds are suggestions, stop and wait for the lift instead of squinting forward.

A pool may be an impossible, clear blue, deeper than the ground beneath allows. Do not trust optical depth. These are sheets, not lakes. Heel-testing a blue pool is no safer than any other.

Breathe through a light filter mask if you are prone to allergies. Spores rise and assemble, disassemble, reassemble in mid-air into frost-like structures that catch the dim light. The air smells of iron, peat, green rot, sweet underneath like a flooded cellar left to breathe. Your exhalations will hang visible a second too long and join the general air. That is fine. It is also a sign you should not expect privacy, stealth, or a dry breath to clear.

### 5. Ears

It is loud, constantly. Every reed is a hollow flute in moving damp air, a low chord shifting imperceptibly. Above that, the drip chorus. Beneath that, a subsonic pressure you feel in teeth and sternum as the earth shifts its saturation. When the mist lifts, the drips sharpen, the chord rises, the hum drops away for a moment, as if the basin exhaled.

Closing your ears will not close it. Use gentle earplugs to take the edge off the chorus, but do not seal yourself completely — you need to hear the change in register that tells you the mist is about to lift, and the occasional single note that is not water.

Do not navigate by sound alone. You will hear drips that have not yet fallen. Do not flinch at them.

### 6. Gear, electronics, and multiverse equipment

The moss forms circuits, luminous-brown, thick as felt, carrying pulses far faster than plant tissue should manage. You can watch a faint brightening travel, dewdrops sorting into lattices along its path. The basin thinks in chemistry, phase changes, and signals that circle its whole extent in seconds.

Your phone, camera, and beacon are electrical, warm, and conspicuously signal-emitting. They may be picked up, sorted, reflected. Recordings may lag like reflections do — out of sync with the event. Paper maps will dissolve. Instruments may give you readings that are almost right, and then, in small places, entirely not, because the physics here is almost right, and then entirely not: drops hang in interference nodes, light bends upward, a material is dry and wet at once.

Use your eyes and memory first. If you must use a device, keep it in its hard case when not in use, expect interference and damp, and do not try to "communicate" with the moss, tune it, or play music into it. It is a technology with no technicians. It does not need a user.

And do not drink the water, however clean the mineral sweat beading from the weeping stones looks. It has been filtered through stone and cleaned through reeds, yes — and it is also carrying the basin's coordination chemistry. Bring your own sealed water. The weeping stones are beautiful and egg-smooth, and they will be slippery under your hand. Do not rely on them for balance, and do not move them. Their spacing looks intentional, fractal, placed. It *was* placed, by sheet-floods and freeze-thaw and the slow turning of saturated earth. Moving one is vandalism in a place where arrangement is survival.

### 7. How to be there

For an ordinary traveler, a short visit is the right visit. A few hours in subjective time, timed to the mist tide rather than a clock, is enough to learn the place. Staying overnight means sleeping on saturated peat with moss pulses under you, no dry bivy site, no anchored hammock — dwarf trees unspool rather than branch, wood smooth as bone, vines hang downward into pools rather than climbing for you.

If you do stay still — and you should, at intervals — the place will show you what movement hides. Stay still long enough and you will see the signal wave cross, the new shoots extend, the green tide move faster than any wind. That stillness is not idleness here. It's how you perceive.

Do not chase the grazer. You will rarely see it, only its disturbance: reeds parting in an orderly way, mist lifting in its wake at knee height, a perfect circle of still water where a track should be, filling in and vanishing. It does not run from you. It continues, uninterested, at the pace of the basin. Mirror that.

Leave no artifact. You will find this almost comically easy — footprints gone, breath joined, sound in your bones — and that is the point. Pack out everything, even waste if you can, and do not count on burial; the fungus will take it cleanly, but an ordinary traveler does not subcontract sanitation to an alien ecology.

### 8. Leaving

Plan your exit by tether and by endurance, not by distance. You may feel you have walked far and be beside a mound indistinguishable from the one you passed. You may look down into a shallow pool and see sky through it and feel vertigo. That is time pooling.

Know your cold limit, your foot limit, and your patience limit before you go in. When the drip chorus muffles and light dims further — mist building — that's a cue to find a hummock and wait. When it lifts, moves are easier, the chord changes, and for a moment the basin exhales. Move then.

Come out a little damp in all the ways that matter: eyelashes beaded, shell beaded, pack beaded, bones humming a chord you didn't ask for. You will not have conquered it, mapped it, or left a mark on it. If you were gentle, the most you will carry back is the memory of a place that runs itself perfectly without a builder and without a brain, and that for a few hours, because you stayed still and stayed warm and stayed unhurried, let you be inside its body without trying to own it.
