If you are going there as an ordinary human — no climbing the way you climb rock, no instinct for wild food, no way to force reality to stay put — prepare for a mountain that is not a mountain, a city that is not a city, and a world that is moving too slowly for you to notice until it has moved past you.

You do not need to be tough. You need to be *slow, anchored, and self-sufficient*. The place will not hurry for you, it will not warm you, it will not give you water in any usable form, and it will not promise to give you back the same bridge you crossed. It is not hostile. It is breakdown. That is harder to deal with than a monster.

### 1. Expect the light, altitude, and cold first

The light arrives before anything else. It is overexposed white-blue, with no warmth, bouncing off pale metal, ceramic and resin until even the recesses between structures are lit. Lamps never turn off, and in dark interiors the wall itself can be bright as noon, cold as winter.

Prepare your eyes and your body for this contradiction:

* **Wrap-around sunglasses, goggles, and a blackout sleep mask.** Not ordinary shades. The light comes from everywhere and from below, casts two shadows — one north, one south — and leaves afterimages that physically hang in the air for a breath after you look away. Do not reach for an afterimage. Do not trust shadows for direction.
* **Sunscreen, lip balm, hat, neck cover.** You are high, the air is thin, and light bounces off everything. Your skin will burn while your hands freeze.
* **Layers you can add and remove in the same spot.** The metal under your hand can be cold despite full glare. A panel that has held sun for days can be scalding. Your breath will show for half a second even in sunshine. The air is thin and cool, it dehydrates you, and exertion will hit harder than you expect. Spend your first hours just standing, breathing, letting your eyes adjust. Altitude will make you clumsy on sharp edges.
* **Stiff, puncture-proof boots with serious grip, thick gloves, kneeling pad.** There is no bedrock, no dirt, no worn path. The ground is flooring, decks, roofs that are walls that are cliffs. The scree is not stone — it is shards of panel, curled sheet, snapped conduit, glittering and sharp, with bright, fresh insides. Corners are still 90 degrees. Nothing has been softened for you. Damp makes a permanent film on metal, and the moss that indicates that damp is slick.
* **A dust mask / respirator.** The soil is sweepings: shredded insulation, metal dust, plastic fiber compacted into a grey mat. It will get in your lungs and your food if you kneel or sleep without protection.

Do not assume any structure is old and stable because it looks rusted — a tower can be there again behind you with the rust ten years further on, or the paint fresh. Fresh breaks are brittle. Test every handhold. A rope anchored to a container, hull, or furnace shell is only as good as that piece of manufactured thing.

### 2. Pack like you will not be rescued quickly, and will not find anything

This is a high-saturation of *made* presence per volume, and almost no human-usable resource.

Bring, sealed and on your body, not in a cache:

* **Water — plenty, for far longer than you plan.** There are no rivers. Water lives as film and bead. It condenses at night in hollows, makes plate-sized pools in discarded machine housings, makes a dark line in gutters, and never runs. Those pools will have silvery fish turning in them — do not drink them, do not judge depth by them, do not eat them. Local rain is a pocket of sky sweating for a few meters and vanishing. You cannot filter a film.
* **Food with a months-long shelf life, plus 50% extra.** Vegetation has come uninvited and sparse: pale moss, wiry grass in the sweepings, white and rust-orange lichen, a vine that is mostly root. It is a suggestion of green, not a crop. Do not forage lichen or moss.
* **Insulated sleep pad and bag.** Flooring is cold. You will be beside, above, and below other structures at all times — claustrophobic and vertiginous at once. Sleep in a dry, sun-warmed hollow if you can, not in a damp condensation pocket where water will breathe in and out for hours. A hammock is useless; there are no trees and no trustworthy anchors without testing.
* **Headlamp, even though it is always bright.** In interiors the light may have no door and no direction. In deep lattice it will be warm and never fully bright.
* **Notebook and waterproof pen, chalk, colored tape, string.** See below. Paper matters more here than a phone camera.
* **A home anchor.** Your name, home address, a photograph, a token that only you would know — written down, not just remembered. This place makes alternatives of itself in the margins. It can show you a tower aged and a tower fresh. It can, in principle, show you a *you* aged and fresh. Do not follow a duplicate, do not accept a duplicate token, do not argue with it.

### 3. Learn to navigate a place that doubles

Do not navigate by memory, distance, or landmark recognition. They are the first things to fail.

In plan it is a valley — central basin, branching secondary basins, ridges of cranes and stacks — but in experience you are always near-touching something. Terraces overhang terraces. A walkway can be the roof of another walkway and pass close enough to read a stenciled number on a factory wall that rises three levels above you.

So:

* **Mark physically, with dates.** Chalk a symbol and the day you made it on the tower you passed. Photograph it *with* the chalk. A tower you passed a moment ago may be behind you when you turn, rustier or fresher. Without a date you will swear it is the same one and walk in a loop for a day that lasts so long a crane moves a meter.
* **Count steps and run string.** Two stacks that should be far can be near when you are not looking at them. Distance stutters. Pace, drop string at bridges and stairwells, measure with rope before you decide a gap is small or a climb is safe.
* **Stay in sight / voice of a companion, tethered at edges and on bridges.** A bridge may look unmoved, but halfway across the gap becomes a different gap on a different level, and you finish on the other side of the basin. That is not a failure to cross — it is what crossing does here. Do not go back on the same bridge expecting the same return. Your return path may be a different level, a stair that went down and arrived, damp and mossy, at a level that is also up.
* **Stop when a level repeats.** Mark it, sit, listen for the local tick and drip — there will be a tick every few seconds somewhere, and a drip out of phase with it. Those two sounds can be a fingerprint for a hollow, but they are everywhere. Use them as a resting anchor, not as proof you are home.
* **Do not chase echoes.** Often a sound arrives *after* its echo. You will hear the drip, then the click of the machine that caused it. Shouting carries poorly in thin air and arrives edited down to a whisper. Use flags, hand signals, and closeness. If you call for help, wait for the delayed answer.

If you get lost in a stair loop, do not keep climbing to “correct” it. Stop, mark the tread — condensation may bead in the shape of the stair you just left — drink, breathe, and backtrack by your string. Energy is altitude-limited; a loop will exhaust you.

### 4. Time, sound, and patience

Everything industrial is slowed into near silence. Factories the size of basins, conveyors wide enough to drive on, cranes that straddle a district, furnaces that exhale heat on a cycle measured in years. A crane lifts at a meter an hour. A conveyor inches. A furnace will warm its district for a season of slow years, then cool for another.

Prepare for boredom and for waiting as a survival skill:

* **You will not be hurried out.** Do not stand under a suspended lift assuming it is inert forever. It will move eventually — on its schedule, not yours.
* **Keep a clock and a journal, even if you hate them.** A bright period lasts so long things have time to exist twice. Moss creeps until you have been gone for years and return to find a seam greener. Without a written count you will lose days, and a human body loses track in a place where the tick is the only metronome.
* **Expect temperature to change for reasons that are not weather.** A district can be in a warm exhalation or a long cool. A sun-baked ceramic wall can be hot enough to burn; the metal beside it can stay cold. Dress for both in one hour.
* **Mentally rehearse doing almost nothing.** Sit and watch birds wheel slow in the glare and cast no real shadow. Watch a crane for an afternoon to see it has moved. The place is mostly quiet — a muffled bird call as if through a thin wall, a hum you feel more in your teeth than hear, tick, drip. That quiet, with that much visual density and no other people, will wear on you. Bring work with your hands, memorization, prayer, music you carry inside. Silence here is not emptiness; it is the machinery being patient.

Do not touch humming panels, snapped conduit, or furnace shells that are warm. The electrical hum that hurts your teeth may mean a filling, a nerve, or a live system. Wear gloves. Keep away from vents where beetles work the warmth — they are a sign the vent is active in a cycle you cannot perceive.

### 5. Wildlife and the things in the pipes

Fauna is abundant because built habitat is everywhere.

Pigeon-like birds nest by the thousand in dormant engine mouths. Moths and flies hang thick around never-off lamps so you walk through them and they part and close. Lizards bask on long-warmed panels and dart into ductwork. Deep inside, where it is warm and never fully bright, there are blind soft things in pipes and many-legged things in insulation.

Ordinary rules, intensified:

* Look before you put a hand, knee, or boot anywhere — floor, duct, engine mouth, machine housing. You will be stepping over lizards and into their refuge.
* Do not disturb nests, do not clear moths with your hands (eyes and lungs), do not reach into a pipe to feel around.
* Treat every pool, film, and damp seam as contaminated and not drinkable, no matter how clear. The fish have learned the damp and the slow; you have not.
* Lichen crusts and moss mats mark long-term conditions: moss = persistent damp, lichen = long sun. Use them as information, not as bedding or food.

### 6. How to be there without fighting it

The most important preparation is mental. The weirdness is not an event. It is a condition of overbuilding: reality built on itself so densely, so high, with no natural substrate to anchor it, that it has begun to thin and double in places.

So do not try to correct it. Do not insist the tower is the same tower. Do not insist the stair went down. Do not insist the gap is the gap. Acknowledge: “It is also the other one, a decade on. I am here, on this level, at this tick.” Note it, mark it, move on slowly.

Move at the pace of the place. Hurrying is what will cut you on a fresh shard, what will make you step on a lizard, what will make you trust a stuttering distance, what will make you follow the afterimage of a bright panel with your hand.

When you stand finally — cool air in your lungs, bright light on your face, on a floor that is a roof that is a wall — you will understand the intuition behind all this packing: this is not a place built on a landscape. This is a landscape that has been built, recently and industrially, until it broke gently and kept going.

Your job is not to fix it, farm it, or outrun it. Your job is to be a brief, careful, anchored human inside it, with water on your back, chalk in your hand, eyes protected, and enough patience to let a crane move a meter while you learn, once again, which tower is behind you.

**Quick checklist before entry:** wrap sunglasses + goggles + sleep mask; layered clothing + hat + gloves + puncture-proof boots; respirator; sunscreen; headlamp; rope + carabiners (test anchors); insulated pad and bag; sealed water and long-life food for weeks longer than planned; first aid; notebook, chalk, tape, string, clock; home anchor token on paper; companion tether if traveling with anyone; and a plan to mark, date, and backtrack — because the bridge may not bring you back the way it took you in.
