It is found as heat before it is found as a place.

On a low, shimmering plain of cracked mineral plates there is a single dark seam, no wider than a hand, breathing. Hot air comes up out of it in slow pulses, smelling of baked clay and crushed green leaves. You have to kneel to see it, and then descend — twenty hand-cut steps, worn concave in the middle, into a hollow that is not a cave in any ordinary sense. It is one chamber. A pocket. The whole world of it can be walked end to end in less than an hour, roof to floor to far wall, and yet once you are down inside it, it does not feel small.

The floor is a shallow bowl, low as a sink, and the ceiling is a dome low enough to make the place intimate, close as the inside of a great vessel. The dome is stone ribs and translucent plates of pale mineral set into it like panes. What light there is comes through those plates — never direct sun, never bright. It is a perpetual, dim dusk, greenish-brown and soft, strongest where the plates are thin and falling to deep shadow at the edges. The air under it is hot. The floor is hot underfoot even in shade. The stone walls radiate it back. There is no wind to cool it except the wind that the place makes for itself.

There is no water here. Not a puddle, not a stream, not a well, not dew, not rain. The roof seals. The high vents the old builders cut let air in and out, but no moisture ever settles. The dry channels paved from the central plaza to the perimeter wall have never carried anything but dust. The ornamental basin in the plaza is a cracked clay ring, empty for longer than anyone can remember. The only liquids in Urmesh — for that is the name left cut in fired brick over the inner gate — are the amber saps inside the plants, and a dark oil in the old kilns that has long since dried to varnish.

And yet the vegetation is lush.

It does not grow everywhere. In most of the bowl there is nothing but fine pumice dust and shattered glassy plates and the compacted clay of streets, bare and empty for great distances. Then, abruptly, in the low seams and against the walls and in the corners of buildings, it erupts: mats of broad, paddle-shaped leaves, dark green-black turning burgundy at the edges in the heat; tangled kiln-vines that climb the dome ribs toward the light-plates; resin-trees with trunks no thicker than a wrist but crowns vast and overlapping; tufts of dry fern that are not ferns and do not curl for water. The leaves are thick and waxy, made to catch dim light, and they work without water. Their roots do not drink. They grip pumice and split mineral dust and take their living from heat, from air, from stone itself. Inside the leaf is not water but a hot, turpentine-smelling resin that beads and drips and hardens in the sun-pale glow. Flowers open without visitors — sulfur-yellow, waxy, spicy — and set themselves. Fruit is a hard pod that swells, ticks, and finally bursts.

That is the one strange rule of the place, and everything else has settled around it.

The city was built here for that heat and that dryness, and for the clay. It is urban in its habits, not in its crowding. The builders were primitive in the plainest sense: hand tools of stone, clay molds, bricks baked in the kilns they themselves built, pumice blocks stacked and mortared with ash-clay, fiber mats woven for roofs and looms, rope bridges over the dry streets. There is no metal machinery, no wheel ruts, only sled marks in the dust and the deep grooves where feet and dragged loads wore the paving smooth.

Layer upon layer is still legible because nothing has been cleared away. A perimeter wall, low and slumped. A stepped central plaza with empty market stalls, their poles still lashed where the awnings rotted off generations ago. A row of kilns with blackened throats. A granary with its doors warped shut, full of dry plant husks. Houses in loose blocks — one story, then a second added with a narrower stair, then an annex leaning against it — spaced astonishingly far apart. Avenues radiate from the plaza, paved in cracked plates, and between one house and the next there may be fifty paces of naked dust and a single clump of ash-tongues. Urban order, but density almost to vanishing. The city is mostly empty space held in shape by its streets.

Everything in it is old in the way of things that have reached equilibrium. Nothing looks new, and nothing looks freshly ruined. Corners are rounded. Walls lean a degree and have stopped. Dust has filled every joint to a level. Vines have grown into mortar and the mortar has grown around the vines. A loom stands in a house where a broad leaf has pushed through the floorboards and curled over the warp threads, and neither plant nor wood has yielded. It is a settled form, long-existed, weathered into its own state.

No animal lives here. No insect, no bird, no lizard, no worm in the dust. The builders left dusty hand-prints in a baked clay floor in the kiln row, and the prints are the last sign of animal life. The plants pollinate themselves, seed themselves by wind and by explosion, and keep the place without any other help. At night the hollow is utterly without animal sound, and that makes the sounds it does make seem louder.

And it is loud, at intervals that have nothing to do with creatures. The heat works on the stone all day. In the dim midday, when the light-plates brighten a fraction as the sun moves outside, the floor plates expand and crackle with snaps like fired twigs, sometimes a volley of them rolling across the bowl. Seed pods burst with a flat pop that echoes off the dome. The vent pipes built into the walls sigh and then roar as a thermal updraft sets up, a long flute-note that vibrates in the empty houses. Sap drips from a resin-tree onto a clay pot and ticks, amber on baked earth. Dry leaves, when a current crosses the plaza, rustle like rain that never falls, and then settle.

The place is in motion in cycles, not in tumult. Morning: leaves unfurl toward the plates, dust lifts in slow spirals where the floor is hottest, vines release a drop of sap here and there. Midday: heat shimmers above the streets, stones tick and crack, pods begin to go off one by one. Evening dimness deepens, the dome plates dull, the plants close their flowers and fold their broadest leaves to hold their resin. Night: contraction sounds — small, sharp ticks from walls and kilns cooling — and the slow, creaking adjustment of a house that has been in that exact lean for a hundred years. In the deepest low places new shoots push up through paving cracks, split a plate, and take the light that is there.

That is the whole of it: a hot, dim, dry pocket sunk low under the plain, sealed under a stone dome, where a sparse hand-built town sits empty in vast dust, and where exuberant green life makes its living from heat and mineral and light, and nothing with a heartbeat ever comes to live among it again.
