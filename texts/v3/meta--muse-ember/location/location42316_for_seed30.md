It is smaller than you expect, and higher.

You do not arrive at a view. You arrive inside a room.

The summit is a shallow basin, not much wider across than a cathedral is long, its floor a cathedral floor too — vast, flat planes of fresh stone sloping almost imperceptibly to a low, surrounding wall. From the middle you can see the other side. From any edge you can see the middle. Beyond that wall there is, first, a drop, and then a sea of cloud far below your feet. The horizon is not a horizon. It is the turning down of the world.

And yet your ears pop. Your breath comes short, warm, and instantly fogs. The air is thin and close at once. This is height behaving in the wrong way: not cold, not dry, not clear. Warm air has been lifted and lifted here and cannot escape, so it sits, thick with itself, and condenses on everything that is cooler than it, which is almost everything.

The light never really arrives. It is a permanent overcast morning, dimmed again by the cloud deck you are standing on, so that color drains to wet grey, to black, to a dull moss-green. Shadows are soft and rare. Sometimes the dim lifts a little, to a paler dim, and you think it might be day, and then it sinks again without ever becoming night. In the darker phases, other lights come on.

The stone is the first true thing, and it is unfinished.

There is no soil here in any old sense. No horizon of loam, no rounded pebbles, no canyon carved patiently. The erosion is almost zero because there has not been time to erode. This is geology in its first draft. The mountain is still being pushed up, audibly. It cracks under your feet with small, sharp reports — not the boom of a landslide, but a tick, a pop, a splintering inside the rock, as if something counting down is being let out. Fresh faces are raw, angular, unweathered, their edges still keen enough to cut. New seams open in the morning and by afternoon are already weeping and healing. Scree has not settled; it grows in place, lifting, leaning, still attached.

Where water has touched it does not run away. It stays.

Water here is not a river or a lake. It is a condition. The rocks weep. Fine beads form on the stone and run together into rivulets no thicker than a finger, which sink again a few meters later into pores or cracks that are still opening. The air itself is water near its point of becoming rain, so your eyelashes gather it, your sleeves darken, droplets slide off your hands as if your skin were also stone. In shallow hollows, water pools a finger deep, clear and warm, never quite still. It trembles even when there is no wind. In some pools it bubbles coldly, though the stone underneath is warm to the touch. In others it rises, very slowly, up the faces of black filaments planted in the water, against any sense of downhill, until it reaches a tip and falls as mist.

It rains, often, but not as a storm that passes. The basin makes its own weather in a small, hurried cycle: condensation, a heavy dimming, a patter that is loud on stone and on the taut membranes stretched between the artifacts, a pause in which everything steams, then condensation again. You are never dry for long. Nothing is.

Vegetation has no foothold, literally. A few low mats have claimed the few places where new dust has accumulated in a sheltered crack. They are not forests or even scrub, but dark, rubbery films, olive and grey-green, upholstering the stone. In the pools, translucent strands sway, less plants than algae learning to be plants. Look for green and you will mostly find absence — long stretches of wet, bare rock where nothing grows because there is no earth to grow in, no sun to grow with, and because the ground is still moving, still warm, still too new. Where the mats do grow, they grow fast and thin and disappear fast, grazed or washed away, and a week later the stone is bare again. The place is balanced with emptiness. Between any two presences there is a lot of space, a lot of shining, unspeckled stone.

That emptiness is not quiet, and it is not empty for long, because the fauna is abundant and it does not live on the plants.

You hear them before you see them, and in the basin sound behaves strangely, amplified and returned. The walls of fresh stone are a perfect resonating bowl. A dripping pool ticks like a clock. The filaments sing. And the animals drum.

They move in swarms, in pulses, in loops that fill the whole small basin and then evacuate it. Skitterers the size of mice but with too many legs, blind to the dim light, navigate by touch and by the heat of the stone; they run in tight, clicking columns across the flats, their feet making a dry, loud patter that echoes off the walls like rain on a tin roof despite the wet. Winged things hang in the condensing air like drifting ash until they drop, and their wings buzz at a pitch that makes your teeth ache. Pool swimmers flash silver under the finger-deep water, reverse direction without turning, and vanish into the stone through cracks too narrow for them. Larger shapes move along the outer wall, up and down, clinging, calling — a low, hooting chorus that is almost speech if you do not listen too closely, and completely other if you do.

They are strange in the way this place is strange. Some are only visible when wet. As they dry they become clear as glass and then invisible, though you can still hear their feet. Some casts no shadow but makes a sound-shadow, a dent in the humming. Their tracks appear on the damp stone a second before their feet touch down. They eat almost nothing that is green. They filter the condensing air. They scrape the microbial film from the new stone. They eat each other in a fast, formal ballet of predator and prey that never depletes because the cycle is so quick — born, swarming, eaten, born again, within the same warm, wet day. The population is high, the biomass low, the motion constant. When a swarm passes, the basin is suddenly full of life and noise, and when it has passed, it is suddenly bare and ringing again.

Scattered across the bare stone, far apart — often you can walk the whole basin and see none — are the built things.

A low wall, one stone high, built in a rough spiral, as if something had tried to make a windbreak and then lost interest. A dome of something like fired clay, half sunk, its door long since filled with weeping water. A square cistern, empty, its interior lined with faint handprints that the stone has grown around but not yet healed. A leaning roof on four posts, its underside blackened. These are small, human-scaled, ordinary in their ordinariness. They look like someone came, intended a hamlet, built three or four shelters, and stopped. They are not ruins — ruins would be older, more worn. They are partial remains, interrupted.

And interspersed with them, not obviously separate, are the other things.

Black glass filaments rise from the pools and from cracks, sometimes alone, sometimes in a thicket, sometimes in a perfect ring. They hum. They warm the air around them. At their tips, water climbs. Their surface is smooth beyond any natural stone, and too smooth, and when you touch one — and you must, because curiosity is another kind of condensation — it is warm, faintly vibrating, and your hand comes away dry. They do not rust. They do not tarnish. They collect dust and then shed it as light. At dim phases they pulse, a slow, deliberate sequence of faint blue-white along their length, like a message being sent up.

There are rings half-buried in the stone, perfect circles of a dark metal that is not metal, that ring when the swarm-drum passes over them. There are needle towers, slender as reeds, that sway even when the air is still and make a clear, high tone that joins the dripping and the drumming in a chord that never resolves. There are lattice shelters — not built, but grown — whose frames open and close like lungs, letting the warm air in and out, their membranes expanding with the changing pressure. They are engineering, but not as we understand it: no welds, no seams, no fuel lines, no heat waste. Structures that assemble themselves from the dissolved minerals in the weeping water, that repair themselves as fast as the young mountain cracks them, that take in the condensation and give off heat and tone. They are beyond what current physics describes, not because they break it loudly, but because they simply do not explain themselves. They grow like crystals and behave like organs and work like machines built by something that never had to choose between the three.

They are also, like everything else here, emerging. The filaments are still extending, a millimeter at a time you can watch if you are patient. The rings are still sinking as the stone rises around them. The lattice has not finished its roof. This is not a finished technology deployed and abandoned. It is a technology still being born, in a place still being born, on a peak still being born — three unfinished things growing into each other.

That, perhaps, is the rule of the world here: nothing erodes, everything accretes. The mountain does not wear down into a valley. It builds up. The machines do not decay into scrap. They absorb the fresh stone and the fresh water and become more of themselves. The fauna do not leave middens or bones that linger; they are recycled while still moving. Even the faint built things are being gently taken back — not by rot, but by growth, stone swelling around their foundations, water seeping up their walls, filaments leaning toward them as if listening.

The sound, by now, is a landscape of its own. It is loud, insistently loud, in layers. The deep, irregular tick of the mountain splitting inside itself. The hiss and patter of the self-made rain. The high, sustained singing of the needles, which rises and falls with the light. The drumming of feet and the hooting on the walls and the buzz in the air, which together make a rhythm so constant you stop hearing it as separate sounds and hear it as the place breathing. And underneath all of it, the hum of the lattice, a bass note you feel in your chest when you stand near a pool, matching the slow pulse of the stone's own warmth.

You could walk around the basin in twenty minutes. You could learn every pool, every filament, every low wall, every crack that sings. And still it would not be learned, because by the time you returned to the first pool, the water level would have changed, a new seam would have opened, a swarm would be boiling through, a filament would have grown a hand's breadth taller, and the light would be dimmer or brighter in a way that made the whole grey palette shift.

It is a small place, a high place, a wet place, a warm place in the dim. A place where there is almost no vegetation and far too many animals, almost no building and far too much technology, almost no erosion and far too much becoming. A cathedral of new stone, roofed with cloud, floored with pools, filled with sound.

And it is not finished becoming.
