The valley has sunk below its own weather.

That is the first thing you understand, standing — if standing is the word — in water up to your knees, sometimes your waist, in a basin so flat it has no high ground to offer. The land goes on being land underneath you, but you will not touch it for long stretches. It is a wide, low, regional floodplain, ten, twenty kilometers across in theory, but in practice it ends where this warm haze ends, which is perhaps fifty meters away. Layer after layer of rain, steam, and mist hangs at different heights, each one a slightly different shade of green-grey. The light is dim, permanently twilit, the sun a rumor behind cloud and water-column. Everything is wet, not just from the rain that doesn't quite fall and the water that doesn't quite sit still, but from a saturation in the air itself — atmospheric water, a second sea you breathe through.

The water is everywhere and in no simple form. In the low places it is a ponded, opaque, warm bath, thick with suspended silt and pollen and spores and fine industrial powder. In others it braids into channels that cut and re-cut themselves in minutes. In the higher places — and here high means two meters — it is only a sheet, a hand's breadth deep, running clear over concrete foundations and black mud, catching the dim light like a mirror that reflects things not present. And above all that, the rain has decided not to fall. It hangs in a wall, in curtains, in slow vertical fog that drips from nothing and soaks your shoulders from below. You can be submerged while walking on what looks like dry ground.

Underneath, the earth is young. Very young. This basin was torn and dropped and flooded recently — geologically speaking, yesterday — and nothing has had time to soften. The scarps where the ground cracked are sharp as broken pottery. New cones of black, glassy mud rise out of the water and steam, untouched by erosion, their edges still cutting. Sinkholes open with a sigh and refill. A ridge that was a sharp spine this morning is a soft archipelago by afternoon because the water is worrying at its unweathered core. The mud is warm, sulfurous, and slick; it sucks and releases. The rock, where you can see it, is fresh basaltic foam and fractured concrete and peat so black it absorbs the little light there is, like a material that arrived this season.

And on this fresh, drowned, sharp ground, vegetation is obscene with life.

It is lush, but not forest-lush. It is submerged-lush. Forests of translucent kelp rise from the bottom, not rooted so much as anchored to pipes and wreckage and each other, their blades unspooling in slow spirals. Reed-bells cluster in dense, near-touching thickets so thick you cannot push through without tearing them, and they tear and regrow audibly, a soft ripping. Floating gardens knit themselves across the water surface — mats of mirror-lily and steam-fern, interwoven so tightly they will bear a step, then give way all at once in a sink of green. On every vertical surface — on rusted iron, on tilting cooling towers, on the sides of pump houses — a skin of moss grows, thick as fur, in pale chartreuse and rust-red. Ferns grow out of cable bundles. Vines grow out of vine-tangles to make new vines. There are no seasons here, and no grazing. Nothing eats this. Leaves are never bitten. Whole plants die standing, bleach silver, and add to the mats. The result is a layered, high-saturation density: bottom mat, midwater kelp, surface mat, hanging moss, aerial fern unfurling into the steam. You see ten plants for every meter of water, and behind them, ten more.

It would be a garden drowned in dim, warm, silty water, if not for the machines, and the fact that the garden is growing through the machines.

The built world here is scattered, not ruined in the grand way. A single pump house, wheelhouse door swinging on its hinge, interior still electrified and humming. A derrick, its legs furred with algae, holding a cable that runs into the water and vanishes. A half-sunk conveyor whose belt still moves, carrying nothing but wet leaves in a slow, pointless circuit. A cooling tower tipped like a broken tooth, its interior a cathedral of dripping moss. A substation on stilts, amber lights flickering in sequence. Every few hundred meters, something like this, then only water and plants again. Small structures, partial remains, faint marks — a painted number on concrete, a flooded road with white lines rusting under the sheet-water.

But the technology is not dead. That is the second obscene thing. The tech level is industrial and upscaled and still running with no one to run it. Pumps churn in circles, moving warm water from one flooded bay to another for no reason except their timers have not stopped. Turbines spin faster and faster in flooded pits, generating electricity that lights those amber lamps and then goes nowhere. Algae-presses in low sheds squeeze the surface mats into dark pellets and drop them back in. Floodgates clang open and shut on a schedule laid down decades ago, sending surges through the kelp forest that bend everything violently in unison. The machines are not being operated. They are being tended, almost, by the vegetation: roots have found the warm pipes, moss has sealed the leaks, kelp has reached the intakes and keeps them from clogging. The machines hum because the plants need the vibration to grow — they lean toward sound — and the plants thrive because the machines churn nutrients from dying leaves and warm the water and stir the silt. A closed, automated ecology of metal and leaf.

There is no animal life to interrupt it. Not a fish in the warm water, though the water is perfect for fish. Not a bird over the mats, though there is open, dim sky. Not an insect on the flowers — there are flowers, pale and closed, that never open because nothing needs pollinating. The absence is not quiet, which makes it louder. The deafening sound of the place is all non-animal noise: the deep, chest-deep roar of dozens of pumps and turbines working against water, the hiss of steam vents under the surface making the water bubble like a kettle, the constant crash and slap of the surge-gates, the groan of metal cooling and contracting in the wet air, the ripping and snapping of the vegetation itself as it grows into machinery and tears itself free. It is a roar you feel in your teeth. You cannot hear your own steps. And layered under it, precisely because no animal calls break it, is the sense that this is a world that has loudness without voices.

And then the place begins to break its own rules, as if the roar and the churn have shaken something loose.

The water surface does not ripple outward. It ripples inward, toward the disturbance, in places. A reflection in the sheet-water will show a clear sky and a sharp horizon that you cannot see when you look up — the real sky is grey and low. A factory you just passed will appear again, farther off, at a different angle, because a sheet of water is acting as a second air and bending the view. Plants grow down and up at once, emerging from the bottom and from the hanging rain, their two ends meeting in the middle of nowhere and continuing through each other. A mat of lily will repeat the same leaf pattern, impossibly, over an area the size of a town, as if the valley was printed from one template and not finished. Sound arrives before it happens: you hear a gate clang a half-second before you see the steam burst that causes it. The dim light has no source you can find, and yet the translucent kelp glows, very faintly, a green that doesn't illuminate so much as make the darkness visible.

This is not a stable system. It is turbulent in the Solaris sense — creation and destruction as its normal metabolism. Every few minutes the current reverses in a bay and the kelp forest bends double, then straightens and begins to grow the other way, leaving a visible kink in every stalk. A new island of woven vegetation will rise, steaming, under a turbine's outflow, hold for an hour, then founder and sink under its own weight, taking a length of pipe with it. A fresh mud cone erupts near a foundation, cracks along its sharp crest, and collapses back into the water with a grunt of displaced steam. The pumps, following their blind timers, flood one channel and starve another, so a dry, cracked square of concrete that was an island at noon is a lake by dusk, and the lake is an island again by night. Nothing erodes slowly. Everything is either brand-new or suddenly gone.

You walk — wade — and the place is near-touching everywhere: water against skin, plants against water, pipes against plants, moss against metal, steam against all of it, sound against eardrums. Warm, dim, submerged, lush, young, dense, industrial, animal-less, deafening, and coming apart. The valley goes on past the haze, bay after bay of the same warm drowned works, each one running its machines, growing its un-eaten gardens, breaking its light and water a little differently as you try to look at them.

It does not want to be described. It wants to keep changing while you describe it.
