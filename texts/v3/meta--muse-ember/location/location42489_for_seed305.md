It is not a plain that you look at. It is a plain that looks back, from everywhere at once.

From a distance it is white. That is the first lie it tells. At altitude — high enough that your ears pop and your breath comes short, high enough that the horizon falls away beneath you instead of meeting you — the light does not come from one sun. It comes from the sky, from the stone, from the ice trapped in the stone, from ten million polished carapaces turning over and over. It is too bright for a cold place. 2.9 of a sky, unfiltered, thin, blue-white at the zenith and so flat at noon that shadows are almost absent, only a short, hard ink under things. Your eyes water constantly. You learn to squint through lashes crusted with frost.

The cold is the second truth, and it underlies everything. It is not winter cold, which has seasons and thaws. It is the cold of exposed height, a patient, mineral cold that lives in your teeth. On clear days — and most days are clear in that relentless light — the air can be well below freezing and still feel like glare, not warmth. Your lungs burn. Moisture freezes on the inside of your nose as you breathe.

The land itself is young. You can tell because nothing has been worn soft yet. This is a steppe lifted recently, in geological terms, up out of a colder sea or torn free of an ice sheet that has not finished retreating. The ridges are plates, freshly broken. Cliffs are sharp enough to cut a boot. Scree has not yet settled into slopes; it is a field of knives and shattered quartzite, iron-banded shale, pale feldspar still fuming faintly with oxidation where a crack opened. There are no river valleys, no rounded hills, no soil in any human sense. There is only rock, and on top of the rock, life, so thick it makes the illusion of soil. The uplift is still going on. A finger's width a century. Slow.

Water is a rumor here. It does not rain in any generous way. What falls is sleet, occasional and hurried, that rattles on stone and vanishes. What stays is dampness: a perpetual condensation. As the sun hits the cold stone, as warm bodies exhale onto cold leaves, as frost melts a millimeter at a time under a leaf, everything is beaded. Cupped rocks hold thimblefuls of water that take days to evaporate. The moss is always wet to the touch. Your coat darkens at the shoulders not from rain but from fog you walk through, generated by the land itself breathing. Islands of true meltwater exist only where the industrial life below makes heat enough to keep a spring open — a dark, steaming, mineral pool no larger than your hand, surrounded by white rimed ice. Everything drinks by licking.

And yet, for all that cold and dryness, it is lush.

The lushness is not green in the summer-meadow sense. It is pale, coppery, luminous. Vast mats of moss and liverwort spread across every flat surface, shoulder-to-shoulder, a centimeter thick, twenty kilometers across and more, following the contour of the uplift for as far as you can see. Because the scale is vast — not a hill, not a valley, a weather system unto itself. A storm that forms over the western scarp will take a week to die over the eastern. A herd migration is a front. You can walk for days and the composition does not change, only the light shifts and the noise changes pitch.

Between the mats grow furnace-ferns: fronds of translucent tissue veined with iron, that open like hands in the bright and close at the first gust. Pipe-reeds rise in hollow, jointed stalks from the cracks, each stem a resonator. Their leaves are broad and fleshy and stone-cold until you put your palm to them, and then you feel the heat they store. That is the rule that holds the paradox together: the plants are lush because the animals are warm. The animals are everywhere.

Fauna is not a feature here. Fauna is the terrain.

The ground crawls. The air crawls. Every rock is coated in mites, springtails, blind beetles clinging upside-down. Every moss mat is grazed to a uniform height by a moving carpet of millipede-like conveyor worms, each segment linking and unlinking in slow ripples, transporting spores and chewed leaves in surface grooves on their backs like little trains. Overhead, in the bright, there are birds in numbers that blot the light in shifting veils — not flocks, but a continuous medium, like snow that flies in lines. Their wings click. Their calls are not songs, but percussive, metallic chirps, struck like tuning forks.

There is no built thing here. No hut, no wall, no road, no artifact left behind. And yet nothing in nature is less primitive. This is an industrial ecology, in the strict sense.

The great beasts that dominate the mats are quadrupeds the size of houses, moving with a slowness that seems impossible for so much mass: one deliberate step per minute, a footfall that sends a shiver through the moss a hundred meters away. Their flanks are plated in bronze and black iron that grows like horn and is shed like hair. From their chests vents steam in slow, rhythmic sighs, and inside those chests a crop-stone furnace turns, grinding rock to powder, separating iron and copper from the stone they eat. What they excrete is not dung in any simple sense, but sorted, polished components — beads, rods, small gear-like ossicles, thin sheets of metal — dropped onto the moss where other creatures immediately take them up.

Smaller stampers — low, armored, many-legged — patrol in dense herds that fill the plain from horizon to horizon. Their heads are mills. Their hips are bellows. They walk into a patch of moss, lower their heads, and over the course of an afternoon reduce a year's growth to feed, to heat, to metal. Around them, a cloud of beetles that are themselves engines: beetles whose abdomens glow dull red as they metabolize, beetles with electric organs that arc and weld their own shells shut after a molt, beetles that abandon a spent shell that becomes a useful housing for a lichen.

The plants do their share of the industry. The furnace-ferns photosynthesize light, yes, but they also smelt. Their roots, which look like pale fingers gripping quartz, secrete acids over decades, etching the rock, freeing metals, storing them in nodules that swell season by season until a storm or a grazer tears them free. The pipe-reeds generate current. On a bright, still day you can hold a reed and feel a tingling crawl up your wrist, the static discharge of millions of hollow stems humming together. At night — a brief, dimming night at this latitude, never truly dark — the mats flicker faintly where the current leaks.

Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to be empty. Density is the second law here. There is no bare stone except the vertical cliff faces, and those are packed with nesting guano, with lichens, with birds clinging in rows, with insects hanging in curtains. There is no silence because there is no space for silence to gather. The sound is overwhelming, but it is not frantic. It is slow sound made huge by numbers: the distant, continental clank of herds striking stone, the constant, low grinding of crops and mills from every direction at once, the click and whirr of wings, the hiss of steam, the sigh of wind through pipe-reeds — all of it at a tempo that leaves room between beats, but no room between sources. You do not hear an animal. You hear the combined industry of a continent.

And time, above all, is slow. Dynamic change here does not happen in events. It happens in accumulations. A moss mat grows an inch in a decade. A great beast lives three hundred years and is only sexually mature at eighty. A shell takes a season to weld. The red iron scars in the cliffs spread a millimeter a year. Even decay is slow; a dead creature does not rot into soil, because there is no soil to rot into. It is frozen, dried by wind, and then patiently disassembled by neighbors who need its metal and its heat and its water. A carcass may sit for a generation, a white monument, while beetles take it apart piece by piece at a pace appropriate to the cold.

That slowness makes it strange, beyond the metal and the light. Animals bud off smaller versions of themselves that unfold like tools from a case. Plants reproduce by releasing sparks — dry, charged seeds that drift in the wind and if they land on a warm body, root there and grow a coppery patch crusted on fur or shell. Mating, where you can identify it, often looks like two great stampers standing flank to flank for a month, their plates fusing at the points of contact, exchanging a slow arc of current, then separating, each a little heavier, a little brighter.

To enter it is to be absorbed. You cannot stand still for long; something will climb you to investigate your heat. You cannot lie down; the conveyor worms will route themselves over you as the most efficient path. Your footprints fill in behind you with moss and mites before you have gone ten steps. The bright, cold, damp air condenses in your lungs and makes you cough, and the cough is echoed, multiplied, picked up and stamped back at you by something miles away.

It remains, throughout, unbuilt and untouched. No hand has shaped a thing here. But every thing is shaped, by mouths and roots and arcs, into tools. The plateau is a factory that was never constructed, only grown — vast, young, sharp, loud, crowded, and still, slowly, in its cold bright light, forging itself out of stone, leaf, and animal heat, one patient cycle at a time.
