It is low first, and then it is wide.

The Ahrit Lowland does not rise. It spreads. A warm, flat plain of sun-baked clay, gypsum and salt, so level that for most of the day the horizon is a straight, pale line cutting the sky in half, and a man standing up can see weather coming for hours before it reaches him. There are swells, but they are broad and slow as a held breath — rises of a few paces over a distance of a mile, shallow pans where the ground dishes and then lifts again. No hills, no canyons, no valley carved by anything. 

The ground is young. It is still being made. Every night fine dust settles and calcifies in the cool; every day the sun bakes it down again. Salt grows up in little white blades through cracks in the paths, pushes a paving stone askew, bubbles in a courtyard wall and flakes it off. New sediment gathers in the low places and hardens into a crust that will crack by noon. Old tracks fill in, new ones braid themselves elsewhere. The plain has not yet settled into its final shape, and the things that live on it have not settled either — their houses slump, their streets shift a hand's breadth each season, their fences are rebuilt where the earth swelled and snapped them. Erosion hardly enters into it. This is a place of accretion, of raw, unfinished geology still laying itself down in thin, bright layers.

There is no water to be seen. No river has ever cut it, no lake shines in it, no spring bubbles. The sky does not rain. A traveler crossing for a month would see no open water at all — no stream to drink from, no wet mud, no blue reflection. The nearest wetness is inside living things, and even that is guarded fiercely. What rain the world might have elsewhere, this place has traded for something else: a deep, patient dryness.

And yet it is green. Not jungle-green, not wet-green, but green all the same, and packed. The vegetation is a desert lushness — tough, waxy, grey-green and vivid in turn — that covers the plain as continuously as the salt does. Thorn-trees with bulbous trunks stand in thickets so dense a person must push between them, their bark peeling in dry strips. Rosetted succulents the size of barrels sit shoulder to shoulder along every path, their leaves tipped with soft spines. Tall, dry grasses lean over each other in waves when the warm wind passes, though the wind makes almost no sound in them. Creepers climb anything that stands still long enough — walls, roofs, dead trees, each other — until house and plant are hard to tell apart at a distance. Melon-like gourds lie half-buried in the dust, fat and ridged. Under and between all of this the ground is not bare earth but a low, spiky mat of herbs, lichen, and salt-tolerant shrubs, blooming at dawn in small, pink-white flowers that close by midday.

Life here runs on dew. At night the flat, clear sky lets the ground radiate its heat away. The broadest leaves grow cold first, and dew forms on them in beads that run down to the roots in rivulets no thicker than a thread. Salt in the soil draws moisture from the air before dawn, deliquescing into a faint damp that plants drink through shallow roots. In the early morning the whole plain is briefly cool and soft underfoot, and then the sun takes it all back. Plants open, animals drink from fruits and cut stems, people lick dew from leaves if they must. Everything that lives stores what it can in fat, in pulp, in its own body, and spends it slowly. It is a closed economy of moisture, held inside bodies, never allowed to pool.

Into this green, dry plain the settlement has grown, and grown without pause. There is no edge to it that holds for long. Town merges into town across the horizon, wall touching wall, garden touching roof, street running straight for a mile and then fraying into a dozen narrower lanes between thickets. The building is all of the place itself: walls of packed earth and dung and woven fiber, beaten flat by feet and hands; roofs of bundled reeds and dry thatch and flattened hides stretched over frames of green wood; raised causeways of compacted clay crossing the salt pans so feet do not break the crust; low, stepped houses built up against each other for shade, three and four thick in places, their flat tops used for drying gourds, for sleeping in the hot season, for growing extra creeper gardens. Markets are clearings where the mat of plants has been cut back and the ground packed harder — dusty squares full of woven baskets, bundles of salt, strings of dried meat, bundles of herbs. Pens are fences of thorn and living hedge. Shrines, granaries, workshops — all are the same materials, all low, all soft-edged. Nothing rises above the trees for long, and what rises is pulled down by weight and weather and rebuilt.

There is no device in it. No metal, no glass, no wheel turning, no engine, no lamp that burns through the night. Everything is dug, woven, packed, carried, or grown by hand and claw and tooth. Travel is on foot, in long walking processions, or on the backs of the grazing beasts. Work is done in the cool hours and rested through the heat. At night the lowland goes properly dark under ordinary stars, and the only lights are none at all — people sleep by touch, by the smell of their own houses, by the vibration of neighbors stirring through a shared wall.

The fauna are everywhere, and they are part of the architecture. Lizards run along the sun-warmed tops of walls. Insects work the flowers in a constant, soft hum that is less heard than felt in the air. Birds nest in the thatch and in the densest thickets, calling only at dawn, a brief, rusty chorus. Burrowers tunnel under streets and houses, their spoil heaps appearing overnight, their tunnels collapsing in the soft salt and being dug again. Herds of small, hoofed grazers move through the commons and between the houses in slow, endless processions, cropped short by the plants they eat and allowed to pass by the people who herd them, and the herds are followed — at a respectful distance, at a silent distance — by lean predators who hunt by scent and by the tremor of feet through the ground. Scavengers work the edges of the settlements. Climbers live in the roofs. Nothing is far from anything else. Density has eaten all the empty spaces — plant on plant, house on house, animal on animal, all of them packed into a single continuous skin over the plain.

And it is quiet. That is the strangest thing, and the most constant. For all the packing and all the movement, there is almost no sound. The ground is powder and leaf-litter and packed earth; feet make a soft *poof* and nothing more. Walls are thick and fibrous and swallow voice. The dry air carries little. The wind, when it comes, moves the grasses in a slow shiver you can watch but barely hear. Animals here do not signal loudly. They tap a foot, they brush a flank, they release a scent, they press a body against a neighbor. Even the birds, abundant as they are, seem to have learned the place's rule: speak once at first light, and then be still. A dust devil may cross the plain at noon, spinning up a thin pillar of salt and dust that travels a mile without a whisper. A herd may pass through a street, hundreds strong, and the only noise is the soft percussion of hooves on dust, muffled and endless, like rain on a distant roof — except it never builds, never breaks.

But it is never still. The activity is constant, just silent and slow and everywhere at once. Plants are opening and closing their flowers, pushing out a new leaf, dropping an old one. Salt is growing through a floor. A wall is being re-packed by a family who will leave it to slump again by next season. A caravan of walkers is coming in from a settlement three days away that looks, from here, like the next street over. Herds are migrating on a scale that makes the plain itself seem to move — the grazing commons eaten to stubble in one district while the next district, a week's walk north, is knee-deep in regrowth after its dew season. Predators are circling. Prey are listening through their feet. Weather of its own rolls across this vast lowland: mornings of low, ground-hugging mist that is not quite fog, made of dew evaporating; afternoons of shimmering heat and drifting dust; evenings when the light goes amber and long and all the shadows between plants and houses lengthen and merge. In the west, where the salt pans are widest, the plain is whiter and sparser, the settlements strung thinner along the causeways. In the east, the thickets are darker and the houses are almost lost in green. In the north it is a fraction cooler and the day's work lasts longer. It is one place, but large enough to have its own climates threaded through it.

The rules are plain once you live there a while. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced from outside. Salt is food and poison and building material and money, all at once. A plant is shade, and fruit, and fence, and house-frame, and after it dies, firewood. A beast is meat, and hide, and dung for walls, and a herd that must be moved before it eats the gardens. A house is not finished, it is maintained — propped, re-thatched, re-plastered, abandoned to the creepers when its family moves on, reclaimed by the next family who needs the shade. The ground will take it back or push it up, and so the settlement does not argue. It moves a little, constantly, like the plants it lives among.

It is, in the end, a city that is also a field, that is also a desert, that is also a construction site that has been going on for as long as anyone can remember and shows no sign of stopping. From the air — if there were air to see it from — it would look not like a city at all but like a pattern in the green and white, a vast, low, textural weave of life stretched flat under a normal sun, warm, packed, humming with silent motion, and still, layer by layer, coming into being.
