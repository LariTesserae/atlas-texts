There is no weather down here, and no sky to have it in.

You go down past the cold blue, past the place where the light lets go entirely, and then the water changes — not at a line, at a softening. It thickens, warms, takes on a soupy yellow-green. That is the lid of the place. From above it would look like a stain. From inside, it is simply the ceiling: a warm pocket of sea trapped in a basalt bowl four hundred metres below any surface that matters, three hundred and fifty metres across, no wider than a hamlet if you could hold it in your mind.

They call it Therm Hollow on the charts. The people who live there call it the Low.

The first thing is the silence. It is not empty. It is padded. Water at this depth, in this warmth, takes sound and makes it into pressure. A dropped wrench does not clang. It says *tok*, far away inside your own skull. The big pumps under the main street do not roar; you feel them through the soles of your boots and in your molars, a slow breathing of the floor, and only once in a while, when the metal warms and settles, a pipe somewhere sings a single low note and lets it go. Voices are helmet voices, radio-hissed and close. Bubbles are rare and therefore notable — thin silver strings rising from the vent stacks, lazily, as if in no hurry to get anywhere.

The second thing is the warmth. The water is bath-warm, thirty-four degrees, and it holds. Your suit's heaters cut out and stay out. A bare hand, if you are foolish enough to take a glove off in the street, comes back feeling it has been held. The town's heat is not an accident. A field of alkaline vents to the south of the bowl feeds the pocket, and the pocket feeds the town. Everything is plumbed into everything else for heat.

The third thing is that you can see nothing, and then all at once, too much.

The bowl is walled. Basalt rim, black and wet and old, rises all around like the inside of a cup, and beyond the rim the cold ocean resumes, darker and clearer and indifferent. Inside the rim, the floor is flat with slag and silt, and on it sits the town, packed so tight there is nowhere for the water to be empty.

That is the governing fact of the Low. Nothing here has space. Houses are pressed to factory walls. Factory walls are pressed to cooling towers. Cooling towers are pressed to apartment stacks. Pipes cross overhead so low you duck, and where pipes do not cross, kelp does. Conduits do double duty as handrails, laundry lines, and trellises. Crates, spare fans, stacked ingots, bicycles with magnetic wheels resting against walls — every corner has been filled, and then filled again. The streets are not streets so much as remaining gaps between presences, and you learn to walk the Low the way you learn a labyrinth: by habit, by touching the familiar pipe, counting the third porthole from the bend in Sinter Lane.

It is a real town, not a station. Settlement-scale and layered and organized, built over sixty-odd years and never planned all at once. The pump quarter is deepest, down-slope to the south where the pressure helps the intakes. The smelters are there too, low sheds with sawtooth roofs that serve no purpose under water except that they were copied from a surface pattern book and nobody ever bothered to change them. Their chimneys are the vent stacks — tall mineral towers crusted white, banded orange with bacterial mat, soft with sponge. Ore comes in on conveyor tubes from the vent field, rocks tumbling slowly in clear water like stones in a river. Ingots go out, a few at a time, on the lift cable. The work is industrial and scaled and patient: crush, wash, smelt, cast, repeat. The forges run day and light — there is no night, only the dimmer shift — and their glow is amber through portholes fuzzed with algae.

Above the works, stacked and leaning, are the houses. Pressure cubes riveted and re-riveted, painted in colours that were once bright and are now a uniform faded teal under the film of life. Windows are round and small. Doors have numbers stencilled on them and names painted over other names. On the second level of one stack there is a shrine made from a decommissioned fan, and on the third level of another, someone has carefully hung diving suits to dry — as if the water will ever dry them. The market square is eleven metres across, and that is considered generous. It sells hot tea pumped through a coil that sits in the smelter waste-line, algae cakes, batteries, lamp oil, pruning shears, and small bright fish that are not for eating but for looking at, in a tank, because someone wanted a luxury.

In the centre is the heart, if the Low has one: the pump house, bigger than the other buildings and not much bigger, with three fan blades the width of a room turning so slowly you could watch one revolution and miss it. The fans are the oldest machines in town. They are also, now, partly plants.

Because the other governing fact of the Low is that the vegetation is winning, gently, and has been winning for a long time.

It is lush in a way that has nothing to do with sunlight. There is almost no sunlight. What light there is comes filtered from far above in a blue-green dusk, from the amber grid of street lamps, and, at intervals, from the algae themselves. That is enough for the reds and olives that grow here, because they are not really eating light. They are eating warmth and mineral wash and the steady nutrient soup the smelters put out. Iron-weed pours down factory walls in sheets. Fan-grass grows in perfect rosettes through fan grates, in ducts, in the corners of kitchens, one frond per slat. Kelp trees — proper trees, trunked and branching — stand around the rim of the bowl higher than the houses, and sway, if that is the word, an inch to the left over the course of an hour. Seagrass beds have colonized the silt between the concrete slabs of the main street and softened it. Anemones open in shoeboxes left by the door. Orange and white sponge fills the joints of the pipes. The groves are tended. Pruning is a municipal service, with a schedule, and a crew with shears and a list that goes back decades. Every June — a word the Low uses out of habit, though there is no season — they cut back the kelp from the lift cable, and every July they thin the fan-grass from the pump house, and the fans turn a fraction slower each year, and nobody minds.

The strangest thing, and the thing the residents mention only when they are showing you around, is that the algae on the smelter walls flickers. When a batch is poured, a wave of pale blue light runs across the weed on the sheds, quick as a blink, and then fades. The plants are lit by the heat cycle. The whole north wall of the town breathes with the forges. At the dimmer shift, when the lamps drop to half, the seagrass beds give off a faint glow of their own, greenish, like a field holding its breath. It is dim here always. Your eyes adjust after a week and do not adjust back for a month. The outside of the rim, beyond the basalt, is the only true dark, and the town keeps a viewing port in the east wall for people who want to look into it. The kelp trees lean that way too.

Nothing here looks new, and nothing here looks ruined. That is the third fact, and it explains most of the others. The Low is settled. Sixty years of silt, mineral accretion, algae, and slow hands have brought it to a state it can stay in. Rust forms and stops, like skin over a wound. Paint bubbles in predictable tiles. Buildings list a degree to the south and stay there. Stairs have filled with silt to the third step and that is their final height. A tool dropped in the street sinks slowly, lands with a soft *tok*, and within a year it is fuzzed orange, then green, then part of the wall. The place does not erode in the dramatic sense. It accretes. Mineral crust thickens around pipes a millimetre at a time, so that the oldest conduits are becoming white-boned, and the corridors are narrowing, very slowly, and every few years the council has to ream out the Sinter Lane underpass with a boring head, because the passage is growing shut. In another twenty years, a room in the pump quarter will be too small to enter. Nobody seems alarmed. It is understood that the town is growing inward, the way a shell grows.

The dynamic of everything is slow. The smelter cycle is measured in shifts, not minutes. The kelp grows a handspan a year. A falling pipe can take a season to finish falling and nobody will catch it. The vent field warms and cools over decades; the town has a whole vocabulary for the current phase of the vent, and the phase of the vent is older than most of the residents. Time in the Low is something you measure by growth. The sea outside has tides. Inside the bowl, the water does not move much at all. It hangs in its warmth, circulating only because the pumps insist, and the plants insist, and the heat insists.

The fauna are rare, and that is not an exaggeration. In your first month you will see perhaps a dozen pale vent crabs the size of a saucer, picking their way over the slag with terrible patience. A pair of long eels lives in the old cooling tower and comes out at the dimmer shift to hang in the water like grey ribbons, and the whole town knows them by the affectionate, inaccurate names they have given them. Mackerel shadows pass at the rim sometimes, on the cold side, and do not come in. A single blind octopus lives in the archive tank and is considered, by common consent, a resident. Beyond that, the animal life is the crew themselves: forty-three permanent, and a rotating dozen, in bulky suits on the street, or in the few dry pods where the air is a stale luxury that smells of machine oil and laundry, or asleep in their bunks while the water presses gently on the hulls all around them. The absence of animals is part of the quiet. There is nothing to chatter in the kelp. There is no swam of fry. The town's ecology is mostly plant and machine, holding each other up.

It is strange, in the end, not because it is impossible, but because it is so fully itself. A warm, dim, flooded hamlet in a bowl at the bottom of the sea, growing slower and smaller and greener, making ingots out of vent rock and tea out of waste heat, tended by two generations of people who prune the fans and ream the tunnels and listen for the pumps to breathe. Everything is wet. Everything is warm. Everything has room for nothing else, and is, by some long local logic, exactly enough.

When you leave, you go up through the lid, and the cold takes you again, clean and blue and indifferent, and your suit's heaters come on, and the silence becomes the empty kind. Behind you, below, the lights stay amber, the weed flickers with the forges, and the Low goes on, inch by inch, growing inward.
