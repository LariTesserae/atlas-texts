First there is black.

Not an empty black. A wet black, cold enough to hurt through gloves, pressing evenly on ears, on chest, on the insides of joints. Water is everywhere and there is no surface to it, no shore, no weather. Up is only a direction toward a blacker place, down is a soft resistance of silt. You move the way things move down here: slowly, because the water will not let you waste anything, because even a kick sends a small weather of particles trailing behind you that hangs there for a long time after you have gone.

A light, then. Far off in the sense that distance is hard to judge when there is nothing to compare it to. Another light, smaller. Pinholes of amber. A swath of blue-white that fans down and stops, cut off by the dark. They do not brighten as you approach so much as they accumulate, until the black is no longer total but patterned, like seeing a constellation inside a closed eye.

That is the edge of the place.

You are in a low bowl of the seabed, perhaps two hundred and forty metres across, no more. The floor of the ocean here is flat silt, but the town has made its own floor and its own border. A low berm of slag, broken concrete, old ballast and drowned machinery arcs around the whole hamlet, only a few metres high, covered in a fine, undisturbed skin of silt except where the currents worry at it. Inside the berm the water is stiller. Outside the berm there is nothing: more silt plain going on and on, dark, occasionally stirred, and then black again. From one side of the town you can see the lights on the opposite side, dimmed but present. It is a small world with a fence it made out of its own waste, and the fence has been there long enough that the silt has forgotten the sharpness of it.

Inside, density begins all at once.

It is not a scattering of huts. It is a town grown against itself. Warehouses form the ground floors, houses sit on the warehouses, workshops lean against the houses, and a second town of containers and cable drums and stacked pipes has grown up in the gaps between them. Streets are canals of black water, narrow enough in places that you could touch a wall on either side if you opened your arms. Bridges of grated steel cross them at shoulder height. Ladders go down into flooded doorways. Submersible cars are parked hull to hull along a curb that no longer means anything to a road, their running lights left on for no one in particular because lights are how space is marked.

Everything is near-touching. Cables are strung building to building so thickly in places they become a low ceiling, sagging under their own weight and their coat of life. Pipes branch like arteries, then like vines, then like roots looking for somewhere to enter. A crane that has not lifted anything in memory still moves in its slow cycle, turning a small arc, pausing, turning back, because its motor is still wired to a timer set before anyone now working there was born.

The buildings are industrial before they are anything else. Riveted steel, poured concrete, glass thick as a fist in the portholes, copper gone green where it is exposed and gone bright where it is polished by passing hands. Paint is mostly gone. What remains is the colour of wet oxide, olive, rust, the grey of concrete that has been underwater so long it has become a kind of stone again. The place is not newly sunk. Nothing here is sharp. Edges have been softened by silt and by the patient work of water finding every seam. A ship's hull is the foundation at the centre, and generations have built out from it until the hull is less a ship than a piece of geology the town decided to keep. Courtyards have filled with silt to the smoothness of river stone. Roofs — the highest roofs, still several metres below the open water — have slumped a little and then stopped, settled into a state they can live with.

It is an old, established place. Not in the way a forest is old, but in the way a machine left running is old. It has come to a balance with its own erosion.

Because heat leaks, the town huddles. That is the secret of its density. Outside the buildings the water is close to freezing, one or two degrees, a cold that does not so much touch you as occupy you. Inside even the flooded ground floors it is warmer, ten degrees near the engines, five in the living rooms where they bother to heat them. Warmth is the currency. Walls are shared not for space but to keep heat from escaping. The factories are in the basements and the backs, the smelters and the battery cradles and the vats where replacement parts are grown chemically and slowly, and their waste heat is piped deliberately through the floors above them. You can tell where a building is warm by what lives on it.

Light is the other currency, and it is spent sparingly. There is no sun down here and there never has been in the town's memory. The darkness is a real material, thin at the edges of the lamps, absolute a few metres beyond them. Work lights point down, yellow-white and blue-white, making small cones in the black where marine snow — the endless slow fall of dead things and mineral dust — drifts through and vanishes. Portholes glow a deep amber, steady or blinking in patterns that are not quite signals and not quite not-signals. A resident walking a canal will carry a lamp, and the lamp will be the brightest thing around them, and the dark behind them will close immediately. You learn distance here by how a light fades, not by how it brightens.

And silence. The silence is thick, almost physical.

Sound travels too well in deep water; a clang in a town this small would be heard outside the berm and far beyond, and would carry for miles in the black. So the town was built to be quiet, or the town learned to be quiet because the engines were wrapped in oil and rubber and old blankets of insulation until their noise became a thud you feel in your sternum rather than hear in your ears. The great pumps cycle and sigh. A crane turns. A submersible docks with a soft, apologetic bump. Regulators breathe. That is nearly all of it. The silence is not an absence of activity. It is activity muffled. Things are constantly moving, just not loudly.

If you put a hand flat against a main pipe you can feel the town's pulse travel the whole hamlet in a second: a pump starting in the factory at the north end registers at the houses at the south, a slow, steady thump-thump that everyone knows by touch. People navigate by it more than by sight. They hold rails and conduits the way surface people hold a banister. The town is, in a sense, one connected body of metal, and the metal is its nervous system.

The ecology is sparse, and honest about it.

Vegetation does not make forests here. It makes suggestions. Patches of dark ribbon kelp cling to the slag berm where the current is steady, waving at a speed that is almost geological, black-green and translucent at the tips. On the warm sides of pipes, on the sunless but heated north faces of concrete, there is a film of algae, a slime that ranges from green to a rusty brown, thick enough to grip. In the outflow channels behind the factories, where nutrient water runs warm and slow, it thickens into mats. Everywhere else the town is bare metal and stone. The plant life exists only where heat or waste gives it permission, isolated islands of it that tell you more about the plumbing than about any garden.

Fauna is rarer still. Small translucent shrimp gather in the cones of the lamps, picking at what falls, invisible until they move. Blind fish hover outside the berm in the dark, silvery and waiting, but they do not generally enter the town; the lights and the warm water are not for them. A solitary crab keeps a culvert in the east canal, moving with the terrible slowness of a thing that has never been in a hurry. Occasionally, at the very limit of a light, a larger shadow passes beyond the berm — too far to identify, too indifferent to be a threat. The town was not built as a habitat. Life has simply settled into its margins and decided that the margins are enough.

Yet for all that sparseness, nothing here is still.

The currents make their lanes even in the still pocket inside the berm. Silt lifts and resettles in patterns you can watch for an hour without seeing them repeat. Marine snow falls at different speeds in different streets, faster where a pump pushes water, slower where the buildings block it, so that the air — the water — seems to have weather. Bacteria bloom on rust and turn it slowly back into food for other things. Condensation forms on the inside of cold portholes and runs down in threads. The containers sweat a faint oil that makes rainbows on the canal surface, which is not a surface at all but a boundary between warmer, slightly lighter water inside the town and the cold black outside.

The engines cycle. The vats bubble without sound. The cranes turn. The submersibles come and go across the berm in the dark, their lights a pair of small moons that you see leave and do not see return. Once a day, or once a week, or on some longer cycle that no one outside would measure, a load is docked, unloaded by lit figures in heavy suits, and the town takes in a little more of whatever it needs to keep being itself.

That is, perhaps, the strangest thing about it. No one with any clarity can say what the town is for anymore, if it is for anything beyond keeping the town going. It makes spare parts for its factories. It makes power for its power plants. It refines minerals from seawater and stores them in containers stacked floor to ceiling in the west quarter, and the containers are never shipped out in any quantity that matters. New people — if they are people, if the suited figures who move between the lights are a permanent population and not just tenders of a machine that outlived its makers — arrive rarely, by submersible, and stay, and learn the town's one rule, which is not spoken so much as absorbed: do not waste heat, do not waste light, do not make a noise that will travel, do not leave the berm without a reason, and do not drop anything you cannot retrieve, because dropped things do not fall. They drift, very slowly, until they settle on the silt outside or on a roof inside, and become part of the town's slow geology.

It is a hamlet that went down and decided, collectively and without discussion, to stay down, and to make a home out of the staying. The water holds it up and holds it in. The dark holds it together. The cold has made it honest. Everything is dense and close and layered because closeness is warmth, and everything is quiet because quiet is survival, and everything is old and weathered and settled because it has had time — a very long time, measured in rust and silt and generations of algae — to become itself.

Beyond the berm, the ocean resumes, indifferent and unlit. Inside, the lights continue in their amber and blue patterns, the pipes continue their pulse under your palm, the marine snow continues to fall through the cones of light and out the other side into black, and Hushport, as its people call it when they call it anything at all, goes on being a small, warm, industrial constellation at the bottom of a cold sea.
