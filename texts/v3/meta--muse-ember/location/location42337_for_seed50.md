**The Unfinished Ocean in the Sky**

You arrive in the middle of a sound.

It is not that the sound announces the place. The sound *is* the place, first and last. A low roar that lives in the molars, a high hiss on top of it like water being torn, a cracking and re-cracking underneath everything, nearer and farther at once. It does not get quieter when you wish it to. At times you feel it more in the chest than in the ears. Anything you would have said here has to be said slowly, and mostly to yourself.

At first you think you are drowning.

The medium around you is water — not as a floor you lie in, not as a river you stand in, but as the air itself made liquid. You breathe it. It fills the ears and the sinuses, it beads and runs and re-forms on skin, it is cool without ever quite becoming cold. Somewhere west of cool, somewhere between deep spring water and high mountain air, and the coolness never leaves you. Your breath comes out as a second, smaller mist inside the larger mist, and then is taken away so fast you cannot tell if you exhaled it or if it was always there.

After a little while the drowning thought gives way to the mountain thought.

Because the light is perfectly, unremarkably normal.

That is one of the strangest things here. Ordinary daylight, white-gold, mid-afternoon in tone, not twilight, not glare, not the blue-green gloom of the deep sea. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. Cloud and water scatter it so thoroughly that shadows are blunt and brief, and you cannot point to the sun. Your eyes do not strain. It is just there, as if someone had left the sky on.

And then, in a thin place in the floor of the medium — for there is a floor, of a kind — you glimpse, very far below, a ridge. Then another, and the white slash of a glacier, and the dark green-black of a forest that is only a rumor at this distance. Vertigo, but a vertigo made of water, so it comes and goes like a current. You are high. High enough that the air below would be thin if you were in air, high enough that the peaks down there are small. But you are not falling. You are submerged in the height itself.

Which brings the third thought, which is the truer one: this is not a sea, and not a mountain, but a pocket.

You cannot see it from outside. From outside it is only a brighter patch in the side of a storm cloud, a place where the rain wall seems to hesitate. From inside, it is contained. You could cross it, in a straight line, in a few minutes of swimming through sky-water. No one with a map would mark it. It is embedded, hidden inside a larger weather the way a bubble is hidden inside a wave.

The boundary is a wall of rain.

You do not so much cross it as pass *through* the roar. On the far side the sound changes timbre — less wind, more hammering, droplets striking other droplets as they are born — and then you are inside. Behind you the wall is still deafening, but it is now a curtain you can sometimes see: a vertical sheet of falling-upward and falling-downward spray where the cloud is shearing itself apart and putting itself back together.

### Terrain without ground

There is no shore here, and no bottom in any useful sense, and that is why the terrain is so difficult to describe. The ground is a gradient.

Down is where the heavier droplets gather for a while before the updraft takes them again. Up is where new droplets are born with a soft pop in mid-medium, hang there glittering, and then are pulled sideways. Between them run currents, ridges of faster water, valleys of slower water, all moving, all invisible until something — a filament, a scrap of cloth, your own outstretched hand — crosses them and tumbles.

The pocket breathes. It swells, then contracts, then swells along a different axis. Walls that were ten paces away approach to two, recede to twenty. Objects in the empty middle do not so much float as *hesitate* in the water-air for long, weird moments before they begin to fall again, or rise, or drift sideways against the current that ought to have them. It is turbulent in the way a storm is turbulent, and also in the way a single breaking wave is turbulent — creation and destruction at the same instant, on a small scale, over and over.

And here is the other paradox that settles over you once the sound becomes background: for all that roar and churning, the pocket is mostly empty.

Vast, bright, empty space between presences.

You can look for a very long time through clear sky-water and see nothing but droplets passing like slow snow in reverse. No fish-shoals, no forests, no crowds. The deafening sound fills a void that, visually, is almost unspeakably unfilled. Half the time, crossing from one side to the other, you meet nothing at all. Half the time you meet something that was not there a minute ago, because it has just finished coming into being.

### Emerging

Nothing here is finished. That is not a metaphor.

Geology, such as it is, is meteorological and embryonic. On the slower currents, a scum of mineral dust and ice crystal begins to knit together into a crust thin as breath, then dissolves back into the medium before it can be called stone. A droplet merges with another. A filament grows an inch and then is eaten by light and water and reabsorbed. The pocket itself is pre-formation — a lake that has not yet decided to fall as rain, or to stay as cloud, or to become a flood that will run down the mountain you glimpsed below. It is raw, under construction, just-coming-into-being, and it may never complete the construction before it un-happens.

Things here do not erode the way rock erodes. They un-happen. A shape softens at the edges, loses its claim, and is water again.

### Sparse life

What life there is works on the same principle: a little, and rarely, and in threads.

Along the light-side currents, where the normal light is strongest, hang the vegetation — if vegetation is the word. Not plants rooted in anything, but green-black filaments, algae and sky-moss and things in between, strung as if on invisible lines between droplet membranes. They photosynthesize in almost nothing. There are only a few strands in the whole pocket, spaced far apart, swaying not in wind but in current, tasting of iron and rain. Where one is, the water smells faintly, cleanly green. Where one is not — most places — there is no smell at all, only the mineral coolness.

Fauna are rarer still.

In hours of empty bright water, you may see nothing with a pulse but yourself. Then, perhaps, it crosses. A glider.

It is the only kind of animal the pocket seems to admit, and there may be only a handful in existence at any one time, perhaps only one at a time. It swims through the sky-water on slow, shallow wing-strokes, like a ray that has learned to fly by not flying, translucent at the edges, opaque in the center, trailing a wake of disturbed droplets. It does not avoid you, exactly. It does not notice you. It crosses the whole pocket at its own speed, indifferent to current and to your presence, and then is gone into the rain wall on the far side, or dissolves into the same emergence that made it, and the pocket is empty again.

You understand, slowly, that rarity here is not scarcity of food. It is a rule of the place: nothing lives here in numbers. Everything that lives here is alone, or nearly alone, with a vast distance between presences.

### What was built

And yet someone, or something, built here. Scattered, small, primitive.

Because the pocket is empty, the artifacts are startling when you find them. They do not belong, and so they stay in the eye.

A half-woven raft of reeds, lashed with sinew or stripped bark, drifting in a slow tumble, half-rotted back into fibre. A cup of baked clay tied to a float of bladder, still holding a palmful of sky-water that is not quite water. A tiny windbreak of sticks pushed into a crust that is now only a memory of crust. A cairn of smoothed pebbles that you cannot imagine being stacked here — on what? — until you remember the crusts form and un-form, and perhaps they were stacked on a temporary shelf. A knapped stone tool. A patched piece of hide, stitched with bone needle, smelling faintly of smoke that has no business being this high.

No large structures. No settlements. Just these faint marks, this handful of hand-made things made with early tools and manual craft, abandoned in a place that is still being born. Whether the builders were the gliders' ancestors, or visitors who fell up through the rain wall, or a people who lived on the mountain below and left things to the cloud, you cannot tell. The artifacts are as unfinished as everything else. Edges soften. Lashings loosen. A woven thing becomes a suggestion of weaving.

It is a strange comfort, to find them. Proof that the pocket has had a past, and also proof that its past is dissolving.

### The rules, insofar as they hold

The day deepens — though the light stays normal — and you begin to learn the place by its habits, not by its map.

Sound arrives before sight. Because you are in water, the roar travels fast and close. You will hear the cracking that means a new crust is forming a long moment before you see the glitter of it, and you will hear a glider's passage as a pressure change in the water before the shape appears in the empty bright. Sometimes, in the contraction of the pocket, an echo seems to precede its source, as if the medium remembers the noise before the thing that made it has finished making it.

Droplets fall up in bundles. When the pocket contracts, the gathered heaviness at the bottom is suddenly lifted, and a whole cluster rises together, silently, against all your mountain-born expectation. Then it falls again, just as suddenly, and the roar swells.

Breathing is circular. You inhale mist. A moment later, downstream, that same mist may be exhaled by the pocket as a finer rain that never reaches ground. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is kept, for long.

Distance is untrustworthy. Two things that look close may be separated by a shear current you cannot see, and two things that look far may be brought together in one swell of the breathing wall. You cross the pocket differently each time, though it is, by any measure, small.

And the whole thing is unstable, in the Solaris sense: it creates itself by destroying itself. Droplets pop into being. Filaments grow. Crusts knit. The rain wall tears and reseals. The pocket may, at any moment, decide — if deciding is the word — to collapse into a downpour that will soak the high ridge below for an hour, or to expand into a cloud that will drift for days, or to do neither and simply un-happen, droplet by droplet, back into the storm it came from. It has no fixed shape to protect. Its turbulence is not an accident of weather. It is its nature.

You find you are not wet in the way you are wet in a river. You are wet the way a thought is wet in a dream — pervasively, without a surface. Cool, bright, normal-lit, deafening, empty, emerging, high and hidden and full of water that is also air.

After a while you stop looking for the edges of the landscape in the ordinary sense. There are only currents, and filaments, and perhaps a glider, and a lashed raft tumbling slowly in the distance, and the sound.

The pocket goes on being born around you, and un-being born, and being born again a little differently, in the sky above the mountain, in a room of water with no floor.
