**The Sinterhouse**

First there is the height, and the heat, and the emptiness that goes with both.

The air is thin enough that breath comes short at the top of the ridge, and the ground underfoot is hot in a way that has nothing to do with the sun. It radiates upward through the soles of your feet, up through the rock, as if the mountain were still cooking from below. There is no water here at all — no seep, no tarn, no dew in the morning, no rain that anyone can remember. Anything wet would boil away as it fell. The stone is dry to its center, pale and sharp, powdered at the edges with a fine mineral dust that does not clump, does not wash, does not settle into soil.

And then, in the middle of that barrenness, you find the pocket.

It is not built. There is no door, no wall, no footprint of a hand anywhere in it. It is a hidden room that geology has only just stubbed out — perhaps ten paces across, perhaps less if you measure honestly, but inside it the proportions refuse to stay honest. The ceiling is a low vault of sintered rock, black glass and rust-red slag fused together, and the floor is the same material still in the act of becoming floor. It is raw. Edges have not been worn. Nothing has been eroded into shape yet, because erosion here has barely begun. New crystals are pushing up through old crystals. A crack that was open an hour ago is already half-sealed with a lip of fresh glass. A pebble is not a pebble; it is a nucleus, growing. If you stand still long enough your footprint fills in, slowly, like cooling concrete deciding to forget you.

This is an embryonic place. It is pre-formation. The world is still under construction, and no one is constructing it.

Between the bare, hot expanses — because inside the pocket, too, most of it *is* bare — there are the gardens.

They are sparse. That is the first surprise, and it stays. For meters at a time there is only shining, empty mineral floor, so empty you can hear your own pulse in your ears, and then suddenly a clump so lush it seems to contradict everything around it. Three such islands, four if you count the one that grows in mirror image from the ceiling. Each island is a thicket, layered and profuse, leaves overlapping leaves in greens that are almost indecent against the grey and copper ground.

They have never known water.

They do not drink. Their roots do not hunt for moisture; they go down and sideways after heat, braiding themselves tight around veins of hot rock, and they breathe in mineral vapor the way other plants breathe in air. What runs in them instead of sap is a clear, ruby resin, thick as warm honey, that weeps from stems when you brush them and does not dry. The leaves are broad and waxy and dry to the touch, many of them ribbed like corrugated iron, many of them enamel-smooth and slightly translucent, so that sunlight passes through and lights the veins from within. They smell of hot resin and scorched sugar and faint ozone, like a workshop left running in an empty field.

There are no animals. Not one. No insects in the flowers, no birds in the thicket, no lizards on the hot stone. Nothing here moves under its own power. Everything moves the way a factory moves when left to itself: slowly, by pressure and heat and expansion.

Because that is the other surprise, once you stay long enough to notice it. This place is industrial without ever having been built.

The thickets are engines. Some of the plants are intakes, their leaves always angled to catch light, their surfaces fins to shed heat. Some are smelters, their roots swelling and contracting over the course of days — a bellows-cycle so slow you would miss it if you watched for only a day — driving mineral salts up into their stems, calcining them, breaking them down, and laying down new material in layers, cell by cell, like a pipe being printed in hot metal. Some are reservoirs, storing that ruby fluid under pressure until, hours later, a joint pings and vents a thin hiss of hot vapor. The flowers, when they open, do not open for pollinators. They open like furnace doors, glowing orange at the throat, and shed their pollen as a brief, silent spark that arcs to the next plant across the empty floor. Cross-pollination is electrocution. It is quiet, precise, and impersonal.

Light, at least, is normal. The sun that finds the slit entrance is ordinary white-gold daylight, colors are true, shadows are the length you expect. Nothing alien there. In a place that is otherwise breaking, that ordinariness is a relief, and then it is not.

Because at the center of the pocket, direction stops behaving. Walk east across the floor and you arrive back from the west, having passed the same thicket twice from opposite sides. The ceiling-garden is not a reflection; it is the same garden continuing, root to root, and at the meeting point the leaves do not touch — they interleave, which should be impossible. Uphill and downhill are the same slope. Your voice, when you speak, is mostly swallowed by the silence — this place is almost perfectly quiet, except for the occasional tick and ping of cooling slag, and the very slow sigh of a plant venting — but sometimes, if you listen, the echo arrives a fraction *before* the sound finishes leaving your mouth, delayed by a coldness you cannot feel.

Time does not pass here so much as accumulate. The day-night cycle is muffled by the vault; heat builds and builds and barely falls. The plants grow in pulses separated by long, patient stillnesses. A leaf that was half-formed in the morning will be half-formed again next week, and then, in the blink of an afternoon when you are not looking, complete. Seeds are older than their parent plants. The pocket itself is older in the middle than at the edges. That is the breakdown, if you need a name for it: not chaos, but a world that has not yet agreed on its own rules, and is taking its time learning them.

The atmosphere holds all of it together — hot, thin, metallic, dry as a kiln. It smells of iron and resin and hot stone. The dust gets in your lungs and stays there, because there is no water to clear it. Sweat evaporates before it can run. You feel the heat as a constant pressure, not as weather. There is no wind to speak of. The air moves only when a plant breathes out, which is to say, occasionally, and even then it moves as if reluctant.

Nothing here will last in the form you see it, and nothing here has lasted yet. The floor is still setting. The thickets are still assembling themselves, layer upon layer, smelting their own nutrients out of rock that has never been soil. In a hundred years — or in the pocket’s own slower reckoning, in one long breath — the islands will have spread, or folded back, or become something else entirely, and the empty spaces will be empty in a different way.

For now it sits hidden on its high, hot ridge, sealed in its unbuilt room, lush and barren at once: a garden running like a factory, a factory grown like a garden, with no water, no animals, no artifacts, and no hurry whatsoever to finish becoming a world.
