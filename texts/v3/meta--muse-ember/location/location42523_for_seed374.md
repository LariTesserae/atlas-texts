It is flat for as far as the eye can go, and the eye does not go far.

The sky sits low, a dim grey ceiling thickened with steam and condensing mist, and the sun is only a paler disc behind it, giving no shadows and no real noon. The land meets that sky at a level horizon in every direction — no ridge, no rise to climb, just a wide, low basin at near the bottom of the country, where water has nowhere to run off to. What ground there is feels unfinished. It is a sheet of pale mineral mud and sinter, newly laid and still laying itself down, cracked into broad polygons, ridged in fresh, soft banks, smoothed in other places to a slick plaster. Stones are sharp and unweathered. Ditches and channels are raw cuts, not yet worn into familiar ruts. There is nowhere the polish of age, no old gully, no old tree. The whole place looks as if it has just come into being and is still being made.

Underfoot it is always damp. Anywhere you dig a finger deep you meet water, because the water table is right there under the skin of the earth. Warm pools spread out knee-deep in the hollows, their surfaces filmed with grey and faintly green microbial mats. Narrower channels — some natural, some dug by hand — carry that water in slow sheets across the flats and straight through the settlement, so that lanes are often a mixture of stone paving and warm, ankle-deep flow. On shaded stone, on roof slabs, on the sides of walls, water beads and weeps despite the warmth of the air. The air itself is warm and saturated. Clothes never dry fully. Skin never quite dries. Condensation forms on your forearms and drips from eaves in a constant, irregular percussion, and the mist pools ankle-deep in the mornings — and at midday, and at night — never lifting, never thickening by any measure you could name.

And there is no green at all.

That is the first thing you notice after the wet. You search the water’s edge for reeds, the flats for scrub, the walls for a weed in a crack, and there is nothing. No plant life takes hold here, on this young mineral ground, in water too warm and too salted with heat-brought minerals, across a plain that is still depositing. There is no soil in the ordinary sense, only mud, silt, and crust. What grows is simpler: thin fungal films in damp corners, grey lichen-like patches on stone, microbial scums rippling on the pools. The town is built without wood for that reason too. Wood is a treasure brought in on backs and pack animals from the drier rim beyond the basin, saved for a beam here and there, for a handle, for a scaffold pole. Everything else is stone cut from the flats, mud brick baked hard in the warm air, plaster, clay pots, leather, bone and shell. Houses are low, flat-roofed, heavy, their thresholds raised a hand’s breadth against the water that wants to come in. Their roofs are slabs weighted with more slabs. Their walls sweat.

The town does not end. That is the second surprise. Scale here is open and regional, not a small cluster tucked in a hollow. From the mist, more huts emerge, then more, lane after lane, terrace after terrace, stretching out across the basin in irregular districts around the largest pool clusters. It is dense to the point of crowding — near-touching houses, compound walls abutting other compound walls, workshops shoulder-to-shoulder with bathing platforms, storage yards wedged between homes, layered up slight artificial terraces because the flat ground will not drain. Densely packed is how you survive when ground is scarce and wet: you build in layers, you stack, you share a channel, you share a warm pool.

And everywhere it is half-built. Foundations for plots that have been claimed but not yet roofed. Walls stopped at waist height, with courses of stone waiting beside them. A house being re-raised because its corner has sunk a finger’s width in the soft ground. This is not a ruin, and it is not a finished city. It is a settlement still coming into being, in a landscape still coming into being, and it holds that unfinished state steadily. New building goes up at the same rate as old ground settles, old channels silt and are re-dug, and the sum never changes.

The work is primitive and manual, and loud. Without engines or much metal — only simple iron and copper, stone hammers, chisels, hand tools — everything is done by hand close to the body. You hear the clack of hammer on stone all day long, fire popping from dozens of small hearths, water being bailed by bucket and leather sack, pots being shaped, hides being beaten at the tanning troughs by the warm channels. People shout to be heard over the place itself, because the place itself never stops shouting.

Sound is the governing fact. It is nearly deafening, and it is constant. Under everything is the hiss and rush of the ground breathing: steam vents in the flats beyond the houses making a continuous roar like a distant waterfall or a kettle chorus, water running in sheets down the channels, dripping from every ledge. Over that, the human layer — voices at close range, dogs barking, chickens, ducks quacking in the lanes, the scrape of feet on wet stone. Over that again, the animal layer, which is abundant beyond what the bare ground would suggest.

Fauna are everywhere, in layers. Midges and small flies rise in dense clouds over the warm pools, thick enough to breathe through. Frogs and toads sit in the shallows in the lanes, newts slip under stones, small fish dart in the cooler feeder channels, turtles bask on warm mineral ledges. Swifts and other aerial birds scream and wheel just under the mist ceiling, pigeons nest by the thousand in wall cavities and under eaves, herons wade motionless in the channels. In and around the houses: dogs lying half-submerged in warm shallows to cool or warm themselves, ducks paddling through the thoroughfares, chickens scratching at mineral ground for insects, goats and pigs tethered in small stone pens, fed not on local forage — there is none — but on grain and scraps and dried fish brought in from outside the basin, and on the waste of the town itself.

That is how the ecology holds together without plants. The warm water feeds microbes, the microbes feed insects and small fish, the insects feed birds and frogs, and the town feeds its domestic animals from beyond and from within. Warmth and water and year-round shelter mean nothing has to stop for winter. Breeding goes on. The birds never migrate far. The insects never die off. A dense, warm, wet, dim town is a perfect, permanent nest for things that live off water, warmth, and people.

Light, heat, water, and noise all obey the same rule: they do not change. There is no season to dry the ground or clear the mist. There is no storm front to lift the steam or bring a new wind. The water in the pools is at the same level today as last year. The roar at a given vent is at the same pitch. The mist hangs at the same height over the same huts. Activity moves — people walk, animals call, hammers fall, water flows — but the place itself is in equilibrium. You could leave for years and return and find the same dim, warm, wet hush-roar, the same unfinished walls, the same bare mineral flats stretching into mist on all sides, the same town spreading past the immediate view toward other pools and other streets you haven’t seen.

It is, in the end, a normal-seeming place made extreme by its constraints: a low, warm, steaming basin where the earth is new and the water is near the surface and nothing green can root; a dense, primitive, stone town building itself endlessly on that wet ground; and a loud, crowded life of people and animals living in perpetual damp and dimness, listening to water and steam and each other, and never quite finishing, and never decaying, either.
