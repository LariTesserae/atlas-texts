**Kell Hollow**

You do not climb into it. You sink into it.

It is a low basin, flat as a floor, ringed at a distance by low spoil-heaps and the tired fence-line of an old industrial estate. From the middle — from the small, brick station platform where the rails end in buffers — you can see the whole place at once. It is hamlet-size, a ten-minute walk from one end to the other, and it closes around you. No hill to look from, no valley to look down. The horizon is made of warehouses.

The ground is not soil. It was never soil here. It is compacted cinder, gravel, broken concrete, and pans of cracked mud laid over slag. Rain, when it comes, does not soak in so much as it darkens the surface and stays there. Wheel-ruts are polished. The canal that cuts straight through the middle carries black water that does not flow; it only reflects. Puddles sit in footprints, in the hollows of manhole covers, in the rims of upturned buckets, and they are always the same brownish depth.

You look, out of habit, for a weed in a crack, grass on a verge, a tree behind a terrace. There is none. Not a blade. The cracks are clean. The verges are ash. The gutters hold only a grey-green felt of fungus and a mineral crust, damp to the touch, that smells faintly of wet iron and old bread. No moss, no lichen that pretends to be a plant, no ivy on the brick. This is a post-life place in the botanical sense: everything green has either left or been paved over a long time ago, and what remains is stone, metal, and the living film that feeds on damp and on waste.

The town itself is urban, but sparsely. Terraces of red-brick houses, two storeys, windows blind or boarded, stand with wide, empty yards between them. Factories interlock with them — a boiler house with a chimney smoothed by a century of wind, a rolling mill, a substation behind chain-link, a water tower, a crane frozen with its hook in mid-air over a yard of rusted sheet iron. Everything is layered. Newer corrugated sheds are bolted over older brick. Concrete blocks patch a wall that was brick before that. Pipes run at three heights along every exterior, copper and iron, sweating.

It is cool here all the time. Not cold enough for frost, but the kind of cool that gets into your hands from a rail or a door handle and stays. Your breath fogs, briefly, even at midday. In the depths of the boiler house the engines are warm — they have to be, they are still running — and the air there is a pocket of heat, but two steps outside it, the damp takes it back. The light is ordinary daylight, flat and grey-yellow through high cloud, sun high enough to cast a short shadow. It never gets brighter. It never gets evening. It is always, stubbornly, late morning.

And damp. That is the rule of water here. It does not rain in any proper sense. Once in a while a fine misting comes down for a minute, too thin to wet your coat, and then stops. Most of the moisture arrives another way: condensation. Overnight — if you can call it that — the cold metal sweats. Pipes bead. The undersides of eaves weep in steady drips. The concrete exhales. You hear it more than you see it: a ping in a downpipe, a ticking drip into a puddle that never rises, a hiss in a valve. The whole place feels as if it has just been wiped down and left to dry, and never quite did.

Everything is worn to its final shape. That is the erosion here. Nothing is new and nothing is collapsing. Brick corners are rounded. The rails are bright on top where they have been polished by weight, dark at the joints. The canal banks are worn smooth as stone steps. Soot on the walls has been rained into streaks that have stopped streaking. It is a settled landscape, weathered into equilibrium. Things have crumbled exactly as much as they ever will.

The machines contribute to that feeling. A turbine hall hums at a pitch you feel in your teeth. A conveyor turns, empty, with a rhythmic clang that repeats. Fans rotate in the substation. Work-lights are on in warehouses where no work is done. Total, industrial loudness — transformers, pistons, the wind forced through vents — but it is not a roar that rises and falls. It is a current. It goes on, and on, at the same volume, and the ears, after a while, stop trying to parse it and accept it as weather.

Then you notice the animals.

At first, one. In the yard of the mill, a thing the size of a large hog, low to the ground, barrel-bodied and six-legged, its hide a wet, wrinkled grey in which grit and brick-dust are embedded like aggregate in concrete. Its back is plated, segments of mineral slag grown into the skin. Its head hangs low, a blunt snout working slowly over the concrete. Its feet end in black, iron-hard hooves that click.

Then another, and another, coming around the corner of the boiler house in a loose procession, following the gutter line. Then you look up, and the wall is covered. Under every eave, in every window frame, clinging flat to the brick and the corrugated iron with all six legs, are smaller ones, hundreds of them, motionless except for a constant, fine twitching of legs, like a shivering. The roofs are shingled with them. The underside of the water tower is a beard of them.

They are overwhelming. Not in the sense of a single herd you can walk past, but in the sense that there is nowhere in Kell Hollow that is not already occupied. The empty yards are not empty. They are slowly traversed by grey bodies, bumping gently, making way for each other with a sigh. The streets are choked in places, not blocked, just narrowed by animals sleeping in the wheel-ruts. They do not fear you. They step around your boots with the same indifference they give a pipe. You will, within an hour, have to pick your way over a back, or wait while one settles its bulk exactly across a doorway, its breath — damp, musky, faintly metallic — fogging your ankles.

They are strange up close. They have no eyes you can find. There are small, milky pits where eyes should be, skin-covered. They navigate by clicking — a rapid, insectile *tik-tik-tik* from under the snout — and by feeling vibration through their legs, which the concrete and the iron carry beautifully. In a place full of machines, they have learned to listen to the buildings. When a turbine clangs, their heads lift in unison, as if listening. Their bodies are hollow in the chest, and when they call, it is not a roar but a deep, resonant bellow that makes the corrugated walls hum in sympathy, a sound like a factory whistle answered by another factory whistle, from inside the animals themselves.

They live on nothing you would recognize as food. They graze the fungal felt that crusts the damp concrete, the north sides of walls, the inside of drains. The fungus, in turn, feeds on the mineral pellets they drop and on the plates they shed — grey flakes that curl and fall from their backs in the yard, which the fungus colonizes and they later eat again. They drink by licking condensation from pipes and from each other's plated backs. They are fat on this, and sleek-skinned, and they never seem to age. A scarred one with a torn plate on its left flank is in the same doorway every time you pass, on the same circuit.

For they walk in circuits. That is the other rule. A procession files along the canal, turns at the station, follows the rail line to the substation, loops back through the mill yard, and begins again. It is not a migration. It does not go anywhere. It is a river of animals that has worn its own channels into the concrete — the long, polished grooves you thought were erosion — and the river is always at the same height. The animals on the walls are not young, they are not growing. There is no birth you can see, no death. If one falls, others nudge it back toward the wall. The number, as far as you can tell by staying, does not change. Thousands, always thousands, moving slowly enough that the landscape as a whole does not move at all.

That is the equilibrium of Kell Hollow. Energy loops. The engines run and make heat and sound and no product. The animals eat the fungus that eats the animals. Water condenses on cold iron, drips, is licked up, is breathed out, condenses again. The rust does not spread past the stain it has already made. The fungal felt stops at the same edge on the wall, year after year. Nothing greens, nothing rots, nothing builds, nothing falls down. The light stays at late morning. The cool damp stays at the same cool damp. The loudness stays loud, without crescendo or fade.

You realize, standing in the middle of the station yard with animals clicking and clanking around your feet and a conveyor clanging empty behind you and condensation ticking in the pipes overhead, that you are not watching a place that is dying or a place that is thriving. You are inside a place that has finished — a small, sealed, industrial town that reached its final state and now holds it, perfectly, forever. It will be exactly like this tomorrow. The same herd will be in the same yard. The same puddle will be the same depth. The same bellow will answer the same clang.

And the overwhelming thing, the strange thing, is that this feels less like an ending than like a habitat. It works. It just does not change.
