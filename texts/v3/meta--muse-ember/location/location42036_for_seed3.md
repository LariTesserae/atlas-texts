**VARKESH**

There is no road up to it. That is the first thing that changes as you rise.

You ascend in a cable car, or on a geared railway that climbs in slow, patient stages, and the light does not get brighter as it should. It gets thinner, and more even, and more grey. The sun is a pale smear behind cloud, never quite a disc, never quite gone. Below you the world falls away into a white sea that is not water, only more cloud, and above you there is only the same cloud, closer. The air is cool against your teeth. Your breath shows. The wire sings.

Then the plateau breaks through.

It is not a mountain in the finished sense. It has not been softened, or cut, or given time to settle. Its edges are still the edges of the break that made it — vast slabs of black basalt and pale granite laid up against each other at sharp angles, their faces fresh and unweathered, their corners unrounded. There are no river valleys here because no river has yet had the patience to find one. There is no soil in the way lowland soil accumulates. There is only raw stone, still faintly warm in the deep cracks where the uplift has not finished cooling, and scree that has slid once and will not slide again for a very long time.

The mountain is still emerging. That is its chief activity. And Varkesh has been built on top of it while it does so.

The city is not one place. It is a region. From one high point you can see the next district miles away — a cluster of towers on a neighboring peak, a foundry with its tall stack unmoving in the damp wind, a housing terrace cut flat into a slope, stringing between them all the low catenaries of cableways, power lines, hand-ropes for walkers, conveyor bridges. Between those settlements there is nothing for long stretches except plateau: empty, luminous with wet stone, patched with moss meadows. The sparse is part of the plan, if plan is the word. You cannot build densely here; the ground will not hold the same shape twice, the wind comes organized, and space is needed for the work itself. So the city sprawls vertically and across distance, a town-or-valley-size network with great silences between its parts. You walk a street, the factories stop, the lamps stop, and for two hundred meters there is only fog, wet rock, and the sound of your own boots — then another street begins, as if continued by a different hand.

In the streets it is unmistakably urban and unmistakably industrial, even at a distance. Riveted steel apartment blocks, ten, twenty stories, banded with walkways and washing lines that never quite dry. Layered construction everywhere: a new brick façade built three inches in front of an older brick façade, scaffolding left in place because the building inside is still inhabited and the scaffold has become a trellis, a pigeon house, a storage cage. Cranes stand over rooftops, not lifting, only holding, their hooks swaying a fraction in the wind. Rails loop around entire districts at different levels, never quite level. Vent towers, bell towers, water towers on iron legs.

The engines never stop. They turn slowly. Great flywheels in the foundry district complete one revolution a minute and you can feel them in your feet through the wet stone. Looms weave, in slow rows, the heavy canvas for condensation nets. A boiler house sighs, a winch grinds, a distant stamp falls — and returns, a second after, as if the mountain itself has repeated it. Noise is constant, but it is not sharp. At this height, in this cool damp air, sound carries too far and arrives too thickly. Wind passing through the scaffolding of a half-built tower makes a chord you can hear from the next peak. Ravens shout. Bells are rung to mark shift changes all across the plateau, out of time with each other, and the echoes come back before the bells are struck again.

Water, in Varkesh, is a rumor that dampens everything. There is no rain in the ordinary sense, not often, and no river to drink from. The plateau sits in the cloud rim, and moisture condenses on cold stone, on iron, on wool, on skin. Everything is damp to the touch. Copper wire nets are strung between poles like sails to comb mist from the air; they drip all day into long troughs, into cisterns, into bottles that people fill at public taps and carry home. Taps drip, roofs drip, the undersides of bridges drip in a fine, patient rain that does not soak you so much as settle. A few sheltered hollows hold real pools, black and still, where the slabs intersect just so, and there algae makes them green and frogs sing at night in a loud, wet chorus. But mostly Varkesh lives on condensation, and the whole city smells faintly of wet iron, hot oil, ozone from the generators, and whatever is cooking in the canteens.

And in that damp, on stone that has never been eroded smooth, life is absurdly lush. Because nothing washes away and nothing decays quickly, vegetation does not so much grow as accrue. Moss forms lawns, then meadows, then soft green forests a few inches high across rooftops, across abandoned platforms, across the shoulders of statues. Ferns burst from drainpipes and gutters, bright and frilled. Vines climb the scaffolding and the factory walls, heavy with small alpine apples in walled gardens tucked between casting sheds. Lichen paints the raw slabs in rust, gold, and a blueish green that glows in the dim light. It all grows slowly — a tree fern you can watch for a season and see no difference — but it is everywhere, because it is never cleared, never storm-torn, never scoured. In Varkesh, a door left shut for a year may be laced shut with moss by spring. People open it anyway.

The fauna is louder and more numerous than the people. Between the sparse towers the air is crowded. Swifts pour from the vent stacks at dusk in black curtains, screaming as they rise. Long-eared bats flicker in the halos of the always-on lamps. Ravens nest inside active cranes, build their twigs around the machinery, and steal bearings while the machines turn. Herds of shaggy, sure-footed goats graze the factory yards and the slag fields, cropping moss off hot metal in the morning. Mice fill the grain stores. Moths — thousands of them — swirl round every light, so that at night the lamps have actual weather around them. In the condensation tanks, small blind fish have established themselves, palely circling. A mountain fox will cross an empty district at noon as if it owned the street, which in a practical sense it does.

That abundance in emptiness, that industry on a newborn peak, does something to perception. Reality here is not broken all at once, it is broken slowly, patiently, like everything else.

Distances lie. On a clear damp day a tower two kilometers off looks close enough to touch, and you walk toward it for an hour only to have it withdraw. Compass needles near the foundries wander, not wildly, but they drift and settle and drift again, as if north is undecided. People navigate in fog by rope handlines stretched between lamps, and sometimes the ropes meet a lamp that is not on any map, burning in empty space above a void, lighting nothing. Shadows do not fall where you expect them: the dim sun is above, but a second shadow is cast upward from the cloud sea below, so that at midday you can stand in two skyshades at once. Birds cast their shadows not on the plateau but on the clouds beneath you.

The factories make things. That is still their purpose. But what they make is strange by lowland standards because the city is trying to manufacture permanence for a place that has none yet. Varkesh casts enormous anchor plates, counterweights, and brake-wheels — parts for a mountain that is still rising, to slow it, to pin it, to persuade the strata to settle. The castings sit in yards waiting for a geology perfect enough to receive them, and while they wait, moss grows over them, birds nest in their bolt holes, and new rock slowly forms around the slag heaps, trapping a wrench, a boot, a dropped lunch tin, in a thin skin of stone that gathers, millimeter by millimeter, in the damp. Fossils are being made in real time, and everyone is aware of it and continues to work around them.

Things happen twice here, with a delay. You hear a boiler sigh in the factory below, and a moment later you hear it again, from above, though nothing is above but cloud. A bell rings in a district you can see, and its echo answers before the rope is pulled again. Children born in Varkesh learn to wait for the second sound before they answer, as a habit of politeness.

Nothing erodes. That is the underlying rule from which all the rest follows. A dropped bottle stays sharp and clean on the wet stone for years unless someone picks it up. A mistake in a street alignment persists for decades, grass growing up through it, people walking around it. Buildings do not crumble; they are overgrown, re-dampened, re-riveted. History does not wash away, it layers — rust over paint over moss over dust over more moss. This is why the city feels at once ancient and unfinished. It has been building for the better part of two centuries and the mountain beneath it is still younger than the city built on it.

At night — and night is not very different from day, only darker and the lamps more distinct — the whole plateau becomes a constellation of points across a black, wet plain, each district haloed in its own fog. The sound does not lessen. The flywheels keep turning. Somewhere a swift screams at a moth, somewhere a goat steps carefully between slabs that have never been rounded by weather, somewhere a new slab, somewhere out in the emptiness, rises a centimeter and no one hears it except as a tiny, deep click in the stone under their feet.

Varkesh is not finished, and it is not falling down. It is accruing.
