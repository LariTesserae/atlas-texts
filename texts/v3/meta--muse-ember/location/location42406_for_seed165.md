First there is height, and then the sense that height is not a peak but a refusal to come down.

You are on a broad, open plateau that tips slightly towards a horizon that never quite arrives. It is the size of a valley, or a small country of ridges, but it is divided so sparsely that size does not help you. Between one stand of stone and the next there may be a hundred paces of nothing. Between one living thing and the next that nothing again. The place is regional in its reach — you can see its far edges on clear parts of the dim — but it feels empty at human scale, as if most of it has been quietly omitted.

The light never resolves. It is not night, and it is not day. A pale, flat glow comes from all around at once, as if the sun is below the world but annulling darkness from beneath, or as if you are inside a thick shell of ice that diffuses everything to grey. There are no shadows that move, and almost no shadows at all. What shadow there is lies fixed to the north sides of stones, colder than the stone itself, and stays the same length whether you have been here an hour or a day. Colors are leached to stone colors: slate blue, basalt black, rust where lichen has colonized a sheltered face, the dirty white of old snow that has forgotten how to melt away.

The air is thin — at this elevation your breath comes short without effort, your ears keep popping for no reason — and cold in a deep, radiating way. It is not winter cold with wind. It is the cold of rock that has never warmed. It comes up through the soles of your feet. And yet it is damp.

Not wet. Damp in pockets. Moisture arrives as condensation and stays where it condenses. In the hollows between stones, mist has fallen and not dissipated; it lies in shallow basins with a hard, almost liquid edge, and you can step over it or stoop into it and find it cold and beaded and still. On the undersides of overhangs, water gathers in slow pearls that lengthen, tremble, and then retreat back into the rock before they can fall. There are tarnlets, no larger than your hand, frozen at the surface but liquid a millimeter below, that mirror the grey sky and keep a different reflection — a brighter sky, with the sun in it that is not there above.

These pockets recur without pattern, in an otherwise dry expanse of gravel, scree, and shattered slab. Everything else is dry to the touch, but the damp is everywhere in promise. Occasional rain is implied rather than experienced here; the cloud deck hangs below the plateau, a sea of white that you are standing above, and from it moisture rises and condenses on anything that sticks up — a stone, a tuft of grass, your sleeve — and then, instead of dripping, it simply remains, a film that neither evaporates nor increases.

The ground is old in a way that has nothing to do with age as decay. Erosion is established, settled. Edges are not sharp, because frost and wind took them long ago, and slopes are not crumbling, because they have come to rest. Scree has sorted itself and stopped. Cliffs are not falling; they are balanced at a state they have held for a very long time. The gravel is smooth in the hand, wind-polished, and it does not shift underfoot into new arrangements. If you make a mark, it will stay — a footprint, a scratched line — for as long as you are here, unsoftened, because nothing here works to erase it, and nothing here works to work at all. The whole surface has the look of something weathered into its own final form and then left alone.

Vegetation honors that spareness. There is almost no relief of green. Instead there are crusts: black and rust-orange lichen that follows the seams in the stone, each patch maybe the size of a coin, a dollar, a hand, appearing a few meters apart and then not again for a long stretch. In the cracks where damp collects most reliably, there are cushions of moss, dark olive, frozen mid-growth, their tips beaded with ice that does not melt. Very rarely, a single wiry tuft of grass has taken hold in grit, one tuft to a square meter, bowing outward in the same posture all the time, as if it grew that way once and has been holding the pose ever since. No forests, no meadows, no season. Just the thin distribution, patient and accidental.

Animal life is rarer still, and mostly inferred. A pale, ptarmigan-like bird sits on a stone so perfectly colored that you first notice it by the inconstancy of its eye. It does not move while you watch. It may not move at all in any sense you can measure, so slow is its metabolic bargain with the cold. Elsewhere there are signs that have not faded: droppings white on black rock, a feather pressed into ice, tracks that are crisp as if made this morning and yet are, you realize, weeks old and will be crisp tomorrow. You may, in a whole day of traversing this openness, hear one call — a short, stone-like whistle that seems to come from below the ground and above the sky at once — and not hear another. The fauna, if it can be called that, has thinned itself to almost an absence, not because the place cannot support it, but because the place does not invite it to be active.

The built things are in keeping with this thinning. They are scattered and small, and they take more effort to find than the stones. A low ring of unmortared rocks, ten stones in a circle no head-height, with nothing inside it. A single slab leaning against another, forming a shelter that shelters nothing. A line of cairns, each no higher than your knee, running across a flat for fifty meters and then stopping, aligned to nothing you can see — not to the dim sun, not to a water pocket, not to each other in any honest geometry. There are faint marks, too: parallel scratches on a vertical face, too regular to be frost, too worn to read. No metal, no glass, no device, no path. Whatever made them used stone and patience and then became stone and patience itself. They have settled into the erosion, half absorbed, as if the plateau grew them slowly and then forgot.

And over all of this — over the emptiness, the damp pockets, the single tufts, the rings — is the sound.

It is loud. Not in bursts. It is a continuous, low roar, as if the mountain is resonating. At times it is like surf heard from very far below, a boom that rolls through the gravel and up through your boots and into your chest. At other times it is higher, a creak and grind of ice under pressure, mineral singing, a humming that makes your teeth ache. It does not swell or fade with gusts, because there are no gusts. It does not come from a single place. It comes from the ground under you and from the ridge opposite and seems to arrive a fraction of a second before you look in its direction. When you cup your ears, it gets louder, and emptier somehow. The emptiness in fact amplifies it. With so little to absorb it, sound travels for miles across the plateau, bouncing between settled slopes, stacking on itself until the air itself seems to be vibrating, even though nothing in the air is moving.

That is where the wrongness begins to declare itself, not as invention but as breakdown.

The ridges duplicate. Not obviously — not two mountains side by side in cartoon fashion — but there is a second ridge, fainter, offset by a thought, running parallel to the real one and overlapping it like a double exposure. When you blink, it aligns. When you turn your head, it slips. A stone twenty paces away, when you turn back, seems for an instant to be also at your boot, small and near and impossibly detailed, and then resolves to distance again.

The horizon folds. On the far side of the plateau, the grey glow closes into a ring that is too perfect, too close, as if the world is a bowl and you are in the middle of the bowl looking out at its rim a short way off, and at the same time the curvature is unmistakable and far. Distance lies in both directions at once.

Your own shadow — when you remember you should have one — falls two ways: one faint, proper shadow, and another, slightly paler, that points in a direction the light cannot account for and that does not move with you. The water pockets reflect a sky that is not the sky above you. In them you can see a weak sun that never breaks the surface fog.

And through all of it, nothing changes. This is not stillness as pause before movement. It is equilibrium. The mist pockets do not lift. The roar does not crescendo into collapse or subside into silence. The lichen does not spread. The bird does not take off, though your eyes swear it breathes. The tracks do not fill. The temperature does not fall at "night" — there is no night to fall into — nor rise. The dim light does not brighten or redden. If you stay, the place will not progress. If you leave and somehow return, it will be exactly as you left it, down to the pearl on the underside of the overhang, lengthened but not fallen.

It is a world that has weathered into a state where weather can no longer alter it, high and cold and damp in isolated pockets, loud with a sound that has no source, empty except for the occasional crust of life, the occasional ring of stone, and the persistent, quiet insistence that reality here has come slightly unstitched — and that unstitched state is itself perfectly, permanently settled.
