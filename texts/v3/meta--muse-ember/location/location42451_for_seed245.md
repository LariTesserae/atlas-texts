It isn't a plain, exactly. At first it looks that way — low, flat, wet,
going out to a horizon that never quite settles — and then you notice
your feet are not on ground.

The floor is made. Everywhere the eye lands, under the thin film of
water, under the steps, is laid work: broad slabs of pale slag and
fired clay set edge to edge, courses of brick running in one direction
for a hundred paces and then, without explanation, turning ninety
degrees. In places it is concrete-like mortar that never quite set,
soft enough that a boot leaves a print that fills, slowly, with warm
water. In places it is tile, and ceramic shard, and hammered sheets
of dull copper and tin lapped over one another like scales. Where
cracks open — and they open often — you can see the lamination
underneath. Layer on layer on layer, all of it artificial, all of it
still curing.

A low hill here is not a hill. It is a stack of terraces, large and
crudely rectangular, a metre or two high, their edges still sharp
because nothing has had time to wear them down. A valley is a shallow
dish left by a mould. The whole landscape is less eroded than it is
accreting, coming into being out of its own first draft. Fresh work
appears in the middle of old work. Edges are raw, chalky, wet. Things
do not fall apart so much as slump, sigh, and give up their shape
halfway through.

And then, far away, there is something built. Then, for a long
distance, nothing.

That is the other impression that keeps returning: sparseness inside
vastness. The made floor extends farther than you can usefully walk,
farther than weather should be able to hide, farther than the dim
light can comfortably reach. But most of it is empty. Kilometers of
unoccupied flooring, marked out with pegs and chalk lines and rows of
bricks laid two-deep and then abandoned, tools left where a hand set
them down. Clusters interrupt the emptiness at irregular intervals,
like islands, like sentences with long pauses between them.

A wall, maybe two hundred metres long and three metres high, standing
alone, plastered on the inside face and only outlined on the outside.
A courtyard whose far side is finished and white, whose near side is
still a scaffold of lashed poles and hammered struts. A roadbed
raised a hand's breadth above the floor, going straight for as long
as you can see, then ending in a neat pile of bricks, as if the idea
of it had run out. A stair in the middle of nowhere, twenty steps
going up to air, the top step still damp and therefore, every so
often, there is another step. You do not see it happen. You look
away, you look back, and the stair is taller.

Everything is on this hand scale, blown up to a size hand work was
never meant to hold. You can read the tools in the surfaces. Hammer
dents the size of a fist in copper sheeting. Finger grooves in the
clay bricks. Chisel marks in stone blocks too big to be dressed by
chisel. Mortar smeared by palm. Nothing is machined, nothing is
engineered in the slick sense. Rope pulleys, copper rivets, stone
hammers, crucibles where slag was melted in small, smoky fires that
have long since gone out and will, presumably, be lit again somewhere
else. It is primitive making carried out on a planetary floor, and
therefore it is everywhere unfinished. The builders — if builders
there ever were in any number, if they are still here at all — made
the whole world out of the things you could make with your hands,
and the world is too big for the hands to finish. So it stays in the
state of being made.

The heat is not from the sky. The light is dim — a smeared, amber-
grey light, as if through dirty glass, the sun only a pale coin
behind a low ceiling of vapour that is itself unfinished. Shadows are
soft and short and unsure of themselves. But the heat comes up from
below, through the soles of your feet, through the floor where the
curing slabs are still warm from their own making. The air near the
ground shimmers faintly. At knee height it is hotter than at head
height, and head height is already too hot to breathe comfortably.
It is the heat of a kiln after the door has been opened and left
ajar, the heat of wet stone in a closed room, the heat of work
happening somewhere under the surface, perpetually.

Wetness is the other constant, and it does not mean rain, though rain
of a sort passes over. The air is saturated. It condenses on every
cooler surface and drips, incessantly, from the undersides of
overhangs, from the edges of slabs, from the courses of those great
weeping blocks that stand like boulders but are only misfired bricks
the size of houses, weeping warm water down their faces in dark,
vertical streaks. The floor is never dry. Footprints fill. Puddles
lie everywhere, thin and warm and alkaline, tasting of metal and
clay if you touch them to your tongue. They are not natural pools;
each one sits in a shallow, deliberate dish, or in the hollow left
when a slab was lifted and set down again.

Steam rises in slow columns from cracks and from places where the
floor has recently been revised. It does not dissipate so much as
hang, gathering, moving sideways in a weather of its own. Over one
sector, forty kilometres away, you can watch a small storm assemble
itself — a darkening, a rising of dust and steam, a slanting fall of
grey water that never quite reaches the ground where you stand. The
whole vastness has room for separate weathers. Here it might be still
and dripping. There, the air could be scouring grit across the
floors until the tiles ring. Then it clears, and the light changes,
and the cleared sector looks different. New lines have been laid.
Something has been taken up and something else put down while the
storm was over it.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest part of the turbulence.
This is a world in a fever of remaking, and yet the sounds are
small, muffled, infrequent, the way sounds are in a thick, wet,
absorbent place. Drip... drip... separated by long pauses, from
places you cannot locate. The tick of a cooling slab, like a
fingernail against a tooth. A soft, enormous thud, somewhere far
away, the sound of a wall deciding to slump — you feel it more in
the floor than in your ears. A steam crack sighing open, then
closing. Very distant, irregular hammering, a heartbeat of work that
does not keep time, and then nothing for a while.

There is no life in the familiar sense. No grass, no leaf, no
insect, nothing flying, nothing tracking across the wet floor except
your own marks, which fill in behind you. What there is instead is a
low, fungal ecology of second-hand things. Pale veils and grey-green
crusts crawl over abandoned scaffolds and the insides of unfinished
rooms, eating the fibres and glues and organic binders in the mortar
and helping the clay to fall back to dust. Rust blooms on iron and
copper like flowers, spreading in perfect, artificial rings. Salt
blooms in white cauliflower clusters where the heat has dried a
weeping wall. Spores ride the steam, fine as flour, and settle
everywhere, and nothing eats them. The only movement that is not
weather or water is the movement of things made to move: small,
blind clay shells on hammered legs, or no legs at all, dragging
themselves from pile to wall with a single brick that is, somehow,
always the same brick. They go about their primitive business with
no malice and no understanding, and if they stop, they stop in the
middle of a step.

Reality does not hold still here, and not in a dramatic way. It
forgets.

A doorway in a finished wall opens onto the same courtyard it opened
onto yesterday, but yesterday the courtyard had a fountain basin in
it, and today the basin is a pile of tiles and the fountain is only
implied by a damp patch on the wall. A room has more interior than
its exterior could allow — you measure the outside with long strides,
you measure the inside with longer ones, and the numbers refuse to
agree, and after a while you stop measuring. A puddle reflects a
tower that is intact and white, while the tower itself, twenty paces
away, is half-built and open to the sky on one side. The reflection
is not wrong, exactly. It is earlier. Or later. The light is thin
enough that you cannot tell which is the copy.

Things are easier to build here than to keep. A bridge thrown up
over a drainage channel in the morning is, by evening, a low sill
that water runs over, steam rising where the mortar is still soft.
A wall that was unplastered when you passed it on your way out is
plastered when you come back, and the plaster is cracking already in
the heat, and the crack is dripping. Measurements drift. A corridor
you counted at twelve steps is fourteen on the return, and the extra
two steps are wet and smell of fresh clay. Nothing is hostile. It is
simply that the world is not finished being decided, and the
decisions keep revising themselves.

Once in a while a whole cluster goes through a fever. The heat
rises perceptibly. The steam columns thicken. Bricks soften at the
edges, enough to slump when no one is watching, enough for a
scaffold to collapse upward into a different arrangement. A platform
that was a platform becomes a shallow trough full of warm water, and
the water, in time, becomes a platform again, with the trough's
outline still visible in the surface like a memory the floor cannot
shake. Creation and destruction are not opposites here; they are
phases of the same damp labour, and the labour is turbulent, and the
turbulence is quiet, and the quiet is hot.

That is the rule, if there is a rule: nothing is natural, nothing is
finished, nothing stays. The floor will cure a little and then be
lifted. The water will never leave; it will only change its place
and its phase, condensing on a wall, pooling in a dish, rising as
steam, falling again as that grey, slanting rain over a sector far
away. The heat will rise when work is being done, and work is always
being done somewhere. The light will stay dim, because the sky is
still only a vapour held up by habit. And in the long, sparse
intervals between the built things, the empty made floor stretches
out, wet and warm and expectant, waiting for the next attempt to
give it a shape — and the attempt, like all the others, will be
crude, and huge, and damp, and already beginning to forget itself by
the time it is done.
