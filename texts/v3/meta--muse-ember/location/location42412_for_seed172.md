You do not arrive at it across a landscape. You arrive at it through a landscape.

The door is a service hatch set low in a sloping wall of poured ceramic and steel, one of thousands of identical hatches on the outside of a manufactured peak. It is high up — the air outside is thin enough to make your ears pop and your lungs work too hard, the sky is washed and close, and the wind comes at the walls on a constant pressure that never finds a place to break. From the outside, the peak looks like a mountain that was poured rather than raised: terraced, sharp-angled, and new. Up close you can still see the seams of the moulds and the crisp stencil marks on the retaining fins. Nothing has softened. Nothing has had time to rust, crumble, or settle into the color of the place.

Inside, it is smaller than you expect. That is the first strangeness. All that elevation, all that structural mass, and the world you enter is a pocket — a single complete interior, roughly a hall, embedded in the crown of the thing like a seed in a stone. It is a landscape contained entirely within a room, and a room that is a landscape if you stay long enough.

The cold meets you before your eyes adjust. Not a chill brought by a draught, but a settled, structural cold that lives in the floor plates and the handrails and remains there no matter how long the engines run. Frost feathers the inner glass of the high slits and stays exactly as it fell, needle by needle, without melting or spreading. The light, when it comes, is ordinary: a normal, white-yellow daylight from those same slits, steady in color and angle. It does not move across the floor. It does not warm anything. It is the light of a place that has been lit once, perfectly, and left that way.

And the sound. The sound was already there before the hatch opened.

It is deafening in a way that has no weather to it. Not a roar that builds and breaks, not a clang and echo and silence. A constant, industrial pressure of noise, as if you were standing inside a mill, inside a jet on a test stand, inside a crowd of engines all running at the same marked speed. It gets into your teeth and your sternum. You cannot hear your own boots. You cannot shout and be understood by the person standing next to you; you would have to put your mouth to their ear and still be guessing at the words. The walls themselves are part of the instrument — the outside wind is drawn down through a labyrinth of intake ducts around the chamber and forced across tuned resonators in the ceiling, while below, the engines themselves give off their own flat, electrically clean hum. Together they do not fluctuate. There is no beat, no rise, no fall, no distant lull. A minute sounds like an hour sounds like a year.

It is only after a while that you notice the machines are not, in any visible way, doing anything with it.

### The ground

There is no ground. There is a floor engineered to look like a ground.

It undulates in deliberate, low terraces: broad pads of pale ceramic-concrete grooved for traction, trenches three fingers wide filled with cable, ridges of heat-exchange fins rising six feet high like black basalt columns, cliffs formed by the vertical faces of switchgear cabinets and stored turbine housings. Everything is made. No rock, no soil. Where a natural hillside would have scree, this has cut bolts laid in rows. Where it would have a riverbed, this has a dry channel for coolant pipes — sealed, double-walled, humming faintly through their metal, carrying a purple oil and a thin, dry lubricant mist that makes the air taste of hot metal without ever quite warming it.

The materials are young in the way a factory floor is young when it has just been commissioned. Edges are razor sharp. Paint is unchipped. Warning labels are perfectly legible. The mineral-gray dust that lies in the grooves between plates is the same color everywhere, as if it were sprinkled from a single bucket, and it does not drift; the air inside never learns to move. There is no erosion here, only the *appearance* of a place that was cut yesterday and will look identical tomorrow, next month, in a decade. Erosion has been designed out. Decay has been designed out. The whole interior exists in a frozen equilibrium: everything at its peak condition, nothing allowed to pass it.

And it is sparse. For all its volume, most of it is empty. You can stand in the center and see a hundred feet of unoccupied floor, a single row of pillars far away, a lone cabinet, a lone planter, a lone overhead crane parked mid-span with its hook hanging still. The machines are not crowded together the way a working factory would be. They are scattered at measured intervals, like exhibits, like a landscape that has been thinned by an invisible hand. The emptiness is not absence; it is the main material. The sound fills it completely, so the silence you would expect from all that space never arrives.

### The living things, and the un-living

There are no animals. Not at the high slits where birds should be turning in the thin wind. Not in the shelter of the machines where rats, insects, spiders would make a home. The cold, the noise, the sterile dryness have made it uninhabitable to anything that has to eat, drink, mate, or hear. It is a pristine absence that feels, after a while, like a rule of the world rather than an accident of it: nothing with eyes or wings or a pulse is permitted to finish its life here.

There is vegetation. Sparsely.

In planters sunk flush with the floor terraces — one here, one thirty meters away, one farther still — are tufts of a cold-adapted scrub, gray-green and stiff, no higher than your ankle. They are clones of each other. Each mat is the same radius. Each leaf cluster repeats the same pattern. They have clearly been bred for this: for no water, for constant light, for constant cold, for a level of noise that would kill most living tissue. The mouth-dry air gives them nothing to drink, and they do not need to drink. They feed on mineral vapor and light alone, and having been selected to do so perfectly, they do not grow. Year after year they remain at the size they were trimmed or engineered to be. They do not flower. They do not shed. They do not turn. They tremble only a little, in place, under the vibration of the floor — a shiver that never becomes a sway.

It is the only softness in the place, and it is soft in the way a well-made model is soft. It makes the rest of the strangeness harder to deny: a world where even the plants have been brought into equilibrium.

There is no water anywhere. No drips from the pipes, no puddles, no streams, no condensation except the frost that formed when the outer skin was coldest and has never melted since. Your mouth is dry after ten minutes, not from exertion but from the quality of the air, which is dry in the deep, mineral sense: stripped, recycled, old. Everything that in another landscape would be changed by water — rust, rot, rot's smell, the rounding of a cliff, the slump of a slope — simply cannot happen. The steel stays bright. The concrete stays sharp. The dust stays dry.

### The rules

After a while, you begin to understand what kind of place this is by what never happens in it.

The light never changes with time of day. The sound never changes with load. The temperature never rises when an engine passes its peak or falls when a door is left open. No machine ever cools and no machine ever overheats. A conveyor on the far terrace carries an identical, finished engine housing in a perfect loop, arriving at the same point, in the same position, making the same faint click against its stop, forever. A fan spins with a blur that is always the same blur. The production cycle, if there ever was one, is stuck at its most efficient instant — a single eternal moment of industrial perfection, repeated without wear.

Time has no gradient here. Nothing is earlier or later. Nothing is new and nothing is old, because everything was made new at once and has been held there.

That is why the place feels so young and so unnervingly preserved. A natural high place would be carved by wind and ice into softness. This one was carved by other hands, recently, and then withdrawn from the process. It is a pocket landscape not so much built to last as built to *not change*.

You leave by the same hatch, and the outside wind is somehow quieter than the inside, which unsettles you more than the noise did. The peak stands in its cold, bright, normal light, sharp and new against the sky. Behind you, through the sealed skin, the roar continues at exactly the same pitch it had when you entered, and will have when no one is there to hear it — a deafening, cold, dry, empty music for a world of sparse green tufts and perfect, unmoving machines, high up, hidden, and never, ever, different.
