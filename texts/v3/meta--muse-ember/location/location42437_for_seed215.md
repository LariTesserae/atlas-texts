**The Vessik High**

At first there is only the damp.

It gets into your boots before you have walked a hundred steps, welling up through the peat in cold sheets, beading on stone that weeps even when it has not rained. The air itself is wet, not in the way of mist on a hill but as a habit, as if the sky were constantly condensing and forgetting to fall. Your breath fogs. Your fingers go numb holding the strap of your bag. This is high country - not a peak, but a plateau lifted far enough that the lowlands to the south are a rumor of haze below a hard horizon - and the cold here is a normal, everyday cold, the kind people comment on while they wait for a tram.

The ground is unfinished. Rocks are sharp, unsoftened. Cliffs shed fresh scree that has not yet had time to round, and the river channels are still arguing with the land, shifting their beds weekly, abandoning one gravel bar for another a few meters over. There is no deep soil. Under the thin, spongy skin of moss and peat there is raw basalt and shale, still coming into shape. In places the ridge is literally still rising, a few millimeters a year - too slow to feel, fast enough that survey stakes have to be re-driven, and the city has learned to build *with* that rise rather than against it.

That is the second thing you notice, after the vastness. And vast is the right word. The plateau does not end so much as repeat. You can stand for an hour and watch a weather system make its mind up a hundred kilometers away: a curtain of rain walking eastward in a straight line, cloud shadows racing over moor, fog standing up in one valley while the next stays clear. The light is ordinary daylight, pale and even, the kind you get at high latitude in late spring, and under it everything is grey-green and wet stone.

Vegetation is sparse, and determined. Tufts of sedge no higher than your ankle. Colonies of lichen on the north sides of rocks. A kind of dwarf birch that never gets to be a tree, holding its leaves close to the ground against the wind. They grow not in meadows but in lines where water gathers and where the stone beneath them is old enough to have held still. Most of the land is bare moor, peat cut by puddle and runnel, rocks sweating.

And yet, impossibly, it is full of life.

The sky is never empty. There are stoop-hawks in loose spirals, small dark birds that dive and seem to vanish into the grass and then rise again with something wriggling. Ground-nesting plovers call once, miles apart, and are answered once. In the shallow sheets of water - for this is a marsh-edge at altitude, a whole upland where water lives near the surface - frogs and newts are thick as spilled seed in the warmer days, and the insects rise in clouds around your head whenever you stop moving, a living weather of their own. The shaggy moss-calves graze in herds that move like a slow tide across the plateau, eating not grass, which there is barely enough of for them, but the wet biofilm that grows everywhere: on fence posts, on warm stone, on the sides of buildings. They are not endangered or precious. Someone has to shoo them off a platform.

The city is here, too. Not in the way you expect a city.

Vessik is urban, unmistakably - it has districts, names, transit lines, markets, regulations about noise after ten. But the density is low to the point of loneliness. Buildings are spaced kilometers apart, each one large, each district an island in the moor. You can walk for an afternoon between a housing stack and the next maintenance tower and see no one, then come upon a cluster of a dozen structures, lit, occupied, with a shop and a school and a tram stop, and all the wet streets between them, and then nothing again but moor and birds.

The buildings are made of materials that do not quite behave. Their stone is pale and seamless, without joints or rust or stains, and when a delivery cart clips a corner the mark knits shut by morning. Their windows do not so much reflect the light as hold it. The condensation collectors that stand in every district have no pipes, no fans you can see, no moving parts at all; they simply stand in the fog and the fog thickens on them, and then runs clear into cisterns, and the surfaces stay a few degrees warmer than the air around them for no reason the engineers of the last century could write down. The trams have no wheels and make no sound except for the soft tick of cooling. The roads re-route themselves around a new gully rather than being repaired. At night, after a heavy dew, a new floor will sometimes be there on a tower where yesterday there was only a roof. Nobody remarks on it. The residents of the Highlong district complain about the damp in the stairwells and the moss-calves on the platform, and about a construction front - a slow, silent advance of piled basalt plates that is building a new ridge to anchor the plateau against sliding - that is going to be loud next season, though it so far is not.

That is the quiet of the place. For all its motion, it is mostly quiet. The vastness swallows sound. Water drips from eaves in a slow, irregular rhythm. Wind moves through the sedge with a sound like paper. A stoop-hawk screams, once, and the echo does not come back. The tech does not hum. The construction fronts move without engines. Even the abundant fauna seem to call sparingly, as if saving their voices for the long distances between them.

And it is active, constantly. This is not a static moor. The migration herds cross in a season-long procession. The river sheep, the hawks, the midges - there is a pace to them, a predator-prey tempo visible if you sit still long enough: a hawk stoops, a plover flutters up, a calf herd shifts, a cloud of midges lifts from a warm collector like smoke. Overhead the weather does its own work, independent of the world below the plateau. And under everything, the land itself is still being made: new stone exposed, new channels cut, new ground printed and anchored by the city that lives on it, so that erosion here is not a wearing away but an emerging - a place only just coming into being, year after year, wet and cold and on the way up.

You left your footprints in the peat when you arrived. By the time you look back, water has risen into them, and the wind has bent the sedge over them, and the light has changed a degree.

It is a city built on a mountain that is still being built.

It expects to be rained on, and fogged, and grazed, and walked over, and to go on becoming after you have gone.
