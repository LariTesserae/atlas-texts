Paludra is not entered so much as descended into. There is no hill to climb, no viewpoint. The land simply flattens and stays flat, and then you are in it, and the rim all around you is low — a levee, a line of old quarry rock, a wall of brick and slag heaped long ago and now weeping thin sheets of water down its face. From anywhere in the town you can see that rim. In twenty minutes on foot you can walk from one edge to the other, but you will not be bored, because between the edges there is mostly not-town.

The light is wrong for the heat. The air presses on the skin like a held breath, hot and wet and heavy, well over thirty degrees even in the dim. The sun is only a pale, milked disc somewhere behind a permanent low ceiling of steam and cloud that re-forms every afternoon. On a clear hour it comes through the leaves of the sky-trees and the grey-green haze over the settling ponds, and so everything is lit the way the bottom of a shallow river is lit: diffuse, greenish, shadowless. Every lamp in the town is on. They buzz faintly in the day and make small sodium pools on the wet concrete at night, but they never really overcome the twilight. Things look older and softer than they are.

Water is at the surface here, and it stays. Not in a river — in everything. The ground is a low sponge of silt and cinder and clay that never fully dries. Puddles join into sheets, sheets into slow canals cut along the old street grid, canals slip under warehouse floors and out the other side. If you stand still in a flooded plaza you can watch the water table rise a finger's width over an hour, then fall again. The walls of the boiler houses sweat. The rocks at the edge of town weep. Tin roofs gather condensation overnight and shed it in a constant, random rain long after the sky has cleared, so that the leaves are always dripping and the gutters are always running and your shoulders are always damp, though technically it is not raining.

Paludra is urban, but sparsely, as if planned for a larger city and then decided, halfway through, to be smaller. Wide boulevards of cracked concrete lie empty for blocks at a time, flooded to the ankle, their painted lines long gone under silt and algae. A block of brick workers' houses, three storeys, window glass gone, will stand alone in a grove of banana and fern and elephant-ear, separated by a hundred paces of open marsh from the next block, which might be a single riveted-iron factory shed with a chimney still breathing white. The density is air between presences: empty lots where a building was demolished sixty years ago and the lot has since become a pond covered in duckweed; a pump station no bigger than a house, fenced with chain-link gone orange; a stretch of elevated pipeway carrying steam from one boiler to the next, its struts soft with moss.

All of it is old in the settled way. This is not a fresh ruin and not a new town. Paludra has been in this state for a long time and has found its balance. Bricks slump a foot into silt and stop. Door thresholds form tide-lines of black mud, year upon year, so you can read the floods. Paint has blistered and peeled and been replaced by lichen. Rust runs in long vertical weeping streaks down every metal face, feeding the green. Timbers are silver and spongy. A warehouse wall leans against a tree that has grown through its foundation and holds it up. Collapse is exactly balanced by growth, so nothing falls fast anymore. Everything is weathered to the same damp, olive-black, biofilmed sheen.

The vegetation is lush to an almost violent degree. It is not the lush of a tended garden. It is hot-house lush, opportunistic and upright and blindly fast. Vines have laced the factories shut, not destroying them but sealing them, like bandages. Ferns uncurl over the rail lines inside the machine halls. Trees have learned to use the infrastructure: hollow trunks have become water towers, their roots have cracked a pipe and now drink directly from a boiler's condensate line, their crowns have spread over a roof and hold the tiles down with their weight. In the sumps, beds of reeds filter the outflow. In the yards, fast-growing creepers cover copper wiring and conduct — not electricity, but slow, living current. At night the flowers open under the lamps and release a sweet, fermented smell, and the fans that were installed to keep the forges cool now do the work of wind and insects: they blow pollen from one side of town to the other in great visible, clanking rhythms.

There are no animals. That is the strangest part, and the part you notice after the heat. No birds in the trees, though the trees are perfect. No insects in the air though the air would support a cloud of them. No frogs in the ponds, no rats in the warehouses, no mosquitoes even, in all that standing water. A few snails in the dampest corners, perhaps, and the microscopic life that makes the hot mud smell of rot and iron and cooked leaves — but nothing with a face, nothing that calls. The town has been left to the plants and the machines, and the plants have won the ecology by default. What would have been predator and prey is now pump and pressure, transpiration and condensation, growth and rust. The leaf litter composts so fast in the heat that the low ground is perpetually warm to the touch, faintly steaming, releasing small sighs of gas through the mud.

And it is loud. Improbably loud for a place so empty and so dim. You hear it before you see the source and you hear it from everywhere at once, because the town is small. The pumps keep a heavy, arrhythmic heartbeat, cycle on, cycle off, water-hammering in the mains. Every seventeen minutes or so a boiler vents with a shriek of steam that sets the tin roofs ticking and the leaves shivering. Belt drives slap in the open-sided machine sheds. Rain — the real rain, at three o'clock every day, committed as clockwork — drums on corrugated iron so hard you must shout to be heard, then stops as suddenly, leaving only the drip from the roofs. Chains drag in the flooded canals. Sheet metal expands in the heat with a soft, continual popping that sounds like knuckles cracking all over town. The electricity hums. The city runs itself in loops, purposefully, though what it produces now is mostly heat, movement, and more water moved from one place to the next and back again.

Its days have rules that are not quite natural. Morning is condensation: every cold surface is beaded, the brick feels cold to the palm despite the air, and mist hangs at knee-height over the ponds and does not lift until the first boiler vents. Midday is pressure: the heat peaks, the mist boils away, and the plants transpire so hard that the air gets thicker even as it dries. The vines visibly advance — a tendril can be watched finding a new handhold in an afternoon. Afternoon, the storm: black-green clouds drop down into the rim, rain falls vertically and then sideways, the canals rise, the pumps work overtime, and the whole town smells of ozone and wet iron. Evening is redistribution: the rain stops, the lamps come fully on, the machines settle into their night cadence, and the water that fell runs in and out of the buildings through channels cut for it generations ago, cooling the foundations, feeding the roots in the walls. Night is when the growth is loudest, when the creak of expanding trunks and the tick of cooling metal compete, and it is also, paradoxically, the most dim — the lamps make halos in the steam and show you nothing beyond twenty paces.

Paludra was built to condense and to process, to turn hot wet lowland into power and clean water for somewhere else. The somewhere else stopped taking it. The boilers kept their timers. The pumps kept their cycles. The plants took the pipes for veins and the chimneys for light wells and the settling ponds for nurseries, until the line between built and grown became a question of maintenance. It remains a town because its systems remain, a small, open, steaming bowl of brick and leaf and iron where water circulates endlessly, where heat is stored in mud and released at night, where everything is slightly too wet, slightly too hot, slightly too dark, and where the only living sound is the machinery breathing for the things that have no lungs at all.
