The first thing is that it is dark, and warm, and you can hear your own breathing too clearly.

Not cold dark. Not the dark of a cave. This dark has the temperature of a stone left in a closed room all day. Even in the deepest shadow the air against your face is warm, and dry in a way that makes your mouth feel papery within minutes. There is no dew on anything, no damp in the hollows, no fog when you exhale. Whatever moisture a world might have had has long since left, or never arrived. The air is thin and clean and smells faintly of hot iron and warm dust, like the inside of a kiln that has been off for a long time but not yet cooled.

You are very high up. That is not a metaphor. The plateau does not so much end as give up, far off, in cliffs that fall away into a lower haze you cannot see through. The horizon is wide and low and far, as if the world has flattened itself to show you how much drop there is around you. Your ears notice the pressure. The sky above the haze is a smoked violet-black, almost night even though there is a sun — a small, dull, reddish coin that never climbs very high, and whose light comes sideways and weak through dry dust. Stars are already out. Colors do not declare themselves until you are close to them.

And the ground is not finished.

That is the strangeness of the place, and it is a quiet strangeness. Everything here is sharp because nothing has had time to wear it down. There is no soil. There are no rounded stones, no smoothed hollows, no river-cut anything. Water has not carved this. Wind has barely touched it. It is raw geology, still in its first draft: broken plates, fresh fracture faces, glass still clear enough to cut your hand, pumice light as bone. When you step, your footprint stays crisp in the ash, edge for edge, until the ground itself swallows it.

For this ground is lastingly, slowly moving. Not eroding — accreting. The whole tableland is rising, in great shallow steps, like something breathing under a blanket. Eastward, a low ridge that was only a suggestion when you arrived is, an hour later, unmistakably a ridge — a long swell of black basalt pushing up through pale ash, shedding dust as it comes, dust that falls in slow, silent curtains and settles and hardens. Westward, a field of pillars is sinking, not toppling, sinking pillar by pillar into soft ground that closes over them like wax. A crack a hundred paces away sighs open with a faint ticking, shows a dull orange line deep inside, warm air rising from it without sound, and then, over the next minutes, glistens shut. No crashes. No roars. All the turbulence here is muffled and enormous, happening too slowly to be loud, like construction on a planetary scale with all the machinery buried.

The air, despite the movement, is mostly quiet. That is the rule that holds the rest together. You listen for insects and hear none. You listen for birds, for water, for wind in something, and hear none. What there is: the crunch of glass grit under your boots — a sound too bright for this dimness, like stepping on frozen bubbles — the occasional deep, soft boom from far below the plateau, felt more in your sternum than heard, the sifting of ash off a newly risen crest, the faint tick-tick-tick of cooling rock contracting. Then longer periods of nothing, in which the silence itself feels warm.

The materials are mostly variations on black, grey, rust, and bone-white. Obsidian shards lie in drifts. Basalt comes up in columns and in broken slabs stacked at angles that look almost deliberate, though you can see the crystal grain still running fresh through the breaks. There are pans of fine ash, pale as dried clay but never wet, cracked into polygons by heat alone. There are fields of rust-red sinter, iron baked into a crust, rising in blistered terraces. There are white plates — silica and gypsum-like, dry to the core — that lift at the edges like curling paper. Colors appear only up close: amber in the glass, a dull ember-glow in the deepest cracks, the blue-black sheen on a newly exposed fracture where light catches it for a moment and is gone.

Nothing lives here in any way you recognize as living. There is no green anywhere — not in the crevices, not on the north faces, not around the warm cracks where you would expect something tenacious. There are no tracks, no droppings, no nests, no calls. What there is instead is a mineral ecology, if you can call it that. Black crusts creep in lichen-like patterns across the warm plates, advancing a fraction when the stone heats and pulling back, very slightly, when it cools. Fans of crystal grow in rosettes, spiky and translucent, that open toward heat the way a plant might open toward light. They make the ticking sound. They are not plants. They do not eat. They simply grow where the chemistry and temperature allow, compete for the same narrow seams of warmth, and are buried, intact, when the ground rises over them. The newest, highest growths are the palest and cleanest, because they have been exposed for the least time. Height here is youth.

Between these presences there is a great deal of emptiness. That is the second rule. The scale is open — regional, not a valley you can take in at a glance. You can walk for a long time across an ash pan with nothing rising from it except the occasional shard, and the pan goes on to a far terrace, and the terrace to a broken ascent to the north that is higher still and newer still. Then, suddenly, a cluster: a field of black spires throwing long, unmoving shadows; or a single smooth dome; or a jumble of tilted slabs. Then more emptiness. Sparse not because the place is poor, but because it is still so widely spaced in its making — individual features flung apart across a surface that has not yet filled in.

Scattered across that sparseness are the built things. There are not many, and there is nothing among them that hums or blinks or shows a tool mark. A ring of upright slabs, half-buried now because the ground has risen around their bases. A low rectangular enclosure of stacked stone, one wall missing, the interior filled level with ash that has calcified into a clean floor. A forked pillar standing alone like a doorway to nothing, too regular, too vertical to be dismissed quickly as chance, though its edges are unweathered and its base is being slowly swallowed. They are not ruined in the way old things are ruined. They have not been weathered or broken by use. They have been unmade by being outgrown. No one tends them, nothing moves in them, and there is no metal, no glass manufactured, no device of any kind. If they were made, they were made before making had tools, or making itself here simply arrives at shapes that resemble shelter and monument because heat and fracture and weight have the same habits everywhere.

Stay long enough — and the light does not change enough to tell time by — and you understand that this landscape is not a scene but a process slowed just enough for you to stand inside it. A pillar field in the west collapses in slow motion, each column settling a hand’s breadth and stopping, as if the world is testing its weight. A new fault opens in the center pan with a series of tiny, dry pops, and warm dust rises from it in a plume that does not disperse, it hangs. On the far ascent, fresh rock faces are appearing where yesterday there was only ash, their surfaces mirror-bright for a little while before the dim light takes them. Creation and quiet unmaking, at the same rate, in the same silence.

Dark, and warm. High, and still being raised. Scarred but unweathered. Empty of water, of plants, of animals — and yet not empty. What little light there is reflects off the pale ground and comes back up at you, so you see best what is nearest your feet. The ember lines in the cracks provide the only real color at a distance, a dull, persistent glow that does not flicker like fire.

Eventually you realize your footprints from an hour ago have already been half-filled by grit sifting off a rise, not worn away. Nothing here wears away. Things are covered, lifted, sealed, and left sharp underneath for whatever comes next. You are standing, as far as you can tell, on a world in the act of deciding its own shape — a tableland at the top of things, in darkness that holds heat, in silence while the ground continues, patiently, to build itself around you.
