You hear it long before you see anything.

Not a crash, not a storm. A low, deep roar that comes up through the soles of your boots and into your teeth, steady as weather. On the inclined adit down it is already loud enough that you have to shout to be heard, and by the time the tunnel levels out and the rail screws underfoot give way to something soft, the sound has multiplied itself against stone until it fills the body. Somewhere far ahead a crusher is turning, slow and unhurried and inexorable, throwing a thump into the air every few seconds. Ventilators whine. A conveyor chain clatters in a rhythm that never quite repeats. Water falls, too, drop by drop from the high black roof, each one ringing like a hammer on steel because there is nothing else to absorb it.

The descent is cold in a way that deep places usually are not. Two hundred meters below a wind-scoured plateau, the air should be warming. Here it does the opposite. The shaft brings down surface air, and the air that settles in the bottom is heavy, near freezing, saturated, and still. Your breath fogs. Frost feathers the inside of lamp housings and the bolts on the rail. On the tunnel walls, where the rock has been cut, the limestone is pale grey and sharp as a fresh break. Edges that would have been worn smooth in an old cave are still keen. Drill marks are legible. Fallen blocks pile where they landed, unweathered, their faces bright. Nothing down here is old in the way it looks at first. It was opened in living memory, and the cold has kept it young.

Then the adit opens, and the place stops being a tunnel.

The chamber is not a room. It is a basin, kilometers long, perhaps eight hundred meters across, the ceiling a dark vault eighty meters up that you can only guess at in the dimness. You have stood in valleys on the surface that felt smaller. Light comes from above in one place only — the big working shaft behind you, a pale rectangle that has already narrowed to a coin — and from a sparse geography of work lamps. Amber strips along a rail line. A greenish wash at the far bend where the shredder plant sits. Isolated points where a hut window is lit. Between them, nothing. The basin is open, and empty, and it goes on past the reach of the lamps into a darkness that is not quite complete because moisture in the air catches and scatters what little light there is.

And the floor is not floor.

It is plant. Not soil over rock, not a meadow laid on stone, but a felt of it: root, rhizome, moss, and rotting leaf pressed into a spongy mat ten, twenty, in places forty centimeters thick. It covers everything. It climbs the lower walls like a green tide. It wraps the feet of the rails. It has entered the foundations of the small buildings and lifted their thresholds. You step off the ballast and your boot sinks a finger's width and comes up beaded with water; the mat sighs under you and springs back slow. Where the lamps are strongest, the green is vivid. Where they are not, it fades to grey-green and brown, moss and liverwort and sedge and dwarf willow, all low, all patient, all alive at a pace set by cold and by the little light allowed them.

That is the first correction the place makes to your eye. It looks total, because there is no bare ground to be seen. It is sparse, because almost nothing stands up. There are groves, but they are scattered like islands. A clump of birch saplings, no thicker than wrists, twenty meters across, then two hundred meters of flat root-plain before the next clump. A stand of poplars planted once in rows — you can still find the spacing if you look — now thinned by decades of shade and neglect, their trunks wrapped in moss to a man's height. Ferns in a damp hollow under a drip line. Lichen crusting the tops of boulders in pale maps. The density is that of tundra and of a lawn that has been left to go back to itself: everything alive is plant, and most of that plant is thin, creeping, and widely spaced in height if not in cover.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no stream. There is no river cutting the mat, no lake in the low places. Instead, the cold air holds moisture the way a cold pane holds condensation, and the basin weeps it out. Every leaf carries beads. The rails sweat. Your coat sweats. From the roof, where warm surface air meets cold stone in the shaft's downdraft, comes an intermittent rain — not weather, exactly, but a ten-minute patter that starts on the leaves with a sound like fingers drumming, gathers on moss, slackens, and stops, leaving the mat darker and heavier. Pools form only where a hollow or a rut in the mat holds the drip, shallow and brown and still, quickly filmed over with green. The gutters alongside the rail are supposed to catch it. Mostly they carry a trickle the width of a thumb, pumped back up to the surface by a small pump house whose door is locked and whose light blinks, patiently, green.

It is a working place, after all. That is the second correction. The silence of the empty stretches is an illusion; the deafening part is the truth underneath it. The plant is not a ruin. The shredder at the far end runs around the clock, taking in bales of root mat cut by a harvester that crawls the basin on caterpillar tracks at walking pace, slower than walking, its drum turning and turning, loosening the felt ahead of it. Behind it a conveyor lifts the cut material in a slow brown river, and the river travels on overhead gantries that sag and creak, and at the end it is dropped into a crusher that makes the ground tremble through your boots. The crushing is what you felt in the tunnel. It is not violent. That is why it is so hard to bear: it never hurries, never stops, never varies. A generator hums against the west wall. A locomotive — small, electric, scarred — waits idle on a siding with its headlamp off, dust on its roof, moss on its coupling. Pipes run from the generator to the huts and vanish into the mat where roots have found the joints and sealed them with green.

The built things are scattered the way the groves are scattered. A maintenance shed at the junction, its corrugated wall rust-streaked, a pair of boots outside the door stiff with frost. A relay hut halfway across the plain with a stove pipe cold and a window boarded from inside. A rest cabin where someone has left a kettle, a chair, a calendar open to a month three years past. A crane, half sunk, its boom resting on a pile of limestone blocks that have not yet been cleared. Silos at the plant end, half buried where the mat has grown up around their bases over a decade. None of it makes a town. None of it tries. Each structure is a small, faint mark left on a very large green surface, and the surface is taking them back at its own speed, which is slow enough that you could miss it in a lifetime of visits — a millimeter a year on a door frame, a root hair in a cable duct — and fast enough that a man who worked here in his twenties would not recognize the basin when he returned at sixty.

Light, when you are away from the lamps, is the dimness of a winter afternoon under thick ice. The lamps make it worse, in a way, by showing you how dark the dark is: pools of amber on the mat where sedges are almost chartreuse, greenish glare at the shredder casting long shadows from the gantry legs. Everything beyond is a subtle gradation — grey rock, darker green, black air — and your eyes adjust and adjust and never quite finish. Shapes keep resolving. A boulder is a sleeping machine. A grove is a wall. A change in the mat's color is a path.

Life, other than plant, is rare to the point where you start to notice its absence as a presence. No birds — the roof is too high and too dark, and the way down is too long. No insects in the open. Around one lamp, a cloud of fungus gnats rises and settles. In the pump house gutter, a single drowned moth. A mouse, perhaps, has run a line through the sedge from the shed to the grove and back; you see the flattened path, not the animal. Once, high up, something moved across the shaft light — a raven from the surface, lost and already turning back. The only warm-blooded things down here regularly are the work crews, and they move through the basin the way you do, in parkas, in ear defenders, at a shuffle, appearing as lit windows and headlamps that pass and are gone. The noise has driven almost everything else out, or the cold has, or the light has. Whatever the reason, the ecology is plant, and the machines that tend the plant, and the few animals that tolerate both.

The rules of the place are the rules of slow things kept cold. The mat must not dry, or it will burn and take the roof with it and the smoke will have nowhere to go. The roof must not warm, or the condensation becomes a storm and the mat drowns and rots black. The harvester must not go fast, or it tears the felt and the basin takes a decade to knit itself shut. The crushers must not stop for long, or the backlog of cut root on the conveyors freezes solid and must be blasted loose. Everything is paced to the growth of moss in dim cold air — a crop cycle measured in seven years, a grove measured in a generation, a wall of limestone still sharp because the water that would round it never quite gathers enough force to move. Change here is not dramatic. It is the gradual thickening of the mat under your boots, the slow leaning of a gantry, the rust that blooms and is washed and blooms again. The deafening sound is the only thing that does not age.

Stand a while on the root-plain between the groves, where a lamp is a hundred meters behind you and the shredder is a kilometer ahead, and the basin reasserts itself. The roar is in the floor. The air is wet and cold on your neck. The mat, utterly covering, holds no footprint for long; each step you take is erased by its slow spring back, and the pearl of water on the sedge beside your boot trembles and reforms. The ceiling is a rumor. The far wall is a rumor. Somewhere, a drip strikes a rail and rings, clear as a bell in the general thunder, and is gone.

It is not a strange place. There is nothing here that could not be explained by a survey map, a set of engineering drawings, and a seed catalogue for cold climates. It is only strange in scale, in the patience of it, in the way an industrial landscape has been laid down inside a hollow in the earth and then handed over, almost entirely, to plants that grow at the speed of stone — and still, underneath the green, the machines go on turning, slow and deafening and indifferent, as if they intended to outlast the mat itself.
