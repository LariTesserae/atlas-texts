The light here is ordinary, and that is the first surprise.

The sun rises and sets at a familiar pace, warm and white-gold and without hurry, and at noon it falls on the stone as plainly as light falls anywhere. But the air is too thin up here to make a proper blue. Near the horizon the sky is washed pale with dust, and then it deepens, and then, while you are still squinting against the glare, it passes into a hard indigo-black overhead. Stars are not extinguished by day. They wait in the black, patient, and you can see them if you shade your eyes. The light is normal. The medium it travels through is not.

There is no water.

You discover this not by seeing a desert, but by failing to find the usual signatures. No glint. No humidity on your tongue, which dries and sticks to your teeth before you have walked an hour. No smell of wet rock, no mud, no drip, no stream carving a green line through stone. What should be a riverbed is a braided channel of fine grit, pale as flour, and it moves — not with water, but with wind, grain by grain, building little deltas of dust in the basins where the ground dips. It is the only hydrology this world has ever known. Rain is a rumor here. What falls from the sky in a storm is not liquid. It is hail of sand, and a slow, red-brown sleet of dust that hisses on hot rock and is gone.

The world keeps what little water it has sealed away. It is bound in crystal, locked in the deep strata, and carried, in minute, precious quantities, inside living bodies. Nothing is allowed to run free. A creature does not drink from a pool. It takes hydrogen from the air and from the minerals it eats and makes its own moisture inside itself, and keeps it. When it dies, it does not rot. It dries. It becomes a husk, a mineral ghost that will lie on the plain for a thousand years before the wind files it down.

You are at an extreme height, though it does not feel like a peak. The Akar Shelf is not a mountain in the spiky, young sense. It is a plateau the size of a continent's heart, lifted miles above a lower world that is itself lost in a permanent sea of ochre haze — the Lower Dust, which never settles. From the lip of the Shelf you can look out across a drop so complete that the air below simply stops mattering. There are other shelves far off, other high lands, and they look like islands in that haze, but to cross between them is a matter of days of walking across empty plain.

The temperature is warm, wrongly warm for the altitude. The rock is dark basalt and ironstone, black and rust-red and ochre in broad, settled bands, and it drinks the sun. Even in shadow the stone radiates heat through the soles of your feet. At night the ground gives it back slowly, so you can sleep on the warm slabs and not notice the change for hours. Thermal splitting has done the work erosion does without water: day after day the sun bakes the surface, night cools it, and the outer skin flakes away in plates no thicker than a coin. The wind then does the rest, grinding those plates to powder and using the powder to sandblast everything else.

This is why the land looks established, in its own final state. Nothing is sharp for long. Peaks have been beveled, pillars have been turned into balletic columns an aeon of abrasion has polished, arches have been cut until they are thin as eyelids. It does not look devastated or new. It looks *settled*, as if the Shelf has been negotiating with wind and heat for so long that it has reached an agreement. There are gorges, yes, but they are wide, soft-shouldered, filled with aprons of talus that have fused in the heat. There are dry falls, where a dust-river pours over a cliff and hangs for a moment in the updraft before scattering.

Because there is no green anywhere. Your eye keeps searching for it out of habit and fails. There is no plant life at all. What takes the place of a meadow is a mineral bloom.

Across the sun-warmed faces of stone, especially where the industrial towns vent their waste heat, there spreads a dark, branching tracery — stoneblooms, the locals call them, though they are neither stone nor flower in any simple way. They are filament networks of metal oxides and silicate crystals that grow a centimeter in a century, catching sunlight and charged dust and fixing them into a slow chemistry that no plant could survive. From a distance they look like lichen, or like rust patterns, or like veins. Up close they are a forest in miniature, fractal and black-red and copper-green, and they are the first link in everything. They are food by being ground down. They are fuel by being rich in oxides. They are, in the absence of water or leaf, the producers.

And on them, astonishingly, life is abundant.

It only seems empty at first because almost everything that lives here has learned to be inconspicuous, and because everything moves slowly. The Shelf is vast, and its population is spread across that vastness so thinly that you can walk for a day and see only three individuals, and yet the Shelf as a whole teems. Sparse, but abundant. Much space between presences, but presences everywhere if you learn to wait.

The oldest residents are the grazers. From a distance you will mistake them for boulders, and for a long time that is correct. They are multi-ton, low-slung beasts with carapaces like etched basalt, patterned in natural lace where the stoneblooms have colonized their shells and then been scraped clean again. Their legs are pillars of fused mineral and living tissue. A herd — thirty of them, spread over a square kilometre — will be working a cliff face, scraping the bloom with rasping mouths, moving perhaps ten metres in a year. Their heartbeat can be felt through the ground if you lie down, a pulse days apart. When one shifts its weight, dust falls from its flank in a slow curtain, and a whole flock of smaller things startles and resettles. They do not sing. They communicate by the ground, by pressure waves and by faint thermal flashes on their flanks, and by the scrape of their own enormous bodies against rock. You hear them more than see them.

Over the warm updrafts above the cliffs hang the air-fauna. They are not birds. They are rays of translucent, heat-hardened membrane strung on filaments of chitin and glass, each as large as a sail, and there are hundreds of them at a time riding a thermal that lifts dust from the plain. They never land. They feed on the static charge that builds in the dry air, on sand motes, on the faint electrical exhalation of the towns below. Seen from below they are shimmers, moving in a slow, soundless ballet, so quiet that you notice them only by the way the light changes. Seen from above, if you ever get above them, they are like a constellation that has learned to drift.

And then there is the fauna of the cities.

The built world here is urban, but not in the way a dense city is. It is an archipelago. The industrial towns of the Shelf — foundry terraces, smelter hollows, electrolytic arrays, grinding mills — are separated by tens of kilometres of empty mineral plain, linked by elevated conveyor trusses that crawl across the emptiness on spindly legs, carrying ore at a pace that is almost geological. A town is perhaps a few thousand people and a great many machines, spread out in concentric terraces cut into a cliff or built up from slag. Everything is riveted, domed, half-buried against the wind. Nothing shines. Without water to rust it properly, metal does not corrode into orange rot. It develops a pale, chalky oxidation, a dry bloom that softens edges and makes even new machinery look ancient. Steam is impossible; there is nothing to boil. Cooling is done by radiant fins and by dry air blown through vast, resonant towers. Furnaces burn carbon minerals and electric induction and solar concentration, and they run on cycles measured in decades. A smelter is lit and stays lit for a generation, exhaling a plume of fine, dry smoke that does not dissipate but trails as a banner fifty kilometres long, so straight and so patient it looks painted on.

It is, for all that industry, mostly quiet. The thin air mutes everything. You hear your own breath, the whisper of dust against stone, the tick of cooling metal as a tower expands in the afternoon sun — a single tick that rings for a minute. You hear the wind when it finds the perforated sides of an electrolytic tower and plays it like an organ pipe, a low chord that you feel in your teeth. On rare occasions a bearing clanks, or a slab of slag slumps, and the sound travels across the plain with a strange lag, arriving long after you have seen the dust move. The inhabitants — small figures in sealed, dust-proof suits, moving unhurriedly between terraces — talk little. Their voices are muffled. Communication here is practical and patient.

The city fauna live in the heat. Stoke-wyrms, six-legged and scaled in dull metal colors, inhabit the slag channels and cooling ducts, eating carbon ash and excreting ingots of pure mineral. Flat, pale lizards bask on the solar mirrors and do not flee from you, because you are slower than they are and therefore not a threat. In the resonant cavities beneath the towns there are burrowers, eyeless and blind, that eat the stonebloom when it creeps into the tunnels and, in eating it, keep the foundations from being choked. The relationship is old and mutual: the towns were built to process the Shelf, and the Shelf's life has learned to process the towns.

Weather here is large enough to have a life of its own, cut off from the lower world. The Shelf generates its own wind systems, its own dust cyclones that are more like slow towers than storms. A dust tower will rise a kilometre high over a basin and stand there for weeks, turning almost imperceptibly, its top spreading into a veil. It will not rain. It will deposit. A storm that crosses the plain will take three days to cross your field of view, and will leave everything an inch thicker in grit and your lungs full of its taste — iron, salt, hot stone, a faint ozone tang from the electrical arrays.

Strangeness accumulates if you stay. Some of the cliffs are hollow in a particular way, and when the wind is right they sing at a pitch you can hear from two valleys away, a single note held for hours. Objects, long after the sun has passed, cast a faint second shadow — not a shadow, exactly, but a heat-shimmer where the stone is still releasing the day. At night, when the sky is properly black and the stars are mercilessly sharp, the stonebloom fields give off a very low, coppery glow, not light so much as visible warmth. The fauna gather then toward the vents and warm slabs, their thermal flashes pulsing in unison like a slow, distributed heartbeat.

Death, as said, does not rot. In the dry channels you will find herds of the mineral husks of grazers, centuries old, their shells still patterned, their legs still planted as if they merely paused. Their descendants scrape bloom from their ancestors' backs. The dust rivers slowly bury them, and slowly uncover them again.

Of all the things that make this place itself, slowness is the deepest. A landslide here is a rumor for next millennium. A town's slag heap grows a metre in a decade. A grazer crosses a valley in a lifetime. The Shelf itself creeps, a meter a century, carrying its towns with it on a geological conveyor. Erosion has finished its work not by stopping, but by settling into balance. Everything is in its own state, weathered, worn, and continuing.

You leave, eventually, not because the place is hostile — it is warm, and lit, and strangely hospitable in its emptiness — but because you understand that you are a rapid thing, and this is not a rapid place. You walk back across the plain toward the next plume on the horizon, which is a town a day's walk away. The dust-river braids at your feet. A boulder shifts, very slowly, and a herd resumes its meal. Above, the sails drift in the thermal, silent. Below, in the haze, another world waits. Here, on top of everything, the stone keeps its heat, and keeps it, and keeps it.
