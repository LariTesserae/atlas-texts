It is smaller than it feels.

You can stand almost anywhere in it and see the rim. Not a sharp crater — the edge has been worn too long for that — but a low, rounded lip of stone that gathers around a single shallow bowl, as if the high ground had sagged and settled in on itself. Above it, the sky is close and ordinary, blue where the mist thins. Below it, nothing can be seen at all. The bowl sits high, well above the lower country, in the warm belt where cloud gathers and does not hurry away. Its whole extent could be walked in a few minutes. Its whole extent could also be taken in at a glance. And yet to be inside it is to lose any sense of an outside. Sound does not carry over the lip. Light does not come from any one direction. Weather does not arrive so much as condense.

The air is warm and full. It does not feel like rain, and rain, strictly, does not fall here. It forms. It forms on the undersides of leaves first, where you would not expect it, and on the north faces of stones, and, if you stay still long enough, on your own skin, your hair, the back of your neck. Everything is damp to the touch, but nothing is cold. The stone under your hand is warm from the sun and wet from itself. The soil gives in a slow, spongy way, like a mattress that has been wet for years, and releases a smell of green iron and old stone. Water is not in streams here, though there are them — thin, bright threads that sheet over rock and disappear into moss and reappear a metre lower — water is *near*. It is under the surface, pushing up in hollows, beading on clay, weeping from cracks that had stopped weeping a century ago and started again in a slightly different place. Puddles lie in the depressions between stones and do not evaporate. They reflect the sky, which is their only sky.

What there is, is sparse.

Long stretches of the bowl are almost bare: a pale, felted moss pressed flat, dark wet rock smoothed by patient water, black humus that holds your footprint for a long time before slowly filling. The emptiness is not a lack. It is balanced, deliberate-looking. And then, alone, a plant.

A fern the height of a person, its trunk no thicker than your wrist, unspooling one frond at a time over a decade. A clump of broad leaves, each one the size of a spade, glossy and dark, each one cupped so shallowly that it always holds a lens of water it never quite spills. A small tree with pale bark that peels in wet ribbons and a crown so open you can count its branches. Between them, twenty paces of moss and stone. Between them, nothing that moves.

That is the lushness: not thicket, not jungle, but enormity at intervals. When a plant is present, it is entirely present — saturated, dripping, fully grown in a way that suggests no one has ever hurried it or cut it or needed it. Its leaves hang heavy. Vines descend from it, not to the ground, but to a point a hand's breadth above the ground, where their root-tips darken and drink the mist and retreat again. One vine has been doing this for a very long time, growing and un-growing in place.

Nothing here is built, and nothing here is made, and yet the place keeps looking as if it were. Roots have braided themselves around boulders into tight woven rings, loop over loop, as if someone had knitted stone into a basket. Fungal husks grow hollow and hard as unglazed pottery, then split along a clean line. A snapped fern stalk feathers into a point. A crack in the dark basalt opens on a face as sharp as a knapped flake. Early tools, crude work, hand-craft — all of it echoing in growth. There is no maker to gather these things. They are what wet, warm, mineral growth does when it has the same simple materials for a very long time: weave, fold, cup, split, hollow.

The strangeness is not violent. It accumulates when you watch slowly enough.

Leaves do not fall. They thin. A leaf yellows into translucence, sags, and begins to dissolve where it hangs, turning to a clear slime that runs down its own stem and into the soil in a thread so slow you could miss a day of it. There is no leaf litter. There is no rustle. The plants reproduce without visitors. Flowers hang *under* the foliage, pale and downward-facing, opening a fraction in a morning and closing a fraction in the afternoon, letting their pollen fall straight into the condensing air like ash into water. Spores leave the ferns in a fine, visible weather that settles on stone and makes green ripples — pattern following pattern, season following season, without wind to hurry them.

There are no animals. Not a buzz over the puddles. Not a frog in the seep. Not a worm turning the humus. Not a bird, not a track, not a call. The silence is not an absence laid over the place like a lid; it is part of how the place works. Nothing needs to be pollinated, carried, eaten, or frightened. Decay is done by water and fungus and time. The moss eats stone, very slightly. The stone feeds the moss, by sweating. The whole small ecology runs on damp and patience.

Light is ordinary here, which makes it stranger. Midday sun, diffused by a permanent high mist, falls without glare and without heat-shock. Shadows are short and soft and the colour of wet slate. In the shadowed side of the rim, the moss is bluer; in the sun, the leaves turn a deep, almost black green where the water sits on them. Nothing is bright for its own sake. Colour seems to be for no eye at all, and keeps itself anyway.

Sound is the same. A drip forms on the lip of a stone. It swells for — you understand, after watching — a very long time. It hangs. It falls. The ripple it makes in the shallow pool below moves outward in a circle that takes minutes to reach the edge. Somewhere else, a leaf-tip releases its burden, *plip*. A thread of water ticks over rock. That is all. Even a step in the moss makes no noise worth keeping; the ground swallows it. You become aware of your own breathing because there is nothing else to attend to, and then you become aware of how quietly you are breathing, as if the place had taught you.

This is an old place in the way a face is old, not damaged. Nothing is sharp except the fresh cracks. Nothing is new except the water. The rim has slumped into smooth shoulders. Boulders have sunk to their waists in soil that was made by their own slow rotting of the things that grew on them. A fallen branch — there is one, and there has only ever been one that you can find — lies where it fell, halfway to humus, its bark perfect and its heart soft. It is not rotting quickly. It is rotting *steadily*, at the same rate the moss is creeping an inch a year across the stone, at the same rate the weep in the western rock will migrate two hand-widths in a lifetime.

Leave, and return after years, and it will be the same. Not unchanged — the drip that took an hour to form when you were there will have fallen a thousand times, the frond that was unspooling will be open, the pool will be a finger's width deeper in one corner. But you will recognise it instantly, and you will not be able to say why. It has settled into its own state and holds it: warm, wet, high, small, empty between its living things, silent except for water learning its way down stone, doing everything at the speed of condensation.

Stand in the centre once more before you go. The mist has come in a little thicker. Every surface is beaded. The sparse green shapes stand far apart in their pale moss, dripping in no hurry, keeping a place that needs no one to keep it.
