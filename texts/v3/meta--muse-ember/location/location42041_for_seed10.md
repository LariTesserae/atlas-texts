High up, the land has forgotten how to be sharp.

It is a plateau, but not a table. A mountain that died very slowly and was left, lifted and alone, after everything around it wore away lower. Its shoulders are broad and rounded, its valleys wide and tired, its stones polished to eggs and old teeth. Nothing here has a fresh edge. Rain, ice, root-pressure and time itself have worked on it for so long that erosion is no longer an event but a condition: this is the settled, final form of a very ancient place, and still it will not lie still.

The light is dim and undecided. The sun is a pale disc somewhere behind a cold, milky sky, never climbing high enough to warm anything, only to stain the mist a thin grey-blue. Colors have gone muted with wet and cold — olive, bone, black water, rust — as if the world were seen through a film of condensation. And the air is always condensing. Your breath fogs. Plants bead and drip even when it is not raining. Mist does not only fall from the sky; it rises off the bogs in slow columns, off the weeping cliffs, off your own sleeves. Everything is near the point of becoming water again.

Water is not in rivers so much as *in* everything, just under the surface. Step anywhere and the ground gives with a wet squeak, then a more solid sigh. The plateau is a marsh at altitude, a post-rain landscape that never finishes draining. Black pools lie between the hummocks, so still they seem solid until a ripple opens them. Streams are wide, braided, slow, threading through plant-mat for miles before vanishing under it. The cliffs weep. Pale stone sweats from a hundred fine fractures, water sheeting down in cold curtains, beading, breaking, running sideways when the wind finds it. In the hollows, slush holds its shape deep into what would be summer, and the bogs bubble — not from heat, but from a slow fermentation far below the surface, releasing cold gas that lifts the mat and lets it fall again with a soft pop.

And the ground itself is not ground. There is no bare earth to speak of.

Vegetation is the substrate. A living mat — moss, liverwort, frost-fern, lichen, root-fur, decaying ancestor upon living descendant — covers the plateau in one continuous, spongy skin as far as you can see. You walk on plant, not on soil. Where the mat is thin you can feel the stone through it, soft and cold as soap. Where it is thick it flexes, remembers your footfall for a minute, and breathes.

But density here is a trick of scale. For all that plant fills the space, distinct presences are sparse and far apart. You may cross a quarter-mile of empty, wet, furred gravel with nothing taller than your shin, wind combing the mat, and then come upon a single sentinel: a trunk-tree whose bark is plated like a boiler, its crown a slow-turning rosette far above you, drinking the dim light. Or a boulder entirely swallowed by a fruiting vine, the stone only visible as a lump under the green. Or a hollow stem arched over a pool like a bridge no one built. Or a cluster of piston-plants, thick as towers, their sides ribbed and scarred.

They are architecture without builders. Root arches weave terraces down the gentle slopes. Dead trunks become tunnels — you must crawl through a root-ribbed passage smelling of loam and ozone, coming out into a drip-lit chamber where water falls from the ceiling in rhythm. Living stems braid together into lattices over the bogs, shivering in the wind, humming. Nothing has been constructed. Everything has been grown, and grown for so long that the grown things have taken on the air of machines left too long in the weather.

Because this place *works*. It is industrial, not by hands but by nature running at full capacity for eons. In the iron-rich seeps, a race of smelter-roots drives down into the pale stone and extrudes amber resin that runs hot-sweet and then hardens into glossy lumps, smelling of hot oil and copper. They pool, crack, are eaten, are regrown. Elsewhere, whole districts of bog do nothing but distill: hollow fermentation chambers swell and contract in a slow, factory shift, exhaling vapor that condenses on their roofs and runs back in neat, cold rivulets. The piston-plants on the cliffs pump water upward, inflating with a groan, deflating with a hiss, keeping time with each other across distances so vast you only feel the rhythm when you lie down in the mat and let it come up through your bones.

The root-networks are electrified. After rain — which is to say, always, eventually — mycelium fires in waves across the surface, faint blue pulses traveling in straight lines, branching, meeting, sparking at the junctions where moss is thin. They are too regular to be weather. They look like production lines. And on the windward ridges, the turbine-stalks stand alone, sparse as smokestacks, their seed-heads turning slowly, clicking, flinging spores in white plumes that the wind carries off like steam. No factory building. No artifact you could name. Just a cold, wet, vegetable industry that has been running since before there was anything to call it industry, and will go on after the shapes of tools are forgotten.

Life is abundant, but it too arrives in pulses, in crowds, in sudden departures.

For hours the plateau can seem empty, only wind and dripping and your own boots in the mat. Then the ground begins to tremble. The shaggy grazers come over a rise in a band a mile long maybe, animals built like walking compost heaps, their backs plated with moss and small flowering plants that ride them into new country, their legs stilt-long, their breath steaming in great clouds. They do not rush. They advance like weather, shearing the mat, turning bog to churned black, leaving behind them a greener, trampled highway where the plant immediately begins to reweave.

Flocks come and go across the dim sky, blackening it for ten minutes with wings and screaming, then gone, and the sky seems larger for their absence. Under the mat, the anthill-energy never stops: worms like slow bellows, blind burrowers, beetle-trains hauling grit, all of them churning the ancient compost, aerating the industry below. In the pools, things slap and roll. In the weeping cliffs, small grey birds nest in the drip and shriek at everything. Abundant, yes — but nowhere dense in the way a forest is dense. The emptiness between is part of the design. Life must have room to gather speed.

And it is loud. Not constantly, but in assaults. The wind over the treeless mat makes a high, sustained note through the hollow stems, and then drops. A bog will collapse with a cannon-pop audible half a mile off, throwing spray. A piston-plant will sigh and slam its reservoir shut with the sound of a gate. The grazers bellow, a sound that starts in their chests and seems to come up from the ground itself. Ice in the slush cracks like a rifle course. Water dripping into a root-chamber echoes and re-echoes until the rhythm becomes a drum. When the storm hits — and it can hit in minutes, clouds dragging their bellies across the plateau, rain coming sideways, then sunless snow — the whole place roars, and then, just as suddenly, is left dripping and ringing.

Nothing stays. That is the other rule.

The dynamic here is turbulent in the deep, tired way of old things: not sharp, but relentless. The mat heaves in slow swells, as if the plateau were breathing through its skin. Areas rise a foot overnight on gas and water pressure, crack along a weeping seam, flood, and settle again flatter than before. New channels are cut in a day by a single hard rain, and old channels choke with fern and vanish. Islands of vegetation assemble in the bogs at dawn — a raft of root and moss, green and flowering — drift, lock, become ground for a while, then break apart and are eaten by the water they made. Storms build and unbuild the surface. Plants walk, a root-foot at a time, migrating toward the seeps. Entire colonies die back to black slime and are replaced, in the same season, by something that remembers how to be them but is not quite.

It is creation and destruction at the tempo of decay, which here is still very fast. An ancient place does not mean a finished place. It means the same processes have been going so long they have become the landscape itself.

Reality, thin at this height and in this wet cold, breaks down at the margins.

Shadows pool under things that cast none. A pool will reflect not the sky above it but a warmer, sunnier day, with a horizon you cannot see when you look up — and if you bend close, your own reflection lags a breath behind you, older, or younger, in a coat of frost that is not yours. Echoes arrive before the sound that made them, crossing the open mat out of order: you hear the drip, then the pop, then the groan that caused both. The horizon repeats. On clear-enough days you can see the plateau stretching in layers, range upon vegetated range, the same weeping cliff pattern outlined again and again until it seems less like distance than memory. Some plants grow in rows so regular the pattern is almost a grid, lichen and smelter-root making straight seams across boulders, as if the stone itself were being stamped. And water, on strong wind days, will run uphill for a stretch, confused by the pressure of the air and the suck of the thirsty mat, before remembering better and turning back.

You learn not to trust simultaneity. A herd may pass in perfect silence at noon and you will hear their bellowing at dusk, from the opposite slope, in empty air. Ice may melt and refreeze while you watch it, the crack re-healing. The dead plant-matter underfoot is so thick and so alive that it seems to remember being other plants, other animals, other seasons; organisms here carry the shapes of ancestors in their bark and shells long after those ancestors should have been composted to anonymity. In a world this old, the past does not erode away. It settles, and thickens, and becomes the floor.

And yet, for all the churn and noise and growth, there is an exhaustion in it. This is a landscape in its late stage. The grazers’ young are fewer than the old. The sentinel trees put out seed that is mostly hollow. The smelter-roots extrude less resin each decade, though the decades are long and hard to count. Everything is deeply aged, near-flattened by its own weight, settled into a final, soft form — and still moving, because the final form of this place *is* motion: wet, cold, breathing, fermenting, unmaking itself into itself.

From any rise, the scale opens. The plateau does not end at a wall. It fades into other high, wet, plant-skinned reaches, into mist that never fully lifts, into a region of the same sparse giants, the same black pools, the same weeping stone, repeated until distance and dimness swallow pattern. There is no path through it, no shelter, no mark of a hand. Only the mat under your feet, squeaking and sighing, the drip on your neck, the slow blue pulse traveling somewhere below, and the sense that you are very small, walking lightly, very late, across a world that is still industriously, turbulently, and beautifully coming apart into green.
