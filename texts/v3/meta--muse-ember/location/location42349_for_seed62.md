You notice the cold first, and the brightness, and the damp, all at the same time — three things that do not belong together, and so none of them quite settles.

The air is cold against your face even in full sun. The sun is close here, high and hard and white, bouncing off everything pale until the whole valley seems lit from below as well as above. And yet your sleeves are wet. Your boots sink into something springy and waterlogged. A leaf brushes your cheek and leaves a bead on your skin that does not evaporate. There is no rain. There has not been rain in any amount that would make a puddle. The damp is alive: it lives in the air, in the moss, in the cupped leaves, in the undersides of stones.

This is a new place. That is the first rule of it, and everything else follows from it. The land was lifted recently, geologically speaking — a broad table of rock torn up along a fault and left standing higher than the country around it. The cliffs at its edge are not rounded. There are no smooth pebbles, no worn boulders, no patient valleys carved over millions of years. The rock splits in clean, black, glassy faces. Gullies are still cracks, widening a finger's width a year. Scree underfoot is angular and sharp, fresh as broken crockery. In the hollows between ridges, thin mineral soil has begun to gather — dark, almost sterile, rich in iron — and it is there, in those pockets, that the green has taken hold with absurd speed.

For the green is everywhere, and it is dense beyond reason. It should not be this lush on a young, high, dryish plateau. But it is, layer upon layer upon layer. First the ground: a mattress of moss thirty centimeters thick in places, so saturated that a footprint fills slowly with water and then closes again. It is emerald, and grey-green, and in the brightest places almost white-silver where the light bleaches it. Through it push ferns, waist high, fronds uncurling in slow spirals. Through them, shrubs with small twisted stems and leaves like polished spoons. Through them, a sparse canopy of low trees — not tall, not graceful, just many-stemmed and gnarled, their branches hung with curtains of hanging moss and lichen and epiphytes that grow on anything that stays still long enough to be noticed.

Your view is constantly blocked and constantly opened. The scale is regional — a whole valley nested between ridges, with a shelf here, a shallow basin there, a gorgelet no wider than a cart-track, another ridge beyond that and then another, fading into bright haze. But at eye level, the density is near-claustrophobic. Leaves touch leaves. Vines bridge from tree to pipe to rock to rusted machine. You push through a living wall and step into a clearing the size of a room, and the clearing is also full.

Water, here, is not a feature. It is an event, and a very small one. It arrives as fog that rolls up from the lower country in the early morning and shreds itself on the ridges. It arrives as dew, every dawn, so thick that the moss drips from its own weight and the fern fronds carry lines of silver. It arrives, perhaps once a month, as a brief vertical rain that lasts three or four minutes, rattles on the metal roofs, and vanishes into the moss before it can run anywhere. There is a tarn in the lowest basin, but it is not a lake — it is a sheet of water a few centimeters deep spread over pumice and white sand, appearing in a wet week and disappearing in a dry one, leaving a pale ring and a smell of stone. The plants do not drink from streams. They drink the air. Their leaves are sponges. Their roots are shallow mats. The hanging moss on the trees is a net for mist.

And because water is so scarce and so shared, the industry — and there is industry here, unmistakable once you learn to see it — is built around catching it.

The built things are scattered, not clustered. No town. No city. A shed with a corrugated roof bright as a mirror. A concrete pad cracked by frost. A silo on the far ridge, half-sunk in moss. A relay pole with blue glass insulators. A water tank lying on its side, open like a tube, full of fern. A cable spool, a length of pipe that runs uphill through the ferns and disappears, a small stone hut with a door that still swings on oiled hinges. They are single buildings, occasional artifacts, faint marks if you look from the ridge — and up close, they are dense with meaning.

Each node is a small factory, and together they are scaled production. This is not craft. It is mechanized, electrified, patient. One collects condensation with vast, sail-like nets of fine mesh strung between poles; the nets are draped in moss now, and they work better for it. Another presses the moss itself — harvested on a cycle of years — into dense insulation mats, stacked under tarps in a shed that smells faintly of hot oil and green. A third is a loom, automated, weaving fiber from a cultivated fern that grows in near-perfect rows hidden under the wild understory; you would never know the rows were rows until you saw the conveyor belt running straight through them, waist-high, carrying cut fronds on a slow, silent belt into a press house. Wires run like vines between them. Pipes run under the moss, warm to the touch in one place where an engine idles. Generators sit on paved squares, bolted down, humming at a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear.

And that is another rule: the silence. It is absolute in a way that takes time to register. At this altitude, in this bowl, the wind is broken by the ridges and then by the dense green, so the air rarely moves. The moss and the saturated leaves absorb sound the way they absorb water. There are no birds. There is no insect hum. Even the machines make almost no noise, because almost nothing is running at speed. The loom moves a centimeter and stops. The conveyor glides. A press comes down over the course of an afternoon. The whole industrial cycle is set to the speed of the plants it depends on: cut every seven years, press every nine, collect every season. The workers — and there are workers, but they are as rare as everything else that walks — come in the bright months to repair and harvest, and then they leave, and the place goes back to waiting. The only sounds you learn to listen for are quiet ones: a drip from a leaf tip, falling once a minute into the moss; the tick of cooling metal in the sun; your own footsteps, swallowed to nothing, and your own breathing, which seems obscenely loud by contrast.

A slow place. Everything moves on a long duration. The trees add a ring and call it a decade. Rust creeps a millimeter a year across a panel. The scree shifts when it rains, eleven grains at a time. The moss advances across a machine housing at the pace of a fingernail growing. Light travels fast here — that is the exception. In the morning it comes over the ridge all at once, hard and bleached, and the wet leaves throw it back at you until you squint. Shadows are black and instantaneous. The sky is a pale, almost colorless blue, as if the air is too thin to hold color. But the cold does not lift with the light. Shade is still freezing. Metal is still freezing. A sun-warmed stone is a serious pleasure, and the plants know it: they lean toward the warm cracks, toward the south-facing rock, toward the heat leaking from a buried pipe.

The strangeness grows on you slowly, the way everything does. The lichens on the shed wall have grown in straight lines, following the seams of the panels, as if they are reading the building. The fern rows are too straight. The moss on the conveyor belt is not random — it is thickest where the belt's joints are, where the oil and the warmth are. Some of the flowers are albino, producing white blooms that don't close for days, as if they have given up on filtering the light and decided to reflect it instead. In the condensation gutters of the press house — the only place water actually pools, in shallow channels of painted metal — there is a newt, pale and translucent, that lives its whole life in perhaps two meters of channel. It is the only animal you will see. Not the only one that exists — there are mites in the moss finer than dust, and a slug that crosses the shed floor once a season — but the only one that has bothered to be visible. The land is too young, too bright, too cold, and too wet in the wrong way for a fauna to establish itself. Plants colonized first, fast and massively. Animals have barely begun. Humans are the second animal, and also rare, and also mostly absent.

That absence is part of the atmosphere. The place feels tended and untended at once. The machines are tended by the green the way a reef tends a wreck. The green is tended, in its secret rows, by the machines. Neither is winning. A tree has grown up through the frame of a generator and is now holding it upright, its roots gripping the bolts. A vine has sealed a shed door from the outside with a green curtain. A cable has been adopted by a colony of moss and now conducts — poorly, wonderfully — through living tissue. When it rains, the nets fill like sails. When the fog comes, even the engines seem to breathe it in.

By late afternoon the light is still bright, still white, still cold, but it begins to slant and gild the edges of everything — the sharp black rock, the silver leaves, the bright roofs, the white pumice sand. The mist does not come down as a blanket. It comes in threads, in horizontal bands that catch the light and hang, unmoving, between the ridges. You can see, from the high shelf, the whole system at once: the young torn plateau, the glittering dense valley below it, the scattered industrial nodes winking in the green, the tarn shining like a coin in the basin, the other ridges beyond, and the sense that all of it is in its first year, its first century, its first fragile draft of a world that will, in time, wear its edges down and learn to hold water and make noise and grow animals. For now it holds light and damp and silence, and it does so so completely that you begin to speak more quietly out of habit, even though there is no one to disturb and nothing that could hear you anyway.
