The first thing is the sound, and the cold.

The sound is everywhere at once, and it does not come from a place you can point to. It is a low, wet pulsing you feel in your teeth as much as hear, overlaid with a high, ceaseless shimmer — clicks on clicks on clicks, like rain on a tin roof that never stops, like gravel being worried by thousands of small hands. Your own steps do not register in it. Your own breath does not register in it. It fills the dark so completely that silence, when you imagine it, feels like a physical thing you are missing.

The cold is in the stone underfoot and in the air coming off it. It is not sharp winter cold. It is cool and damp and settled, the temperature of deep water and cellars, the same at what must be noon as at what should be midnight. The light is a rumor. A pale, colorless gloom filters through a low, unbroken lid of cloud and mist, just enough to show that the ground slopes away for a long way and that low ridges rise, smooth and rounded, far off on every side. Beyond a few dozen paces outlines blur and merge again. Nothing is bright, nothing is green, nothing casts a hard shadow. The world is in grey and black and dull, wet white — the colors of wet stone, wet skin, wet shell.

You are in a basin. Not a pit, not a gorge, but a broad, low depression of land, valley-sized and open, extending past the immediate into a region of similar basins separated by similar ridges. The elevation is barely above the water table. Water here is not a lake or a river. It is a condition. The air holds it, the stone holds it, the air changes it back to rain for an hour and then takes it away again. There is intermittent drizzle — slow, cool, too fine to splash — that beads on everything and runs in threads along old lines in the rock. Between rains, mist and condensation roll in low waves across the flats, settling as a film on every surface, dripping from ledges long after the rain has stopped, gathering in shallow hollows in pans no deeper than a hand, where it trembles and reflects the gloom. The ground is never dry and never flooded. It is damp to the touch everywhere, spongy in places, slick in others. There is no fresh water to drink, only this perpetual dampness seeping, condensing, pooling in patience.

And there is no vegetation at all.

That is the first strange absence, and it makes the second presence hard to trust. No grass, no moss as a plant would make it, no scrub on the ridges, no vine in the hollows. The flats look, at first, like a plain of river-worn cobbles and flat slabs — basalt and shale smoothed over a very long time, all edges rounded, all hollows worn into balance, no new crack, no fresh scree, no sharp break anywhere. Everything has the look of a thing that has existed in its present form for ages: settled, weathered, in equilibrium with rain and damp and its own weight. The low ridges are humps smoothed by weather. The channels are old shallow scars that the drizzle still follows. It looks like a mineral world.

Then a slab, that you had taken for stone, closes. Slowly. A pore opens in it and shuts. The whole plain, for as far as you can see, sighs with a very slow, out-of-phase breathing — thousands upon thousands of flat bodies pulsing in place, lifting and settling, making that low wet sucking sound that rolled under the clicks. It was never stone. It is a pavement, living, overlapping shingle upon shingle, packed near-touching, layered two and three deep in places, each animal an oval of grey-black, armored hide, perhaps a hand across, perhaps broader, with a fringe of blind, tapping feelers at the edge that feel the film of damp for food. They do not walk. They creep, or they wait, and the bacterial film that grows in the condensation eats the stone's dissolved minerals — that is their pasture, invisible, and the faint filaments of fungus that thread between them are not plants either, only a breath on the rock, thin and sparse. They eat the film. They are eaten in turn.

Between the slabs, in the damp seams, boil the skitterers. Small, pale, many-legged, eyeless crustacean things, as thick as flies in a summer heap, except that there are millions of them and they never stop. They tap and worry and pick at the pavement animals, at each other's castings, at the dead, with a frantic clicking of claws and mouthparts. That is the high shimmering sound. It is deafening not because any one of them is loud, but because the volume of them is uncountable. They are a living static that fills every crack.

From the low ledges and under the lips of the worn ridges hang the third kind: soft, elongated, blind things that droop down and sway slightly, their throat sacs inflating and deflating in slow rhythm. When a sac fills, it lets out the low boom and croak that you felt in your teeth — a mating call, a warning, a way of testing the stone for reverberation. They drop a mucus thread, hang motionless for a long time, then snatch at skitterers with a quick, wet slap. Their bodies bead with condensation; they drink it from their own skins.

And moving through it all, slowly, parting the sea of bodies like a slow tide, are the wanderers. They are few by comparison, which is to say still many, and enormous — soft-bodied, grey-white, blind, with broad, muscular feet that press into the damp film and leave a clean, wet track that the pavement quickly reclaims behind them. They come to feed on the hangers and the pavement, their weight causing a dull percussive shift, a wave of clicks and puffs as the skitterers scatter and resettle. Their pace does not hurry. Nothing in this world hurries, and nothing rests. The pulse of the pavement travels in peristaltic waves across a flat, a rolling breath that takes minutes to cross. The skitterers swarm and disperse and swarm again over a carcass, over a seep, over a place where the rain has brought a faint pulse of minerals from the stone. The hangers sway and snap. The wanderers pass, and pass again, hours later, from another direction, as if on a circuit no one else can see.

Above the pavement, in the gloom, is a fourth layer: a dense, hovering haze of winged things, no larger than your thumbnail, blind, translucent, their wings a blur that adds a constant thin whine to the chorus. They do not land. They hang in the mist, feeding on the moisture itself and on each other, moving in slow, turning clouds that darken a patch of sky and then dissolve. They are sound made visible. You never see one clearly; you see the air thicken.

No eyes work properly here, in this low light. Some of the pavement animals have vestigial lids sealed over pits that glow faintly from within, not to see out but, it seems, to look inward at their own gut-light. The wanderers navigate by the vibration that travels through the wet film and through the stone when the hangers boom and the skitterers tap. The whole basin is a resonating instrument, and the animals are both its players and its listeners. Some exhale their stored water as a fine mist when they pulse, so that the dampness of the place is partly their own breath, remade. The weather and the fauna are not separate systems. Rain comes, the pavement opens a little wider, feeds faster, puffs more mist, clicks louder for a time; then the damp settles back into the stone, and the chorus settles to its steady, deafening pitch.

It is strange in the way of an old place that has found a loop and closed it. The wanderers' droppings and the pavement's discarded shells add, grain by grain, to the ridges that contain the basin. Over ages those shell-beds have been smoothed by drips and by crawling bodies into the low, even humps you see at the edge of vision. New ground is not made by fracture or flood. It is made slowly, by life wearing itself down into stone and stone feeding, by film, the life again. There are no built things here — no nest of twigs, no burrow dug in earth, no artifact of any kind. The hollows they live in are worn, not made. The paths are not paths, only places where bodies pass often enough that the damp film is always thin.

Walk a little, and the scale opens again. The pavement continues. The pans continue. Another seep ledge where hangers cluster thick as grapes, dripping. Another hollow full of upturned shells, black and white, polished. Another long flat where the wanderers have beaten a faint, wandering track in the bacterial sheen, curving, crossing itself, going nowhere that a map would name. The temperature stays cool, the light stays low, the rain threatens and does not quite fall, or falls so finely you feel it only as a sudden slickness underfoot and a brief, louder drumming on the armored backs.

It does not grow quieter as you go deeper in. It grows more layered. Now you can separate the sounds, the way you separate instruments in a deafening orchestra: the deep pulsing of the slabs, the wet slap of the hangers, the percussive shift of the wanderers, the papery whine of the wings overhead, and over all of it the unceasing, high, bright clicking of the skitterers, like a continent of small stones being turned over by small, patient hands.

Nothing here ever sleeps, because there is no signal for sleep. Nothing here ever stops being hungry, because there is always damp, and decay, and the bodies of their own kind. Nothing here looks new, because everything has been worn to its own shape. It is an established world, a balanced world, a world that has come to rest in motion — active, crowded, cold, dark, and damp, with no plant in it and no silence in it, alive from the film of water on a stone all the way up to the thick, buzzing air, and extending in every direction farther than you can hear.
