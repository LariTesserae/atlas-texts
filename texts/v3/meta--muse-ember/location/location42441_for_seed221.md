### The Condenser Pocket

You enter it through a low hatch, and the place announces itself before it can be seen. Sound comes up through the metal rungs and into your teeth first: a low industrial roar, a hiss, a rhythmic clank, water rushing somewhere beyond the walls, and over all of it a living noise so dense it seems less like sound than weather. The light is dim — one caged amber bulb, a green emergency lamp that never quite reaches the corners, the sudden copper glint of eyeshine everywhere you do not look directly.

It is a single room, not much bigger than a storage closet, perhaps nine metres long and four wide, tucked inside the service heart of a station built at a high, cold summit. From the outside it would be nothing; from inside, it is a continent. A slit of a window set high in one wall shows a white void of cloud and a drop that falls away into nothing, and the wind leans against the building hard enough to make the steel tick and groan. The air is thin. Exertion makes your chest burn in a way that has nothing to do with effort. Your breath fogs. The cold is not the clean cold of snow, but the wet, conductive cold of metal that has never been warm.

Everything here is made. There is no ground, only a floor: riveted steel deck plates pitched slightly, like a hillside in miniature, cut through by drainage channels, grates, and trenches. Over the deck run cable ducts, pipes at knee height and pipes at head height, stacked crates, barrels, a hoist track across the ceiling, a scaffold that has been erected and never dismantled. The “rocks” are cinder block and poured concrete aggregate. The “soil” underfoot is an accretion of rust flakes, machine dust, crushed insulation, feathers and droppings and algae — nothing that was ever outside. Even the walls are layered construction: concrete block, then steel panel, then a skin of damp fiberglass, then cables stapled over that. It is dense beyond ordinary clutter. Things are shoulder to shoulder, pipe to pipe, crate to crate, so that to walk the length of the room you must step over, around, and sometimes briefly onto the terrain, and the terrain barely notices.

And the room is not finished. That is its strangest, most persistent condition. On one side, a wall is half-poured, the concrete still dark and weeping as if it were set minutes ago; rebar loops stand up from it like roots. A line of yellow paint stops halfway along a beam. Insulation hangs in unfinished batts. New steel, bright and raw, sits beside steel already filmed grey. It has the look of a place just coming into being — embryonic, under construction, raw — except that it will never come into being any further. The construction does not progress, and it does not decay. It is frozen at the moment of making, forever.

Water is everywhere, but it is never a lake. The room is wet the way a marsh is wet: at the edges, constantly, near the surface. Condensation rains in slow, irregular drips from the cold ceiling and from the sweat on the overhead pipes. The walls weep in threads. The trenches in the floor carry black, oily water that moves with a slow, deliberate current, carrying flecks of algae and rust, then is pumped up by an unseen pump into an overhead tank, only to leak and drip and run back down again. Puddles lie in the hollows of the deck plates, trembling constantly from the vibration of the machines, so they never give a clean reflection. Where water meets metal at the margins of the trenches there is a soft sludge, a made-mud, and your boots go through it into water that is always ankle-deep and always the same depth. High-water marks are stained on the lower walls, and they have not changed.

Nothing grows here in any real sense. Vegetation is sparse to the point of tokenism: a grey-green felt of algae and moss confined to the seams where calcareous water seeps from the concrete, a dozen pale, etiolated shoots pushing up through a floor grate toward a light they will never reach, a film on the surface of the trench water that breaks and re-forms. Life, here, has almost entirely declined to be plant. It has chosen, instead, to be animal.

The fauna are overwhelming. Not present — overwhelming, in the way a forest is overwhelming, but stacked into this pocket, layered by height.

Across the ceiling, on the beams and the hoist track and every horizontal pipe, roost thousands of small soot-coloured birds, starling-like, packed wing to wing, breast to breast, so that the rafters are a single breathing mass. Their heads turn, or seem to; a ripple travels through the flock in slow waves, like wind through a field, though no individual bird takes flight. Below them, in the insulation and inside the cable ducts and between the crates, run rats and mice in such numbers that their squealing is a constant texture under the birdsong. In the trench water, pale and blind, eel-like sumpfish lie in the current in their hundreds, fin to fin, surging upstream all at once with each cycle of the pump. On the wet deck, among the puddles, sit cold-adapted amphibians, motionless for minutes at a time, eyes open. Around the amber bulb and in the film of damp air hangs a snow of moths and midges so thick the light has a halo. On every vertical surface, if you look with a lamp, there are more eyes.

They are fat, and they are deafened by their own world, and they do not startle. The machines have been running since before they arrived, and they have built their lives into the machine rhythm: the hoist travels its track carrying nothing, back and forth with a clank at each end; a valve hisses every so often; the pump pushes water up and lets it fall. The birds call through all of it — a continuous, high, chittering, scolding, croaking chorus that never resolves into silence and never seems to be answering anything. The rats scream under it. The water rushes under that. The low motor hum under everything vibrates in your sternum and your molars. To speak here you would have to shout directly into another person’s ear, and even then the room would swallow most of it.

The ecology is closed, and that is the weirdness of it. The birds drop waste and feathers; the waste feeds the algae in the trenches; the algae feed the tiny crustaceans in the water; the crustaceans feed the fish; the fish, in turn, are eaten — not often, but just often enough. The rats take eggs fallen from the rafters. The mass of birds gives off a collective warmth, small warm islands in the cold air, and their breath, and the fish’s, and the steam from the valves, all of it condenses on the cold steel and rains back down to keep the trenches full. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. A bird that dies — if a bird here can be said to die, and none you will ever find evidence of — is simply replaced in the mass by another, as if population were a property of the room rather than of individuals. The animals do not age in any way you can see. They do not migrate, do not sleep in shifts, do not fall silent at night, because there is no night. The bulb burns the same dim amber at what would be noon and midnight. The water level does not rise or fall. The construction does not finish. The machines do not break, and do not improve.

It is, in the end, an equilibrium of a very deliberate kind: everything moves, and nothing changes. Water drips and flows and is pumped and falls again. The hoist travels. The flock ripples. The fish surge. And the room, at the top of a cold mountain, hidden inside its own built skin, remains exactly what it was when it was first switched on — wet, dim, deafening, unfinished, and teeming — a small, sealed, industrial marsh pretending to be a wilderness, and succeeding.
