**The High Thatch**

It is high, and from below you would not guess it. The land rises not in peaks but in a long, shouldered lift, a plateau the size of a country set a mile or more above whatever lowlands it has turned its back on. On its edge the wind comes up thin and hot off the bare stone, and then it stops, as if it has arrived somewhere. Inside it, there is no outside weather. Storms are born here, wander here for days, and die here. You can see them coming from twenty miles away and watch them turn in slow circles over the green, never sure if they will ever reach you.

At first, standing on that edge, it looks empty.

The light is ordinary — white, midday, without strange color — but the air is thin and the heat sits on it like a hand. Sweat dries before it can run. The ground burns the soles through any thin sandal and yet a few steps away, under something unseen, it is cool and damp to the touch. The horizon does not end in trees. It ends in a motionless, sun-baked shimmer, flat to every side, broken only rarely by a dark shape that is too far to name.

Walk in, and the emptiness revises itself.

There is no soil here, not in the way soil is elsewhere. There is no bare rock, no sand, no dust plain for more than a handspan. What looks from the edge like a blond, dry, sun-cured steppe is, close up, a mat. Ankle-deep, then knee-deep in the swales, a continuous, living thatch of interwoven stems, succulent leaves, root-hooks and runners, red-green and waxy, springy underfoot. It heals behind you. It breathes, slowly, in heat. It is hot on top, moist an inch down. Everywhere you step you are stepping on plant, and the plant is stepping on everything else, holding the thin clay and the weathered stone together in a fibrous skin.

That is the ground. It goes on, in principle, forever. That is the vegetation, total — not as forest, not as garden, but as substrate. The plateau itself is made of it, year laid on year, rotted and regrown, a settled, ancient carpet that has eroded into broad swells and shallow green channels rather than into gullies. Nothing here looks new. The rock that shows through where the mat has thinned is smoothed like bone, rounded, long left alone. The abandoned dry channels run in wide, in-balance curves, taking their form from where water once, and occasionally still, persuades a few stems to lie flat and darker.

And still it is sparse.

Because between the mat and the sky, presences are rare and enormous.

A day’s walk may pass only three or four of them. A plant rises — not a tree as a lowland tree, but a single trunk, as thick as a hut, ribbed and succulent, flaring at the top into leaves the size of doors. Or two, or a hollow stalk big enough to walk inside, its interior cool and smelling of sap, its walls sweating condensation in the dark. They stand half a mile apart, sometimes more, each one a small architecture with its own tenants, birds nesting in the fork, insects in the hollow, predators sleeping in its shade at noon. Standing in that shade you understand the scale: the shadow is a room. Leaving it, you are again in open, humming heat, with nothing higher than your own head until the next one.

The mat, then, is everything. The giants are incidents upon it.

Water is not a feature here. It is a habit, an intermittent one. There are no rivers running in the open. The occasional rain is a violence at midday that you hear long before you feel — a black wall of cloud that builds over the plateau’s own center and drops its water in ten furious minutes. Most of it never lands as rain. It falls as virga, as mist, as steam off the hot thatch, and the mat drinks it in a visible surge: in an hour the low channels darken, the runners lift, tiny pale shoots push up through the thatch overnight and are cropped or hardened by morning. What water remains is not on the surface. It is inside things. Inside the giant stems, stored and sweet. Under the vast undersides of leaves, where cool night air condenses, dripping in a slow, regular tick-tock long after dawn. In hollowed stones at the foot of an escarpment, black pockets a hand across, untouched for years, where frogs no bigger than thumbs sing and are not to be drunk from, because to find them is to know where the animals go when the mat dries.

Dawn is when it is wettest. The whole thatch exhales. A ground mist, ankle high, rises and tears and vanishes by mid-morning, leaving the leaves beaded. Animals come out to lick. People, when you find them, come out to collect — with cupped hands, with woven scoops, with hollow stems pressed to a seep. Dampness here is not climate, it is behavior: everything that can hold moisture does, and everything that needs it learns to visit.

The heat governs the hours. By ten the mat is too hot to sit on without moving. The air carries pollen and spores in a permanent yellow haze that makes distant giants swim. Things close. The giant leaves fold along their midrib, the runners curl, the animals seek the narrow shade or go underground into the cool, fibrous dark beneath the mat where tunnels, worn by generations of feet and bellies, keep a constant damp chill. The plateau seems, then, desperately still — and that is when you first hear how alive it is.

Because of the fauna. They are overwhelming, not in the sense of a single herd you can count, but in the sense that you are never more than an hour from a multitude.

They come as weather. First the insects: a cloud that is not a cloud, a roaring sheet of fliers that darkens the sun for twenty minutes, settling on the mat in a living rustle, lifting again when something underneath moves. They are followed, sometimes, by the grazers — great, slow, six-legged things with moss growing thick on their backs, plowing through the thatch in a broad front, eating a swath a valley wide, dropping steaming dung that the mat instantly takes up. Their back-moss, in the weird logic of this place, is itself a garden, with its own tiny grazers and hunters, so that a single animal is a moving plateau in miniature.

Above them, birds nest in the solitary giants by the hundreds, launching at dawn in a clatter that can be heard across a valley. On the ground, things that look like walking plants — lanky, thorned, photosynthetic predators — move with terrible patience among the grazers, neither quite animal nor quite plant, their skins green and their eyes hooded. They do not chase often. In the established balance here, herds and hunters and swarms follow the invisible wet band that moves across the plateau through the year, and where that band has passed, the thatch is eaten to stubble, and where it will pass next season, it is already thickening in anticipation, root by root.

Hunting, migration, growth, storm, rot: the place is in motion, but at a pace that feels old. Even the plants move. A few — the ones with long, exploratory root-legs — shift a meter in a season, creeping toward a seep or away from a crowd, leaving a pale, withered trail behind them that slowly greens again. Nothing is fixed except the whole.

And the sound. You learn to time your life by it.

Wind moving through a giant leaf does not whisper. It claps and booms and tears, a sail slapping. At dusk, when the heat finally breaks, the insects begin — first one, then a thousand, then an uncountable choir that rises until your own footsteps vanish under it, until speech is a rumor, until the mat itself seems to pop and crackle as pods split in the cooling air with sounds like hand-drums. A grazer herd bedding down makes a noise like a distant avalanche of sighs and grunts. A storm’s thunder, trapped over the plateau, rolls back and forth between the escarpments and seems never to finish. At night, in the cooler thin air, when the mat opens and breathes out its stored moisture, the ticking of dripping leaves is the only regular thing, and the frogs in the stone pockets answer it, and from far off come the long, trumpeting calls of the back-moss herds, carrying for miles because there is nothing here to stop sound and nothing to absorb it but more living fiber.

It is, at its height, deafening in a way that is not frightening because it is so ordered. Everything calls at once, and everything is listening.

Human trace is scattered through it as faintly as the sky is thin. You may walk for two days and see only a cairn of smoothed stones, a broken grinding slab half-swallowed by runners, a circle of woven stalks collapsed into a hut’s ghost. Once, in that distance, a single living hut: a lean-to of giant leaf-stalks lashed with fiber, a small fire that struggles in the thin air and smokes blue, a rack of stone adzes and bone needles, a few gourds of precious stored water, a fence of sticks rotting back into the thatch by design. The people who live here are as sparse as the giants, families of ten or twenty who follow the herds, clip the mat for greens and fiber, harvest dew at dawn, coax fire from sticks, work stone and bone and thorn but no metal, build low because the wind and the plants will take anything high. Their terraces, where they attempt them in the shallow channels, are already half-plant; their trails, where feet have worn a path, are the only places where the mat is actually bare, a pale line of clay pressed between runners.

They do not tame the place. They use it the way the animals use it: shade when hot, hollow when it rains, leaf when they need a roof, a herd when they need to move. They know which stone pockets are for drinking and which are for frogs. They know the hour when the insect choir will cover their talk, and they sit, patient, until it passes.

By evening the light goes ordinary gold, the haze clears, and the plateau shows its true strangeness in plain sight. A grazer goes by, its back a forest in motion, a bird asleep in its moss. A walker-plant pauses mid-step, one root-leg lifted, and will not set it down until night. A giant leaf, folded all day, opens in the dark with a sigh you can hear. The mat, which all day seemed sun-cured and brittle, steams faintly where the day’s heat leaves it, and the condensation begins again on every underside, a slow, planet-scale breathing in and out of water that never becomes a river.

It goes on. The plateau is vast enough to have its own seasons in different corners, its own rain in the center while the edges bake, its own migrations crossing like slow tides through the green. It has been here long enough to settle into itself, every rock worn, every channel green, every ruin made natural — and it is still active, still growing, still hungry after rain, still deafening at dusk. Between its rare giants there is a great deal of open, hot, living ground, and that ground is not empty at all. It is the place itself, thinking slowly, holding water in its fibers, feeding everything that walks or flies or crawls upon it, and waiting, with immense patience, for the next brief fall of water to turn it all suddenly, audibly, green.
