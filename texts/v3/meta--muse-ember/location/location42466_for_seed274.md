It is wet before you can see it.

You step down into it and your boots are damp through, not from a puddle but from the ground itself. The clay gives a little and then holds, saturated. The surface is not mud, exactly — it's packed too tight for that — it's a skin of water over earth, a quarter-inch deep in the hollows, a shine on everything higher. Nothing here is dry. Even the stones at the lip of the place, where the ground rises a man's height and stops the view, are dark with it and bead it out slowly when you brush them.

This is a low place. A shallow basin, not much wider than a hamlet, fifty, sixty steps across if you walk it in a straight line. You can stand in the middle and take it all in at once. The rim is low and rounded, weathered into a soft oval, so you are always slightly below the world outside without ever feeling like you're in a cave. The mist hangs at head height and blurs the rim, and beyond that there is nothing to look at. The scale is the first thing that settles: it is a room-sized landscape, bounded, complete.

And it is packed. That is the second thing. There is no spare ground. Everywhere the floor is shoulder-to-shoulder cobble — basalt smoothed long ago by water, grey clay stones fitted by pressure into a pavement, smaller pebbles wedged in the gaps until there is no gap left to put a finger in. The flat rock slabs at the center touch. The low ledge on the north side touches the pool. A foot comes down on stone, then stone, then stone, and if it misses, it comes down in water an inch deep that does not soak away because the earth below is already full.

The water does not move, really. It arrives. On the north and west sides the rock faces weep. Not in streams, not in falls — in beads that gather along a crack, swell, and drop at intervals so regular you start counting without meaning to. A drip. A drip. A drip, hitting the same pool and making the same expanding ring that overlaps the eighteen rings already there. At the center there is one main pool, maybe three meters across, steaming faintly. Around it, the sheet of water thins until it is just a film over the stones, but it never breaks. It connects everything.

You understand after a while that this is not weather. It is a balance. The water comes up from below as fast as it goes out as vapor, and the vapor condenses again on the coolest surfaces — the underside of the low overhang on the east side, the top of your own shoulders — and falls back. The pool level is the same today as the stain on the rock says it was last month, and the month before that. The rings on the stones are set lines, not tidemarks. Nothing rises. Nothing drains away. Air gets damp, air drips, drip gets absorbed. It goes around in a small circle and stays.

It is warm. Not hot like a fire, warm like bath water left in the sun all day, warm like holding your hand in a dish. The warmth is in the water, in the stones under it, and in the air, which is heavy and does not move. You sweat and it does not evaporate, it just adds to the condensation on your skin. The light is dim, not because it is night but because the sky is a low, unchanging lid of cloud and the mist steals what is left. Colors go flat and grey-ochre: wet basalt black, clay pale grey, the precipitate around the pool a dull buff where the mineral water has left its crust in thin, packed layers. Everything shines, but nothing glints. The light has no direction you can feel. Midday and late afternoon would look the same here.

There are no plants.

That takes longest to notice because your eyes keep looking for them. No grass between the stones — there is no soil between the stones. No leaves, no twigs washed in, no root hairs in the cracks, no moss though the rocks are wet enough for it. The water is too warm and too mineral for most things to root in, and so the place has settled into a different kind of surface life: a translucent, faintly slimy biofilm over the submerged stones, so slick underfoot that you walk more carefully here than on the dry parts, and thin mineral skins at the pool's edge that crack in perfect polygonal plates when your weight disturbs them and then seal over again with a new film. At the very edge of the pool the crust is a few centimeters thick, built up layer by layer, white and ochre, like porcelain. That is the life of this place, if you can call it that: rock growing in water, water growing on rock.

Everything has been worn into its final shape. No sharp edges, no fresh scree, no new scars. The basalt cobbles are eggs. The rim is a swell, not a ridge. Even the broken things have been rounded by the same patience. It feels old in the way a thing feels old when it has stopped changing — not ruined, not young, just settled, in its own state, for a very long time.

The sound is mostly nothing. That is part of the stillness. No wind, because the rim and the mist smother it. No birds, no insects humming. Only the drip, and the soft plink where a bead falls from the overhang onto a stone instead of water, and after an hour, your own breathing, which sounds too loud and too wet in the saturated air. The quiet is not empty — it has texture, the faint hiss of condensation forming and the occasional tick as a stone adjusts a fraction under the pressure of the water, but it never breaks into noise.

Fauna are rarer than quiet would suggest. In the main pool, if you stand still long enough, you may see one small newt, dark against the dark stone, hanging motionless a hand's breadth below the surface, gills feathery and still. It has presumably been there a long time, or something very like it has. On a flat, warmer slab near the southern rim there is a single beetle, shell black and polished, making its slow way across stone that is touching stone. Nothing else. No frog chorus, no swarm. Just shells, empty and tiny and white, wedged in the crevices between the cobbles where they have collected over years — more dead than living, packed in like gravel.

People have been here, but scatteredly, and long ago in the way undated things are long ago. A low arc of dry-laid stones on the windward side, knee-high, half collapsed, one course missing where it fell and was never rebuilt. Three shallow basins scooped or worn into the flat rock near the pool, each filled to the same inch of water, each at the same level as the pool itself, as if they were made to sit in it. A pair of hammerstones of a lighter, foreign rock, left side by side on a dryish stone, worn smooth where a palm would grip them. Shards of rough pottery, half-buried in the clay film, edges softened. A flake of worked stone, snapped and discarded.

No house. No roof. No metal, no fence, no road. Just hand-scale work: things a person could stack, scoop, break, and leave. They do not make the place feel inhabited. They make it feel visited, briefly, from time to time, by someone who also noticed the warmth and the quiet and decided to sit down in the shallow water for a while and then leave the stones as they found them, packed and wet and unchanged.

If you stay, the place teaches you its rule without ever announcing it: nothing here is going to happen. The drip will not speed up. The mist will not lift. The pool will not overflow or empty. The newt will hang there. The stones will stay locked, holding their water, holding their heat, in a perfect, small, saturated equilibrium, dim and warm and wet, with no green thing growing anywhere in it at all.
