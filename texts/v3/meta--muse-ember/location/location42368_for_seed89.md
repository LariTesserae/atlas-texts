At the top of the world there is a hole.

Not a peak in the usual sense — the summit is blunt, wind-scoured, thin-aired, bare stone for as far as you can see and then cloud far below. It is far too high for comfort, and yet the air that comes up out of the hole is warm.

It is a single throat shaft, no wider than a doorway, cut down through old andesite. Everything settled here a long time ago and has stayed. The edges are not sharp. Water and wings and time have rounded them. The shaft walls are scalloped, stained in long brown weeps, polished where thousands of bodies have brushed past. Even at noon, the light down there is only a memory of light — a pale, blue-grey column that weakens as it falls until, twenty, thirty metres down, it gives up entirely. The floor of the chamber below never sees it.

The chamber itself is small. Cathedral-small. You could walk its length in a minute if the floor were clear, and its walls are a single closed curve, one way in from above, one low crack at the far end where warm air sighs out and a few things have learned to live thirsty lives. But small does not mean empty. Small does not mean quiet. Small, here, means crowded.

You descend by sound first.

The drip is nearest. There is no river here, no stream, no pool that lasts. The water parameter of this place is only dampness: the rock sweats. Warm air rising from below and from the bodies below meets cooler stone above and condenses, beads, gathers along the ribs of the ceiling, and falls in slow, irregular taps. It darkens the stone in trails. It collects for an hour or two as a skin in a hollow, and animals gather to drink it with tongues and beaks before it soaks away or evaporates. Everything vertical is wet to the touch. Everything horizontal, near the floor, is dry and crusty and warm.

The crust is guano, layered over centuries into a level plateau. It is white-grey, then grey-brown where damp has had its way, plaster-smooth in places, grainy in others. Under your boots it gives a fraction, then holds. Beneath that crust, and in it, is another world at the scale of millimetres, and it is also loud if you put your ear to it: beetles ticking over one another, mites moving in a faint, continuous rustle, isopods scratching, worms threading through the softened film where a drip has landed.

But the loudness that fills the chamber is the air.

It begins as a murmur in the shaft and becomes, once you are inside, a steady weather of sound. Ten thousand birds, perhaps more — small swifts, dark-feathered, throat pale, built for this thin high air — are packed onto the walls. Their nests are not grand structures. They are half-cups of saliva and feathers and a little moss pulled from the shaft lip, glued to the rock, and they overlap. On the most favoured wall, the nests touch rim to rim, shoulder to shoulder, a living wallpaper that extends from a few metres above the floor to the crown of the ceiling. The birds sit so close that their wings brush their neighbours when they shift. When one launches, a ripple goes through them. When one returns from the pale sky outside — up and out through that narrow shaft at dawn, hunting insects over the sparse, high moorland — it will circle inside the chamber several times, navigating not by sight but by a hail of clicks, and settle into a gap no larger than itself.

The clicks never stop. They interlace with chitters, with the dry papery flutter of wings, with the soft knock of a beak against stone. Farther off it is a roar; nearer, you can separate it into lives. A bird directly above your head sounds as distinct as a tap on a window. A thousand others blend into rain on leaves. At certain moments, when the whole colony turns in one slow, swirling mass in the dark centre of the chamber — a black current, patient, unhurried — the wing-noise swells until you feel it in your sternum.

Darkness here is not an absence. It is a medium. Your eyes do not adjust so much as they retire. The only light besides the faint shaft is what the place itself makes: a sparse, sickly glow on patches of fungi feeding in the guano, greenish, which brightens, if at all, when breathed upon; tiny reflective wet points where condensation catches the shaft light; and, down in that far crack where warm air escapes, a few stubborn tufts of moss and two small ferns. That is the whole of the vegetation. Twelve plants, perhaps, clinging to the one place where there is both a trickle of moisture and the possibility of light. Their roots have found a seam and are slowly, patiently working it open. The rest of the chamber is too dark, too rich in ammonia and salts, too trampled by life, for anything green to make a stand. On the lip of the shaft outside, a cushion plant, a lichen the colour of old mustard, wind-blown grass — the outside world, thin and bright and cold, which only touches the inside at its mouth.

Damp, warm, dark, high — the contradictions hold because life makes its own climate. The rock below still carries heat. The bodies of the birds give off heat. The decomposing guano gives off heat. The air in the chamber stays twenty degrees warmer than the wind at the summit, thick and smelling of feathers, of mineral stone, of guano and the faint sulphur of deep cracks, of wet earth though there is no earth. It rises in a slow, continuous breath up the shaft, carrying feathers, down, dander, small insects, the smell of the colony up to the bright air, and bringing back coolness that condenses again on the ceiling. It is a cycle that has been going longer than any living thing here can remember.

Erosion has done its work and stopped being dramatic about it. There are no fresh scars. The ceiling scallops, where water has dissolved and polished the basalt, are smooth as the inside of a shell. The floor is level to a degree that suggests equilibrium: what falls, what dies, what is shed, is processed, trodden, spread. Birds fall dead and are stripped by beetles in a day. A skeleton of a larger animal — a shrew-like climber that once scaled the wall and starved in the crack — lies half-buried in an older layer of guano, its bones stained faint orange by seeping water, its skull full of beetles. It is the only thing here that resembles an artifact, and even it is not made. The only made things are nests, and the communal curtains of web that harvestmen spin across the dripping ribs of the ceiling, beads of water strung on silk, and the anvil stones on the floor — a dozen water-worn pebbles with polished tops, used by the climbing mammal to crack open the shells of land snails that have wandered in from the shaft mouth. The shells are split cleanly, the anvils otherwise untouched. Primitive work. No building, no walls, no marks for others of its kind.

Time moves slowly at the scale of the place itself. A stalactite above you is growing, but you will never see it. It will grow an eyelash length in a lifetime. The guano layer adds a millimetre in a wet year, hardens in a dry one. The drip pattern changes over decades as a seam slowly closes or opens. The colony changes over seasons — more birds in the breeding months, more chicks, the air thicker, the sound thicker — but the chamber absorbs it. It has room in its equilibrium for the pulse. The swirl of birds in the centre is slow. Even their panic, if a shadow passes the shaft, is a slow wave rather than a scatter. Everything is in its own state, weathered, settled, in balance.

The unusual things are small and therefore easy to miss, which is proper for a place that is not trying to be strange. There is a worm that lives only in the vertical film of condensation on one wall, moving up at night to feed on fungi and down before the drips strengthen, so that if you watch long enough you see a dark line descend the stone in a straight path, certain as a seam. There are crickets, blind, white as curd, that hang from the ceiling by their hind legs in dense clusters, so dense they touch antenna to antenna; when the colony's noise peaks, they sway in unison as if in wind, though there is no wind. There is a frog, no bigger than a thumbnail, brown and warted, that breeds in the transient skins of water on the floor and calls with a sound like a fingernail dragged on wet tile, but it is drowned out except when you are very close to it, crouched, listening through the beetles.

And there is the rule that governs all movement here, and it is not written anywhere: nothing trusts its eyes. The birds click. The crickets touch. The beetles follow chemical trails in the guano. You, if you were to enter and stay, would learn to put a hand out before a step, to let the warm, wet rock tell you where the wall is, to let the air tell you where the centre is, where the wings are thickest. You would be landed on, briefly, by a bird that mistook your shoulder for a ledge. Spiders would lower themselves to inspect your hair. The whole place would treat you as a new surface to be explored, and in a little while, the density would close around you as it closes around everything. There is no empty space that is old. A ledge unoccupied for one night is occupied the next. A drip that falls steadily on one stone will wear a tiny, polished hollow, and that hollow will be used by a beetle as a bath.

That is the world, at room scale, at the top of a mountain, in the dark: a small, bounded, warm hollow, sweating water it cannot keep, with almost no green in it, eroded into smoothness, loud with a single overwhelming kind of life in layers — floor, wall, air, ceiling — each layer touching the next, each sound touching the next, moving in slow, patient cycles that have been going so long that the rock itself seems to be breathing with them.
