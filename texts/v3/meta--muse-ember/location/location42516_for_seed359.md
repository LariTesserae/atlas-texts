### The Coiled Grove

You can walk the whole of it without hurrying, and from the middle you can still see where it ends. That is the first strangeness, and perhaps the least of them: it is small, a hamlet under one interlaced skin of roofs, no more than forty paces from wall to wall, but it is crowded so densely with life and with making that walking it slowly feels long, and walking it attentively can take a lifetime. 

It sits in a shallow hollow on a low dry plain. The plain beyond its ring of walls is not old land. It is pale mineral crust, recently lifted and fractured, the plates upturned with sharp, fresh edges like pieces of a broken vessel. Nothing has softened it. No water has run across it, no rain has darkened it, no wind has worried it into dunes. Heat gathers in the hollow and stays there. The Grove itself rises only a little above this broken floor — low roofs, low walls, a low skyline — but within that boundary, almost nothing is left to nature to make on its own.

The ground under your feet is built. It is packed rubble, uneven slabs, sun-baked brick, clay beaten flat by feet not yet numerous enough to smooth it. Every surface bears the accidents of hands: thumbprints in plaster, coils in the walls where clay was rolled and joined, tiles warped slightly in small kilns, reeds woven too tight in one place and loosely in the next. The builders used stone flakes, bone needles, wooden paddles, reed knives, a few bronze awls passed down and carefully kept. No machines. No wheels except the slow turn of a potter's hand. Timber is scarce and young; most beams are grown poles, vine-ropes, bundled reeds, and branches deliberately trained across gaps until they thickened into structure. A door may be leather stitched to a wooden frame. A window may be amber resin, or woven reed left open to the air. Nothing is machined, nothing is standard, and yet nothing is missing. Wall, roof, floor, pot, basket, mat, tool, clothing, nest — all are continuations of the same few materials, endlessly reworked.

The air is warm all day, though never fierce. It is dry and still and smells of heated clay, lime, green resin, dried herbs, and the faint mineral sweetness of ripe pods. There is no liquid water anywhere in the place. No stream, no rain, no well, no drip from a roof, no puddle after a storm, because there are no storms. What the inhabitants drink they take from pulpy pods and resinous fruits, and what the plants drink they take from dust, air, mineral film, and light. Their chemistry does not run on water. Sap is thick as warm honey and hardens in the sun. Roots are dry as hair. Leaves are waxy, thick-veined, and covered in fine pores that open at dawn to gather the faintest suspended dampness bound in the air — and even that never gathers into drops. 

The light is ordinary daylight. The sun is high and white-gold, the shadows fall where shadows should fall, and the colors are the true colors of clay and leaf. But the air inside is so still, and the walls so thick with plaster and thatch and woven hangings, that sound dies quickly. Most of the Grove is quiet. You hear your own shoe scrape, then nothing. A beetle knocks once inside a beam. A vine rustles when a bird lands in it and then settles again. Somewhere a piece of clay settles in the heat and makes a soft tick. The keepers speak little, and when they do their voices are low and unhurried, absorbed by leaves and cloth and earthen walls. Even the animals are mostly silent. This is not a dead silence — it is a thick one, like the silence inside a well-made pot with the lid on.

And it is green. That is the contradiction the place resolves by refusing to be natural. Vegetation is everywhere, layered three and four deep, because the built world gave it places to live that soil never could. Coiled vines climb the walls out of pots that are themselves built into the walls. Wall-figs root directly in mortar, their roots slowly dissolving brick for minerals, their branches becoming beams, their leaves shading the bricks they are eating. Plaster-ferns unfurl from cracks between tiles, shedding powdery spores instead of seed. Roof-beans hang in long fragrant strings from thatch. Basket-mint and kiln-grass grow in every container that has been abandoned for more than a season, and many containers are not abandoned at all — they are stacked, nested, hung, and planted through, so that a pot holds a plant that holds a pot that holds another plant. 

Nothing grows in wild earth. Even the soil is made: crushed brick and leaf-dust and ash and plaster-scrapings and animal droppings, swept up, sifted by hand, and fed back into the raised beds and terraces that form the floor of the Grove. The plants are lush not despite the dryness but in a different key altogether — deep green, fat-leaved, slow to wilt, slow to grow. A vine may advance a handspan in a year. A fig may take a decade to cross a courtyard. When they fruit, the pods are dry, aromatic, and filled with a resinous pulp that the keepers dry further and store in clay jars. Fallen leaves are not allowed to rot in heaps; they are gathered, ground, and mixed into new plaster or new potting earth. The Grove eats its own shed skin.

The fauna is abundant, and it lives in layers. Under the floor slabs, blind clay-worms move through the made soil, eating plaster-dust and dead leaf. In the beams, kiln-beetles bore patiently through wood and plaster, their domed shells stamped with ridges that look, if you stare at them, like miniature roofs. Moths flutter in the woven hangings, eating fiber and laying eggs in the seams of baskets. Plaster-slugs — dry things, no slime at all — glide across walls leaving a clear film that hardens like resin and peels away in flakes. Clay-lizards bask on warm tiles, their skin granular as unglazed pottery, licking mineral dust from the air. Thatched wrens nest in every eave, puffing out their chests and saying nothing; a few dozen may be visible from one spot, all watching with bright eyes. Small furred burrowers live between bricks, hoarding pods in wall-cavities that were built for them, or that they have obligingly enlarged. The keepers — a few dozen people, patient and dark against the pale plaster — tend, prune, re-plaster, re-thatch, break, rebuild. Their work is never finished because the place is young, and because the place does not want to stay finished.

For the Grove copies itself. That is its law, visible at first only in small things. A tile left on a windowsill will, over a season, be mirrored by a leaf whose veins follow the tile's pattern. A pot overlooked in a corner will sprout a smaller pot from a crack in its side, and the smaller pot will sprout another. A bird's nest woven in the roof will be echoed, months later, in the weave of a nearby basket. A beetle born in a warm upper room will carry a shell a fraction more domed than its mother, like a kiln built inside a kiln. The keepers are always culling these copies — breaking the extra pots, thinning the vines, re-plastering over a too-eager wall — not because the copies are harmful, but because if they are left alone, the Grove begins to think in copies. It forgets which was the first.

Deeper in, the forgetting has gone further. There are rooms that were never built, or that were built once and dismantled, which nevertheless have a coolness, a smell of lime, a chair-shaped hollow in the plaster where something sat for years. A corridor that measured eight paces yesterday may measure nine today, not because anyone extended it, but because a plant has thickened against one wall and a pot has appeared against the other, and the space between has politely adjusted. A staircase in the north wing has, over the years, gained a step that no one remembers laying. The step is fresh, sharp-edged, plaster still pale. Doors sometimes open onto the room you have just left, but rearranged — the same pots, the same vine, the same light, in a slightly different order, as if the room were remembering itself incorrectly. In one small chamber near the center, the light from the window falls across the floor and stays there a little after the sun has moved on, slowly absorbed by the leaves on the wall, as if the plants were drinking it.

Nothing here is an original any longer. All faces are copies of copies. A leaf in the innermost room looks like a roof seen from outside the plain. A bird's plumage repeats the weave of its nest. A keeper's house, seen from above through the vines, resembles the floor plan of the whole Grove, with its own tiny courtyard and its own tiny wall. The smaller the copy, the more clearly the pattern survives and the more the details go wrong. These wrongnesses are not violent. They are gentle, and terribly patient. They accumulate the way dust accumulates, the way a vine accumulates across a roof.

And everything moves slowly. The Grove was completed recently — within living memory — and it still shows its youth. The lime plaster has not greyed. The wood has not silvered. Corners are still crisp, edges still sharp, a half-built room at the east end still waits with its green poles tied and its thatch only partly laid. Seasons here are long and blurred; a year may pass with only a change in the color of the light and a new ring of pots on a shelf. Animals live long and breed rarely. Plants unfurl one leaf in a morning and then rest for months. The whole place is settling, almost imperceptibly, into itself: a wall leans a fraction more toward the sun, a roof-tile slips a finger's width, a beam sighs in the heat. The keepers notice these things the way one notices a child growing — not day by day, but suddenly, between visits, the doorway is narrower, the vine has crossed the window, a nest has appeared where there was only thatch.

That is why the Grove feels so complete and so unfinished at once. It is a made world, totally made, and therefore porous to the beings that live in it; it is lush without water because its plants learned to eat dust and light; it is warm, low, and quiet because heat pools in its hollow and sound is swallowed by its thickness; it is dense because every made thing gives rise to another made thing, and every living thing gives rise to another living thing, and the two kinds of giving have become difficult to tell apart. It is small enough to hold in your view, and large enough, in its copying and its slow drifting, to lose yourself in for years.

Stand in the central courtyard at midday and you can see the ring of roofs, the fractured plain beyond, the dust in the sunbeam. You can see the fig holding up its own wall. You can hear, faintly, a beetle knocking in a beam, a wren shifting in thatch, a keeper humming a single note as she coils clay. The leaves turn their faces up. The pots turn their faces down. Nothing is hurrying. Nothing needs to. The Grove is new, and green, and handmade, and already beginning, leaf by leaf and brick by brick, to forget how it was first made — and to make itself again, slightly smaller, slightly wrong, in the warm quiet of its own interior.
