It is not a place you can see from above. 

On low, cold ground at the edge of a marsh — where the water sits just under the skin of the earth and any digging finds it black and cold within a finger's depth — something has recently happened. The ground has heaved and split. Frost lifted a whole plate of roots and packed sod and tipped it, fresh, so the fracture-lines are still sharp, the turned-up edges still bright and unweathered, ice still locked in the new cracks. Under that plate, in the space a man can only enter by getting low and wet to the knee, is a hollow.

That hollow is the whole of it.

From inside, there is no horizon. Whatever landscape is here, you can take in at a glance, crouching. Ceiling, floor, and walls meet within arm's reach in every direction, a pocket no wider than a small room and no higher than your chest when standing. And yet, once you are in it, it does not feel small. It feels like a place with districts, with outskirts, with distance.

The first surprise is that nothing in here is stone. Or rather, stone is never exposed. The floor is not earth. It is plant mass, packed and alive — a felt of moss, root-hairs, lichen, and the fine, felted rhizomes of cold-water sedges, springy underfoot and giving back slowly after you lift your feet. The walls are the same thing hung vertically, so wet they shine. The ceiling is a vault of living roots, braided by growth into a low dome, dripping perpetually.

Even where rock should be, it weeps. Fresh, sharp shards of frozen sod and broken root-wood jut from the walls where the split opened, and every shard is skinned in a translucent green film. Water sheets over it in slow tears, beads, runs, falls. It is not raining. The air itself is condensing. Your breath fogs and then falls as fine rain from the root-vault, joining the other drips. The streets, where there are streets, hold black mirrors of water a few millimetres deep. Cold does the rest: in the still moments, a skin of ice forms over the canals by morning — if there is morning — and melts by the slow pulse of the drips breaking it.

It is dark in a way that is not night. No sun enters except as a thin grey seam where the root-plate is propped open, a slit perhaps a hand's width across, high on one wall. That seam gives enough light to catch the green of the walls and the wet shine of the water, and nothing more. The rest is lit, if at all, by translucence. Leaves overhead are thick and waxy and blue-green from the cold, and they let through a diffuse, underwater glow, like light through ice. And by eyes.

There are so many eyes.

Vegetation here is not scenery. It is the substrate, the architecture, and much of the population. To stand on living moss is to stand on a structure that is also eating, drinking, and repairing itself. Houses are not built *on* plants; they are grown into them and woven onto them. A typical dwelling is a dome of cured stems and moss felt, lashed and staked to a living root-column that continues to thicken through the winter. The roof is layered — patch on patch on patch, an outer thatch of dead sedge, then a living mat, then an inner felt — each layer still crisp because the settlement itself is young, recently colonised into this fresh hollow. Walls are double: two skins of plant with a gap between them for insulation against the damp cold. Storehouses stand raised on stilted root-legs above the wet floor. Bridges are not timbers; they are roots trained across a canal, encouraged, notched, and tied until they take.

This is a town. Unmistakably, planfully organised. There are broad clearings kept free of growth for traffic, and narrow lanes that run along the weeping seams of the walls, following the water. A main canal runs from the entrance seam to the far wall, branching into smaller irrigation cuts that feed nursery terraces — low shelves of moss where young plants are tended in rows as straight as any field. There is a quarter by the coldest seep, where the walls sweat the clearest water; a quarter by the seam of grey light, where heat-thieving work is done; a scattering of isolated huts out toward the edges of the hollow, with wide, empty spaces of moss between them. Density is low. Much of the floor is unbuilt, unclaimed, just breathing plant mass and trickling water. But the infrastructure is everywhere in its signs: a path worn smooth, a canal bank reinforced with woven roots, a gate made of two crossed stems, a storehouse door that fits its frame exactly.

The tools for all this are primitive to the point of fragility. You can read them in the carvings. No metal saw has cut these stems; they have been chewed, scraped with flint, and worried through with bone needles. No nails hold the lashings; they are sinew, plant fibre, and thin lengths of copper wire hammered flat and bent around a joint — simple metal, scavenged and reused, the only hard thing here, and it tarnishes green in the wet. A roof-patch is sewn, not nailed. A canal bank is pinned with root-stakes driven by hand. The craftsmanship is not crude. It is careful, because it has to be. Almost everything they make will be eaten, rotted, frozen, or grown over if it is not maintained.

And yet the builders are not the overwhelming thing here. The fauna are.

For every visible structure, there are a thousand lives inside the substrate. Peel back a cushion of moss — not that you should — and it is animal as much as plant: springtails like pale commas, mites moving in slow procession, translucent worms the width of hairs threading the root felt, froglets flattened against the damp walls, blind salamanders with white, lidless eyes nosing through the canals. The water is never empty. Skaters dimple it. Things underneath flash. The walls at night — and it is always night below that seam — are pricked with points of eyeshine, hundreds at a time, blinking out independently.

It is overwhelming not because it is fast or noisy. Cold slows it. Almost everything moves at a pace you can miss if you blink. The sound of the place is mostly quiet for that reason: moss swallows footfalls, drips are muffled by felt, the air is too wet and cold to carry far. What you hear, once you are still, is irregular: a drip from the vault, a soft tick as a root grows a hair's breadth in the cold, the faintest click-chorus from the far wall, a sudden, brief patter when a hunt goes through the canal — a ripple of silver under black water, a salamander or a dozen small predators moving in a coordinated wave, and then quiet again.

Predator and prey cycle here on a scale of millimetres, which makes them look like weather. A colony of root-grazers will strip a terrace in a week, and the plants will push new growth, and the predators that follow the grazers will thin out, and the grazers will return. The town moves with it. Houses are abandoned and reabsorbed; a wall that was a boundary becomes food. Nothing is allowed to dry out or to stay still. Water must be shed or stored, channels must be kept open against the growth that wants to close them, ice must be broken from the canals by the traffic of small feet. The whole place is active, in motion, but the motion is the slow, relentless motion of wet things in the cold: seeping, dripping, swelling, freezing, thawing, growing through a roof, being eaten, growing again.

The strangeness is in the lack of a line between categories. A house is also a plant. A plant is also a road. A road is also a meal for the grazers who maintain it by eating its edges. A dead animal becomes a brick in a wall; a living animal becomes a thatch for a roof. Some of the root-bridges keep growing long after the houses they served are gone, arching over empty moss, still carrying water in their hollow hearts. Some of the animals tend the plants the way the town tends the animals, and no one alive remembers which came first.

If you leave, you do it backwards, low, through the same wet seam, and the light outside — even the flat, cold light of the marsh — feels brutal. The pocket closes behind you. From above, there is only a tipped root-plate in low wet ground, sharp-edged, recently made, already mossed over. You would step over it without knowing that inside, in darkness and cold and perpetual water, in a space you could cover with your body, there is a whole town breathing on a mattress of plants, crowded with eyes, quiet except for dripping, and busy, always busy, growing and being eaten and rebuilding itself in the dark.
