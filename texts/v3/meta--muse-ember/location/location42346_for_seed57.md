You could cross it a dozen times and never know it was there.

The ground is low and level here, a broad basin of cold, silted water where nothing rises very far and everything spreads sideways. Submerged grasses and dark weed cling to the flat in loose, shivering mats. It is not deep in the way the open sea is deep, but it is deep enough that there is no dry place to put a foot, no surface to break toward, no air between one breath and the next. The water has weight. It fills the ears and presses gently at the joints. It is cool throughout, the kind of cool that finds its way through cloth and settles in the hands until they go clumsy.

The pocket lies under a slab that has only recently fallen.

That is the first thing that becomes clear, and then it unmakes the rest. The overhang is not an old cliff worn smooth. It is a roof of pale blue-grey mudstone, torn from the bank above and slumped down into the basin, still splintered along its edges where it broke. The rock has not had time to learn roundness. It breaks into knives and flakes. Fresh faces are exposed, banded and sharp, with corners that catch and tear at a careless hand. A fine dust of stone hangs where the fall disturbed the silt, though even that dust is settling at a pace too slow to notice. Somewhere in the slab a crack is still widening, a hair at a time.

Under its edge, half-buried in silt, is a parting in the algae curtain — no more than a handspan wide, a shadow where strands have rubbed thin against stone. You would step over it, if you could step, for it does not look like an entrance. It looks like the sort of darker place that happens everywhere under a root mat.

But inside, the dark is different.

The water inside does not quite mix with the water outside. It holds a straight, glassy edge against the murk, as if a lens of clearer water were lodged under the rock. Through that edge the green half-light from above dies quickly. Within a body’s length — the pocket is only as long as you are lying down, and barely wider than your shoulders — it becomes almost entirely dark. What light remains is a seep, a bruised green that comes through the algae curtain and is caught, and lost, and caught again by the foliage.

For the place is lush, in spite of the dark. That is its stubborn fact.

Inside, the vegetation does not grow in the manner of the meadow outside. It grows inward from every surface at once. Floor and ceiling and walls are all but indistinguishable, because all of them are covered. Ribbons of algae descend from above, dark olive and translucent as wet paper. Fans of leafy weed rise from below to meet them and interlock in midwater. Between them are braids and hairs and small, moss-like cushions, pale from lack of light, faintly ivory where sponge colonies have thickened. Hydroid plumes sway, though there is no current to sway them. They sway in opposing directions, down here and up there, as if answering to a tide in another direction, or to some centre of gravity that is not down.

There is nowhere empty to put a hand. Reach, and you displace a curtain of filaments that closes behind you more slowly than it opened. Reach again, and the same growth is there, rearranged. The stone beneath is rarely seen clean. It shows only in slivers between the braids: a fresh, laminated face; a cluster of unrounded pebbles locked in silt; a shard still leaning where it fell, its edge bright. The density is not beautiful in the way a forest is beautiful from a distance. It is crowded and intimate, a small room whose walls have grown fur and then fur upon fur, until the room itself is mostly living matter and the space for water is the space between leaves.

It is quiet here in a way that feels artificial, though nothing artificial has ever been here. The silence is thick. Far above, through the slab and the water, comes a dull double-thump at intervals too long to count — the surface, remembering itself. Nearer, sponges pulse. It is so slow a pulse that you feel it before you hear it, a faint sigh in the water against your skin, repeated and repeated, like something breathing in its sleep. Once, very close to your ear, a sound arrives that belongs to the far wall; once, a sound from the entrance comes from behind your head. The sounds are not loud. They are displaced, as if the pocket were turning them around inside itself.

Active life is rare. The presences are almost all rooted, sessile, colonial — plants and the animal colonies that live like plants. No fish crosses. No school turns in the dark. In the whole time you stay, one pale shrimp comes to the curtain and pauses, its eyes catching what little light there is, and then, without looking in, moves on along the outside of the rock. It is the only moving animal you see, and it does not enter. Everything else that is alive here is attached.

There are marks in the stone. At the back, where the silt is darkest, a flat slab has been scored with five shallow notches, struck with something pointed, too regular to be a fracture and too pointless to be a tool. Beside it, half sunk to its rim, is a cupped fragment of fired clay, the colour of dull brick, its broken edge worn a little now but still plainly made. And in a corner, three uncut stones lie in a rough crescent, enclosing a patch of silt that is a shade darker than the rest, as if something small had once been built there and the sea had taken the rest. Whether the stones were laid or merely slumped into that shape, the pocket does not say. They are the only built things in it: a fragment, a score, a low and ruined arc. No device, no light, no machine of any kind. The place knows only water and rock and growth.

The weirdness comes on gradually, as the dark does.

You turn to leave, or think you turn, and the entrance is behind you and also, impossibly, beyond the far wall, the same curtain of algae seen from the other side, with the outside light in the wrong place. The room is longer behind you than it was in front. When you stretch an arm back to touch the opening and an arm forward to touch the stone, both hands find their surfaces at once, though you had measured the pocket as longer than that.

Bubbles do not rise straight here. Released, they lean sideways as they climb, flattening against nothing, drawn toward the walls as if the ceiling were not up. A flake shed from the fresh rock falls, but so slowly it seems to hang, turning end over end, and by the time you have watched it for what feels like minutes it has travelled no farther than a finger’s width. The water within the water holds its edge. You can see a line where the clear dark of the pocket ends and the cloudier green of the basin begins, and the line does not waver, though the two waters are the same water, at the same temperature, in the same cold sea.

Up and down have been negotiated away. The algae growing from above and the algae growing from below meet in the middle and braid together, and in the braid the direction of growth is lost. Your shadow, in what light there is, falls two ways at once, thinly, from the entrance and from some reflection that has no source you can find.

It is, above all, slow. The crack in the slab will open in years. The sponge will pulse in its minute. The weed will add a fraction of itself in a season. A new splinter will part from the roof and join the still-unsettled silt below, and the pocket, which is younger than the plants in it, will take another small step toward being an old place, its edges gradually blunted, its curtain gradually thicker. But for now it is in that first, sharp stage of being — recently torn open, recently flooded with dark, recently overgrown — and the sharpness is still on the rock, and the crowding is still unthinned, and the silence still has the quality of something that has not yet learned what sounds it should make.

You could leave by the handspan of shadow under the slab, and the water would close its clear edge behind you in a moment, and the basin would go on spreading flat and cold and green around it. The pocket would remain, hidden under its fallen roof, packed with leaf and sponge, dark, quiet, cool, breathing at sponge-pace, holding its water inside its water — a small, fresh, flooded room at the bottom of the world, waiting for the very slow work of erosion to make it less new.
