The plateau does not have an edge in the way a hill has an edge. It spreads. From below you see a cliff of dark basalt, hundreds of metres high, weeping in vertical streaks, and above it a flat top that continues farther than the eye can comfortably follow. From above, standing at the rim where the lift brings you up, you see the same thing in reverse: the sea of plain, the clouds occasionally trapped below it in the morning, and in every direction a city laid out over the stone as if the stone itself had decided to build.

It is named Marrek by the people who live there, and after an hour you understand why they do not call it a town. A town would end. Marrek goes on in districts, in terraces, in smoke layers, until the horizon. On a clear day — and the light here is plain, ordinary daylight, white and even at midday, shadows short and honest — you can see factory chimneys forty kilometres away still working. The weather is its own. Warm air rises off the sunlit roofs and condenses where it meets the high, cool rock, so a low deck of mist hangs over the centre for hours after the rim has dried out. Afternoon rain rarely falls as rain. It falls as a fine, warm drip from every pipe and cornice, from the undersides of bridges, from the cliff face itself.

You are high — high enough that your ears popped on the way up, high enough that the wind finds you even in the sheltered streets, high enough that at night the stars are sharp — but it is not cold. It is a warm highland, equatorial in feeling. The stone holds the heat. At noon the bricks radiate it back into your legs as you walk. Your shirt sticks to your back not from exertion but from the damp that never quite leaves the air.

That damp is the second thing you learn. Water here is not a river or a lake in the usual sense. It is everywhere near the surface. Springs break out in the middle of warehouse walls. Basalt cliffs sweat even in a dry spell, darkening, then brightening, then darkening again, leaving mineral fans of pale travertine where they have done it for centuries. The city is cut through with canals in a loose grid, lined with cut stone, carrying slow, brown-green water at knee-height and shoulder-height in different districts. There are cisterns under squares, reservoirs behind factories, cooling channels behind smelters, a hundred leaking junctions. Gutters run even when it has not rained. Condensation beads on the inside of engine houses and runs in rivulets down the steel. Puddles in the high streets do not reflect the sky so much as they hold a permanent, shallow mirror of it. It is not a flood, it is not a marsh; it is a landscape that has learned to weep in every available place, and has been doing so long enough that everything has settled damp.

The ground itself is all mineral. That is the third thing, and the strangest, though Marrek has long since stopped finding it strange.

There are no plants. Not a tree. Not a shrub. Not a line of grass along a curb. Not a weed in a crack — and there are cracks everywhere, widened by water and time. The builders paved over the plateau in layer after layer until there was no soil left to root in, and the rock beneath is hard basalt that sheds what little dust falls on it. Between pavers, in the damp seams where water lingers, there is not green but fungus: black and ochre mats, orange crusts, pale films that spread and recede with the seasons like a slow tide. It grows on wet concrete, on the shaded side of brick, on the timbers under the canal bridges. It is living, it is dense, it is everywhere, but nothing rooted and leafed has taken hold in living memory. The eye keeps looking for it, out of habit, in the courtyards, along the canal edges, in the corners of the terraces, and keeps finding only stone, brick, steel, and fungus.

Because the stone is old, and everything here has come to terms with itself. Erosion in Marrek is not dramatic. It is established. Steps in the main stairways are worn into smooth, shallow bowls where millions of feet have passed. Handrails on the tram bridges are polished to a shine by hands. Thresholds sag. Limestone facades are streaked and softened, their corners rounded. Brick, where it is exposed to the canal spray, has lost its sharp edges and grown a kind of skin. Nothing looks new and nothing looks ruined. The city has weathered into its own state and holds it. Factories that were built before anyone now living can remember still produce at the same steady pace. The canals still carry the same slow flow. The plateau, which might have been scoured and broken, has instead been planed and terraced into a working surface that repeats itself for kilometres with only small variations: a smelter district where the air smells of hot metal and fish, a textile district where the mist is white with steam, a residential terrace where laundry and cages and laundry again hang over the street.

The built density is close. Houses are stacked on workshops, workshops on warehouses, warehouses on the vaulted waterworks underneath. Streets are not wide avenues but rooms outdoors, roofed half the time by washing lines, awnings, bridges, pipes. The industrial side is not hidden. Engine halls are open on one side so you can see the pistons turning in unison, slow and massive. Cranes move above the canal cuts. Electric lines sag from pole to pole, thick with perching birds. Smelters glow dull orange in the afternoon and throw up a thin smoke that flattens out under the high cloud deck and is carried slowly to the rim. It is mechanized, electrified, and scaled up, but it is not fast. That is the tempo of the whole place: slow.

And over, under, and through all of it is the fauna. Overwhelming is not too strong a word. Marrek belongs to animals in a way it does not belong to its human builders.

In the air, first. Rock pigeons in numbers you stop counting after the first thousand. They nest in every ledge, every unused chimney, every hollow under a bridge, in white, guano-stained colonies that make whole facades look as if they have been painted. Swifts cut low over the canals in the heat. Gulls ride up from the cliff and scream over the reservoirs. Bats pour out of the tunnel mouths at dusk in a continuous, silent river. In the canals, the water itself seems alive before you see why: carp and eels threading the slow current so densely that in the smelter channels they change the flow, snails coating the stone in a living grey, mussels clinging to the pipes, frogs calling from the damp corners under the bridges. On the ground, rats run the gutters in broad daylight, lizards sun on the warm bricks, cockroaches and moths and flies thicken around every warm engine and every scrap heap. Cats and dogs — feral, sleek, entirely urban — patrol the terraces. Bees and wasps work the fungus blooms and the open food stalls. Spiders have webbed every rafter in the engine halls.

They are not visitors. They live here at a density that matches the buildings. You cannot find an empty wall for long. A ledge left unoccupied in the morning will be occupied by afternoon. A filter in a waterworks will be furred with nesting material by evening. Workers expect this. They oil the gears where pigeons push twigs into the housings, they lift the grates where eels have tried to climb, they sweep guano from the tram tracks before it makes them slick. The animals, in turn, live off what the city cannot help giving them: warmth, water in abundance, fungus mats that never dry out, waste from the mills and kitchens, shelter in every layered crack. It is a complete ecology built without a single plant, except the imported grain and meat that moves through the markets and leaves its scraps behind.

The sound is the result. It is loud, but not in bursts. It is a constant superposition, so loud you stop noticing it and then notice it again when a pump rests for a moment. Water dripping and rushing in the canals, pumps thumping in the waterworks, the low hum of the electric tram, metal clanging in the workshops, and over it all the animal chorus — cooing, chirring, calling, splashing, the scrabble of claws on stone, the whirr of wings in the tunnel mouths. At noon the whole city seems to be breathing through its mouth open.

Walk for a day and you will have seen the same pattern repeated with variations: high sun, warm damp air, wet stone underfoot, a canal beside you, a factory ahead, a wall above you white with nests, fungus in the seams, no green anywhere except the faint green-brown film in the canal water. Walk for three days and you are still in Marrek. Walk for a year, the old residents say, and you will return to a street you left and find it almost identical — a little more worn, a little more stained, a colony a little larger, a pump a little slower. Nothing hurries. The cycles here are long: the slow pulse of the water table, the slow growth of the fungal mats, the slow movement of the bird flocks between districts, the slow polishing of stone by water and feet.

That is what makes the place feel settled rather than abandoned, and alive rather than mechanical. It is vast, it is high and warm and wet, it is built and industrial and crowded, and it is dominated by living things that are not plants. In the ordinary light, with the canals running and the engines turning and the pigeons shifting on every available inch of ledge, Marrek does not feel like a ruin of a greener world. It feels like a world that chose, long ago and without ever quite deciding to, to become this — a plateau city of stone and water and machines, worn smooth by time, and completely, insistently, full of animals.
