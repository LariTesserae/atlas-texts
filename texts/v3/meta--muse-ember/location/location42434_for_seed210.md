At first it is only cold, and the wetness of the cold. It gets into your mouth with your breath. The air is thin and dense at once — thin because you are high, dense because it is full of water that has not decided to fall yet. It beads on your eyelashes, on your sleeves, on the back of your neck, and runs, when it runs at all, in the wrong direction by a fraction. You are not in rain. You are in condensing air, in the moment just before rain, held there.

The light is dim and sourceless. There is no sun-path, no shadow that points honestly. Everything is lit to the same grey-green luminance, as if the inside of a cloud had been stretched out and laid down as a sky. Your eyes adjust, and then realize adjustment is pointless: the light does not change, and therefore does not have to be adjusted to. In that flat light, colour is low and wet. Greens are deep but muted, stone is black, water is the colour of diluted iron. You can see a long way and nothing is clear.

You see it all, because it is small. That is the first strange mercy of it. This is not a country. It is a room, a grove, a cathedral hollow — a single bowl perhaps two hundred paces across, set high. You can hold the whole of it in your mind at once, and yet you cannot stop being surprised by it. The walls of the bowl are new rock. Not old mountain worn smooth by weather, but fresh-split stone, sharp as broken pottery, slabs leaning in at impossible angles like the pages of a book slammed shut. The edges cut your boots. There is no soil rounded over time, no valley leading down. Underneath your feet the ground is still being itself for the first time: young, un-weathered, knife-edged. In places the stone is glassy, in places it is spongy with trapped water. It weeps.

The weeping is everywhere. Thin sheets of water come out of cracks and do not flow downhill so much as spread, and stop, and hang. Pools sit on top of the high rock as if this were a marsh at the bottom of the world, not a shelf at the top of one — marsh-edge water, black and still, filmed with pollen and iron. Ferns grow in the pools. Reeds grow out of bare stone. Moss grows thick and lush where it grows at all, in great, velvet-black mats that give underfoot like felt. When you touch the moss it is cold and saturated and it holds the print of your palm for longer than it should.

But the lushness is sparse. That is the arrangement. Between the lush places there is emptiness — long flats of wet stone, polished by nothing, pocked with small sharp holes, where condensation gathers in drops and waits. A thicket, then fifty paces of naked album of rock, then a single tree, then a pool. The presences are scattered with a terrible regularity, as if set out by a hand that wanted silence between them and failed. The tree, when you reach it, is enormous for the scale of the place, its trunk fluted and hollow, its canopy leaning over the whole bowl like a vault. Its leaves do not move. Not in a wind, because there is no wind. Not in your passing. They are lush and they are utter, dense, unfallen, and they do not tremble. There is leaf litter under it from what must be eternity, but no leaf is in the act of falling.

The life is abundant, and that is where the silence fails.

You began in quiet, with only the drip that is not a drip. Then the sound came, not as a crescendo but as a condition. It is loud. It is the loudness of a place with no soft surfaces: the grove is a drum. Every small noise is thrown back by the fresh rock and the wet canopy, multiplied, returned. There is hissing, regular as a valve releasing, every few seconds, from the reed beds. There is grinding, low, from underground — stone on stone, but rhythmic. There is buzzing, electrical, too clean to be insects alone, and clanking, distant and near at once, with no thing to clang. Frogs in the pools pump. You can hear them as pumps: a sucking in, a labouring exhale of steam. Birds — or things that have learned bird-shapes — call in identical phrases, over and over, in perfect synchrony from different corners of the grove, never finishing, never varying. The whole bowl hums at a constant, industrial volume. It is not getting louder. It is not getting quieter. It is a steady state of noise.

It takes a while to understand the industry of it, because there is nothing built here. No path, no ruin, no artifact left by a hand. The ground is untouched. And yet everything is made-looking, mass-produced, machined by a logic that has nothing to do with hands.

The reeds are pipes. Their stems are hollow, jointed at exact intervals, translucent, and they hiss when the pressure in the stone changes — which it does not, except in cycle. Something below the bowl breathes through them. The bracket fungi on the tree trunk interlock like gears, a whole wall of them, pale and layered, and they turn, very slowly, grinding spores to dust that falls and is reabsorbed by the wet stone. Moss grows in standardized mats, like felt laid on a factory floor, only it is alive and it breathes in unison, its surface rising and falling a millimetre at a time. The insects that crawl over it — thousands of them, identical, copper-winged, sparking faintly at the joints — are electric motors with legs. Their thoraxes crackle with stored charge. They march in files between the sparse presences, precise, unhurried, ceaseless. They do not hurry because there is nowhere to hurry to.

This is a world running as an engine without ever having been built. Production is total and net is zero. The fungi grind, the reeds hiss, the frogs pump, the insects march, the stone weeps, the water condenses, and nothing accumulates and nothing depletes. It is a young place, fresh-emerged, that has already settled into equilibrium — or rather, into a loop. If you watch a droplet form on the tip of a leaf, you will see it swell, darken, hesitate, and you will realize you have seen it hesitate before in exactly that way. If you look away and look back, the long-legged thing standing in the pool will be in the same pose, with its head at the same angle, though a moment ago you would have sworn it turned. New individuals appear where old ones were, doing the same thing. Life here does not grow; it repeats. It does not change its mind. The seasons are not a gradient. Time is a closed circuit, and the circuit is complete.

And reality, having to run a circuit, keeps breaking at the seams.

The seam is at the edge of the bowl. If you walk to the rim — and you do, drawn by the need to see what is below the high place — you find white cloud below, but it does not move, and it is not below, exactly. It is flush with the rim, like another surface. When you lean, your reflection in the pool behind you leans at a different time. The echo of the hissing arrives before the hiss. A tree casts a shadow that falls toward the light. Water that spreads across the stone stops mid-sheet and hangs in air, quivering, neither falling nor returning, held in a pose of falling. The pattern of the grove — pool, tree, reed bed, flat of weeping stone — repeats at the edge, overlapped, like wallpaper incorrectly hung, grove over grove, faintly transparent. You walk five steps and count your steps twice. You turn around quickly and for an instant the bowl is longer than it was. The fauna, abundant though they are, have legs that are left where they should be right; a bird lands and only then do its wings fold. Sound behaves as if it has weight and body as if it has none, and at certain places in the bowl the condensation is so thick that your own breath comes back to you as mist in front of a face that is not yours, though the face is. 

Nothing about this is maintained. Nothing is repaired. It was torn, perhaps — a fragment of a wetter, lower world, uplifted in a fresh fracture, still trying to run its marsh processes on top of a mountain, still trying to run its factory processes without a factory, still trying to run at all — and the tearing has not healed. The edges are too sharp because they are new. The processes are too regular because they are the only ones that fit. The weirdness is not an event. It is the material. The rock is wrong, the water is wrong, the light is wrong, and they are wrong together, consistently, in a way that makes the place hold together like a held breath.

So you stand, or walk slowly, in this small high room. Cold air in your throat. Dim light on wet, sharp stone. Lush places far apart. A tree that does not sway. Pools that do not ripple unless you have already seen them ripple. An unending, even, industrial loudness with no place to point to and say: there is the machine. No machine. Only life that has taken the shape of machines because the place could not afford any other shape. No change, because change would require a gradient, a before and after, and this place has sealed itself into a present that is complete.

And it will be like this when you look again. And again. The droplet on the leaf will swell, and hesitate, and be exactly where it was.
