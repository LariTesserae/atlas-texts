**The Weeping Hollow**

It begins as a floor, not as land. Long before you see a wall or a roof, the ground under your boots has already been made. There is no patch of natural dirt here, no grass, no wild stone, no place where the earth simply continues. The floor is packed clay stiffened with ash, dung, shell grit and crushed pottery, trodden and re-trodden until it gives back a dull, hollow sound. In the damp places it takes the print of a foot and fills it with water. Even the stepping stones are not stones at all, but large, irregular bricks, their edges worn down, their surfaces pocked where generations of hands have pressed them into place.

The whole place is small enough to take in at once, and small enough that its boundary is never far away. From the middle, on a clear and quiet moment, you can see the woven fence and low clay bank on every side — a ring of hurdles, mud-brick and bound reed perhaps eighty paces across. Beyond it there is no high ground, no dry field, only low, misted water-land fading into the dim. The Hollow itself is set down into that low country, slightly sunken, so that the water table is always close beneath the floor. It is a hamlet-size enclosure built in a hollow of its own making, and the world outside it is mostly weather, water and obscurity.

Everything here is handmade, and it shows. The bricks are forearm-long, straw-flecked, uneven; you can find the marks of fingers in the clay, the seams where one repair was smeared over another, the knots of rope and reed lashings that hold a wattle wall together. There is almost no metal. The tools left about — a stone hammer, wooden spades, reed knives, baskets, a small loom frame in one corner — are the kind that could have shaped all of it. The roof is a great low thatch of reeds laid over poles and clay pillars, sagging a little in the middle, and when the wind moves it makes a dry, papery rustling that sounds almost like rain even when no rain is falling.

That roof is why the light is so dim. Daylight reaches the interior only as a general greying, cut into pale ribbons where the reeds have thinned or where the builders left deliberate slits. At the edges, under the eaves, the gloom deepens. The air is cool all day, cool enough that the clay of the walls always feels cold to the hand and a damp sleeve never quite dries. Moisture hangs in it. It condenses on the underside of the roof and falls, drip by drip, in a slow, irregular rhythm that never fully stops. The walls weep, too, in seams and cracks, and the floor shines with a thin, permanent wetness even far from the pools.

Because it is small, and because it is mostly air, the emptiness is striking. Under the roof is one broad, hollow volume, undivided for the most part, and most of that volume is simply empty. Between the pools are long reaches of bare, ridged floor. A basket here, a clay pot there, a spill of fired shards, a workbench pushed against a partition — each presence is isolated by a disproportionate amount of nothing. You can cross the entire central space without passing anything at all, and your own footsteps are the loudest human thing in it. The few small rooms built against the perimeter are mostly vacant, their doorways low and crooked; one is blocked entirely with fresh mud, another opens directly onto a water channel. In a place so made, emptiness is not an absence of building. It is the building’s main material.

Water is the other main material.

The floor is not flat. It is a terrain of raised walking ridges and sunken pans, all of it shaped by hand. The ridges, knee-high and sinuous, are the paths; the pans are shallow basins of smooth, hard clay, each one ringed with bricks, some with a little brick stair descending into them. Narrow runnels link basin to basin, and reed siphons loop from one to the next. Around the outside, a clay-lined ditch collects the roof’s runoff and carries the surplus out through a wooden sluice in the bank, guarded by a grille of woven sticks.

The water does not behave as it would in a natural marsh, though it is never still. Rain strikes the reed roof and is shed inward as much as outward, running down pillars and along gutters shaped like vines. Groundwater seeps up through the joints of the floor. Condensation forms steadily on the cool roof and falls back. The basins seep into the layers below and return as damp at the wall bases. It is a closed, handmade cycle: the Hollow drinks its own water and sweats it out again. Even in the driest spell the pools do not vanish; they only draw back a little, exposing more of their polished, concentric rings.

Green, by contrast, is a rumour.

There is no soil for a plant to grow in, so plants grow out of the building instead. Moss makes soft green cushions in the joints of the bricks. A dull film of algae greens the edges of the pools and the wettest walls. In the one deep basin where the water stays longest, a thin stand of reeds and cattails has taken hold, pale and sparse, rooted not in earth but in accumulated silt and decay caught against the clay. A single creeper climbs beside a drainpipe from a large pot, its leaves few and dusted. That is more or less the whole of the vegetation: a few pioneer species colonising mortar, waste, and the cracks where the made world has begun, very slowly, to fail.

The animal world has no such hesitation.

The noise reaches you before the animals do, and it is loud. The central basins are frog nurseries, and their chorus can fill the hollow completely — hundreds of voices rising and falling in a pulsing, percussive roar that sets the dripping from the roof into the background. Tadpoles move in the shallows in dark, drifting clouds. Toads sit motionless on the ridges, then hop with surprising speed when a shadow crosses them. Around the water are the smaller, ceaseless lives: midges and moths outing at the light slits, slugs and snails working the weeping walls, beetles and woodlice in the damp timber and clay, spiders stringing webs across corners and channel mouths. Under the eaves, swallows have built mud nests against the pillars. In the roof voids, bats hang in the day and pour out at dusk in a flickering, chattering stream. On the drier ridges, lizards bask in the weak light; mice and rats slip along the wall bases; now and then a small water snake crosses a basin, and the frogs’ chorus breaks and reforms around its passage.

It is an abundant, active little ecosystem, but it runs on almost no plants. The frogs eat the insects, the bats eat the moths, the spiders take the midges, the lizards take the beetles, the snakes take the frogs. Everything else feeds on algae, on detritus, and, strangely, on the building itself — beetle larvae mining the straw in the bricks, slugs scraping the walls clean of film, termites and borers quietly hollowing the pillars. The Hollow is being eaten, slowly and steadily, by the very life it shelters, and that eating is part of its balance.

The strangest thing is how deliberately it has been built to be inhabited this way. It is a marsh that never existed until someone made one, and the workmanship is full of small, primitive accommodations: nesting niches bored into the walls at bat height, ledges for swallows, woven perches sticking up out of the basins for frogs to call from, brick steps so that small animals can climb out of deep water. Several of the doors lead nowhere useful. A gutter is shaped like a tendril. The roof’s sagging silhouette, seen from inside, is oddly like a canopy of leaves. The builders did not simply live with the wet; they imitated the wet, built a counterfeit wetland, and the animals have accepted the imitation so completely that it functions as the real thing.

Nothing here is new, and nothing is falling down.

The erosion is old and settled. Path ridges have been polished by feet to a shine. Pool basins have deepened where bodies and buckets have worn them. Bricks have softened at the corners, channels have incised themselves, mud patches have accumulated in layers like tree rings. The house leans a fraction where the ground gave, and the roof has sagged to accommodate it. When a wall cracks, water finds it, moss follows, and someone — the few human inhabitants, distant presences who move slowly through the empty spaces and mend things with fresh clay — patches it, adding another handmade layer to the whole. The result is not ruin but equilibrium: decay, repair, seepage, rebuilding, all turning slowly at the same pace.

So the Hollow persists in its own state: cool, dim, wet, loud, hollow, made. It is small enough to hold in the ear — you can hear a frog at the far basin as clearly as one at your feet — and empty enough that you can stand in the middle of it and feel alone in a building full of life. Above you the reeds drip. Below you the water moves through its handmade channels. Around you the chorus rises, breaks, and rises again, and somewhere in the walls, in the dark between one brick and the next, a hundred small mouths are quietly consuming the place that made them possible.
