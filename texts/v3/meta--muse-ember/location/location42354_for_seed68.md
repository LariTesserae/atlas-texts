It is smaller than it sounds.

You hear it first, from the low ridge where the road cuts through — a steady, unhurried thump, a clank, a whirr that never quite rises to urgency, and under it, louder still, the birds and frogs. Then the mist lifts enough to see that there isn't much to hear it *from*. A hamlet, really, though built like a town: a grid of roads, power lines sagging between poles, low warehouses and workshops and a row of plain houses, all set down in a shallow, cool hollow. The horizon is close in every direction. Behind you, a line of poplars. Ahead, the pale stump of a scarp. To the sides, fields gone to lush fallow, empty lots knee-deep in grass. You could walk the whole place in twenty minutes, and from its edge you can see the whole place at once. The boundary is not a wall. It's the damp air itself, hanging a little lower at the margins, and the sudden vertical drop where the young land ends.

The land is low and new. That is the first oddity everyone notices once they get used to the noise. The ground is flat — a basin, not much more than a few meters above the water table — but it hasn't been smoothed by time. At the north and west edges, the earth simply stops. A clean face of grey stone, ten or fifteen meters high, still sharp. You can see the stripes of soil where it was cut, the fresh cracks that haven't opened, the edges of the quarry-cut terracing behind the sawmill where no scree has yet softened the angle. The road that climbs out is cut through the same stone, its retaining walls bright, unweathered concrete with corners you could cut yourself on. Newer buildings wear paint still glossy. Older ones are only beginning to rust at the seams. Everything casts a crisp shadow at noon, because nothing has had time to round off.

And yet, in that sharp newness, green has arrived in force. Vegetation here is not sparse the way a desert is sparse. It is lush to the point of impatience. Ferns grow in the culverts. Moss thickens down the north sides of every brick wall and up the legs of the forklifts. Blackberries and nettles colonize the empty plots between factories — and there are many empty plots, which is the second thing you notice. For an industrial settlement, this one is strangely unfilled. A press shop, then a hundred meters of wet meadow. Three houses, then a timber stack going soft at the edges, then an orchard nobody has pruned in years, then a generator shed by itself in a field of clover. The built world is scattered, balanced by emptiness, and the emptiness has filled with life. Trees are tall and mature — sycamore, ash, lime — but they grow as individuals, spaced far apart, their canopies not touching, as if each was planted for shade for a single roof. Between them the understory is thick: cow parsley, meadowsweet, wet grass that never quite dries.

Water, here, does not behave like water in a river country. It waits.

This is a damp place, not a wet one. Rain comes intermittently, a thin, cool drizzle more often than a storm, and then stops. It doesn't race off. It settles. Puddles form in the ruts of the yard and stay for days, their surfaces skinning with pollen, denting when a crow steps in. There are no streams to speak of — only seeps. Along the foot of the scarp, springs bead out of the fresh stone and collect in shallow, muddy pools where frogs have already laid. Pipes leak in a slow, patient drip that has carved its own little stalagmite of rust and mineral under a valve, and the drip, drip, drip is the clock of the place. Inside the workshops, it's always just after rain. The cool outside air meets the faint warmth of idle motors, and condensation gathers on the undersides of corrugated roofs and on cold steel, beading and running in very slow lines down the walls, long after the sky outside is clear. In the morning everything metal is beaded. In the evening, it is beaded again. The air smells of wet soil and hot oil at the same time, and neither smell ever quite clears.

It is cool even in summer — a low, shaded cool, the kind that makes people keep a jacket over the back of a chair in June, that keeps the iron cold to the touch at noon. The light is normal: ordinary grey-white daylight, overcast more often than not, sun high and flat when it does come, glare on wet tin, electric lights off except in the sheds where the work is close. Nothing is dim like a forest floor, nothing is dramatic like a cliff at sunset. Just a plain, even illumination that makes colors honest: the green very green, the rust very orange, the concrete very pale.

The industrial heart is low and spread out. No chimney forest. A sawmill with its log deck and its slow conveyor that moves with a sigh every few minutes. A metal shop that makes hinges, brackets, buckets — things for farms — with presses that thump in a rhythm you could set a watch by, seven seconds, seven seconds, all day. A power house with two generators that hum and throw a little heat into the damp air, and enough pipework to go to every building and to nowhere in particular. Warehouses with roller doors, a water tower that rings faintly when the wind finds it, a small station house, a hall with a noticeboard. The infrastructure is dense along the roads — cables, ducts, conduits, drains, a loop of tarmac that is already patched and re-patched — and then, beyond the last pole, it just stops, and the fields resume.

The sound never stops, because so many things are doing it. The presses. The saw. The conveyor's chain. A forklift beeping in a slow arc. And the fauna, which is abundant to a degree that would be surprising anywhere else this built-up, has learned to be louder than the machines. Starlings chatter in the rafters of the open shed, thousands of them at roost, a sound like tearing silk. Frogs from every puddle keep a chorus that rises and falls with the presses, as if they were answering. Crows shout from the tops of the loose trees. Pigeons coo in the eaves. At dusk, bats come out in a thin, fast stream from under the bridge of the conveyor, and moths crowd the yard lights in a slow, damp snow. In the empty lots: rabbits, hedgehogs snuffling through wet grass, a fox that trots the road at the same time every evening, unafraid. Rats live happily in the warm wall cavities of the generator shed. Cats sleep on the motors. Sheep from a smallholding beyond the scarp sometimes get into the orchard and stand, damp and placid, between the houses while people walk past them with shopping. Everything has found a niche — the warm pipes to dry dew out of feathers, the rain barrels to breed in, the timber stacks to burrow in, the ledges of the fresh scarp for nesting swallows who sally out over the meadow in the normal light like stitches.

That is the slight weirdness, if you want to name it: how completely the living things have moved in, and how little they mind the thump. They do not flinch at the press. They time their calls to it. A frog will croak on the off-beat and the whole puddle will follow. A crow will wait for the saw to finish its cut before screaming, as if politeness were a rule. And the condensation inside the sheds keeps falling in its slow indoor rain, feeding mushrooms on the inner faces of the timber, so that inside it smells of forest as well as iron, and the walls weep even on dry days. No one with a sense of place finds that strange anymore. The water stays, the green stays, the machines turn — slowly. For this is a slow place, for all its noise. Nothing hurries. Rust creeps. Grass in the fallow lots grows and flops and grows again without being cut. Seasons pass in a muted way; spring lingers, autumn lingers, the air stays cool. The drip under the valve has been dripping since the pipes were laid, and the pool below it has grown by maybe a finger's width in a year. The presses have thumped at the same interval, at the same pitch, for as long as anyone can remember, and the stone face at the edge of town is still sharp, still fresh, as if the land only just arrived and hasn't made up its mind yet to settle.

You end where you began, at the scarp, looking back over a place you can take in at a glance: scattered roofs, wires, trees standing alone, fields, the thin white line of someone's washing, a generator shed in a meadow, puddles catching the normal light. It shouldn't hold together — industry and lushness, sparseness and a town, new stone and old trees, loudness and slowness — but it does, because everything here is patient. The water waits in its pockets. The plants wait in the empty lots. The animals wait on the warm metal. The machines wait for the next seven seconds. And the place, small and cool and damp and green and noisy, waits with them, still young, still sharp, still becoming itself.
