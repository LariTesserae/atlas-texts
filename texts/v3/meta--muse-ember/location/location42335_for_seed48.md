From the causeway the basin opens all at once, and it does not close again for miles.

It is low country, flat to the edge of sight. Sea on one side, a shallow drainage plain on the other, the port town of Brinemouth laid out between them like something poured and left to settle. The sky is pressed down low — not dark, exactly, but smothered. A warm grey ceiling of monsoon cloud and factory steam hangs over everything, and the sun, when it can be found, is a smudge behind it. Noon has the light of late evening. The sodium lamps in the yards come on early and throw thin yellow pools onto wet concrete, and go on burning in the gloom.

Heat is the other constant. It comes up through the soles of your feet from the concrete long after the rain has stopped. The air does not dry you; it holds moisture against skin. Sweat stays, breath is short, metal railings are warm to touch even in the shade. Puddles do not evaporate. They sit, shallow and warm as bathwater, reflecting the cloud, and join other puddles, and join the canals, until the whole ground seems to be a sheet of water held only by pattern.

This is not a new place. It has worn itself into its shape over a long, wet lifetime. Concrete is spalled and patched in squares, asphalt has been laid over asphalt until the road surface is a geology of its own. Brick warehouses weep: after rain, water percolates through their walls in dark streaks and beads at the mortar. Corrugated iron is rusted through in vertical tears, pipes sweat condensation along their whole length and drip steadily, as if it were always raining somewhere inside them. Rail lines sink a little into the mud between sleepers. Tanks are ringed with salt tide-marks and old spill stains. Nothing looks fresh; everything looks settled, weathered, in balance with the damp.

The ground itself is made, not found. Reclaimed marsh, peat and silt and building rubble, paved over and flooded again by a water table that is always two spades' depth down. Dig anywhere in a yard and you will find water. The canals that cut the town into blocks are not really below street level — on a high tide, or after a heavy downpour, they rise to lap the curbs. Pumps run often, small engines on concrete plinths, keeping cellars and underpasses from filling entirely. The whole basin drains slowly, sideways, toward the sea, and would drown in a week if the pumps stopped.

And between the buildings there is almost nothing.

That is the strangeness of the scale: it is urban, unmistakably — refinery columns and cracker towers in the middle distance, grain silos, a fish cannery, a rendering plant, smelters, cold stores, a power station, warehouses, rail sidings, truck depots, a scattering of two-storey worker houses with laundry hung to dry in the damp — but it is spread thin. Firebreaks, flood lots, chemical separation distances, yards for storing drums and containers and scrap, empty concrete aprons meant for expansion that never came. You can walk five minutes between one factory fence and the next, past flooded lots where the water lies still and brown and reflects nothing but cloud. From a silo top you see the whole town, and most of it is empty space. The horizon is uninterrupted: stacks, cranes, water, more stacks, and beyond them the sea-flat and the far, low hills dissolving in heat haze.

There is no green.

Not the green of a park, not a tree, not a verge. The rain that falls here runs off concrete and iron into grey-brown canals. A seed that lands in a crack will sprout for a day, perhaps, and then be drowned by rising alkaline water, or sun-baked on hot masonry, or washed into a drain. The soil, where any is exposed, is saline, compacted, oil-stained. So the town has learned to do without plants entirely. What grows instead is the other sort of life: a black mould bloom on the north sides of walls where the sun never quite reaches, a slick of grey-green bacterial slime in the slow corners of the canals, fungal crusts on damp timber, the white salt flowers that effloresce on brick after a dry hour. Oxidised copper roofs go a dull verdigris. Glass is filmed. But nothing photosynthetic, nothing that needs a season. The palette is mineral — wet grey, rust red, sulphur yellow from the refinery earth, the brown-black of water — and it stays that way through every rain.

Into that emptiness the animals have come in numbers that make the emptiness feel crowded at ground level.

They live on what the town discards, and the town discards a great deal. Grain spills from the silos feed rats by the hundreds in the culverts, and the insects that live in the spilled grain, and the geckos that live on the insects, and the cats that patrol the warehouses after the rats. Fish processing waste and warm effluent feed the canals: air-breathing catfish and carp and tilapia thrash in water just warm enough to poach in, their backs breaking the surface in a constant, silent boil, especially when a factory discharges a fresh surge twice a day and the whole canal suddenly moves. Mosquitoes and midges hang over every still puddle in visible clouds; when you pass, they lift and resettle. Water beetles dive from the surface film. Crabs work the muddy culvert mouths at low tide. Frogs call, briefly, from storm drains, and stop when a truck passes. Skinks and house geckos cover the warm walls in the evening, hunting in the light of the lamps, perfectly still until they are not. Water snakes cruise the canal edges. Herons and egrets stand knee-deep in the flooded lots, patient and grey, waiting for fish. Above them, the air is worked over by birds in overwhelming, unhurried numbers — feral pigeons on every ledge and tank top, mynas and sparrows in the rafters, swifts stitching under the eaves, gulls and terns over the harbour, bats flickering out from under the bridges at dusk. Dogs and cats, well-fed on scraps, sleep in the shade of idle machinery, lifting their heads only to watch the rats.

It is an active place, and a quiet one. The activity is low and constant, rather than loud: water moving in the canals with the tide, steam venting from stacks in soft plumes that never quite rise, heat shimmering off roofs, the slow swing of a crane, the endless drip from pipes, the ripple of fish. The factories mostly run themselves; you hear them at a distance as a muted throb, a clank somewhere far off across an empty yard, a train's brakes squealing two streets away and swallowed by the damp air. The animals, for the most part, do not make noise at this scale. The city hums under the weather rather than over it.

That is the rule of the place, in the end: water near the surface, heat in the air, light kept dim by cloud and steam, and a town built of weathered concrete and steel that has been emptied of plants and filled, instead, with animal life feeding on the industrial detritus of a wet, hot, low coast. Rain comes and goes without changing anything fundamental; the ground stays wet, the walls keep weeping, the canals keep boiling with fish, the geckos keep their places on the warm walls, and the empty lots stay empty — flooded, reflective, waiting — while everything that lives here goes on working through them, quietly, in the grey light.
