It does not begin with a border. You climb into it slowly, and the climbing stops feeling like climbing at all.

The land rises — high, much higher than it should be for this much water — in long, weathered swells. Not peaks. Not cliffs with sharp edges. Everything here has been rounded by a very long time of rain. The hills are broad-backed and settled, like old animals lying down, their shoulders covered in a wet skin of green and brown. You can stand on one and see the next, and the next, and the next, until the mist takes them. On a clearer hour you might see further, but the light never quite clears, and the land never quite ends. It is large enough to have its own weather, and it does: clouds are born over the far bogs, travel a little way, drop their weight, and die before they reach you. One valley can be in rain while the ridge you are on holds ordinary, grey-white light. The sky feels close and normal, but the air is always cold.

It is never dry. That is the first rule, and everything else obeys it.

Water is not mostly in rivers here, though there are rivers — braided, slow, iron-colored in places, fast and white where they fall. Most water lives elsewhere. It weeps from rock faces in dark streaks, even when it has not rained. It condenses on leaves and runs, unhurried, into the soil. It stands in sheets so thin you walk through them without noticing, then gathers in peat pools the color of tea. The ground is saturated to just below the surface, so that every footstep finds it. After rain it does not puddle and vanish; the whole plateau holds it for a while, like a sponge that has learned to be patient, and then gives it back slowly through a thousand small channels you can follow with your eyes but never quite map.

The cold keeps it. Even at midday your breath shows a little. The water in the springs is near-ice, and numbs your hand instantly, though the deeper compost of the bog is, paradoxically, warm. Under the moss, under the fallen leaves, fungi are working in the dark, dense and industrious, decomposing at a scale that warms the peat from within. You can feel it through thin boots — a faint, steady heat in a cold place, the waste-heat of a landscape running its own factories underground.

Because nothing is bare for long, and nothing is allowed to be bare.

Vegetation is lush, but not the lush of warm forests. It is low and packed. Trees grow thickly, crooked from the wind, their canopies interlocked so tightly that little light reaches the floor. Under them there is another canopy — of ferns, of broad-leaved shrubs that grow in the shade of the trees that grow in the shade of other trees. On the open bogs, where there are no trees, the ground is a crowded mat of sedges, cotton-grass, sphagnum in greens and reds, and plants whose names you would have to learn slowly because they are all growing through each other. Moss grows on moss. Lichen grows on the moss. A fern grows out of the lichen. In time, everything is covered by something else that is alive. There is no empty surface to stand on. Even the stone, where it shows, is softened with algae and veined with roots.

And yet there are no buildings. No roads, no fences, no markers, no paths that persist. If anything was ever made here, the land has taken it back fully and smoothed it into its own form — metal into soil, stone into bogstone, field into marsh-edge. What remains is almost nothing, and what is almost nothing is already being digested. You will not find a shelter. You will not find a clearing made by hands. The place is untouched not because no one has come, but because it does not keep what is made. It processes it.

That is the industrial part of it, though there is no industry you could point to.

This landscape works at scale, continuously, without rest. The rivers are its conveyor systems, sorting stone by size, grinding it down, delivering minerals to the flats where fungi and roots will take them apart molecule by molecule. The waterfalls — and after rain there are hundreds, spilling over ledges that were dry in the morning — fill air with static. On still, damp days you can feel the prickle on your skin, hear an occasional soft ticking in the mist, as if the whole plateau is lightly electrified. Plants grow in unnatural rows on some terraces, as if planted, processing light in shifts. Beetles are metallic, polished, green-black, and build with the thrifty precision of engineers, sealing chambers, moving water. The bogs themselves are vast chemical works: peat that locks carbon for centuries, releases it in slow pulses, filters every drop that passes through it until the water downstream runs clear and cold. Everything is produced, broken down, and produced again at a volume that dwarfs any single creature.

The fauna are abundant, and mostly felt rather than seen.

Deer-sized grazers move in herds through the high marsh, parting the sedges, their bodies always wet to the belly. Amphibians fill the pools so densely that the surface moves. Under logs and in the leaf litter, invertebrates are beyond counting — worms turning the saturated soil, snails bright on stems, insects that do not fly far, that walk instead, because the air is heavy with spores and seeds and it is easier to stay low. Birds are there, but they are not the sound of the place. Predators move through the same wet corridors, following scent-currents, and the whole system is in motion: herds migrating between high summer bogs and lower winter shelter, fungi fruiting in waves, willows leafing and dropping without any season you could neatly name, because the rain comes and goes without calendar.

But it is quiet. That is the second rule.

For all this life, for all this moving water, the sound is muffled to almost nothing. The spongy ground absorbs impact. The dense vegetation absorbs call. The animals, mostly, do not call. They communicate by touch, by chemical trails smudged on wet bark, by the slow vibration of stems, by postures held in the mist. What you hear, when you stop, is very quiet: a drip that lands and does not splash, a sedge bending, your own breathing, the faint click of a beetle, water moving somewhere under a mat of roots. It is not empty silence. It is a silence that is full — packed, the way the land is packed — with presences that have chosen not to make noise.

Light, when it comes, is ordinary. Normal daylight, washed a little by high cloud, saturated by wet color. Greens are deeper here because they are wet. Browns are richer. The red of sphagnum, the rust of iron in the streams, the white of cotton-grass — all of it holds its color because the light is not harsh and the water does not let it fade. At dusk nothing dramatic happens. The light simply thins, the mist thickens at ankle height, then at knee height, and the cold settles in a little more. The land continues working in the dark, at the same pace.

There is a mild strangeness to it, the kind that grows on you rather than startles you. Some spring pools are warm from below and grow algae in spirals. Some trees have grown their roots above ground, stepping over bogs on stilts of wood. Some slopes are covered in a fine, rust-red dust that is not dust at all but spores, which will rise in a cloud no wider than your arm if you brush them. Lightning, when it comes in the summer storms that build over the plateau, does not always strike down; sometimes the air simply lights from within, a brief corona over the wettest flats, and everything smells of ozone for an hour.

It has existed in this form for a very long time, and it will continue without you. The erosion is finished and unfinished at once: the channels are settled, the hills are at peace with the rain, but the water is still moving stone, and the bog is still creeping, centimeters a year, over stone that was once bare. The balance is not delicate. It is saturated. There is no room between presences for anything new to insert itself, and yet everything is still changing — leafing, rotting, flooding, drying for a day, flooding again.

You leave, eventually, not because you have reached an end, but because the mist comes back down and the tracks you made behind you have already softened and filled with water. The high wet land does not mark your passage. It closes behind you, green over green, weeping over stone, working and working in its own cold, vast quiet, with no built thing to show for all that industry, and no need of one.
