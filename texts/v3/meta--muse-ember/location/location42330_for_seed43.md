From above, there is almost nothing to find.

A low, long roof of warm clay tiles and lime-plastered thatch, baked by the sun, running with a thin scatter of grass that no one planted and no one quite pulls up. A single stair-hole in it, brick-lined, with a heavy wooden door hung on bone hinges. That is the whole surface presence of the Undermade.

You go down, and the world announces itself before you see it.

Sound comes up the stair-hole first, and heat with it. Not oven heat, not close cave air, but the accumulated warmth of a small place where many bodies work and cook and sleep in a volume only a little larger than they need. A loom clatters above a hammer striking above a child shouting above chickens complaining below that. Liquid drips, somewhere, into clay. A bell — small, hand-cast, rung by a rope — marks the hour, not because anyone needs it to, but because by the time the sound has climbed the shafts it has become part of the air.

Below, it is not dark. That is the first surprise, and the defining one. The Undermade can be walked in ordinary daylight.

This is a hamlet. Perhaps eighty or a hundred people, a few dozen families who count as one extended household to outsiders. From the entry stair to the farthest wall is sixty paces. One wall of thick mudbrick encloses it all, a single oval ring pressed against the earth that was dug away long ago, though no earth is left to show for it now. In plan it is a stepped bowl. Three terraced rings descend to a central court at the bottom, each ring built out from the wall, each supported on timber posts and brick piers, so that from the center you can look up through layers of bridges, balconies, lofts, drying racks, and roof hatches to a strip of sky cut into squares by the shafts.

Everything is made. That is not a figure of speech here. It is the law the place was built under, and has been maintained under ever since. No natural substrate is allowed to stay exposed. The floor — beaten clay, then plastered, then re-plastered — is worn concave in the middle of every passage where feet have passed for generations. The walls are mudbrick inside and out, whitewashed in patches, grey with smoke in others. Even the supporting “rocks” that hold up terraces are bricks shaped and fired to look like rocks. The rafters are split, adzed, and fire-hardened timber. The mats underfoot are woven reed. The pots are clay. The handles on the tools are bone. The fuel is dung dried into bricks. When a crack opens to raw earth, it is filled and smoothed the same day. If you dropped a stone here, in a week it would be ground into mortar or carved into a hammer head. Nothing keeps its wild shape for long.

It is dense in a way that takes time to get used to. Nothing is wasted vertically. Shops are built underneath houses, and storage lofts are built above shops, and animal pens are built underneath both. Laundry hangs from walkways at head height so you duck. Gourds, bundles of herbs, coils of rope, spare loom shuttles, caged birds — all hang close enough to brush your shoulder. Chickens dust-bathe in the dust of the middle terrace while cats sleep on the warm wall-caps above them and a goat nibbles tethered rope in the central court, its feet polished by a hundred other hooves. In the passages you do not so much walk past people as negotiate past them, elbows in, stepping sideways, carrying your own bucket with care so as not to knock the rows of earthen jars that line every shelf. The whole hamlet sounds like it is touching itself.

The work is all hand-work, and it never really stops. That is the second defining fact: primitive tech, pursued without pause and without much machinery at all.

There is a small forge at the lowest level, a clay kiln hood with bellows worked by a boy, producing perhaps one new iron nail or knife blade a day. Everything else is wood, stone, clay, fiber, and bone: stone hammers, wooden looms that thump and snap in a dozen doorways, spindles, adzes, rope pulleys that haul water and grain up and down the light shafts, hand carts with solid wooden wheels for moving bricks the twenty paces that matter. No wheel turns for more than a few minutes at a time because there is nowhere far to go. Potters shape while they talk. Weavers sing to keep time with the shuttle. Someone is always replastering a wall, always re-tying a thatch edge, always sweeping ash from a hearth into a pile to be made into new bricks. The technology is simple, but it is multiplied by density and by hours: early tools, kept very busy, in a small room.

Warmth gathers as you go down. The top terraces, near the hatches, are dusty and relatively dry — flour dust, soot dust, the fine grit that falls from the tiles. They are what an outsider would notice first: parchment-dry timber, dry brick, dry laundry snapping in shaft-drafts. Dampness lives in pockets. At the bottom, by the kitchen and the communal bath hollow, the air is moist and close. Steam rises from a great blackened pot and from a sunken clay tub where people wash in rotation. Breath and cooking condense on the cooler northern wall in a slow bead that gathers, runs, and falls in a line they have known for generations. Beneath that line someone has permanently set a shallow bowl, and next to it another, and next to those a row of jars with cloths over their mouths. Condensation is not a problem here; it is a supply.

Water in general arrives the same way: intermittently, locally, and then held. Rain, when it comes, does not soak the hamlet so much as perform in it. It pours down the light shafts in bright curtains, hissing on the hot tiles, splashing into the central cistern dug — built, lined, and re-lined — at the court’s low point, and into a dozen smaller basins placed strategically under every drip-line. For twenty minutes the place is loud with drumming water. Then it stops, and the dripping continues for hours, tap, tap from eaves into bowls, because everything that was wet slowly gives its water back. In the very bottom corner, where the floor dips a finger’s width, a shallow puddle almost never entirely dries after rain. Frogs live there, small and mud-colored, and algae greens the rim of the clay. Elsewhere, damp means a darkened patch of plaster, a smell like wet brick, moss beginning at a crack. The Undermade as a whole is dry. It is a dry place that has learned to drink in small sips.

Vegetation is sparse for the same reason. There is no soil except what people have mixed into boxes and terraced planters and woven baskets. A few hardy herbs — thyme, dust-mint, a bitter leaf for tea — grow in wall pockets where plaster has been scooped out and filled with compost, watered from the drip bowls. Cuttings of vine have been trained along strings up the sides of the light shafts, branch by stubborn branch, to reach real sun, and in summer they throw a wavering green shade across the stair. One or two potted shrubs sit by the entry, more as furniture than as plants. Sprouting grain is set in saucers near the kitchen, a crop tasted more than harvested. And the moss. The moss is the loveliest thing in the Undermade when it rains: fine, bright lines appear along the mortar cracks of the northern wall, as if the bricks themselves had learned to photosynthesize. It grows only on made surfaces here. Wild ground, being none, grows nothing.

Fauna, by contrast, is abundant, and layered from roof to floor like everything else. At the very top, in the dark inlets of the chimneys, bats hang by day, squeaking awake at dusk. Swallows and sparrows nest in clay cups that were built for them a hundred years ago and are repaired every spring; they return to the same cups, and the pigeons that coo under the eaves have their own small ledges, cleaned — after a fashion — by the women who feed them. Mid-level belongs to cats, who patrol the wall tops, and to the many house mice that live inside the brick pores, and to the insects: crickets in every warm crack, flies over the kitchen, beetles in the grain jars. The central court is for chickens and a single pig and a few goats, who are also, in effect, waste processing and alarm systems. In earthen tanks by the bath, small fish eat algae and mosquito larvae. Earthworms work the compost boxes. A tame owl lives in the forge rafters and is fed, and in return takes moths and sometimes a mouse. At night the cats hunt the mice and the owl hunts the bats’ dropped insects and the chickens roost in their built coop, and it all balances in a noisy, close-quarters predator-prey that never leaves the walls.

It is loud at midday in a way that seems impossible for its size, because every sound is trapped and reflected by plaster and brick and low ceilings. The looms thump against the hammering against the children chanting their lessons against the splash of the bath tub against the bell for the meal against the goat bellowing for his tether to be moved. Even conversation has to rise. People shout greetings up two terraces. A door that is not quite shut bangs in every draft. The drip bowls add a high, perpetual counterpoint. At night it drops, but it does not go quiet; crickets take over, then the owl, then the drip, then someone in a loft coughs and shifts and a pot lid clinks. The Undermade has a lower volume, never silence.

Erosion here does not mean ruin. It means the place has settled into its own long-existed state and is now wearing smoothly, like a river stone worn by hands. Steps are hollowed in the center. Door edges are rounded to the thickness of a thumb where shoulders have passed. Plaster shows twenty layers where a hand has smoothed over the last repair without quite removing it. Tools are shiny where grips have polished them. Smoke has blackened beams to a rich, even brown. A brick that was chipped a decade ago is now a soft edge that a child has learned to feel for in the dark without looking. Nothing looks new because nothing is new, and yet nothing is collapsing because repair is not an event but a rhythm. Worn, settled, in balance — the whole hamlet feels like an object that has been held a great deal.

The light shifts all day and tells the time more accurately than the bell. Sun falls in sharp, normal rectangles down the main shafts, moving across the terraces. Dust and midges turn in the beams like snow. On a cloudy day the light goes flat and even and lamps of oil and fat are lit in niches, although no one seems to need them much. On a clear day the topmost rooms feel almost outdoors, and the bottom court, even at noon, is in a cool, green-tinted shade from the vine strings. People plan their work around those sun-patches without thinking: drying here, fermenting there, sitting to weave where the light falls.

Perhaps the only truly unusual thing, though it is not very strange at all once you are used to it, is how completely the living things here have accepted the built world as their world. The swallows do not build; they occupy. The frogs live only in clay jars and in the one permanent puddle that people will not pave over. The vine has learned the strings. The moss knows the mortar. Children are born here who have never touched unworked earth, and who think of a “stone” as something fired in a kiln. If a wild seed blows down a shaft and sprouts in a crack, it is either transplanted into a box or carefully removed, because an unmade thing staying on its own would be a kind of failure. An outsider might call it odd. Inside, it feels complete.

Weather moves through quickly here, because there is so little air to hold it. A wind comes down a shaft and lifts laundry and sends a ripple of voice across the court. Rain drumming, then dripping. Sun warming the tiles until the whole roof sighs and radiates heat down into the top rooms at night, keeping them warm even after the hearths die. Fermentation bubbles in a covered jar in the kitchen. A hen hatches in the coop. A repair crew replasters the south wall, mixing new mud with old bricks ground to powder. A kiln is fired and the forge boy works the bellows in time with the potter’s wife singing. All of it overlapping, all of it in motion, multiple processes at once, none of them large.

If you stand in the central court at about four in the afternoon, with sun in the shaft, a goat pulling at its rope, chickens clucking under the basins, the smell of wet clay and boiled grain and dung and thyme all warm in the air, you can hear the farthest loom and the nearest drip at the same time, and feel the heat of the tiles above and the damp cool of the bowl at your feet. It is small, below the ordinary ground, entirely made by hand, dense, loud, warm, only damp in places, thinly green, thickly alive with animals, and old enough to be smooth.

The Undermade does not feel built upon the earth. It feels built instead of it — a single, settled, hand-made place, worn into balance, that its people remake a little every day so that it can go on being exactly what it is.
