At the very top, where the air gives out, there is a room.

You would not know it from below. The highest ridge narrows and narrows under a perpetual lid of cloud, the light never quite deciding to become day, and then it stops, not in rock, but in work. A spiral of small flat terraces, hand-laid, climbs out of the mist to a point no wider than a doorway. There is no bare summit. There is no natural substrate to stand on at all. The mountain's crown has been built up, layer by layer, of shaped clay and stone and woven matting, damp at the edges, warm to the touch despite the thin altitude. Vertigo is constant. One step beyond the last terrace is cloud, and beyond that, a drop so complete it feels like the underside of the world.

The room is hidden in that point. A low flap of reed mat, heavy with condensed water, hangs over a tunnel that you must crawl through on your knees. Your shoulders brush both sides. The tunnel is not dug; it is constructed, a tube of coiled clay and lashed wood, and it smells of wet earth and smoke and hot herbs before you ever enter.

Inside, heat hits like an opened oven door, and stays. It is dim — a low, amber dimness from embers and from a few pale, fungus-lit niches in the walls — but the heat is total. Your skin begins to sweat and then to condense, droplets forming on your forearms as on the walls. The elevation outside is a fiction in here. Inside, the pocket is tropical, saturated, close. The air does not move so much as churn: steam rises from a central kiln, hangs, thickens, runs down the walls in slow rivulets, collects in hand-shaped channels, pools in basins scooped from solid clay blocks, and drips again from the low dome overhead as a fine, warm rain that falls not straight down.

Everything is made, and everything is crowded.

The floor is a mosaic of uneven tiles, each one pressed by a palm, each one still soft in places where it has not yet set. The walls are reed mats plastered inside and out with mud, the ceiling a dome of overlapping baskets. There is no empty surface. Shelves of braided fiber sag under rows of pots, seed bundles, skeins of dyed fiber, bundles of herbs tied with hair cord, stone hammers and bone needles and wooden spades. Tools hang from every nail and peg, within arm's reach of other tools. Pots hang from pots. Baskets are stacked inside baskets. Whatever is not in use is stored, and whatever is stored is being repaired, or is waiting to be fired, or is already half-unmade to be remade.

The lushness is not wild. It invades the built-ness. Ferns burst from wall-pockets where soil has been packed by hand into the plaster. Moss and bright green algae fur the wet sides of the kiln and the water channels. Vines — bean, squash, a bitter leaf that curls like a question mark — climb the reedwork, climb the shelf legs, climb the people. Small trees grow in large clay urns by the doorway, their roots circling visibly against glass-hard clay. The vegetation is dense, succulent, over-fed on steam and on the constant drip, and it is tended like livestock. Nothing grows from the earth. Everything grows from a container a hand has made, and most of those containers are themselves sprouting mold.

Sound is the first thing that makes the room feel larger than it is, and then smaller. It is loud, unceasing, multiple. Stone on clay: *thock, thock, thock.* A loom clacks with its wooden sticks. Water slaps into basins, hisses on the kiln. A shallow fire crackles despite the wet. Somewhere a group of voices — few, but joined — chants in a pattern that resets every few breaths, not quite a song, more a counting. The wind is shut out at the tunnel, but the work makes its own weather. Under it all is the deep, irregular breathing of the kiln as it draws, and the steady tick of cooling tiles contracting in the heat.

The people move through this with a practiced, packed choreography. There are not many. You pass the same hands — dark, clay-stained — at the wheel, at the loom, at the fire, at the garden urns, and then again a moment later in a place you have not yet seen. They are making things with primitive means that match the place: coil pots, weave mats, press tiles, lash frames, fire without metal, cook without vessels that would survive. No machine hums. No metal tool except for a single, dull knife passed from hand to hand. Everything is finished by hand, and most things are not finished at all. A wall stands half-plastered, its reeds showing like ribs. A dome above the kiln is still bubbling up from the heat, soft clay swelling into shape. A shelf is being built while pots are already set upon it, still wet.

That is the rule that holds the place together, and then breaks it. Nothing here has eroded. Nothing is old in the way stone is old. The edges are fresh, the colors unfaded, the clay un-cracked because it has never dried, never been left alone long enough to weather. The room is emerging. It is under construction in the most literal sense, and the construction does not proceed in order. You watch a roof being woven first, then the walls being raised to meet it, as if the intention of the place arrived before the place did. You watch a pot being glazed before its clay is wedged. A tile, broken in the morning crush, is found whole by evening, its crack running backwards into the hands of the person who will break it tomorrow. The builders do not seem surprised. They simply work around the surplus, using things that exist for the second time.

The weirdness is not an event. It is the medium. Rain inside falls in a slow circle, returning to the basin it came from without ever quite landing. A doorway you entered at ground level, crawling, opens inward onto a short stair that faithfully goes down and deposits you, breathless, higher than the summit. Shadows separate from the objects that cast them and pool independently by the kiln, where everything is slightly darker than it should be. Echoes arrive before the sound that made them — you hear the hammer's report a fraction before the hammer falls, and the chant precedes the mouths by a breath. In one corner, the heat is so intense that moss grows black; in the next, inches away, a basin of water steams with cold. Time in the room is not linear but layered: the kiln seems to be firing, has fired, and will fire in the same cycle, and the herbs hung to dry are simultaneously green, brown, and seed.

Wild life, of the kind the mountain outside would normally offer, is almost absent. That is part of the bargain. No birds nest here, though a single grey moth — blind, pale as unglazed clay — circles the embers in a slow, devoted orbit, and there are three small frogs that live in the wall channels, translucent, breathing through their skin, appearing and vanishing in the condensation. Otherwise the only fauna is the garden: the vines, the fungi that bloom overnight on the tile floors and are eaten by noon, the tiny white worms in the soil of the urns. A predator-prey of sorts continues without animals — fire eats the damp wood, wood eats the clay, clay eats the water, water eats the fire, and the plants eat all the structures that hold them, gently strangling loom and shelf alike.

And yet the place is violently active. Not still, not preserved. Steam cycles, water cycles, fire cycles, weaving cycles, growing cycles, all overlapping and interfering. A plant will put out a tendril in the time it takes to blink, testing for a handhold. The dome will sigh and expand. The floor tiles will shift in the heat, making a low, tired creak under your knees. Smoke finds a new crack and pours through it. The builders shift, chant, hammer, pour, press, lift, and begin again on the thing beside it, because there is always a thing beside it, packed so tight that starting anything means continuing everything else.

When you crawl out again — and the tunnel, you notice, is now a little longer, and the flap a little heavier — the summit outside seems to have grown by another terrace. The cloud has not lifted. The dim light has not changed. But from out there, looking back at the tiny built point above the sea of mist, you can hear the room continuing: the hammering, the chanting, the hiss of water on hot clay, the rain that falls inside and never reaches the outside world. It is a pocket, a thumbnail world, at the highest possible place, hot and wet and overgrown and overmade, still being built, already finished, and no longer entirely obeying the same reality as the mountain it crowns.
