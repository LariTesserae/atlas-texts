There is no morning here.

There is no first light to adjust to. Your eyes do not adjust, because there is nothing for them to do. Above you is not sky, exactly — it is the same warm, wet black as the ground, only less firm. The black is not empty. It is close. It presses, damp, against your face, and condenses there. In a breath or two your lashes are wet, your sleeves are wet, the back of your neck is wet, for reasons that have nothing to do with rain. The air is too full to hold any more. It rains sideways off metal, off rock, off your own skin, in a slow, patient weeping that never quite starts.

You are walking, though you would not call it walking so much as wading through a country that is only half-finished.

Under your boots the ground yields and then doesn't. Black water, ankle-deep in the hollows, knee-deep where it chooses, covers almost everything. It is not a lake. It has no shore you can find. It is a skin over the land. Water up from below, not down from above — the water table has risen up through the soil and sits there, warm. In places it is bath-warm where it has passed through something hot and old. In places it is cold enough to make your toes ache, but mostly it is warm, in the way a held breath is warm. Your feet sink into silt that gives with a soft sigh, then find suddenly a sharp edge, a fresh stone, a slab cut at an angle too clean for weather.

That is the first surprise, if you can call it that, in a place that is still being made.

The land is young. Not young like a meadow in spring. Young like something recently shoved up, or laid down. The banks are sharp. The terraces are sharp. There are low cliffs a man's height, their faces still raw grey, fractured in straight lines, no soil softening them, no roots holding them, no rounding of the edges by water. The stones at their feet have not been tumbled. They are angular, they cut. Where the mud cracks, the cracks have not yet filled with leaf-mould; they smell of new rock and old iron. New ground is still emerging — imperceptibly, in the way a finger-nail grows. If you crouch by one of those fresh ridges and stay long enough, you will feel it lift against your palm. A fraction. Then another fraction. It does not happen fast enough to watch, only fast enough to notice, after an hour, that the ridge that was at your ankle is now at your shin.

It is low land, and open. That is the second thing. Because you cannot see, you measure openness by other means: by how long a pipe runs under your hand before it ends, by how far an echo of your step travels before the black water takes it, by how far you can go without meeting anything and still have the sense that there is more country beyond the more country. This is not a room. It is a valley, and then another valley beyond it, and then another, stitched together by shallow lakes and causeways. At least seven basins, maybe more, each with its own weeping rock face, its own slow pump-house, its own fields of black water. You will never see the whole of it because the darkness forbids it, but you can feel its extent in your calves, in the ache that tells you that the surface you are on, while low, does not end.

The density is what makes the quiet strange.

It is mostly quiet. That is true. You can hear your own breathing, slow because the air is warm and full, and the drip of water off a ledge somewhere to your left, irregular — drip, drip, long pause, drip. A low hum, felt more than heard, in your teeth and in the soles of your feet when you stand still. No wind worth mentioning. No birdsong, because there is no light for birds to read the morning by. And yet the place is crowded to a degree that makes quiet feel like a kind of pressure.

You cannot move without displacing something.

In the water, things brush your legs. Not fish in the familiar sense — long, blind, pipe-thin things that turn away at the last moment, their sides slapping warm against your shin, or gathering in curious, dense shoals that part around your boots and close behind you. On the banks, underfoot, there is soft bodies, a great many of them, layered. Amphibians the size of hands, warm as stones in sun, that do not hop so much as flow. Slugs with the texture of wet rubber, climbing in overlapping curtains up the sides of concrete blocks. Things in the mud that make the mud feel alive, that shift when you weigh on them and then shift back, rebuilding their nests in the print of your boot before you lift it again. In the air, just above the water, a cloud layer of insects so fine you feel them only as a tingling on your lips, as a faint warm static against your cheek.

The fauna is abundant because there is food that does not need light. That is the whole economy here, and it took you a while to understand it.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of discouragement. You will walk a hundred paces and find nothing green to touch. Here and there a clump of reeds, black and tough as cable, growing not in soil but in the seams where concrete meets rock, their stems leaning toward a light that is not there. A pad of moss, pale grey, felted, on the north side — north is a guess — of a wall, spread thin as skin. A fern, or something fern-like, that folds its fronds away from your fingers with a soft, tired sigh, as if it were listening rather than seeing. Anything that grows here grows toward warmth, toward seepage, toward the hum. It does not photosynthesize. It does something else, slowly, using the minerals in the water, the heat off the pipes, the faint electrical fields that prickle the air. It is enough to be sparse, and to be strange in the sparseness: a plant will grow in a perfect duplicate of its neighbor, stem for stem, mirror reversed, as if copied by a machine that learned to copy badly.

Because there are machines. That is the third thing, and it changes the first two.

You find them by heat first. A pipe runs through the black water, hot enough to steam — though the steam is invisible in the dark, you feel it wash your hands, you hear it hiss in the silence, one long exhale every few minutes. The pipe is layered with other pipes, big ones and small ones, and with cables thick as your wrist, and with the animals, who cling in rows to the warm metal as if to a reef. Further on, your hand finds a wall. Wet concrete, pitted, beaded with condensation. A door, heavier than it should be, standing open on a hinge that does not squeak. Inside, the air is warmer still. Inside, a slow machine.

Not a fast machine. Nothing here is fast. A great wheel turns, one revolution perhaps a day, perhaps a week, its paddles dipping into a channel of black water and lifting it, lifting it, setting it down again on the other side. A conveyor, half-sunken, extends out of the building into the marsh and disappears, its belt still, its rollers crusted with silt and with small blind crabs using it as a causeway. A factory shell, no roof, its floor a shallow pool, its interior threaded with copper wire that has greened and grown into the walls like a vine. A chimney, tall enough to be impressive if you could see it, that gives off no smoke — only heat, and a faint buzzing that makes your molars ache.

They are electrified. You can feel it in the air before you touch the fence: hair lifting, a metallic taste on your tongue, a tingling in the damp skin of your forearms. The charge runs along rails half-buried in mud, along fences that run for a long way and then stop, mid-field, for no reason. Along the pipes. But none of it makes light. That is a rule here, and it is not a natural one. Years ago — and years here are long, patient things — the works stopped making light. All the energy goes to heat, to motion, to the slow lifting of water, to the faint hum in your teeth. On the walls inside the pump-house there are fixtures, dead glass, black with silt. Whatever light once tried to be born here was absorbed. The wet air took it. The black water took it. Or the place itself refused it, and the refusal has settled in, like silt.

The buildings, when you find them, are scattered. They are not a town. There is no town. They are points: a single shed two valleys over, its corrugation rusted through, its inside full of nesting animals and warm, wet straw that was never straw. A watchtower leaning at an angle, its ladder broken halfway up, its top occupied by something large and slow that does not move away when you approach, only breathes. A row of concrete blocks sunk to their windowsills in a lake, connected by a causeway that goes nowhere and then, after a while, is the only road. A fence. A locked gate, locked against nothing. An artifact now and then in the mud — a boot, a tool chest, a sign whose letters you can feel with your fingers, smooth and familiar, and that say something that does not quite make sense in this context. They are partial remains, not ruins. A ruin implies a downfall. These seem to have been left, and then the land grew around them, and then new land grew under them, and they are still, in some small way, in use. A machine will still turn, once a day, for no product you can name. A pump will still lift water from a lower blackness to a higher blackness, forever.

And then the wrongness, which has been there from the beginning, begins to insist.

It is not dramatic. It does not flash. Because everything here is slow — the water rising, the land lifting, the machines turning, the animals growing over decades in the warm mud — the wrongness is slow too. It accumulates, like condensation.

You put your hand in a pool that is still — the water here is often still, in the way deep water is still, holding its surface perfectly flat — and you feel, reflected back at your palm, not the cold, but a warmth that is your own warmth arriving a moment late. You hear your step in the mud, and then, a half-second later than it should be, you hear a second step, a fraction out of time, stepping where you are about to step. You walk in a straight line along a causeway, counting your paces, and you arrive at the pump-house you left an hour ago, but from the opposite side, and the door is now on the other wall, and the water pattern on the concrete is different, as if it had been rained on from inside.

The animals do these things too. You will brush past a hand-frog — soft, blind, translucent — and feel it again a few paces on, identical, its skin at the same temperature, its breathing at the same rate, as if the same individual had been copied forward along your path. In the reeds, there is a plant that grows in a spiral, and then, two clumps later, the same spiral reversed. Measured animals change their count if you count them twice: in a hollow of mud there are six warm bodies along the rim, and when you come back they are seven, and they are all the same size, and none of them remembers being new.

If you stay, your own body begins to participate. Time loses its corners. You do not know if you have been walking for an hour or a day, because the light — the absence of light — gives you no markers, and the machines give you only their slow tick. A full turn of the great wheel might be a morning. Might be a season. The fauna, meanwhile, go on their cycles regardless: blind fish spawning in warm pipes, their eggs hatching in layers — larvae upon larvae, dense as gravel — amphibians mating in the shallow black water where the chemical traces of the factories make the water buzz faintly against their skin. They do not hurry. Nothing does. A tree, if you could call one of these sparse stalks a tree, might add a ring in a decade.

Erosion is still happening, of course, but it is young erosion, which is to say it is mostly creation. The sharp scarps shed new stones. The new stones are colonized before they settle. The silt that washes off the terraces feeds the water, which feeds the filter-feeders, which feed the things that flow like amphibians, which feed the slow birds that fly into you in the dark with soft wings and enormous, blind eyes, landing on your shoulder out of curiosity and then leaving, their weight gone before you can decide if it was real.

The place holds itself together with a kind of wet, warm density. Pipes within pipes. Nests within nests. Water within the air, air within the water as bubbles rising off the machines. Moisture beading on every surface, running in thin, patient lines down the concrete, down the rock, down the animals' backs, down yours. The quiet is not empty; it is saturated. It is the sound a crowded place makes when everything in it has learned to move slowly and to make as little noise as water.

You will not map it. Maps fail here in the usual ways and in one unusual way: a map drawn in the morning — and there is no morning, but people draw them anyway, in the back of notebooks, by memory — will show a causeway in one valley that, when you go to walk it, is in the next valley too, continuing. A contour line will not stay where you put it, because the ground is still lifting, a fraction at a time, under the line. A measurement of the water depth taken with a stick will give you a different depth the second time, because the stick has been moved in time as well as in space, and the water was deeper then, or will be.

So you do a different thing. You learn the rules by living under them for a while, the way you learn the rules of any place that is older than its machines and newer than its soil.

No light travels here. Touch is the truer sense. Heat is the truer sense. Water is always near the surface, and often above it, and it is warm more often than it is cold. The land is low and open and still being born, sharp and raw, and getting higher while you stand on it. The works are scattered, industrial, electrified, and slow; they make heat and motion and hum, not illumination, and the animals have taken them for habitat, layer upon layer. The plants are few, and they grow toward warmth and current, not toward sun. The animals are many, and they are blind, and they are everywhere, and they are mostly soft, and they make almost no sound, which is why the place is quiet. And everything is slightly wrong, in the way a copy is wrong when it is made by a slow machine in the dark over a very long time: repeated, reversed, arriving late, arriving early, present in two places at once, not present when you look for it and present when you stop.

It will go on being wrong, and wet, and warm, and dark, and slowly getting newer, for as long as anyone cares to stay. The great wheel will make its next turn. The ridge will lift another fraction. The fish will brush your legs again on their way past, in their dense, patient shoals, and the water will close behind you as if you had never been there, except for the print of your boots, which the mud remembers for a while, and then, slowly, forgets, and then, slowly, remembers again.
