It is not dark.

That is the first surprise, and it changes everything else. Daylight comes over the edge in a broad, unhurried wash, grey-blue and true in color — moss green, limestone grey, bog water brown-green — quite ordinary daylight, the kind that shows the dust in the air and the wet shine on a leaf. And yet all around, above eye level, is rock. You are below the surface of the country, inside it, in a bowl open only at the top.

You enter by a cleft, really — a fresh fracture in the rim where the ground has split and slumped. The track down is not a road. It is a worn line between fallen blocks, made and remade by feet and paws. The sound here is not rain. It is water that never stops arriving through the stone: drips from overhangs, thin threads running down fracture planes, a soft ticking, plinking, seeping that goes on whether it is raining or not. The rock is wet to the touch even where the sun touches it. Condensation beads and runs. Where a slab has fallen recently — and many have, their edges still sharp enough to cut a glove, their faces unweathered, pale, only just beginning to take lichen on the north side — water finds the new crack and weeps out of it in a slow, dark stain.

The cold is immediate but it is not the clean cold of a summit. It is damp cold. It gets into the back of the throat, into wet boots, into fingers. Breath fogs at midday. A thin skin of frost can still be on the shaded peat at ten in the morning in the deepest hollows, and the water in the side pools holds a skim at night. This is a cold place that is also a wet place, and the two together make it lush in a way that has nothing to do with warmth.

As you work down around the fallen blocks, the rim withdraws. First it is a wall, then a high shoulder, then it disappears behind you altogether. The sky becomes a ceiling, an oval of overcast light directly overhead. The valley floor opens below, and opens, and keeps opening.

This is not a hollow in miniature. It runs for kilometers in a chain of linked basins — a long sunken marsh, a town-or-valley-size depression sunk into a cold limestone plateau and ringed by near-vertical, very young walls. The erosion is fresh. The strata are laid bare in horizontal bands, unsoftened by soil. New gullies cut the slopes after every freeze-thaw, carrying angular pebbles down in small, noisy — no, in small, muffled avalanches. The river that feeds the place comes over the rim at the far end as a waterfall on one of those fresh faces, white for a moment, then dark again as it drops into the marsh and loses itself. At the other end, lower, it gathers and disappears again, sighing into a field of sink-cracks in the peat, going back underground the way, one suspects, it came. The whole system is held in a kind of geological held breath: recently collapsed, recently exposed to the sky, still settling, still sharp.

The floor, when you reach it, is not solid in any human sense. The water table is above the ground. Peat is saturated black and springy; step and your boot sinks a handspan and water wells up black and cold around it. Springs bubble up unbidden through the moss in clear, icy beads. Between them are channels of standing water, dark, slow, reflecting the ceiling of sky. Everything that lives here lives at the edge of water or in it.

And the vegetation is not sparse, not struggling. It is packed, low, and unremittingly green. Sphagnum moss in thick, spongy carpets. Sedges and cotton grass in dense tufts. Watercress and marsh marigold and buttercups in the shallows. Dwarf willow, alder saplings, dwarf birch crowded together into impenetrable thickets that rustle without wind. Ferns in the dripping shade of the walls. The green is the green of things that never have to want for water and never have to endure a hard freeze at the root — cold-adapted, yes, but waterlogged and fed by limestone and by rot, and unpruned by drought. Nothing dries out. Nothing is bare for long.

It is only after a while that the pattern in the marsh declares itself, and then it cannot be unseen.

This is a built place.

Not built with tools. There is no metal here, no glass, no straight line cut by a blade, no artifact that did not begin as something alive. Everything is built with tooth and paw and bill and body, out of mud, twig, root, reed, bone, and living plant. And it is built at settlement scale, in layers, with the patience of infrastructure.

The main channel is a street, of a sort, kept clear by current and by the bodies that use it. Across it, at regular intervals, are stepped dams — beaver work, generations of it — rising in terraces like a rice terrace inverted, each pool a level lower than the last, each dam woven so tight with willow and mud that water holds behind it in a sheet. Along the shallow margins stand the muskrat lodges in clusters, dome on dome, in rows along the water, each with its underwater entrances and its small ventilation chimney of hollow stems poking through the roof. In the reed stands, the birds have woven towers — hanging, braced, many-chambered nests lashed to reed stems above the water, swaying together. The banks are a terrace in themselves: thatch of roots and peat raised and re-raised by voles and water voles, pierced by hundreds of burrow mouths, each one a door, black and round, opening just above the waterline. Root bridges cross the narrower channels where willow saplings have been bent and grown together. Causeways of packed mud and root run between the lodges, not wide, but continuous, never quite dry, worn smooth by traffic.

There is no empty ground left over. This is the other shock of the place. Density is not high, it is total. Every square meter is a presence or the trace of one: a moss mat rafted with duckweed, a footprint in the mud, a tunnel under a hummock, a nest, a burrow, a feeding platform of chewed stems, a half-sunken log held fast by algae, a clear run of water kept open by swimming. Look closely and the green thickens into other greens — the camouflage of a frog's back, the eye of a fish under the surface film, the paired tracks of a vole. Over, under, and between: underwater burrows under the floating mats, mats under the reeds, reeds under the willow canopy, canopy under the rim of rock. The town is stacked vertically as much as it spreads.

And it is alive, abundantly, noiselessly. At first you think it is empty because it is so quiet. The cold air pooled in the bowl is still — the walls stop the wind, the dense vegetation stops what is left — and the wet absorbs sound the way it absorbs everything. What you hear is almost nothing: drips, a soft splash where a fish turns, the rustle of sedge in the river's undertow, chewing somewhere in the reeds, the low croak of a frog muffled by water, a duck's wing brushing a stem. A heron can hunt in the shallows without a sound. An otter moves through the main channel as a dark line under the surface, breaking it only with its nose. Teal and wagtails and snipe work the margins. Mink slip between lodges. Pike hold in the deep pools under the dams. Newts crawl in the cold shallows. Freshwater shrimp and caddis larvae work the channels in the mud. It is a full predator-prey world, and it runs at a low volume, as if everything here has learned that loudness does not carry and is not needed.

The smell is peat and cold mud and algae and musk, clean and faintly sweet.

Nothing here is strange. That is perhaps its most unsettling quality. No glow, no monster, no impossible thing. Only the excess of perfectly ordinary processes, pressed together until they look like a city. Water, cold, and growth, packed into a hole in the ground, left to build.

It is also constantly in motion, though quietly. The river pulses with melt from the plateau, with rain that arrives frequently and then keeps arriving through the rock for days afterward. Water levels rise a finger's breadth in the afternoon and fall again at night. Ice forms and splits and melts in the margins. A dam is breached by a flood and is rebuilt before dawn, twig by twig. A lodge is abandoned and slumps into the peat within a week, and muskrat build on top of the slump, raising the ground millimeter by millimeter. A willow thicket is eaten back; a new one takes its place. Everything built must be maintained, because in this wet cold, dead wood rots, mud slumps, and water is always trying to level the terraces back to a single sheet. The city is a dynamic equilibrium — building and sinking, growing and rotting — that has held for a long time because the work never stops.

You learn the rules of the place by standing in it, and they are simple. Water is master: you do not drink from the still pools, you do not store anything dry, you do not expect to walk far without wading. Cold settles: it pools in the bowl at night and hangs as mist in the morning that burns off only when the sun is high, so dawn is white and silent and the marsh is invisible under a skin of fog. Building is survival: a structure that is not repaired becomes part of the peat. And nothing is wasted: bone is used in a dam, a dead reed becomes a lodge wall, a dead animal becomes peat, peat becomes a hummock, a hummock becomes a nest.

From the middle of the marsh, looking back, you can see how far it goes. The built terraces step down toward the sink-field, and beyond the place where the river disappears, the bowl narrows and opens again into another basin you cannot quite see, and beyond that another. The rim is continuous, the sky is continuous, the water is continuous. There is no center to this town and no edge to it, except the rock walls that hold it all in and the places where water comes in and goes out.

If you stand still for a long while, your footprints fill with water. Your breath fogs. A water vole crosses the causeway in front of you, pauses, and goes on. Somewhere in the reeds, something chews, patiently, at a stem. The drips keep their slow account on the limestone wall behind you. The light stays ordinary, the cold stays damp, the green stays impossibly, densely alive — and the city, with no tools and no noise and no rest, keeps rebuilding itself out of the marsh that is forever trying to take it back.
