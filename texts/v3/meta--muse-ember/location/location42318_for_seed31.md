The rock face is weeping.

Not with a stream, not with a fall — with a sheen. All the way up the new cliff, water beads out of the stone in a thousand places, joins and runs in a thin, clear sheet over the fresh fracture planes, and drips at the bottom into moss. The stone has not had time to round itself. It is sharp, angular, pale where it has recently split, black where it is still wet. Edges would cut a hand. Clefts are clean, unfilled. No scree softening the base, no deep soil. Just water finding every crack the uplift left in it, and leaving nothing in return.

You climb by hand-cut steps, chipped into the basalt, each one slick and worn only by the feet of the town above. The air is warm even this high, warmer than it should be, sun on a high equatorial shelf. Your skin stays damp without sweating. Mist ghosts off the rock and off your arms. The sky above is entirely ordinary — a normal blue midday, a normal white sun, shadows falling the way they do anywhere else — but from up here it feels closer, and the air is thin in your chest on the climb. There is almost no wind to push the mist off. What wind there is moves slowly, and the whole climb is quiet except for the drip, drip, drip of the cliff into its own basin, and the muffled rush far below where the sheet-water leaves the plateau edge and drops to the plain.

When you reach the top it does not close in. It opens.

The plateau spreads past the immediate view in a regional sweep, town-and-valley scale: ridges within ridges, all sharp and young, still lifting, in a sense, against the light. Below the edge, the low country is a sea of haze. Above, the high ground goes on for miles of open, waterlogged meadow and shallow basin. Vegetation is lush, but it is not jungle. It is thick and low and green in a saturated way — ferns in the damp hollows, grasses up to the knee, broad-leaved shrubs along the channels, moss over every stone, water plants crowding every pool edge. It grows in patches, because the ground itself is patchy here: a pool, a mat of fern, a dry hummock with grass, a pool again. Water is everywhere near the surface. It shines between the grass. It fills footprints. It fills the straight, hand-dug ditches and sits in them, perfectly level, month upon month.

The most noticeable thing is what does not move.

There are no birds over the meadows. No flies come to your face when you stand still. No bees work the shrubs, no beetles under the stones, no frogs in the pools, no fish in the clear water. The lushness has no animal in it to eat it or pollinate it or sing over it. The plants are the green, spore-bearing, wind-spread, water-spread kind that can manage without animals. The fruit trees in the gardens are hand-pollinated by people with brushes, because there is nothing else to do it. Flowers are mostly pale and unscented. In a normal place this would be an impossible ecology. Here it is simply the ecology. You get used to walking through a landscape that is biologically complete on the surface and empty underneath, and then you keep walking, because the quiet makes you listen more closely.

The town announces itself first by its waterworks.

Straight lines cut across the curve of the land: stone-lined channels carrying spring water from the weeping cliffs to basins and fields; low retaining walls terracing the meadows into levels that hold water at a constant depth; paved causeways of flat basalt slabs laid by hand across the marshy ground, wide enough for two people, crossing and recrossing; small arched stone bridges over the channels. It is organized, settlement-scale construction, layered and deliberate — a town in the open. And it is built entirely with primitive means. No metal nails, no sawed timber with iron, no wheels doing much work. The houses are stone at the base and dark timber above, thatched with the meadow grass, joined without hardware. Tools are chipped stone, wood, bone, woven fiber. Pots are clay. The channels are clay-piped where they go under a road, stone where they run open. The bridges were set stone by stone, and laid flat.

And it is sparse.

Between house clusters there are long, unbuilt intervals — an empty furrowed field, a wide pool, a meadow with no fence. Houses do not crowd. A household may have fifty meters of wet green to its neighbor, a hundred to the next. The plaza is a large flat paving with a central basin, always full to the same mark, its water plashing softly from a pipe. A storehouse, a communal cistern, a few workshops open to the air. All of it quiet. People move at a walking pace, speak low, step on the wet stone. There is no dog. There is no livestock, no chicken scratching in the yard. Food is root crops, greens, grains, hand-pollinated fruit, things grown in the level, saturated terraces. The work is manual and unhurried because the making of things is manual: weaving, mending thatch, redigging a channel with a stone spade, carrying water in woven baskets.

Everything is damp. Wood never fully dries and takes on a dark, polished color. Cloth hung inside rooms stays cool. Stone underfoot gives a little. In the morning, condensation forms on every surface and runs down walls in fine lines. The air is warm and full, sometimes white with mist in the hollows, sometimes clear enough to see the whole shelf and the far, sharp ridges beyond the town, but the mist always returns to the same hollows by evening. It does not bring rain in storms. There are no storms. The wetness is not weather, it is condition: the plateau is so high and so recently raised, so full of springs, that water is simply always coming up and sitting and coming up again.

That is the rule of the place, and you feel it as equilibrium rather than as inaction. The pools do not rise or fall. The channels do not flood their banks. The gardens stay the same green. The houses are repaired with the same stone from the same fresh-cut face on the cliff, and they look new because the stone is new and because nothing destroys them: no fire weather, no wind, no insects in the thatch, no freeze, no drought. The cliff still weeps in the same places. The sharp gullies have not softened. A place can be young in its geology and old in its habits, and this shelf has reached a balance between the two — uplift and water, building and tending — and it stays there. Day after day the light is normal, the temperature warm and even, the sound almost nothing: a drop into a basin, a footstep on wet slab, the faint continuous thread of water falling off the edge far away, leaves that do not rustle because they are too wet and the air too still to rustle them.

From the plaza, if you look west, the town is a pattern of lines and pools and roofs scattered far apart across an open green table, with the new, sharp ridges rising behind it and the high plain stretching unbroken to the other side. It is a city made by hand, in a wet, warm, animal-less highland, suspended in a silence that never changes — and nourished, continuously, by the water that keeps finding its way out of the rock.
