It does not announce itself the way a lake does. From the level of the surrounding ground it is only a dip, a coolness, a place where the air is different. You step down a slope of loose, grey earth and the ground falls away faster than it should, bench by bench, until you are standing well below the fields and roads above. The rim is not a sharp crater. Whatever cut it was made a long time ago, and everything since has been slumping, softening, and settling into place. The edges are rounded. The loose material at the bottom of each step has come to rest at the angle it will keep. There is nothing fresh or raw about it. It has the worn, finished look of a place that has been left alone for long enough to reach its own shape.

The pit is small. From the one dry shelf where you can stand, you can see the whole of it. It is perhaps thirty-five metres long and not much more than twenty across, wider than it is deep, and most of what is inside the boundary is water. The water comes up almost to the lowest bench and covers the floor completely. There is no beach in any ordinary sense — only a narrow margin of wet silt where the slope meets the surface, and then the surface itself, flat and still, taking up most of the view.

The air down here is cool in a way that has nothing to do with shade. The pit is below the surface of the land, and the cold air sits in it and does not get moved. Even on a bright day, when the light above is ordinary and strong — true daylight, grey-blue at the edges, colouring things as they are — the light inside is muted by the walls. The sun only finds the water for a short time around midday, when a pale patch moves across the centre of the surface. For the rest of the day the water is in the shadow of its own rim. It is not dark. It is the even, cool light of a room you never quite leave.

Under the surface, the water is clear. That is the first surprise. You expect a flooded hole to be turbid, but you can see to the bottom, five or six metres down, and the bottom is flat. It is not a natural floor. As you wade in from the shelf — the water is cold enough to make your ankles ache, and the cold does not change as you go deeper — the slope under your feet gives way to a straight bench, and then to a level plane of silt. The bench is submerged. Its edge, visible through the water, runs in a line too regular to be made by weather. The silt on the floor is laid down in thin, horizontal bands, grey, then darker grey, then a rusty brown, like the layers of some manufactured material poured and left to settle. In places the silt is firm, in places it is soft and gives underfoot with a muffled sigh. Small stones are scattered sparsely across it. A boulder that fell from the wall long ago, another, another. Much more space than objects. The emptiness is the main material here.

The walls above the water are the same stone and earth, weathered smooth in patches where water has run over them for years and then stopped running. There is a dark mineral stain at exactly the waterline, all the way around, marking a level that does not move. Below it the stone is darker and damp. Above it, the benches dry out to a pale, dusty grey. Moss has taken hold on the north-facing side, a thin, bright green skin in the places that stay shaded and wet, but it stops abruptly where the stone gets too dry or too bare. Nothing else has colonised the walls. They are too steep, too still, too poor.

Vegetation, where it exists at all, is sparse and in the wrong places to make a lush pond. Five or six tussocks of sedge stand in a broken ring where the shelf is shallow, their stems grey-green and motionless. They do not spread. They have the look of plants that arrived once and have simply persisted at the limit of what they can tolerate, without thickening or flowering much. In the water, there is algae — not a bloom, not a green scum across the surface, but a dark film growing on the stones and on the one flat object down there, a crust that makes them look older. That is all. No weed forest. No trees leaning over. The pit is too cold, too sheltered from wind, and the margins too narrow and shifting for anything else. Outside the rim, on the higher ground, there is grass and bracken, but the boundary is sharp. Inside, the plants are few because the water does not feed them in any generous way, and because nothing in here feeds anything else.

That absence becomes the thing you notice most, once your eyes have adjusted to the clarity. The water is transparent and sunlit and nothing is in it. No fish shadows pass over the silt. No insect skaters dimple the surface. No frogs. No dragonflies dip and rise. You stand still for a long time, knee-deep, and the silence is not an effect of mood but of fact: there is no animal life in this water at all. It is not poisoned in any dramatic, steaming way. It is dead by accumulation of small, ordinary reasons. It is cold all year, because it is groundwater coming up into a hole that was dug down to meet it; the water is the surface of an aquifer, exposed. It is still, because the walls keep the wind out and nothing flows in or out to make a current. It is slightly acidic and loaded with dissolved iron from the spoil around it, so the silt is banded with rust. The few plants grow slowly and do not shed much that could rot. Nothing has the food, the oxygen, the movement, or the seasonal change it would need to start a life cycle. And so nothing starts. The pond has no ecology beyond the plants, and even they are barely managing.

The industrial past is everywhere and nowhere. There are no buildings. No sheds, no gantries, no engines. The pit itself is unbuilt. But the whole geometry of it is industrial: the benches, the flat floor, the straight submerged edge, the bands in the sediment as regular as the output of a process. At the north wall, just above the waterline, is the mouth of a single pipe, iron, rusted to a colour close to the silt, encrusted at the lip. It does not drip. It has not dripped in a long time. Down on the floor, under a metre of water, lies a square slab of concrete, its edges sharp, aggregate showing through the grey, four drilled holes in it, covered in the same dark algal crust as the stones. That is the extent of the made things. Two artifacts, both below or at the edge of the water, both long since incorporated into the silt.

What remains industrial is the water itself. In the sheltered corner where nothing moves, a thin film lies on the surface — iron oxidising where the groundwater meets the air, going rusty and faintly iridescent, a skin left by chemistry rather than by machinery. It breaks and reforms in the same place, in the same pattern, without ever quite spreading or disappearing. The smell of the place is not the smell of a natural pond. It is wet stone, cold mud, iron, and a faint, distant smell of tar or hot metal that comes over the rim from the industrial ground beyond. If you climb back up to the edge, you can see it: spoil heaps, a fenced substation, the line of a pylon crossing the sky. The hum you have been hearing without noticing — a low, even electrical note, very quiet — comes from there, muffled by the walls. Down inside the pit, the hum is softer than your own breathing.

Sound here is mostly the absence of sound, punctuated by things that do not vary. Occasionally a drop of water falls from the wall into the pond, a single tick and a ring that expands slowly and fades without reaching the far side. Your steps in the silt are thuds that the water swallows. A breath shows white in the cool air. There is no wind noise because the wind passes over the top of the pit and never enters it. There are no birds. The place is quiet in the way a sealed room is quiet, except that it is open to the sky.

And it does not change. That is its rule. Rain does not raise the level; it runs off the rim or soaks into the silt and the aquifer takes it without perceptible difference. Summer does not lower it; the groundwater holds it up. The mineral stain stays at the same height. The iron film forms in the same corner. The sedges do not grow taller in any way you can measure from one visit to the next. Return in November or in June and the water will be the same temperature, the same colour, the same distance below the bench. The pit was dug, the stone was taken, the machines were removed, and the water came in and settled, and since then everything in it has been working toward equilibrium and has arrived. Nothing decays quickly, because nothing much lives to decay. Nothing builds up, because nothing much grows. The erosion is finished. The chemistry is finished. The light comes and goes above, but the water below keeps its own time, which is no time at all.

It is not a wilderness, and it is not a factory. It is the small, cold, sunlit hollow that was left between the two — a place where an industrial process ran its course and then stopped, leaving behind a body of water too still, too cold, and too mineral to be anything but itself, empty and stable and ordinary, under the normal light of the sky.
