The water does not end. You are not *in* it the way you are in air, looking toward a horizon that stands apart from you. You are inside a pressure, a cold with thickness, that reaches in every direction and lets nothing arrive sharp.

Above, very far, a dim blue disc fades into grey. That is the surface. Below the disc the blue thins quickly to a permanent dusk. There is enough light to make out shape for a few tens of meters — 1.0 dim, twilight without morning — and beyond that, forms announce themselves by being only slightly darker than the water they displace. Marine snow falls through it slowly and without pause.

You have come down to a summit. It is high. Behind you, if you turn, the ground drops away steeply into a darker, stiller abyss a kilometer or more below. In front of you the ground levels and keeps leveling. This is the top of a seamount, a table in the ocean, drowned long ago and lifted, in its own deep time, above the floor of the sea. The water here is barely above freezing. Cold settles into joints and slows everything — your movement, the animals' movements, and the building.

The first surprise is under you. There is no ground here in any natural sense.

For as far as you can see, the plain is paved. It is not sand, not rock, not mud. It is a crust, many meters thick in places, made of interlocked tubes and plates and woven, spongy latticework fused into one another until the seams are worn smooth. Where a pale fragment has recently broken off, you can see inside it: hollow, layered, like the inside of a shell or the bark of a tree, not solid stone. Everywhere the crust goes, it is crust. In the deepest crevice between plates, the bottom is also crust — an older generation, flattened and polished. You could travel for days across this summit and never once brush a natural substrate. The builders long ago covered the original rock and then covered their own work, and then covered that, until the world here is entirely made.

And yet nothing here is made with a tool.

There is no metal glint, no straight line, no right angle, no wire, no plastic, no cut surface. Nothing sharply worked. Everything is curved, secreted, gnawed, re-secreted. This is a city built the way a termite mound is built, the way a reef is built, but continued without interruption for millions of years until scale has made it unrecognizable as anything small and living. The materials are mineral but not mined: calcium carbonate laid down in tubes, silica in sponge spicules, chitin hardened and replaced by stone. Erosion has had eons to work on it. That is the second surprise: how *old* it is, and how settled.

Nothing has a crisp edge. Towers that must once have grown steeply have been rounded by current and age into soft, fluted pillars, like worn teeth or river stones enlarged to architectural size. Broad domes that were once ambitious have slumped into low disks you could swim over without knowing they were ever vertical. The summit as a whole has been near-flattened into its final form: a vast, high, mostly level plain of made material, its highest achievements worn down to humps, its history legible only in layers.

The structures are sparse, which makes them stranger.

Abundance here is local, never regional. On a single tower, life crowds in thousands — tube mouths opening and closing, sponge pores pulsing, anemone-like polyps waving translucent plumes that are *not* plants. There is no vegetation at all. Nothing photosynthesizes in this dim cold. The waving things are animals, mouths and filters, pale cream and translucent because color has no use here, flashing a faint cold blue only when startled. Small blind crabs walk the pavement on long legs, sea spiders pick their way over the tubes, shoals of pale eelpouts hover in the lee of a pillar and scatter like ash at a touch.

Then the tower ends, and there is emptiness. Fifty meters of plain. A hundred. The crust continues, unbroken, but nothing stands on it but scattered nodules no larger than your hand, and the falling snow of particles. You can swim for an hour between presences. The waste of space is part of the design: the plain is a connective tissue, the towers are events.

When you come to one, you understand the rule of the place. The towers lean, in a subtle way, into the current. They grow where water moves past them, bringing food. In still water pockets, nothing grows tall. On the upstream edge of a dome, the tubes are densest and freshest, a pale new skin. On the downstream side, the crust is older, darker, bored with holes, occupied by sheltering fish. The whole plain is oriented. Everything is a filter, arranged by flow.

This is also why the place, for all its quiet, is turbulent.

Down here the storm on the surface you will never see becomes an internal wave — a slow surge that rolls over the summit every few days, dropping the temperature by a degree for an hour, lifting a veil of pale dust off the crust, making every plume bend the same way at once. For a while the fish leave their towers and hang in the current like kites. Then it passes, and the calm returns so complete you feel your own heartbeat as a sound.

And under that, a slower turbulence, the dynamic that really runs the world: collapse and regrowth, on a timescale you can almost watch if you stay long enough. A tower, grown heavy with its own mineral weight, settles. Not with a crash — sound dies quickly in cold water — but with a slow sloughing, a side sheering off in a silent slide and settling in a cloud. New tubes sprout on the fresh break within weeks and years, pale and eager. A dome that stood for five thousand years becomes rubble plain in a season, and the rubble plain becomes foundation for the next dome. The builders eat the abandoned and build with the eaten. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to be. Growth and ruin are the same process, turning.

Sounds belong to that process. Mostly, it is quiet. No wind, no waves, no birds, no insects. The water itself gives a low sigh as it passes through the lattice of the crust, a pressure-change you feel in your ears more than hear. Now and then: a click of a shrimp somewhere inside a tube, the scrape of a crab's leg on stone-that-is-not-stone, the deep, muffled groan of a large section settling into a new position, felt through the water against your skin. The quiet is not empty. It is muffled. Everything that happens here happens underwater, at low temperature, at a pace set by cold chemistry, and is softened to a thud.

Ecology follows from the building. The scaffold worms and sponges *are* the architecture, and the architecture *is* the habitat. Fish do not make nests; they move into empty tubes. Crabs do not dig burrows in earth; they gnaw and widen old tube mouths. A predator that hovers over the plain has nowhere to hide. A prey animal that wishes to live must live *in* the made world or not live at all. The towers are apartment, food source, and shelter in one secreted object, and when the animals that made a chamber die, the chamber outlives them by centuries and is colonized again. Individuals are brief. The crust is ancient.

That is the rule, in the end: nothing here is natural, and nothing here is artificial. What could have been rock is now a reef; what could have been a reef ten million years ago is now near-flattened pavement, worn to a settled form; what is pavement today is being bored, eaten, filtered through, and will be tower again. High, submerged, cold, and dim, this is a place that has built itself completely over and then, with equal completeness, worn itself smooth — and is still, in the current, building.

Ahead of you the plain goes on. Another low dome rises out of the dusk, rounds off, disappears. The made ground does not end at any edge you can see. It extends past the immediate, regional, beyond the valley of your vision, a high table swept by water, paved wall to wall with the patient work of animals who had no tools but time.
