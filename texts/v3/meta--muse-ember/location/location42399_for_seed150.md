From the ridge above, it does not look like a place at all.

The mountain is young, and it still looks it. The glaciers have only just left this high basin; the cliff faces are black and angular, cut fresh, their edges sharp enough to slice a glove. Fresh scree rattles in the wind. There are no rounded hills here, no soft old slopes worn smooth by weather — the rock is newly exposed, newly broken, still settling. Ice remains wedged in the cracks long after the last snow should have gone, clear and hard as stone.

And below that new, raw stone, set into a small, flat shelf of it wider than a cathedral nave but not much wider, is a green mass too regular and too dense to be forest. It fills the whole shelf from cliff to cliff. There are no clearings, no waste ground, no empty lots between presences. Every surface is occupied, and then occupied again above it. From up here you can take in the whole of it at a glance — a hamlet squeezed into a grove, streets and roofs and walls all the same living color, breathing out a thin veil of mist into the cold air.

Dim light. The sun is somewhere above the cloud deck, but down in the basin it never really arrives. The light is a flat, grey-green, softened further by three and four layers of leaf. Even at noon the lanes are twilight.

You descend the sharp scree until the green begins to resolve.

First it is a wall, not a boundary fence but a thick knitted thicket where the wind comes over the ridge. Alpine shrubs have been pressed by snow and wind into a low, interlocked hedge, their stems bent sideways, their roots holding the young gravel from sliding. It is the only thing between the open mountain and the interior, and it is alive, wet to the touch, frosted white on the windward side and dark and beaded with condensation on the lee. There is a gap in it, just wide enough to turn sideways through, where tendrils have been braided and re-braided back from the passage for as long as anyone has maintained it.

Inside, the arrangement becomes unmistakable, and strangely urban.

This is not a forest that a town has been built in. The town *is* the vegetation. There is no stone building here, no cut timber, no brick, no metal nail, no glass, no device of any kind. Everything standing is growing. The ground under your boots is not earth. It is a spongy felt of roots, rhizomes, fallen leaf, and moss, inches thick, saturated and cold, giving under your weight and springing back. Where the root-felt is thin you can glimpse mineral soil and crushed rock, but plants have covered it like a skin. Plant is substrate here. Plant is roadbed, wall, roof, container, bridge.

The lanes are tight. They must be kept open, or they close. Overhead, stems arch and meet, low enough that you brush them with your hat, dense enough that the sky is only a rumor. Underfoot, shallow water is everywhere but never deep — this is a wet place in the marsh-edge sense, not a flooded one. Cold springs seep up through the root mat in black, clear threads, welling between the domes and finding their way out through woven culverts of hollow reed. They make shallow, mirror-still channels that run along the sides of the paths, covered in places by floating mats of tiny leaves. Everything drips. The air is so full of moisture that your breath, and the collective exhale of the packed plants themselves, condenses and hangs. Icicles form on the eaves of leaves where the wind catches them and melt an hour later, dripping steadily back onto the mat below. It is post-rain, perpetual, without ever quite raining.

The buildings are the plants that have been encouraged — by wind, by snow-load, by generations of hands shaping and grafting and braiding — into the shapes their species already wanted to make.

A house is a hollow bulb, a root-swollen cushion plant grown up and over until its crown meets its neighbor’s, its interior a single chamber with walls that yield slightly when you lean. Its door is a flap of tough leaf that rolls inward and unrolls behind you. Its windows are not holes but thin, translucent membranes stretched between stems, amber with frozen sap when cold enough, letting in that dim green light and rippling in the draft. Its chimney is a vertical hollow reed, blackened slightly at the top not by soot but by cold, serving as a vent for the small warmth of bodies and respiring plant tissue inside. Up close you can see the construction: the outer wall is living bark and moss, the inner wall is smoother, polished by shoulders and hands. Embedded in the living wall, in cross-section, is the older city — dead stems frozen solid, a scaffold of previous growth that has been overgrown and strengthened by new growth weaving through it. Layer upon layer, the city built on its own husks, because nothing here is discarded; in this cold, dead plant matter does not rot quickly. It freezes, it persists, it becomes foundation.

Next to the bulb houses stand longhouses made of interlocked culms, like reeds grown to the thickness of a man’s thigh, their hollow interiors stacked two or three high and connected by root ladders that are themselves still growing. There are reservoirs: plants with distended, water-filled bases that swell and shrink with the melt, their surfaces slick and algae-pale, serving as cisterns for the dry spells of deep cold. There are granary husks, large seed-pods with thick walls, cool inside, frozen at the edges, holding dried fruit and root stores. There are whole blocks — you learn to read them by texture rather than sign — where the plants are of one kind: a quarter of dense, low, cushion-forming shrubs making solid, insulated blocks that hold heat; a quarter of tall, hollow-stemmed reeds making airy, vertical blocks that catch the wind and channel it; a quarter of creeping, mat-forming ferns and mosses where the ground stays marshy and filtered.

Root bridges span the water channels. They are not built so much as coaxed: two roots trained across a gap, thickening year by year until a man can cross without breaking them, their undersides still wet and bearded with fine rootlets drinking the stream. Where a bridge is neglected, it thins; where a lane is neglected, the overhead stems meet and the lane becomes a tunnel, then a sealed wall, then a new dome. Activity is everywhere, but it is the slow activity of cold things. At the dimmest hour, leaves turn almost imperceptibly toward the light. Stems tick as they contract in the cold. A drift of snow held on a roof-leaf suddenly releases and slides in a sheet, hissing. A root probes a crack and thickens. The whole city is in motion, in the way a glacier is in motion.

It is mostly quiet. The packed vegetation and the wet air swallow sound. You hear your own breathing, too loud. You hear water — not rushing, but constant drip, trickle, the soft pop of a sap bubble freezing. You hear the creak of a living wall in the wind, and the faint ticking of the root mat settling under your feet. There is almost no birdsong. Fauna is rare here.

When you do see life that is not plant, it is a surprise and it moves quickly, as if aware of its rarity. A pale, almost white bird, grouse-like, silent, flickers from one canopy layer to another and is gone. A single large insect, leaf-green and leaf-shaped, clings motionless to a stem for the whole time you watch it. In the spring channels, a translucent salamander the length of your thumb hangs in the current, gills feathery, barely moving. Down in the damp leaf litter, a slug-like grazer no longer than your finger scrapes algae from a wall, leaving a clean, pale track. They are too few to prune the city, too few to pollinate it in any reliable way — the plants here are largely self-grafted, root to root, sharing nutrients through underground connections so that one wounded dome can be fed by its neighbors. The tenders, the handful of people who live in the upper chambers and spend their days bending, tying, thinning, mouthing a graft closed, are themselves sparse. You may walk an entire lane and see none of them.

The rules of the place are the rules of its materials, and they are strict because they are biological. Nothing can be separated. If you cut a wall, the wall dies back to the cut, and the chamber loses its insulation to the cold. If you block a vent reed, the interior sours and warms and the membrane window thins. If you let a lane close, you must reopen it by bending stems back and holding them with living ties until they hold themselves. The city must stay wet, because the root mat that is its foundation will shrink and crack if it dries, even in the cold. It must stay connected, because isolated plants cannot survive the wind off the ridge. And it cannot expand beyond the shelf. Beyond the thicket wall, the wind is unbroken, the rock is too steep and too new, the cold is too unmitigated. Young gnarled shrubs planted out there lose their grafts in a single winter. So the city grows only inward and upward, denser and denser, layer on leaf-layer, until from the roof — a living turf of moss and dwarf grass, springy and wet, where you can stand and see the whole oval of it at once — the lanes below look like a single, breathing organism with a town’s worth of chambers inside it.

At night, or as close to night as this dim place gets, there are no lights. No lamps, no fire, no glow of any kind. The interior of a house is green-dark, smelling of crushed stems, wet moss, cold earth, and your own breath. The temperature outside is below freezing in that thin, high air, and your fingers find the living walls warmer by a degree or two where the packed biomass respires — a small, collective heat, enough to keep the membranes from freezing solid. Water continues to seep and drip. Somewhere overhead, a stem releases a drop onto a leaf, and it rings, very faintly, like a distant bell.

You leave by the gap in the thicket wall, back onto the sharp scree. Behind you, the green mass gives no sign of the lanes and domes and reservoirs inside it, only that too-regular, too-packed density filling its small basin to the edges, mist rising from it, frost on its windward rim. It looks, from the ridge, like a forest that has learned, in just a few cold generations since the mountain was born, how to be a city — and has done it entirely with the only material it was ever allowed: itself, growing.
