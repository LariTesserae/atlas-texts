It is small enough that you understand it all at once, and yet there is enough emptiness inside it that you never quite do.

A low hollow, hamlet-sized, maybe two hundred paces across in any direction, bounded not by walls or by height but by a soft, eroded rim. The rim is not a hill so much as a settling — earth and stone that has slumped and weathered until its edges have gone round. It rises only a few meters above the floor, and from inside you can see it all around you, a continuous, low brow, breaking the wind and holding the air in. From outside it would barely register. That is the first rule of the place: nothing here is sharp, tall, or sudden.

The light never strengthens. It arrives flat and dim, a warm grey diffuseness, as if under a ceiling that is always there. There is no sun disc, no moving cloud-shadow, no hour you could name. Colors are low, muted by it — damp earth is a brown that reads almost black, stone is pale but never white, plant matter is a dull, dusty green. Shadows exist but they are soft and shallow; the light falls almost evenly, so that nothing stands out in silhouette, nothing is hidden in deep dark. It is the kind of dim that your eyes adjust to in a minute and then forget, until you notice how quiet the colors are.

The air is warm and still, and damp in the way of air that has been breathed over many times. It does not bring weather with it. It hangs. You can feel it on your skin as a faint, constant moisture, not sweat, not rain, just humidity made tangible, and on your breath as warmth without heat. There is no breeze to speak of. Occasionally — rarely, as if the place has to remember how — a faint movement stirs a tussock of grass, and then stops. Mostly the air belongs to itself, in equilibrium. What evaporates from the wet ground soaks back into it as condensation on the cooler faces of stones. What falls as a fine, intermittent mist re-settles. Nothing accumulates, nothing depletes. The hollow has arrived at a state it can keep.

The floor is correspondingly settled. Erosion here is not dramatic; it is finished. The soil is a fine silt-clay mix, gritty underfoot, hoof-smooth in the worn places, softer and slightly yielding where it stays damp. It gives a little under your weight, then holds. Low banks have slumped into long, gentle curves. Scattered stones are rounded to the hand, their corners long sanded away, surfaces pitted and filmed. There are no fresh breaks, no crumbling edges, no fresh scars. Everything looks as if it has been there for a very long time and has been allowed to become exactly what it is, with no further argument from water or wind or time.

Water is present the way dampness is present — not as a river, not as a lake, but as a habit. It collects in pockets, shallow pans in the floor where clay seals the bottom and the rim is just high enough to keep it. There are five or six of these, irregular, each perhaps a few meters across, never deep, their surfaces mirror-still and dark, reflecting the flat sky. Between rains — and rain here is rare, a slow, sparse spitting of warm drops that does not so much fall as settle — they lose a little to the air and gain a little back from condensation beading and running down the stones that ring them. They do not flow out. They do not overtop. They persist at the same level for as long as you watch, which is to say, indefinitely.

Around their edges, and only there, the ground darkens further and grows a thin skin of moss, black-green and damp to the touch. Lichen grows on the shaded sides of the larger stones in pale, crusty discs. Everything else is drier at the surface, though never dry inside.

Vegetation is sparse because the light is sparse, and the hollow is honest about that limit. It is not a lush place thwarted by drought; it is a place that could be damp and warm forever and still not grow much. You walk stretches of bare earth — ten, twenty, thirty paces at a time where there is nothing rooted at all, only silt, a few smooth stones, a thin scattering of fallen leaf matter that has rotted down to almost nothing. Then a tussock of coarse grass, individual blades stiff and upright, roots making a tight, low mound. Then more emptiness. Then a single low shrub, woody and stunted, its leaves small and thick and a little dusty, branches hugging the ground. Then more emptiness again.

The plants that do live are patient and unshowy: the moss in the wet rings, a few ferns tucked against the damp stones where water beads longest, rosettes of tough leaves hugging the soil, lichen. There is no canopy, no forest, no undergrowth to push through. Density is low in every sense — few things, and those few far apart, with wide, empty intervals between them that are not barren so much as simply unoccupied. The eye rests.

And there is nothing living in them. No animal life at all — no birdsong, though there would be no birds to perch in so sparse a growth; no insects moving in the air or under the stones; no tracks in the soft silt, no droppings, no burrows, no nests. The absence is not a menace, not an uncanny quiet. It is just a fact of the place, as plain as the dim light. The ferns do not unfurl for anything to eat them. The leaf matter falls and rots in the damp, unhurriedly, feeding the soil and the moss in a closed, plant-only cycle. If you stand long enough, your own breathing seems loud in comparison, and then even that fades into the general stillness.

Sound, accordingly, is mostly quiet, and what sound there is has been worn to a minimum. The air makes almost none. The grass makes almost none. The only regular event is a drip. From a low overhang of stone at the south rim, or from a high stone at the edge of one of the pans, stored water finds a point and falls — a slow, irregular tick, plink, into a pool, making a ring that widens and vanishes. Sometimes a trickle, no wider than a finger, runs over slick stone for a meter and disappears into silt. These sounds do not fill the hollow; they appear in it, isolated, and then are gone, leaving the quiet more complete than before.

Human marks are present in the same scattered, settled way as everything else. There is no technology here — no metal, no glass, no device, no wire, nothing manufactured that would catch the light. There are only a few faint, partial remains, as if someone built a little, long ago, and the place reabsorbed it.

On the north side, where the rim is lowest, there is a line of stones sunk to knee height, set in a low wall that runs for perhaps fifteen paces and then stops. It is not a ruin so much as a suggestion now — a few upright slabs weathered smooth, gaps where stones have settled away, earth built up against the base. Near the center-west, there is a ring of smaller stones, no larger than a step, the remains of a hearth or a pit, with earth inside it darker and finer than the surrounding floor. A single slab lies flat nearby, pitted, its edges rounded. Further east, a broken post — or a branch grown straight — has sunk and softened at the base, standing no taller than you. A faint path is worn between these things, not marked, not maintained, just a slightly smoother line in the silt where feet once passed often enough to compact it. Now grasses grow in its edges and moss creeps into its middle.

Nothing else is built. No shelter remains roofed, no tool lies waiting. The stones hold their positions because that is where erosion left them, in balance. If you touch the wall, it does not wobble; it feels like part of the rim. If you stand in the ring, the ground inside it is no different from the ground outside.

That is, in the end, the character of the hollow: a small, low, warm, dim, damp room in the world, bounded, still, and complete. Water stays where water gathers and does not go onward. Plants grow where light and wetness agree and nowhere else. Stones become round and stay round. The air holds its moisture and its warmth. The light holds its grey. Nothing moves fast, nothing changes direction, nothing arrives or departs in any way you can detect. It is not frozen — moisture beads, drips fall, a leaf rots — but it is finished changing. It has settled, and it keeps its settled form, with wide spaces of quiet earth between the few things that live, and the fewer things that were made, and the pools that reflect a sky that never brightens.
