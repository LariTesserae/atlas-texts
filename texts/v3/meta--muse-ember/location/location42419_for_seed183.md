The light arrives first, and it is cold.

It is not the light of a valley noon. It is white in a way that bleaches, a light with no warmth in it, coming from above and from below at once. Above you the sky is thinned to a bright, colorless glare; below you, far below, a sea of cloud catches the same glare and throws it back up. Your face is lit from every side. The stone under your boots is lit from underneath where it overhangs. There is almost no relief from it, no shade that stays shaded, and still your breath fogs. Your fingers go numb on the stone despite the brightness, because the stone itself has never warmed. It holds the cool of the height like water holds depth.

You are on the spine of something extreme. Not a single peak smoothed by height, but a high plateau-ridge that runs open in every direction, regional in size, breaking into features as you look: a razor crest to the north, a hanging basin to the south, a field of slabs that stretches until distance and light make it unclear where ground ends. The drops are not distant. They are immediate. On both sides the world falls away into cloud and then into nothing you can name. The air is thin enough that sound carries strangely, thin enough that your ears pop with each step up a ledge, thin enough that the horizon seems to bend down and away, as if you stood on a stone too close to the edge of things.

There is no soil here to stand on. There has not been for a very long time.

The ground is pavement, and it is crowded. For as far as you can walk, pale schist and granite slabs stand shoulder to shoulder, lean against each other, stack in layers, rise into pillars and fins and broken walls of stone. In places they are almost touching, leaving only hand-wide passages between them. In others they crowd into a dense, upright thicket, hundreds of weathered towers in a space no larger than a town, their edges worn smooth but their mass pressing close. It is a mineral forest without vegetation. The density is not emptiness. It is saturation — stone presence after stone presence, reflecting light into each other's faces until the whole field glows.

And everything is wet.

At this altitude you expect aridity, wind-dried rock, snow stolen by sun. Instead the rock weeps. Water lives just under the surface and just over it. It beads on the cold stone even when no rain falls, condensing out of the thin air in a constant, silent deposit. Thin sheets, a centimeter deep, lie in the hollows and never drain. They mirror the double sky so perfectly that stepping over one is stepping over a duplicate of the ridge. Drips form in mid-air below overhangs and fall upward for a half-second before they remember to fall down. Melt films crawl across polished slabs, following old channels that have been carved and recarved until they are smooth as veins. The erosion here is established, settled-in. This is not a fresh catastrophe. The edges are rounded by a patience of water and wind. The channels are mature. The stone is satin from having been washed, frozen, washed again for millennia. It is in balance, old, and it keeps its shape while constantly exchanging water with itself.

There is no plant life. Nothing roots, because there is nothing to root in. What looks like growth is not growth. In the sheltered cracks, where the water lingers longest, there are rosettes of quartz, salt flowers that open in the damp and close in the glare, dendrites of clear ice and black volcanic glass that branch like frost on a window. Even simpler, in the darkest seep-lines, are grey fungal threads, not green, not photosynthetic — a mineral-fed film that lives on vapor and dissolved stone, tasting of metal if you touched it. They are the only suggestion of biology, and they make the absence of everything else more complete. There are no grasses to bend, no shrubs to shelter. No birds cross the brightness. No insects rise from the damp. The only animal sound is your own breath, labored in the thin air, and the only tracks in the wet dust are yours filling instantly with water.

The sound, despite that silence, is loud.

Wind moves through the stone crowd and makes organ pipes of the gaps, a roar that rises and drops in chords. Water drips from pillar to pillar with a percussion amplified by stone, too regular, too near. Ice in the crevices cracks like distant gunshots, then sings, a high ringing that vibrates through your boots. Sometimes the slabs themselves hum, a low continuous note when the wind finds the right angle, as if the whole plateau were a tuned instrument left out in weather. The weather here is active and quick. Mist rolls in from the cloud sea below and deposits itself instantly on every cold surface, then burns off in the white light in minutes. Frost forms on the sunny side of a slab and melts on the shaded side of the same slab at the same moment. A shower of condensation will pass over the ridge like a sigh, wetting you through without rain, and leave ten minutes later everything beaded and bright.

Scattered through this crowd are the marks of builders. They are few, and primitive, and old enough to have become part of the erosion. A line of cairns, each knee-high, stacked by hand from slab fragments, walks the ridge toward a summit that is already the summit. In a wind hollow, a small round shelter of upright slabs, its roof long collapsed inward, a floor fire-pit blackened but with no ash that survives the wet. A basin carved into a boulder, polished inside by a stone bowl left beside it. A hammer stone with a groove worn through its middle for a haft, and flakes of worked black glass, sharp still, lying in a water film. Pecked lines, drilled holes, a single low wind-wall. No town, no road, no machine-smell or iron hum. Only hand-tools and hand-stacking, occasional artifacts that the water has not yet taken, as if someone once tried to make a place habitable here and settled for making it bearable.

And then, slowly, as you walk deeper into the stone crowd, the rules begin to fray. It is not sudden. The place has lived with this fraying so long that it is part of its settled form.

Shadows lie in the wrong direction. With light from every side it should be hard to tell, but in the narrow passages the shadows fall toward the brightest source, pooling under slabs as if the sun were beneath the cloud sea. A sheet of water in a basin will reflect a ridge that is behind you and below, and in that reflection the ridge is both farther and closer. Water in certain hollows climbs the side not toward the drain — there is no drain — but toward the highest point of the rim, and stops there, trembling, before rejoining itself. Echoes arrive out of order: you hear the drip from the stone three times, and only on the fourth is the stone struck by your boot. Distance folds at the far end of the slab field; on clear moments you can see the far crest through the shoulder of a pillar, as if the stone were transparent to sight but not to touch. Frost flowers bloom and decay in seconds in the vibration of the humming slabs. North seems to point in two directions at once near the summit, and the cairn line, which you walked straight, loops back past itself at a slightly different height.

Nothing here is alive in any way you recognize, and yet the place is not inert. It is active in a mineral, atmospheric way: dissolving, depositing, condensing, sublimating, cracking, humming, shifting its water in endless small cycles. Predator and prey have no meaning where there is no animal, but stone and water trade places constantly, and light trades with shadow, and the weirdness trades with the ordinary so smoothly you stop noticing which is which.

By the end, the brightness has not lessened and the cold has not gone. The plateau stretches on, open and crowded at once, wet and bright and bare, its old weathered forms holding together while its water and light quietly unmake the world's usual order. It is a place that has existed long enough to learn how to be impossible, and to keep doing it in balance — stone weeping, light freezing, the summit humming under your feet, and no life at all to witness it except, briefly, you.
