**The Still Shelf**

You do not sink here, and you do not rise.

If you let go of a tool, it hangs where you released it, slowly turning as though suspended in syrup. A bubble from your mask remains beside you, neither climbing nor breaking, keeping the shape you gave it. Grains of mineral dust are everywhere in the water, but they do not fall. They form a sparse, glittering snow that never accumulates, never drifts, never settles on the upturned surfaces below.

The water is cold — about eight degrees — and stays that cold from the rock floor to the highest point you care to climb. It is opaque with darkness rather than cloudiness. Beyond a few metres of your own light, the world becomes ink. There is no surface visible above, no gradual brightening, no hint of weather. Sound behaves as if it were wrapped in cloth: a tap on stone dies within an arm’s length, your breathing is too loud in your helmet, and then there is silence so complete that it starts to feel pressurised around your ears.

Under you, impossibly, is a mountain.

The settlement sits on a high, flat-topped plateau, perhaps four hundred metres from rim to rim, rising alone from a much deeper ocean floor far below. It is high ground in every direction, a summit plain, but it has never made an island. The water covers it by hundreds of metres and the sunlight has long since given up reaching it.

The rock looks newborn. Black basalt plates lean against one another like shards of broken glass, their edges still knife-sharp. Fissures are narrow and clean, as if the plateau were split a moment ago and then forbidden to finish falling apart. There are no rounded boulders, no sand in the cracks, no smoothed pebbles, no scoured hollows. Sharp pinnacles rise toward the dark, faceted and unweathered. Even the pale crust that coats some surfaces is bright and undisturbed. There is no green film on it, no algae where the city’s lights touch, no kelp in the crevices, nothing growing at all. It is a mineral place, clean in a way that seems unnatural for a sea.

The city occupies only the central part of the summit, and it is sparse to the point of loneliness. A dozen structures are scattered across the plateau, separated by thirty, forty, sometimes eighty metres of empty dark water. Broad causeways of pale, freshly laid mineral arc between them in a deliberate urban plan, crossing at stone junctions marked by small, steady nodes. But there is no street life. No windows blaze, no traffic crowds the routes; mostly there is only the space between buildings, and in that space the hanging dust.

The buildings themselves are not quite built.

At the centre, a vast ring lies perfectly horizontal over an empty plaza, hovering several metres above the rock, its inner curve outlined in dull amber. To the east stand three inverted cones, broad ends up, tips down, balanced without cables or anchors. Westward, a hexagonal prism intersects a sphere as if two solid geometries had been allowed to occupy the same centre. A long, blade-like structure curves like a shell, and beyond it a cluster of faceted towers rises in angles that should be impossible to cast or weld. Their interiors, glimpsed through dark slots, are cathedral-sized and empty.

Their material is neither metal nor stone. To a gloved hand it feels mineral but warm — warm despite the cold water, as if each structure has been held under a sun that does not exist here. The surfaces are seamless, matte, and unmarked by rust, salt, or impact. Make a small scratch and the warmth spreads faintly beneath your glove; the line closes without a sound. They do not creak under pressure, sway in current, or bear the stains of age. They seem to have been grown or persuaded into shape rather than constructed, and to remain so by some principle that does not care about weight, corrosion, or time.

Amber seams run along their edges and across the causeways. The light is too dim to read by and never flickers. It does not scatter through the water as light should; it stays close to the surfaces it marks, so the city is discovered piece by piece in the dark. Occasionally a small pod crosses one of the lanes at a speed that never alters, never slows for a turn, never hurries. It might take eleven minutes to complete its circuit. It leaves no wake, except for a faint line of displaced dust that does not close behind it.

Everything here is held in equilibrium. There is no current to carry heat away, no thermal layer to find, no gradient of pressure that changes as you rise a few metres. A drop of dye released from your suit would keep its shape, a ribbon suspended in the dark. Your own passage remains visible behind you as a clean track through the dust. If you return after a long absence, the track will probably still be there.

That is why the plateau still looks young. Sediment cannot bury it. Water cannot rub it. Nothing sinks to soften its edges and nothing rises to carry fragments away. Newness is not a recent event here; it is a condition preserved. The city, too, looks simultaneously just made and immeasurably old — its roads sharp, its buildings unscarred, its lights burning with the patience of something that has never needed to change.

Life, when it comes, is rare.

You may see, once in a very long while, a pale animal cross the shelf from rim to rim. It is several metres long, translucent, blind, with broad fins that move like slow fabric. It does not turn toward the lights, does not feed, does not settle. It simply passes through, and behind it remains a broad, faint ribbon of disturbed dust, still visible after it has vanished into the black beyond the plateau. It comes from waters outside the stillness and does not belong to this place. Nothing breeds here. Nothing grows here. The animal is an interruption, not an inhabitant.

At the rim, the rules loosen. The rock falls away into darkness below, and beyond the boundary of the plateau a dropped object begins, almost imperceptibly, to sink. A bubble finally rises. Sound travels a little farther. The field of stillness — whether made by the city or merely found by it — ends there, and the ordinary deep sea resumes its slow, indifferent motions.

From the rim, on a clear moment under your light, you can take in the whole settlement at once: a small, scattered patch of warm geometry on a black summit, roads curving through emptiness, mineral snow hanging motionless above it all. No plants. No crowds. No weather. Almost no sound. Only the amber seams, the cold, and the things that hang exactly where they were left.

You are the largest disturbance it has known in a long time. Even your track will endure.

The place does not evolve, decay, or unfold. It just holds.
