At first there is only the wet.

It comes up through the soles of your feet, warm and spongy, and keeps coming. You step onto a ground that is not ground. It gives, it bubbles, it weeps. Under a thin skin of flat, glossy leaves the whole surface is a mat of roots and stems woven so tightly and so thickly that it has become a substrate in itself — a living peat, a thatch for walking on. Puddles lie everywhere, ankle-deep and steaming, warm as bathwater. In between them the mat blisters and sighs, releasing little jets of vapor that smell of hot green and minerals.

You are high up. Your ears have not stopped complaining. The air is thin — too thin to hold the heat properly — and yet the heat is here anyway, a close, dripping heat that beads on your skin and will not evaporate. This is not the heat of a low country. It is the heat of a place with a hot heart, and the heat is rising through everything. From below, from the built mountain itself, it percolates up in slow, invisible tides. It makes the high air saturate. Clouds do not pass over this place so much as condense inside it: at the rim they gather, spill in, hang for a while over the terraces, and rain a little, constantly, without ever becoming weather. It is always just about to rain. Surfaces weep. Walls weep. Leaves weep from their undersides. Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it.

The light is ordinary, a clean high midday light, and that makes it stranger. Where the steam thins, shadows are sharp. Where it drifts, everything goes soft and pearled and loud with dripping.

Because it is loud. Not with voices. There are no animals here at all — no birds, no insects, nothing that flies or crawls or calls. The loudness is mechanical and wet. Hollow culms hiss as steam finds its way out. Swollen stems pop and crack as they expand in the heat. Water sluices down the faces of walls in sudden sheets, then stops. Somewhere a dome is collapsing in slow motion and you can hear every fiber groan and part. Rain that is not quite rain drums on broad sheaths. The whole landscape is creaking as it grows.

And it is always growing.

That is the first rule here, and it explains the second. You look up from the mat, expecting wilderness, and instead find architecture — but architecture of a kind no builder would recognize. Here and there, far apart, the flat plant-world thickens. A tower. A dome. A bridge with nothing under it. A long wall that runs off into steam and stops. They are not ruins. Their edges are fresh, sharp, young. New splits hiss. New shoots lash out across a facade overnight, stitching a tear shut with living knots. These places are being built at this moment, as you watch, and unbuilt a moment later.

Everything is built. There is no natural substrate to turn to. Even the mountain you are standing on is built, terrace upon terrace of woven root-brick, compressed and re-compressed generations of plant tissue, a ziggurat of thatch and culm and living masonry climbing out of a sea of cloud far below. Even the ground at your feet is built — layered, thatched, re-thatched. Nothing here was left as it was found. It was all made, and remade, by the only makers this world has ever had: the plants themselves.

No tools have ever touched this place. There are no cut marks, no nails, no pots, no carved stone. All the joins are biological. A beam is a stem that grew toward a neighbor and fused. A door is a place where two vines agreed, for a season, not to close. A roof is a canopy that learned to lean. The builders are the building material. A tower is not a plant *in* a structure; the tower *is* the plant, thickened in one place, reaching up to catch more of the condensing air, hollowing itself so steam can rise through its core and keep it warm and wet. A dome is a plant that grew sideways, in a circle, and sealed itself to trap heat and water inside — an empty chamber with fungal-green walls slick with its own sweat. They stand uninhabited. They have always stood uninhabited. They are architecture as a plant strategy: to hold water, to hold heat, to hold a place against its neighbors.

And they are sparse.

That is the other strangeness. For all this growth, for all this total, suffocating vegetation, most of the world is open. Between one built presence and the next there may be a quarter-mile of empty, living flat — just the wet mat, the puddles, the steam vents, a solitary creeper moving slowly across it like it has somewhere to be. The built things do not form a city. They form a scattered archipelago of efforts across a regional shelf of plateaus. From a high point you can see them: one great terraced basin, its rim scalloped with fresh fractures, other basins beyond it beyond a neck of woven ground, each with its one or two towers, its one long bridge built from both sides that never quite meets in the middle, its domes sweating in the sun. The emptiness between them is not barren. It is full, but low. It is full of plants doing the slower work of being ground.

The erosion is young, which here means violent. Because the substrate is plant, it does not erode in the old, patient way of rock. It tears. A warm fissure opens in a terrace and steam erupts, hissing so loudly you feel it in your teeth, and the edges of the split are clean and pale, newly exposed fibers. Water runs silkily down them, carrying shredded green silt. A wall weeps until a section slumps, and within days vines have thrown themselves across the scar, knotting it shut, and the wall is sharp again. Nothing here is allowed to become mellow. The landscape is too turbulent, too busy, to age gracefully. It is always early in its own formation, always just emerged, always being forced up and out.

Its ecology is plant on plant, without end. Nothing eats anything in the animal sense. There is no pollinator, no seed-carrier with wings or legs. Pollen moves on the steam currents and on the water. Seeds are buoyant, tufted, water-borne; they float in the puddles and wash against the bases of towers, where they germinate in the warm seep and begin, immediately, to build. Parasitic vines climb the domes and strangle their supports in an embrace that is also a construction — the dead stem of the host becomes the scaffolding for the parasite, which becomes scaffolding for the next. Dead plant matter does not rot in the usual cool, fungal patience. In this heat and wet it ferments, acidifies, dissolves. The puddles are faintly sour, faintly mineral, and they unmake what falls into them in hours. Then the mat thickens again over the place where it stood.

The dynamics are hyperactive. This is a landscape in storm-state even when the sky is clear. Local weather is born from the ground: a column of steam rises from a vent, cools at altitude, condenses, becomes a small, furious rain that falls only on the tower that made it, beats the dome hollow, runs down its sides in ropes, soaks the mat, feeds the next burst of growth. Growth itself is an event. A culm can shoot a hand's breadth while you watch, its tip thrusting and trembling. A fruit-case swells in the heat until it pops with a crack like a snapping twig, loosing a cloud of seeds into the damp. A bridge-vine, having reached, suddenly decides, in its plant way, to grow down instead of across, and hangs weeping roots into empty air, searching for water that is already everywhere.

Strangeness accumulates at the edges of sense. Some domes grow their roofs inside-out, their smooth surfaces facing the chamber, their rough, thatched backs to the sky. Some towers lean *toward* the steam vents, not toward light, as if light is the least of their needs. Water in the deepest vents seems to drip upward for a moment, caught in the updraft, before it gives up and falls. The mat breathes — visibly, very slowly, rising and falling in broad undulations a few inches high, as if something enormous were sleeping under the whole plateau and turning over in its sleep. There is nothing under it but more plant.

At night — for there is night, ordinary in its darkness — the hissing lessens, the popping continues, and the condensation becomes heavier. The built forms give off their day's heat in visible veils. The puddles reflect what little light there is and hold it, warm. And out of the dark, from somewhere in the open, comes the sound of a stem splitting, loud as a branch breaking under a weight, and then the soft, endless plink of water from a weeping wall that has decided, for no reason you can see, to weep faster than before.

You understand, standing there between two distant towers with hot water at your ankles and living ground under your feet, that this is not a place that was built and then abandoned, nor a wilderness that happened to look built. It is a place that is building itself, right now, wet and hot and high, with no other life to witness it, no tools, no hands, no animals to eat the builders or live in the buildings. Just plants, making architecture out of themselves, scattered sparsely across a steaming, newly-made mountain, loud with their own making, forever on the edge of raining, forever young because everything here is torn down by heat and water almost as fast as it grows.
