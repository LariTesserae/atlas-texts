You don't enter it so much as slip into a place where the ground hasn't yet decided to be ground.

Between two stones that are still growing together — raw, pale, damp to the touch like unfinished clay — there is a crack no wider than a hand, and one moment you are pressing your shoulder through cold air, and the next you are inside, and the cold has not left you. It settles in your knees and your breath. It fogs, briefly, and is gone.

It is bright. That is the first wrongness, and it does not resolve.

You are below the surface. There is no sky here, no hole you came through that you can still see. The ceiling is perhaps two meters above you, a low, uneven dome, and it is not rock in the usual sense. It is thin. Translucent. A slab of mineral and packed frost and something else that has not hardened, through which a white, blue-white light presses down without warmth. Above it, you understand, must be snow, or a bright plain under a hard sun, and the light is being filtered and cooled and scattered until it arrives here as pure glare. It does not come from a place. It comes from the ceiling itself, from everywhere at once, and if you stare at it, it gets brighter at the edges of your vision, not darker.

Everything is bleached by it. The floor, the walls, the living things. White on white on pale grey, with occasional stains of ochre and a faint, bruised green where something has managed to grow.

The room — and it is a room, pocket-sized, intimate — is not much larger than a closet in which you have to turn sideways to breathe. Six paces across at most, if the corners stay still long enough to be counted. They do not always.

The floor is uneven, slightly domed in the center, sloping down to the walls in places that were not sloping a moment ago. The walls are the most unfinished part. They have the texture of something still being born. In places they are spongy, giving under a palm like wet cork, exuding moisture. In others they are waxy and smooth, where mineral has begun to skin over. The ceiling, by contrast, is glassy and taut.

This is not a cave worn out by water. Nothing here has been eroded into shape. It is the opposite: it is accreting. Forming. A blister under the first crust of the world, still inflating. All around the floor's edge, new crust is budding — small translucent crystals like teeth pushing up through the pale sediment, pop and softening in cycles you can almost feel in your molars. A stalactite over in the corner is not pointed and old; it is plump and wet and growing downward in a slow pulse, as if the rock were breathing it out. The whole chamber ticks, very quietly, as it cools and sets and then, impossibly, unsets again.

Because it is still forming, there is a great deal of emptiness. Most of the floor is bare. Long, clean expanses of pale raw stone where nothing grows, nothing sits, nothing moves for a long time. The sparseness is not an accident; it is the condition. Presences are scattered far apart, as if the place has not yet filled in what it intends to contain.

There is damp, but never water. Moisture is intermittent and indecisive. A film condenses on the walls and shines, then beads. The beads grow large, trembling against the cool ceiling, and then they do not fall — or rather, each one chooses a direction. Some fall straight down in a slow, sparse drizzle that evaporates halfway, leaving a faint mineral freckle in mid-air before it vanishes. Some slide sideways onto the spongy wall and are drunk. Some — and you watch this twice before you believe it — rise. A drop detaches from the floor's film and climbs, wobbling, up toward the light, growing smaller as it goes, until it touches the ceiling and is absorbed with a tick.

There is no river, no pool except for shallow, irregular mirrors that appear and disappear with the condensation cycle. In them, your reflection is not quite you. It is older by a breath, or it looks up when you look down. The puddles reflect the ceiling's glow more brightly than the ceiling itself should allow.

What vegetation there is, is sparse in the extreme. It does not grow up from the floor — why would it, when the light is above and the floor is still too young to hold roots? Instead, a few thread-like things hang from the glassy ceiling, roots or fungal filaments or something between, descending a hand's breadth and stopping. They are translucent, almost invisible until they block the light. In cracks near the wall, where the spongy rock holds moisture longer, there are small velvety patches of moss — not green, but a pale, sickly lime that seems to appear only when you are not looking directly at it. And a cluster of tiny transparent fingers, fungus perhaps, no taller than your thumbnail, that inflate and deflate with the room's breathing.

They do not photosynthesize in any normal way. They seem to feed on the condensation itself, on the mineral exudates, on the light that is too cold to warm them.

And yet, in that emptiness, life is abundant.

You notice it first as movement in the bare places. The floor is not still. Under the glare, thousands of specks are crawling — mites, springtails, nematode ribbons no thicker than hair that writhe and re-writhe across the stone, leaving faint, shiny trails that fade. They are everywhere, a low, teeming carpet that is still mostly empty space between bodies. Life at this scale is crowded; life at your scale is solitary.

Larger things move among them, silent. Blind millipede-like creatures, white and segmented, glide along the wall-ceiling junction with dozens of legs moving in perfect, soundless sequence. Translucent, shrimp-shaped things swim — not in water, but through the damp film on the floor, through the air itself in short, darting bursts, their gills pulsing. On the ceiling, upside down by any sensible standard, are slow slugs without shells, their bodies plastered to the glassy stone, leaving slow, bright mucus routes that catch the light. A spider — too many-jointed, too many-eyed, though the eyes are milky and useless — hangs in a web that is not a web but a few strands of condensation stretched between crystal teeth, and waits.

They are not quiet because they are few. They are quiet because the room swallows sound. The spongy walls absorb scrabbling the way they absorb water. What you hear, mostly, is the room itself: a faint granular ticking as a crystal sets, a wet, muffled sigh as a pore opens somewhere in the wall, the soft tick-tick of a leg far away, filtered as if through cloth. Your own breathing sounds too loud, and then, after a minute, too muffled, as if the air has learned to dampen you.

There is activity everywhere once you look. This is not a dormant pocket. It is in motion. The fauna molt in pulses, splitting their white skins along the back and stepping out, leaving husks that are immediately colonized by smaller crawlers. The condensation cycle waxes and wanes every few minutes — film, beads, brief rain, evaporation — and with each rain, the crawlers surge upward, toward the moisture, a brief migration across the bare floor. Predator and prey move in the same lanes. Something hunts by absence: a patch of darkness, roughly animal-shaped, that moves seconds before the creature casting it arrives. When it passes over the moss patches, the lime color flickers out. When it passes over you, the cold deepens.

It is hunting the blind things, and perhaps being hunted in turn by something that only exists in the puddle-reflections, where you can sometimes see a shape in the water that has no body on the floor.

Reality, here, is not broken in a loud way. It frays.

Shadows do not fall from bodies. They fall upward, softly, and sometimes they arrive a moment before the thing that owns them, pooling on the ceiling like a stain. Footprints you leave in the damp film stay bright for a long time, then fill themselves in from the edges, as if the floor is healing or forgetting you. If you walk quickly around the central dome, you arrive back at the crack you entered, but the condensation is at a different stage, and a new crystal has popped where there was none. The room is larger when you turn your back to the light, smaller when you face it.

The light rings, faintly, in your ears when the slugs move across the ceiling. Not audibly — as a pressure, a high, cold tone. Colors bleed where they should not be: a green afterimage of the moss hangs in the air after you've looked away; a faint ochre smear appears on the white wall and is gone when you approach it.

There are signs of building, sparse and incomplete. Near the far wall, someone — something — has arranged three raw stones in a low cairn, half-sunk into the spongy floor, as if the floor claimed them mid-construction. A spiral has been scored into the soft wall with a claw or a dragged crystal, each groove still wet. And there is a single broken arc, like a doorway or a rib or a handle, made of hardened mineral, standing upright and ending in mid-air where the rest of it was never formed. It holds condensation longest. Small things shelter under it. No tool was used here. No metal, no fire, no device. Whatever made these marks had only bodies and patience, and it did not finish.

That is the rule that holds the place together, if any does: everything is in the process of coming into being, and therefore nothing is obliged to finish being what it is. The rock may become solid, or it may soften again. The pocket may open into a cave, or seal shut into stone and be forgotten inside the earth, a hollow that never knew daylight except this stolen, cold glare. The creatures may grow eyes, or lose the ghosts of them. The plants may root, or evaporate.

For now, it is a bright, cool, damp room hidden under the skin of the world, large enough to breathe in and small enough to be held, mostly empty, mostly silent, and feverishly alive when you stop trying to measure it. The light presses down. The water chooses. The floor moves under specks too small to count. And somewhere in the wall, a new crystal ticks, pops, and begins — very slowly, very quietly — to grow.
