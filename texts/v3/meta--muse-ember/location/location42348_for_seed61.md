It begins as a crack, not a road.

You do not arrive so much as squeeze in, sideways, through a seam in cold rock somewhere below a hill that has no name here — because here, there is no surface to name things from, no sky to orient by, no sun to say what time it is. The air drops as you go, cool against the skin, and damp. It thickens. It smells of wet stone, iron, clay, and a little rendered fat from a lamp somewhere ahead.

Long before you see anything, you hear it.

A low, constant thunder, too close to be weather. Water moving under and through stone, not in a river you can stand beside, but in a network of channels cut and worn by hand and by seepage, all at once, all the time. The roar is not loud the way a storm is loud. It is loud the way a small room full of people talking at once is loud — there is nowhere for the sound to go. It bounces off the vault and returns. Dripping, which should be quiet, arrives as hammer-blows. Clinking, scraping, the ring of a copper tool on a stone, a voice raised in the next doorway — everything is amplified, crowded, and overlapping, and it never pauses to let you sort it out.

You emerge, finally, into the pocket.

It is one chamber, vaulted and roughly oval, no longer than a long barn if you walked its floor, and yet it is a town. A whole urban world has been fitted inside a single hollow, the way a termite mound might be turned inside out. It is hidden not because it is distant, but because it is contained. From outside, the rock above shows nothing. From inside, there is nowhere else to go.

The town is built in layers because there is no room to sprawl. Streets are not laid out on a plan so much as settled on the walls, on terraces, on roofs. A house's roof is the floor of the house above it. A stair, steep and narrow enough that you have to turn your shoulders, climbs past a kiln, past a cistern, past three doorways that open at different heights from the same wall. Bridges of flat slate leap over sluices. Aqueducts of packed clay and stone run at head-height, leaking a little, on purpose and by accident, so that the air is always condensing.

Everything is wet. Not flooded — there is a balance to it, an old, established balance — but wet in the way a marsh edge is wet after long rain that never stops and never starts. The rock weeps. Water beads on the ceiling and falls in slow, regular threads. It sheets down the walls in thin, glassy films that move over polished stone at odd angles, as if the surface were guiding it. It pools in the hollows of the floor and is carried away in channels no wider than your hand, and one moment later the same pool is there again, neither deeper nor shallower. The water table is just under your feet. You feel its cool through your soles. Floors are slick and mirror-bright where generations of hands and feet have worn them.

There is no vegetation. You look for green and do not find it, and after a while you understand that green would be an obscenity in this dimness. No grass, no moss, no roots in the cracks, no trees. What grows — if you can call it growth — is mineral and simpler: pale fungal felts in the moist corners of troughs, crusts of salt and crystal that look like lichen from a distance but are only stone blooming. In the shadowed troughs, the town farms that felt, scraping it off with flat bone tools. It is their crop. Everything else that looks plant-like is constructed. The slender vertical forms that rise in clusters near the back wall are not reeds, but chimneys for the kilns, and stalactite tubes cut free to serve as beams, and rows of bone-white struts holding up a terrace. From a distance it reads as a forest. Up close, it is architecture.

The stone itself is old in a way that has nothing to do with newness. This is not a fresh excavation. Erosion here settled long ago into its own state. All edges are softened, all angles slumped, all carvings worn down to a contour your palm can read better than your eyes can. New work, when it is done — and it is done constantly, a wall re-laid, a channel re-lined — is made to look immediately ancient, because the builders use the same re-worn stone, the same clay, the same hammering. The town has the patina of something that has been in its own weather for a very long time and has found its equilibrium with it. Damp stone gives a little and takes a little, and no one sees a change.

Light is dim and constant, as if it were a property of the rock rather than an event. Veins in the walls glow a faint blue-white, a phosphorescence in the limestone, steady as a coal that never burns down. Against that, the small oil lamps — fat in shallow clay cups, tended with a copper wick-trimmer — make brief, warmer pools. They do not banish the gloom; they outline it. Faces emerge and recede. You see shapes more than colors: the red-brown of fired clay, the black of volcanic glass, the dull shine of copper, the pale gray of bone tools laid in rows.

The technology is primitive and honest. No machines, no wheels that turn by themselves, no metal finer than hammered sheet and wire. A forging is done with a stone hammer and a small work-hardened copper awl. A pot is wedged and fired and re-fired. A gate in a sluice is lifted by hand, by a rope of twisted hide, and set down again. The infrastructure is dense because it is manual: hundreds of small, hand-kept things — cisterns, drains, smoke holes, cold shelves, tool racks, bead-lines marking which stair belongs to which household — all crowded shoulder-to-shoulder, all near-touching.

Life, other than the builders, is rare. The builders themselves seem few, and solitary within the crowd, hunched to fit their own low doorways, busy in a way that suggests long habit rather than purpose. Of true fauna there is almost nothing: a blind, translucent fish that turns up occasionally in the distribution channels and is caught in a hand-net; pale worms in the damp mortar, slow as thought; once, very rarely, a cave cricket high on a wall, silent, eyeless, perfectly still. They do not make a sound you can hear over the water. They are presences, not a population.

That is the strangeness of it, its mild but persistent wrongness. Things behave as if the world were remembering them. Voices you heard three terraces up arrive again, faint, a second after the speaker has stopped. The water film on a flat slab moves upward for a stretch before it finds the edge, as if surface tension had a preference. The crystals that grow where the drip falls are harvested, and grow back in exactly the same shape, as if the rock had a template. Fire in a lamp casts almost no shadow. You can stand in one place and hear four different neighborhoods at once, close and distant together, because the pocket will not let sound leave.

And nothing changes. That, more than the dimness or the damp, is what stays with you. There is no day or night, because the light does not rise or fall. There is no season, because the cool is always the cool, and the water is always at the same level, in the same channels, at the same pace. There is no growth of the town outward, because there is nowhere outward to grow, and no decay inward, because the erosion has already finished its work and come to rest. A house is repaired where it always was. A stair is worn a little smoother where it always was. If you left and returned in a year, in ten years, in a hundred, you would come through the same crack into the same roar, breathe the same iron air, duck the same aqueduct, and see the same lamps burning in the same blue gloom — not because time has stopped, but because, in this pocket, it has balanced itself so completely that it no longer needs to move.
