It is low, and because it is low it is wide.

The horizon does not rise. It holds, a thin, unbroken line at eye level, and beyond it the same flatness continues for a distance that makes a day's walk feel like no progress at all. There are no hills to climb into, no valley to descend toward. You are in the bottom of a shallow, old bowl, and what you can see is what there is: open ground, repeated and repeated, until light takes it.

The light is ordinary. That is one of its more unsettling qualities. A pale, warm daylight, mid and even, neither dim nor glaring, colours true but muted by heat-haze that never quite thickens. The sky is a washed grey-blue, mostly empty. No weather moves across it. The air is warm against the skin and damp in a way that has nothing to do with rain — a dampness that lives in the ground and rises only a little way from it. Sweat does not dry cleanly here. Stone exhalations cling. You smell warm mineral, a faint coppery tang, and underneath that the close, mushroom smell of wet felt.

The ground is what the place is made of. A grey-brown siliceous silt hardened into packed clay, iron-stained in places to rust, blackened in others with basalt grit and a glassy, slag-like pebble that looks poured rather than broken. It is cracked into broad polygons, but nothing is sharp. Everything long ago reached the shape it was going to keep. Erosion here is not dramatic. It is settled. Edges are rounded as lozenges, hollows are shallow saucers, cracks are half-filled with silt that has compacted to the same level as the plates around them. There is no fresh scar, no cliff, no new fall of stone. It has the look of a thing that has been worn for an age and then, at last, stopped wearing — or rather, learned to wear and repair in exactly equal measure.

Between the presences there is a lot of nothing. That is the other rule. A patch of damp ground here, a cluster of stones there, then metres and metres of bare mineral crust with only the footprint of your own step to mark it. The sparsity is not empty in the sense of void; it is ordered emptiness, maintained.

Water is present the way damp is present: in pockets.

In the shallow saucers it gathers as lenses a centimetre or two deep, sometimes no larger than your hand, sometimes spread into a long, irregular sheet that reflects the sky so perfectly it looks like a hole cut in the ground. The water is warm — almost body-warm — and it does not ripple except for the faintest tremor that runs through it from below. Above some of the larger pools a thin mist hangs, a hand's breadth thick, and does not disperse. It does not grow into fog and it does not lift. Condensation forms on the undersides of the darker stones at the pool edges and stays there, beaded, neither running off nor evaporating away. The moisture in this place does not travel. It belongs to its hollow.

There is no plant life. No grass, no scrub, no tree, no green thing of any kind. The warm, damp, low ground should by all ordinary logic be lush, and the absence is total and unmistakable, as if life passed through an earlier stage and was left behind. What lives instead is simpler and stranger, and for the most part it does not look alive at all.

In and around the damp pockets grow fungi, in the broadest sense: crusts and mats and low, folded felt. Black glaze on wet clay. Ochre and rust-orange sheets with a leathery, almost industrial texture, like insulation or tarpauline left out in the damp. Pale grey-white filamentous fans that spread across the silica skin and crack where they dry, then heal at the crack without changing it. They have no leaves to photosynthesise with and no roots to drink. They feed, as far as one can tell, on warmth and vapour and the minerals themselves — on the slow exchange that happens between silt, damp, and hot air. Their spores, when they make them, fall straight down. There is no wind here to carry them, only a steady, low air-movement that goes nowhere.

And the ground they grow on behaves, uncannily, like a factory that has forgotten its buildings.

Look closely at the stones. They are sorted. Not roughly, but in the way a machine sorts: graded bands of pebble size running in faint lines across the silt, washes of fine grit in one place, coarser gravel a pace away, as if the plain had been passed repeatedly through a sieve. The mineral crusts build up in low terraces, step after step, and dissolve in the pool next door. The pools receive, the crusts supply. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is carted away. The whole basin is a closed, steady-state process — heat from below, moisture from the pockets, minerals cycling between solid and dissolved — running at full capacity and producing, endlessly, exactly the same amount of itself. It hums through the soles of your feet.

That is the sound. It is loud, and it never changes.

Not voices, not birds, not wind in anything. A deep, low roar like a large engine idling under the floor of the world, a ventilation drone with a tremor in it, and over that, at intervals that have been going on for longer than you can measure, the soft tick-tick of mineral contracting, the grit shifting a fraction, water seeping through a crack somewhere out of sight. It is constant in volume. It does not swell or fade. You find yourself speaking more loudly than you intended, and then you stop, because there is no one who needs to hear you, and the roar does not care. It is the noise of a place that is working without ever moving forward.

There are no structures. That expectation keeps forming and failing. No wall, no road, no fence wire, no concrete slab, no rusted pipe, no bottle left, no ruin of any kind. The mind wants a factory here because the place feels manufactured, and finds, instead, only the manufacture. The industry is not built on the land; it is the land's way of being. Sorted output, steady heat, cycling input, a manufactured emptiness kept perfectly in balance. If anything had been built here, it would long since have been unmade by the same process that keeps the pools at their level and the crusts at their thickness.

Life, besides the fungal felt, is rare to the point of rumour.

If you stay still long enough — and stillness here feels less like waiting than like being accepted into the equilibrium — you may see one. A small, blind, translucent grazer, no longer than your thumb, that lives at the edge of a damp mat and moves with an almost geological slowness along the boundary between wet and dry. Legs like pale wire. It dips its head to the fungus, pauses for a very long time, and moves on a few millimetres. There might be one in a hundred metres. High up, so distant it seems more a disturbance in the light than a creature, something with wings loops on a thermal that is always there, always the same temperature, always blowing in the same direction. You may not see another for the rest of the walk. The place does not need them. It tolerates them.

That is the unusual thing, held at a low, persistent level, like a note that never resolves. Warmth without growth. Damp without rain. A mineral world that sorts itself like a machine and yet has never been touched by a hand. Loudness without event. Colours — rust, grey, black, white silica, the pale greenish sheen of a fungal bloom in a pool — that are natural and look, all at once, artificial.

Nothing here is coming or going. The fungi grow exactly as fast as the silt dissolves beneath them. The pools evaporate exactly as fast as condensation returns the water. The pebbles are rounded exactly to the degree the ground is warmed. It is not frozen; heat is moving, water is moving, minerals are moving, the drone is sounding, the tiny grazer is crawling. It is simply that all of it, accounted together, arrives back at the same place it started, forever. A finished landscape. An old one. You could leave for a year, in any other country a year would change everything, and return to find the same mist a hand's breadth over the same pool, the same black felt at the same edge of the same crack, the same roar at the same loudness in your bones.

You walk on, because there is nowhere else for the ground to go, and the plain outlasts your eye.
