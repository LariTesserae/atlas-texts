### The Sear Vault

You do not find it by looking. You find it by heat, pressing against your face long before you can see anything, and by the way the air gets drier as you climb.

The throat that leads in is a narrow crack in the high massif — near the summit, where the air outside is already thin and fast — slanting down between warm basalt walls into a single hollow chamber. It is small enough to cross in a few hundred paces. A long cathedral of a room, perhaps two hundred and fifty paces long and eighty across, its roof low as a man's head in places and rising in others to a dark, irregular vault thirty paces up. There are two mouths, this one and a high fissure at the far end where a little grey, high-altitude light leaks in and dies almost at once. The chamber is bounded on every side. There is nowhere else for anything to go.

That is the first rule of the place: it is finished. The rock has not erupted here for a time no creature remembers, and no water has ever cut it. The walls are basalt rounded by heat and by time, thermal-cracked into great patient plates, their edges worn smooth. The floor is a swell of pillow-stone and frozen waves of obsidian, dusted everywhere with a flour-fine ash — not sand, for no water has ever sorted it — that coats your boots, your teeth, your tongue. Nothing is sharp. Everything has settled into its own state. The fissures in the floor still breathe, opening and closing with a slow ticking as the stone expands, but they do not tear anything new.

Light, in any usable sense, does not exist here. The only light is the dull red seep from the deepest fissures, like embers under ash, brightening and dimming with the heat-tide. It shows silhouettes at arm's length and nothing more. Colour has been cooked out of the world: grey stone, smoky amber glass, ash-white, the dull copper of a creature's shell. There is green nowhere. There is no plant life at all, and never has been. What look like black frost-ferns growing in the shade of a rock are dendrites of silica and iron, mineral precipitate branching in perfect, sterile geometry. Beautiful, and dead.

The heat is the sun of this place. The floor at the centre is too hot to stand on barefoot, and in places the air at knee-height shimmers as if over a fire that isn't there. Near the walls and roof it is only uncomfortably warm. The difference is everything. Life here is arranged by temperature the way other worlds are arranged by water and light.

And life is everywhere. Overwhelmingly, actually. Packed.

There is almost no bare stone to be seen. The floor is a living pavement. The lower walls are the same pavement turned upward. The ceiling, too, in broad sheets.

The base of it all is the **pavers**. Each is a flat hexagon the size of a man's palm, dull grey-brown, interlocking edge to edge with its neighbours so tightly that a wind could not pass between them. Together they tile the whole chamber in a continuous skin, thousands upon thousands of them, breathing in slow ripples as sections warm and shift. They are lithovores. With a hot enzymatic film they dissolve the very basalt they stand on, ingest the mineral slurry, and excrete a fine, baked powder — the ash you walk on. They have no eyes, no legs in any familiar sense, only a fringe of pressure-feet that grip and release. When one grows too large it splits down the middle into two smaller pavers, and the seam between them is instantly colonised.

In those seams live the **glints**, specks no larger than a grain of rice, bright as polished metal, that dart — slowly, thermally — between the plates eating shed scale and the waste of their hosts. On the pavers ride, like slow turtles, the **hearthbacks**. Their backs are domes of fused, translucent glass segments, up to a metre across, and they move once in an hour, if that often, from cooler rim to warmer centre and back again, soaking heat into slag-organs inside their shells with a faint, contented ticking as the glass cools. A hearthback's back is itself a landscape: glints, juvenile pavers, and small grey mites nest in the grooves of its shell.

In the cracks between floor-plates slide the **ribbons**. Two or three paces long, translucent yellowish, legless, ribbon-flat, with a heat-sensing tip instead of a head. They are the hunters. They do not run. They flow through the fissures, feeling for the warmth of a hearthback, then wrap around its glass dome and constrict until the shell cracks with a sound like dry knuckles. What the ribbon eats, it reduces to powder and slag, which filters down through the pavers and is eaten again. The cycle closes. Rock to paver to hearthback to ribbon to rock again. Nothing leaves. Nothing is wasted. Nothing drinks.

For nothing here has ever known water. There is no rain in the Vault, no seep, no condensation on the coldest stone. Any moisture carried in on a visitor's skin evaporates in minutes. The creatures themselves carry no free fluid to lose. Their insides are dry slag, salt, and hot gas trapped in sealed sacs. They breathe — if that is the word — through slits in their sides that take in the thin, sulphurous vapour rising from the floor. They do not sweat. They do not thirst. They cannot be soft, or wet, or green. Everything must be able to be cooked and dried and cooked again.

Yet the Vault is not still. It is active, but at its own pace. Every few hours the heat-tide turns: the fissures brighten, the centre warms, the pavers ripple outward in a slow wave, hearthbacks inch toward the deep, ribbons rise from the cracks to follow them, and the whole living floor seems to exhale. Then it dims, and the migration reverses. Molting pavers rain their shed plates down from the ceiling — a dry, silent snowfall that never melts — and the glints swarm to eat it before it lands. In the hottest fissures, where even pavers cannot endure, the stone itself pops and ticks as it cools, spitting tiny glass beads.

Because of all this moving and eating and cracking, you might expect noise. There is almost none. The Vault is mostly, profoundly quiet. The basalt swallows sound. The creatures have no voices, no calls, no mating songs. They communicate by pressing against the stone and by warming a patch of their own shell a fraction of a degree — a signal felt in the floor, not heard in the air. What you do hear, if you stand still long enough, is the faint dry shingle of pavers shifting against each other, the occasional dull knock of a ribbon's kill, the soft hiss of gas escaping a pore in the wall, and, very distantly through the high fissure, the thin wind of the summit. It is a silence full of pressure.

Scattered through this living pavement are the built things. There are seven domes, waist-high, baked from the pellets excreted by hearthbacks and stacked by a species of chimney-builder that seems to have died out or merged into the general press of life. They are hollow, cool inside, and used now as nurseries where young hearthbacks sleep in the dark. There is one older structure, a half-ring of huge slabs leaning together like a broken tooth, too smooth and regular to be natural, too weathered now to read. No tool, no device, no mark of any maker remains on it. The pavers have paved over its base, and colony after colony has settled in its shadow. It is built, and it is abandoned, and it serves no purpose anyone here can use.

To walk the Vault you must place your feet between animals. They yield, slowly, reluctantly, the pavers rippling away in a circle around your boot and closing behind you. The hearthbacks do not yield at all; you step around them, feeling the heat come off their glass backs. The ribbons, sensing you, withdraw into the cracks with a soft sigh of displaced air.

Stay long enough — an hour, a day, it is hard to tell which in the dark — and the place comes into its own rhythm, which is not yours. The heat comes and goes. The floor breathes under you. The red light in the fissures pulses like a sleeping heart. Above you, upside down on the ceiling, a second pavement of pavers hangs in the dark, grazing on stone that has never seen a sky. Everything is packed against everything else, everything eating and being eaten, everything waiting for the next tide of warmth to move it a handsbreadth toward or away from the centre.

It is a small world, high up inside a mountain, hot and dark and dry and full.
