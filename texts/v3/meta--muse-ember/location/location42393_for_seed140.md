It is small enough to hold in your hearing.

From the lip of the hollow you cannot see its far wall. There is nothing to see with. The dark here is not night. Night is a dimming, a thinning of light. This is an absence, complete and old. Your eyes do not adjust, adjust, adjust, and at last find a shape. They find nothing at all. After a while you stop trying to look and begin to *arrive* — by the sole of the foot, by the skin of the face, by the place where the air touches you.

You are high. That is the first thing your body knows. The air on the back of your neck is thin and cool where it leaks in from outside, and your ears press and ache as you step down from the bent slit that is the only door. Outside, above this place, the wind is roaring over a summit plateau, cold and dry and bright in a way that has no business reaching in here. Inside, the air is warm.

Warm like a held breath. Warm like standing too close to another living thing. It has risen from deep in the mountain — geothermal heat, slow as a pulse, that has been bleeding upward for longer than there have been words for mountains. It meets the cold roof and the cold wind from the door and falls back on itself as water, and falls back again, and again. This is the whole weather of the place: a closed loop, a still.

The floor is not soil, mostly. It is stone that has forgotten how to be sharp. Everything in this room has been eroded to its final form and then kept there. The rocks are rounded like river pebbles the size of houses, soap-smooth, bone-soft. Where a cliff rises to meet the vaulted ceiling, its edge has melted away over an age that makes human ideas of age seem hasty. Nothing here looks young. Nothing looks as if it is in the middle of changing. It has settled. It is primordial in the sense of a thing that arrived at its shape once and has not bothered to leave it since.

And yet it is wet.

Water is everywhere but never *much* water at once, never a river, never a rain. It weeps. The walls of the bowl sweat in slow tears that run down in crooked, patient lines and darken the stone for a hand's width, then stop, then begin again a few inches away. Condensation beads on the ceiling and grows, grows, until the drop is heavy enough to fall the whole cathedral height of the room and strike with a sound like a struck bell. The floor — where it is not bare stone — is a marsh-edge of black mineral crust and spongy plant matter, ankle-deep in places, giving under the foot with a wet sigh. Small pools lie in the smooth hollows, warm, and occasionally sigh up a bubble, because at this height water boils more easily than it should, and heat is always coming up from below.

Most of the room is empty.

That is the strangeness of it, and the truth of it. The hollow is perhaps a hundred paces across, a grove, a single room with boundaries, and within it the living things do not spread. They clump. There will be a great bare sweep of polished stone, pale now where the weeping has bleached it, mist hovering a foot above it, nothing growing for ten, twenty paces — a vast hollow between presences. Then, abruptly, a sudden lushness: a curtain of growth so thick you could hide in it, dripping, warm, alive with touch.

The vegetation is lush to an almost indecent degree inside those clumps. Ferns that never unfurl to the sun, because there is no sun. Broad, hand-sized leaves, translucent as a mushroom's gill, veined and hairy, feeling their way toward the warm cracks. Mosses in waist-deep mats, black-green and spongy, that hold water like a held sponge and give it back very, very slowly. Vines like braided hair hanging from the walls, their ends rooting in mid-air where the mist is thickest. Everything is pale. Not white — white would imply light to reflect. Pale the color of things that have never needed color: the grey of lichen, the cream of new stem, the dull copper of a leaf that feeds on warmth and minerals and the slow chemical rain of the mountain's interior. Growth here is not driven by light. It is driven by heat, and by sound, and by the fall of water. The leaves turn toward the warm seams in the stone. Some of them, you realize after standing a while, turn very slightly toward *you* — toward the warmth of a body.

Underfoot, death is just as slow and just as thorough. A fallen frond does not rot in a season. It is worked by fungi, by mites, by bacteria, by the patient chemistry of the wet stone, over years, until it becomes stone again. The whole grove is recycling itself in place, and nothing is allowed to leave. The water that fell as rain on the summit a thousand years ago may still be falling here. The air that you breathe has been breathed, and warmed, and cooled, and breathed again, for longer than any animal that lives here has been alive.

And the animals are abundant, once you stop expecting to see them.

They are not large, mostly, and they do not want to be seen. They live by sound and by heat and by touch, and they are *loud* in the way of things that live in a stone bell. The room itself is a resonator. A drip from the ceiling rings for ten seconds in the bowl of rock. A creature somewhere under the moss-mat clears its throat and the whole hollow hums. There are bell-throated things that live in the hollow stems of the ferns, and when they call, it is not a song so much as a resonance set up in the plant, a low, damped booming that you feel in your teeth. There are clickers in the mist — blind, winged swimmers of the air that navigate by echolocation and fill the dark with a rain of tiny, rapid pulses, like someone snapping fingers far away and then very close. There are vast, slow grazers that move across the bare stone at night-through-day, leaving broad, wet trails, their bodies warm as stones in sun. And beneath all of that, in the black crust of the marsh-floor, is a seething abundance: worms, mites, snails without shells, beetles without eyes, a whole second fauna turning the floor over an inch at a time.

You find them not by looking but by stepping. The crust breaks. The mist stirs. Something vast and slow complains, somewhere, and the sound goes around the walls and comes back to you from a direction that is not where it started.

The built things are scattered so widely that at first you think they are part of the stone.

A low black spike, knee-high, growing out of the floor between two moss clumps, warm to the touch in a way the geothermal stone is not warm, humming at a frequency just below hearing. A seam running up the far cliff like a healed scar, too straight, made of a lattice finer than any weave, that slowly, *slowly*, rearranges itself — you would not notice unless you watched for a hundred years, and even then only as a difference in the way water runs off it. A flat disk set into the stone by the door, cold, around which condensation gathers in a perfect ring. There are perhaps a dozen of them in the whole room, no two alike, each alone, each far from the last.

They are not ruins. Ruins are broken and falling. These are old in a different way — beyond the engineering we have, atom-by-atom things, molecular machines embedded in rock that still do their work on a timescale of centuries. They manage the room. They keep the cold places cold so the water will weep there. They keep the warm places warm. They exchange heat from the deep mountain for chemical work in the soil, feeding the plants that have no light to feed on. They have been doing it for so long that the grove has grown over them like hair over a scar, and they have let it, and they will continue. They are perhaps the reason the place exists at all, and perhaps they were only ever here to watch it. The weirdness of it is not that they are strange. It is that they are so quiet about being strange.

This is the rule of the world here, if it has a rule: nothing hurries, and nothing leaves, and heat is what light is for everything else. The dynamic of the place is a slow breathing that takes decades to complete a cycle. A pool fills for a lifetime and empties for a lifetime. A fern grows a finger's width in a century and lives to be a forest. One of the artifacts by the wall will, every eleven or twelve years, give off a single, perfect tone that hangs in the stone for a day and then stops, and the animals will answer it, all of them, once, and then be quiet again.

Stay long enough and your other senses learn. You learn the map by sound: the weeping wall to the north, where the drips fall fastest; the loud clump of bell-ferns to the east, where the mist is thickest; the warm, empty stone to the south, where the grazers cross. You learn that the darkness is not empty. It is full — of water falling, of warmth rising, of living things calling to each other across distances that feel enormous in a room that fits in an afternoon's walk. It is a small, high, ancient room, settled into its final shape, wet and warm and dark and alive, with vast emptiness between its heartbeats, and it has been this way for so long that the stone itself seems to be remembering how to be alive.

You do not leave footprints that last. The seep restores the floor behind you.
