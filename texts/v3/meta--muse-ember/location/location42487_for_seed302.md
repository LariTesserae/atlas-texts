You come up onto it from below, and the light changes first.

There is no treeline to pass through, no soil slope softening the climb. The last incline is stone giving way to more stone, smoothed and weathered until the edge is almost a gesture, and then it opens. Not a summit. A plateau, high enough that the air is thin in your chest and the sky takes up more of the world than the ground does. Far off, ridge after ridge falls away in blue layers, but close in there is no falling away at all. The land extends past where the eye wants to settle. It is open in the way a sea is open — you could walk for a day and only learn that it continues.

At first it looks dry, because it is so bright. The light is white and high and clean,  a midday sun with nothing to cut it. It hits the ground and comes back up again, filling your shadow from underneath so that even where the stones should shade each other, they glare. You squint. The horizon bleaches. The stones sparkle. It seems like a desert of pale gravel at altitude, a barrens.

Then your boot lands and the stone is cold and wet.

It is wet everywhere. Not flooded, not rained on — there is no cloud, no weather passing — but wet in the way a thing stays wet when water has nowhere to go and nowhere to leave from. Between the stones, a film pulls itself across every surface. Small pools sit in the hollows, perfectly level and perfectly still, reflecting the sky so that you see light above and light below at once. Droplets cling in the packed joints where one rock touches another, swell, and fall. Rivulets no thicker than a finger lace their way a few body-lengths and then disappear into the pavement, as if the ground had simply drunk them and decided to keep them.

The rocks weep. All of them. The large ones at the margins, rounded by a very old patience, and the small ones that make up most of the world. Moisture beads on their upper faces even though the air feels cool and clear, because the stones are colder than the air and the air is full. Condensation forms, runs, falls, evaporates, and forms again. It is a closed, quiet circulation, except it is not quiet.

That is the next thing. The sound.

You hear it before you understand it, a roar that fills your ears and chest, constant and without weather in it. It is not wind alone, though wind is moving through it — a thin, high pressure you can feel in your teeth as you breathe. It is mainly water, and multitudes of it. Thousands, millions of droplets falling at once onto stone and into pools, ticking and hissing and splashing in a register too small for any one drop to own. Because there are so many, they merge. A single drip would be a note; this is the whole surface playing at once, a deafening, even wash, like standing inside a waterfall that has been spread thin across a valley. It does not rise. It does not fall. When you stop walking, it does not stop. When you stamp your foot, your own step is swallowed and the roar does not change. You find you are shouting without meaning to, and you cannot hear yourself.

Your voice would be useless here. The animals, such as they are, would have learned that long ago.

The density of the place is in the ground. There is no room between presences. Stone on stone on crystal on water, packed into a pavement that covers everything you can see. The stones are mostly egg-sized, water-worn, sorted by age and current into a kind of order — pale quartz, grey basalt darkened by wetness, flecks of rust and ochre in the mineral crust that binds them. Smaller shards fill the gaps the way sand fills a wall. Even the pools are paved at the bottom with the same material, visible through clear, cold water. You do not walk on this landscape so much as pick your way across its back, each step a negotiation that leaves a brief, dark wet print which the film instantly covers again.

And there is nothing green in it. That is the strangest, plainest fact, and you keep looking for it as if you must have missed it. No grass. No moss on the wet stones where moss ought to be. No lichen rosettes, no shrubs tucked against the bigger rocks, no soil for any of them to root in. The color of the place is entirely mineral: white for the light, grey and black for wet stone, pale yellow and iron-red in thin crusts. The cold, minerals-rich water that seeps up through the pavement seems to have made the ground inhospitable in the way that matters — too hard, too bright, too constantly washed, too poor in anything a plant could use. Life here is not planted. It is thinner than that.

If you kneel — and the roar does not lessen — you will see it. In the damp pores of the stones, in the skin of water over a pool, there are films: dull, translucent, faintly amber or grey, like breath on glass. Bacteria, doing the work plants do elsewhere, gathering light and minerals and making a slime you have to know to look for. They do not move. They do not change in any way you can measure. They are the base of everything, and they are almost invisible.

Fauna, when it comes, is rare in proportion. You may walk for hours and see nothing that walks itself. Then, perhaps, in one of the larger sheets of water where it deepens and slackens, you will find a flatworm. Glacier flatworms are pale, translucent grey, no longer than your thumb, pressed flat to the stone as if the water were holding them down. It moves a centimeter and then stops, scrapes at the bacterial film with a mouth you cannot see, and waits. It has no use for ears in this noise. It feels the stone vibrate, reads the world through touch. There might be two or three in sight if you are lucky. Behind you, very high, a single pale falcon crosses the white sky once and does not land. That is the whole census, as far as you can tell.

Everything here has the settled look of a thing that has already finished happening. The erosion is not dramatic. There are no fresh scars, no sharp new scree, no cliffs about to fall. The stones are rounded because they have been in water for a very long time. The undulations in the plateau are gentle and filled in, the valleys shallow, the whole form in its own balance. Water levels in the pools do not rise after a night or fall by afternoon. Mist hangs in the same far hollows for as long as you watch, neither thickening nor lifting. This is not a landscape waiting for change; it is a landscape that has reached equilibrium and stayed there.

That is why nothing human would leave a mark, even if it tried. There is no path, no cairn, no cut stone, no artifact of any kind, and no reason to expect one. A footprint fills with the seeping film and is smoothed by the next hundred drips. A dropped thing would be polished by water and wind until it joined the pavement. The place absorbs and returns to itself.

The air stays cool against your skin despite the brightness — high, wet, continuously breathing in evaporation off the stone. You smell nothing that lives. Only cold rock, faint metal, clean ozone from the light, and the damp mineral breath of the pools. Each breath is a little shallow. The light, reflected from below, still hurts your eyes long after you have learned to look down.

And the roar continues, deafening and unvarying, a sound made not by one thing happening but by everything happening at the same small rate, forever. The dripping, the seeping, the condensing, the wind over hollow stones. It comes from no one place, so you cannot walk toward it or away from it. You carry it in your jaw and your sternum. After a while you stop trying to listen for variety in it and simply accept that this is the silence of this place — not an absence of sound, but a fullness of it so even and so complete that it becomes a kind of stillness.

You could come back in a year, or in a hundred, and I think the pools would be at the same level, the same stone weeping the same slow tear, the same faultless white glare off a ground packed tight with stone and water and no green at all. It is high, and wet, and bright, and loud, and still. Not frozen, but finished. A world that does not need to move to continue.
