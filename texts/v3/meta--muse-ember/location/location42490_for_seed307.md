At the top of the world, there is a room no one built.

The mountain is new. Not old and softened, not carved by rivers and time. It is still lifting itself — a ridge like a knife-edge of fresh basalt and volcanic glass, so sharp you can see the fractures running through it where the earth cracked apart. The air up here is too thin to hold heat. Your lungs burn. The light is plain midday light, flat and ordinary, grey-white on black rock, and there is nothing to shelter from it. No soil. No moss. No green thing has ever rooted here, no dead twig has lodged in a crack. It is mineral all the way down, and newly mineral at that.

And yet, on the leeward face just below the true summit, one fissure is sweating.

It is no wider than your boot. It weeps. A bead of water forms on glassy stone, swells, runs, and another forms beside it, though the whole slope around it is dry and scoured by high wind. Steam rises from it in a silent thread. Put your ear close and you hear nothing, which is strange, because you can see the steam moving.

You have to crouch to enter.

Inside, the world gets smaller and bigger at once.

The pocket is perhaps an arm's length across, a cupped hollow in the rock you could span with your hands, and it contains a weather, a sea, and a crowd. This is its whole extent. From outside it is a damp sore on a peak. From inside it is everything.

The heat hits first, before your eyes adjust. The back wall is geothermal — not lava you can see, but a deep, stored burn in the stone, the mountain still warm from its own birth. The water that seeps from it comes off boiling in the thin air, which here, under the cap of rock, boils at a temperature that should not be possible and does not feel that hot to the skin until you touch it. On the opposite wall the stone is cooler by a few degrees and the steam condenses there instantly, so it rains perpetually inside — a warm, fine rain that never reaches the floor. It drips from the ceiling, from the side walls, from the sharp, un-weathered crystals that have grown inward since the fracture opened. Everything is wet. The rocks weep. The glass weeps. Your own face weeps, with condensation that is not yours. Your boots slip on silica crust as white and slick as ice, though it is far too hot for ice.

There is no plant life. Not a blade, not a root-hair, not even the blackened creepers you expect in a hot wet place. Even the fringe where the pocket meets the cold mountain air is bare. What grows here — if growth is the word — is a skin. Thin bacterial and fungal films, grey and opalescent, like breath on a mirror, feeding not on sunlight and soil but on dissolved minerals, on sulfur and iron and the waste of the animals. They do not spread like meadows. They thicken where the water runs, they are eaten back in minutes, they reform. The ground itself is the food.

And the ground is moving.

Because the animal life is overwhelming.

It is not a few creatures in a crevice. It is a density that makes the surfaces alive. The bottom pool — a shallow, superheated lens of water perhaps as wide as your palm — is clouded white with shrimp-like swimmers, eyeless, translucent, beating in unison so that the whole pool ripples without wind. They filter the mineral film from the water and from each other in endless, silent churns. Above them, on the wet glass, are mites the size of pinheads, and worms the size of eyelashes, in numbers you cannot count, layered three and four deep, near-touching, shoulder to shoulder, step over step, so that the wall seems to crawl even when an individual is still. In a depression of warmer rock, a tide of blind crablets works back and forth, carrying each other.

They are not quiet because they are inactive. The pocket is one of the most active places you have ever been, and it is almost perfectly silent.

No buzzing. No clicking. No splash. The steam eats vibration. The creatures themselves, for the most part, do not make sound to make — no throats, no wings, no lungs to push air across a reed. You hear only the soft, irregular *tick* of a droplet cooling on hot glass, the faintest sigh as a bubble bursts in the pool and dies before its ring can form. Your own breathing sounds obscene in here by comparison. Predator and prey pass each other in a continuous, soundless procession.

The larger hunters are thumb-long and made of clear, smoked material, like boiled cartilage. They move along the ceiling as readily as the floor. One of them — and there are perhaps a dozen that matter — has learned a primitive trick. It holds a pumice pebble between its fore-claws and uses it as a hammer to crack the silica shells of the shrimp below. Another drags a shard of volcanic glass, still sharp from the young fracture, and uses it to lever open prey. The tools are used and dropped. They are not kept, not cached, not built upon. Nothing here is built at all. There is no nest, no web, no burrow with a made entrance, no artifact left for longer than a minute. The rock has never been worn by a hand. Even the paths of the swarm are not paths — they shift with the boiling cycle.

That cycle is the weather. Every forty seconds or so, the pool builds to a rolling boil, steam rushes up, condenses hard against the cool stones, and rains back down in a heavier patter. The creatures ride it. The grazers surge up the walls away from the heat, the hunters surge down after them when it passes. The whole pocket turns over like a lung breathing — in, out, in, out — and the populations rise and crash with it. Birth is constant in the warm water, death is constant, and the mineral skin is scraped clean and regrows before the rain stops.

That is when the place stops behaving.

On one wall, the water runs uphill. Not a trick of perspective — you brace your hand, palm flat on glass, and feel the sheet moving up past your fingers toward the ceiling, where it gathers into a hanging pool that does not fall. The shrimp in that ceiling pool swim upside down relative to the floor pool, in exact, mirrored unison, and the hunters drink from it as if it were the floor. You tilt your head to look, and the tilt feels like falling.

A droplet falls upward from the pool, bursts against nothing, and for a moment you see your own face in it, although you are looking up. The animals do not react. The big hunter on the ceiling casts a shadow on the floor that is larger than the hunter, and the shadow arrives half a second before the hunter moves into the light.

Walk — crawl — the three steps the pocket allows, and you are back at the entrance, but the entrance is now at your feet and the summit is above you *inside* the rock, a small, sharp peak of glass pointing down at you, dripping. The knife-edge ridge outside is echoed, inverted, in here. It is hard to tell, after a while, whether the pocket is inside the mountain or the mountain is a swelling inside the pocket.

Time does not pass evenly. The pool is simultaneously hatching and dying. A crablet emerges from an egg that has not yet been laid, is eaten by a hunter that will not move for another cycle, and the mineral film it leaves behind is already being grazed. You see the same ripple cross the pool nine times from nine different directions at once.

And still the light is normal. That is the worst part. It is not gloom, not phosphorescence, not the red glow of the deep earth. It is ordinary daylight, leaking in through the boot-width crack, falling straight and plain on the steam, making every translucent body visible, every drop glittering, every sharp crystal throw a clear, hard sparkle. There is nowhere for the impossibility to hide.

You understand, before you leave, that you are not really inside a crack. The crack is the door. The pocket is too hot at the top and too wet at the bottom and too high and too crowded and too silent for any one law to hold over the whole of it. It holds together because it is young — the rock has not yet decided what shape it will be, the water has not yet decided which way is down, the animals have not yet decided which of them will be which. It is a place still forming, and so it is forming in all its forms at once.

When you pull yourself back out onto the summit, the fissure is steaming again, weeping, blind. The wind, which you realize you had not heard inside, rushes past your ears. Below you the world is immense and old and empty of green. Behind you, in the space of your two hands, an entire hot, wet, teeming, silent world is boiling, raining, hunting, hammering shells with stones, and running its water uphill — fresh, sharp, and new, as if the earth had only just cracked open and was not yet sure what to do with what crawled out.
