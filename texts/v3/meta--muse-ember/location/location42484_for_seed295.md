The light goes first, long before the trees do.

You come down from the high ridge in hard, thin sunlight, wind on your face, and the valley opens below you already in shadow. It is a deep intermontane basin, its sides steep and terraced, its floor lost in a canopy so complete it does not look like a canopy at all. From above it has a uniform, woven texture, grey-green and brown, moving in one slow sheet when the wind passes over it. No single tree crown stands out. No river cuts it. No clearing interrupts it. Only as you descend into the damp, warm air trapped below the ridge do the separate bright and dark patches resolve into something else — and then they keep resolving, and resolving, until “forest” and “building” are no longer useful as separate words.

The ground is the first of them to fail you.

There is no soil path. There are no stones to step on. The terraces that flatten the slope are made of packed mud chewed and re-chewed, leaf pulp, insect pellets, crushed shell, fallen wax, generations of droppings cemented by rain and then warmed by sun on the roof above. Your foot sinks a finger’s width, then springs back. It is soft but not yielding, worn into shallow steps and broad ramps that switch back across the slope in curves polished smooth as old wood. Where the terrace edge drops, it is held by a living retaining wall of roots and woven grass and mud tubes, and through that wall water beads and does not run.

Scratch anywhere and you do not find earth. You find layers: a sheet of silk grey with dust, under it a crust of pellets, under that a mat of chewed leaf glued with saliva, under that another sheet of silk. It is a geology made by mouths. Even the rock faces above the valley, where scree and cliff should show, are veneered — swallow nests plastered one upon another until the wall is a pebbled crust, termite tubes climbing in branching runs, bee combs under every overhang, hanging bird nests swaying thick as tassels from every branch that overhangs the void.

Everything here is made, and nothing here is made by hands or by any tool. No cut stone, no fired brick, no metal, no rope not spun by a living body. The builders work with mandibles, beaks, paws, silken glands, wax glands. What they have made, over successions of colonies and flocks and swarms replacing each other on the same sites for longer than any one generation can remember, is not a collection of structures set upon a landscape. It is the landscape. A valley roofed, floored, walled, and tunneled over until there is no natural substrate left to see.

That is why it is dark even in the middle of the day.

The sun does strike the high ridges and the outermost thatch for an hour or two, and the vegetation is lush enough to insist it must be getting light somewhere. But below, the roof is continuous. Woven grass, layered leaves stitched with silk, mud tiles armored with gum and resin, dense mats of hanging nests, wax sheets, the overlapping bodies of vines — together they make a ceiling only a few metres above your head in most places, and in places a second and third ceiling above that. Light comes in sideways, in shafts through gaps, in green dusk under the outer branches. Your eyes adjust and adjust again. Ferns, mosses, shade orchids, bromeliads, and broad, soft-leaved plants that have never needed a blaze of sun make up the undergrowth, and they grow not in soil but in the compost of the buildings themselves — in abandoned nests, in leaf-mould caught on a roof ledge, in the rich guano-cement of a bat roost, in a crack between mud bricks where condensation has sat for a season.

Plants here are not separate from the architecture. They are its mortar and its insulation and its repair crew. A vine is a cable across a passage. A tree trunk is a pole that has been wrapped, tube by tube, in termite mud until its bark is only a rumour inside a column. Roots run along the inside of walls, binding them, drinking the damp that seeps through. When a nest is abandoned, fruit bats drop seeds into it; a fig grows out of it, its roots prising and holding at once. When a mud wall is no longer kept, it slumps, saturates, and becomes a planter of ferns. Building and growing are the same patient process, viewed at different speeds.

Water is everywhere as damp and almost nowhere as water.

There is no stream to follow. Rain comes — a cloud pushed over the ridge by the afternoon wind, ten minutes of warm drizzle that darkens the thatch and hisses on hot roofs, then gone. Most of the moisture arrives as mist, as condensation on cool mud in the morning, as droplets beaded on silk and leaf and your own skin. It soaks into walls and stays there. Moss is saturated to the touch. Bromeliads hold a cupful each, dark and still, and hummingbirds and ants drink from them one sip at a time. In hollows of the built floor, ponds no larger than a hand sit lined with silk and leaf pulp, their surfaces filmed with pollen. Animals lick walls. Frogs call from puddles in roof joints. Everything is wet enough to rot, to grow fungi in soft grey fans, to smell of warm earth and crushed green leaf, but there is never a flood, never a current. The whole place drinks slowly, from the air.

The air itself is warm — warmer than the wind on the ridge would lead you to expect at this height. Body heat from millions of inhabitants, fermentation in the compost walls, sun stored in the day’s roofs and released through the night into the tunnels. It moves sluggishly, at ant speed, from chamber to passage to open shaft, carrying smell in layers: honey and wax from under a roof, guano sharp and ammoniac from a roost overhead, the sweet rot of fallen fruit, the clean smell of a termite tube after rain.

And then there is the life, which is not so much present as overwhelming.

It is not one species doing an ordinary thing at an extraordinary scale, though that would be enough. It is every ordinary thing happening at once, in every layer, near-touching. Leafcutter ants march in processions thick as your wrist along a silk highway, carrying green flags above them. Beneath your feet, beetles and worms and mites work the walls. Above your head, weave birds are almost constantly in motion, arriving with grass, departing with a convulsive chatter. Bats shift in a roost behind a wax curtain. Swallows dart in and out of a cliff face that is itself a solid wall of their old nests. A colony of stingless bees hums behind a sheet of resin so close you feel it in your teeth. On a sunlit edge, butterflies and hummingbirds crowd the flowering vines that have rooted in the roof. In the dark passages, moths, cave crickets, and small nocturnal mammals keep their own hours, which blur with everyone else’s, because down here day and night are only rumors.

You do not so much hear this as stand inside it. The sound is deafening, but not with any one noise. It is a continuous low drone of wings, so pervasive you stop noticing it as sound and start noticing its absences. Over it, a thousand local events: clicking mandibles, chewing, the patter of droppings, the rustle of a thousand leaves being carried, birds calling to birds, frogs in the roof joints, drips in a tunnel, wind worrying the thatch, a branch sighing under the weight of a colony. When a bird lands on the rail beside you, its claws click on mud brick. When a procession of ants crosses your boot, they do not scatter; they climb around you, unhurriedly, as if you were a warm stone that had briefly interrupted their road. The noise does not frighten them. They are the noise.

Nothing in this place is strange in itself. That is its rule, and the reason it holds together. No mind directs it. No architect has drawn it. Each animal does what its kind has always done — plaster, weave, chew, carry, excrete, roost, feed — and because the valley is warm, high, damp, and sheltered, and because a hundred thousand years of small acts have never been cleared away, the accumulation has become total. Males die, colonies split, swarms move one tree over, and the structure they left behind is kept by the next tenants, repaired at the edges, softened by weather at the same rate it is rebuilt. Erosion here is not decay. It is polish. Tunnel ceilings are smooth where a million shoulders have brushed them. Roof sags fall into familiar, comfortable curves. The oldest walls are the roundest, their mud worked into a sort of living brick by rain, moss, and constant nibbling repair. The place looks settled because it is settled; it has reached the form its climate and its inhabitants can maintain.

Change, when it comes, is slow beyond human impatience. A passage can grow a finger’s width in a year. A roof can thicken by one leaf layer a season. A fern can fill a doorway in a decade and become the doorway. You could live a lifetime in one side valley and learn its drafts and its drips and its loudest hours, and the place would look the same to you at the end, except for the soft accumulation of new silk over old, green over brown, one more generation of nests hung beneath the last. The seasons barely arrive. Mist comes at the same hour. The same cloud spills the same brief rain. Fruit ripens, is eaten, seeds fall into compost, grow, fruit again. It is a cycle with almost no gradient, a metabolism stretched out across long durations.

From a high point on the far ridge, if you can find one where the roof thins, you can see the scale of it. The built valley is not one nest, not one mound, not one colony’s work. Valley after valley, spur after spur, the same textured thatch extends past the immediate view to other high basins, other shaded floors, other cliffs plastered with nests. It is a regional thing, town-sized and larger, a landscape that has been continuously inhabited and continuously remade until remaking and inhabiting are indistinguishable.

You descend again into it, and the light fails, and the sound thickens, and the warm damp closes over you like a held breath. Somewhere above, a weaver bird is stitching a leaf to a roof. Somewhere below, an ant is cementing a pellet to a wall. Somewhere between, a plant is rooting in last year’s droppings and drinking this morning’s mist. The place is not built *in* nature. It is nature, built — patiently, deafeningly, in the dark, by everyone who lives here, doing the ordinary work of living, until there was nothing left unbuilt to stand on.
