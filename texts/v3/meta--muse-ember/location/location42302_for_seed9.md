It begins as weather, not as ground.

You emerge into it — in every direction — wet and warm and bright, and you cannot yet see where you are standing. Below you, a sea of cloud. Above you, a pale, almost level sky. The air is thick, condensing on your forearms, on the handrail, on whatever you touch. It is high. That you know before you know anything else: the light has that thin, high-country clarity, the cloud sea is a long way down, and the pressure change in your ears on the way up was real. But it is not cold. It is not thin in the way it should be. Sun trapped on stone and metal and wet green, heat rising from engines below and from the sun above, has made a warm pocket at this altitude, like the inside of a kettle left in the sun.

And then, slowly, the foging lifts from the surfaces — not from the air — and you realize there is no ground.

There is no soil. There are hills, of a kind. There are slopes, terraces, hollows where water sits in shallow, breathing pools. But tap them. Hollows. The hill under your left foot is a stack of ribbed, interlocking shells, poured and bolted and then turfed over. The ridge to your right is a long, low factory roof, its corrugation softened by a mat of moss and sedge and small, bright ferns that have been encouraged to grow thick there to hold the rain. The valley between them is a lane, a catwalk three meters wide, then two, then barely one where the newer construction crowds it. Everything is made. Even the rocks the water weeps from are cast.

This is a small place. Though it is dense, and feels layered and close, you could cross it on foot in twenty minutes if nothing were in your way — and much is. It is bounded. At its edge is a parapet, a fence of greenhouse frame and cable, and then a straight drop into cloud and then into nothing you can see. It is one feature, raised up: a hamlet of workshops, dormitories, vats, and gardens stacked into a little artificial plateau, high above the weather, manufacturing its own.

The water never really stops. It does not rain here in the old way; it arrives as fog intercepted on fins and fins' worth of mesh, and as condensate off hot pipes and cooling walls, and as the deliberate spray of irrigation cycles. It runs everywhere, in a closed loop you can follow with your eyes. Gutters on every edge drip without pause. Pipes sweat at their joints. Where a new module-shell is still curing, water beading from its uncured concrete makes dark, weeping streaks down the whole wall, like an old cliff seep, though the wall is three weeks old. Pools in the low places are not natural depressions; they are utility sumps, shallow and warm, skinned with algae, being stirred by slow paddles to keep them from stagnating. The whole place has the damp of a landscape just after a storm, except the storm is scheduled. Every forty minutes or so the fans on the towers turn, the spray-bars sigh, and a fine warm mist goes over the gardens. Between cycles, condensation gathers and runs and gathers again.

The green is the loudest thing about it, and the least wild.

Because there is no natural substrate, the vegetation is an engineered lushness, and it thrives anyway. It floods the built surfaces. Turf modules — bricks of pressed fibre and algae and mineral pellet — are stacked into living retaining walls. From the gutters hang curtains of fern and water hyacinth, roots dangling in the nutrient runnels, harvesting the runoff before it reaches the sumps. There are trees, real trees, but each stands in a container-vat, its roots in a dark tank you can hear bubbling when you pass, its trunk laced to the frame with cables that it is growing around. Fruit trees have been grafted to grow flat, in espalier across the sides of air ducts. Rice grows in suspended troughs that move two centimeters a day along a track, so that no blade ever has to be in the same light too long. Inside the pipe-vents, where it is always warm and wet, a phosphorescent moss has been seeded; it has escaped a little, and now it creeps in pale green seams along the ceiling of the underwalk, making the shadowed places glow faintly at dusk.

You realize, walking it, that you are walking through time as much as space. The southern terraces are old, by the standards of this place: fully overgrown, their steel struts padded with vines, their workshops occupied, roofs heavy with compost and crops. The northern end is embryonic. There, the landscape is still being made, and that state of making is itself the terrain. Rebar rises like a thicket of winter twigs. A hillside that will one day be a garden is at present a raw slope of aggregate and polythene, staked down, waiting for its turf. Cranes — small, articulated, industrial — stand in the lanes between half-finished shells, lifting another module into place with great care, because everything must be placed and nothing can fall. Survey marks in chalk fade on wet concrete as fast as they are drawn. Scaffolding is the forest here: a lattice of poles that the ferns are already exploring, tasting it. Erosion, in the usual sense, has not happened; there has not been time for a hill to wear away. Instead, there is constant newness, fresh edges, surfaces that have not yet been rained on for a year and surfaces that have not yet been rained on at all.

That is why it is so active, even though it is small. So many processes are running at once, near-touching, layered vertically. Below your feet, through the deck, you can feel the thrum of a press shop and the hiss of steam. Above you, a conveyor carries woven matting from one roof to another in the sun, where it is dried by the heat coming through the cloud sea below. Air moves in cycles: up through the warm stacks, out through the vent fins that catch the fog, in again. The irrigation loop, the compost loop, the nutrient loop, the heat loop — they all cross each other in the lanes, in the walls. A work crew in yellow slicks comes past carrying baskets of cuttings to the compost shredder; another crew, two meters above them on a gantry, is bolting a new water tank into place; a third is harvesting from the moving rice troughs, their boots in warm ankle-deep water. Harvest days, planting days, construction lifts, spray cycles, night-shift drainage — they overlap. Nothing is allowed to be still for long, because a still pool or a still pile of cuttings would rot. The place must keep moving to stay alive.

And yet it is quiet. That is its strangest discipline.

For all the industry — the extrusion lines in the low hall, the lathes, the pumping engines housed in their own damp, insulated rooms — the sound is muffled almost to nothing. The machines are built inside sound-deadening shells of wet felt and recycled rubber, and the water everywhere absorbs the rest. What you hear, mostly, is that water: the constant tick-tick of drips from the gutters, the cluck of pumps, the soft rush in the runnels, the occasional clear clank of a tool set down which stands out precisely because it is rare. Workers speak low, not because they are told to, but because shouting carries too far across the flat, exposed plateau and everyone knows everyone else's shift. A voice, when it comes, is unhurried. Birdsong, in a wild place this wet and green, would be constant. Here it is absent, and the absence becomes a kind of sound itself.

Animals are correspondingly rare — not absent, but rare enough that to see one is an event worth noting to the next shift. There are no insects to speak of, despite the lushness; the fans and the mist keep them from establishing, and any that arrive are treated as contaminants in the nutrient loop. A pair of white birds nests each season in the warm vent of the high tower, quiet, very clean birds, circling once in the morning and then gone into the cloud sea. In the lower sumps, a few amphibians that were brought up years ago have persisted in the pipes, pale and translucent, living on algae. That is nearly all. It is a green place without a fauna to match, and the green is therefore all the more artificial, all the more tended, all the more in need of human hands to do the work that pollinators and grazers would do elsewhere. Drones do some of it, slow and ungainly, bumping gently off cables. Most of it is done by people, in pairs, moving at walking pace through the wet.

The light is normal, which is to say, unremarkable, and that unremarkableness is a relief. High, diffuse daylight filtered through the permanent haze of evaporation, flat and warm, no harsh shadow, no dusk that comes early. Colors saturate: the green of wet turf, the rust of a new pipe joint, the copper bright of a runnel, the pale concrete of a shell where the moss hasn't reached yet. Shadows pool under the catwalks, where the phosphor-moss glows.

If there is a rule to this place, it is that nothing is allowed to be natural, and nothing is allowed to be finished. The soil is imported and made. The hills are made. The rain is made. The trees are made, in the sense that someone decided where a tree should be and built the tank for it. And yet, once made, the made things begin to act like a landscape anyway: the turf slumps a little where it is wettest, the vines find the bolt-holes, the algae chooses the warmest sump and not the one intended for it, the condensation chooses the coldest rib of a roof and weeps there regardless of the diagram. The construction is always finishing in the south and always starting in the north, so the whole hamlet breathes — a new terrace rising, an old terrace sinking under its own lush weight, water being rerouted, a lane that was a lane last month now a wall of beans.

You stand at the parapet at the very top, where the newest frames are still bone-white and the old ferns lean out over the drop, and look back across it. It is a small world, dense and dripping and warm, high above everything, building itself and growing over itself at the same time, quiet except for water, green except for the hands that tend it, alive not in spite of being made but because being made is the only way, up here, to have a landscape at all.
