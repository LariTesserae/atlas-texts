You come up to it from above, because everything here is above everything else.

The road — if it can be called that — is a flattened track of broken schist across a high ridge, pale grey and bare. The air is cool even in full light, and thin enough that your steps sound louder to you than they should. There is no forest to announce a town. There is first only stone, rounded by a very long time of weather, and a few tufts of grass forced up between cracks where a little soil has settled. The horizon is wide and ordinary: a big, normal sky, daylight white-gold and even, the low country far below in a haze you can see but never quite reach.

The town, when you find it, is not hidden. It simply does not extend. It lies in a shallow hollow on the ridge, bounded on three sides by the natural fall of the rock and on the fourth by a low, thick wall that has been rebuilt in the same place for longer than anyone can name. From the gate you can see the whole of it at once. That is its first rule: it is small enough to be taken in, and complete.

Inside, the second rule takes over. It is built like a town, but it breathes like open country. There are lanes, laid out with intent along the contour, and they meet at a small paved square. There are more walls, steps, drains, and retaining terraces than there are houses. The houses themselves stand well apart. Between them are empty yards of beaten earth and flat stone, an unused pen, a fallow plot kept clear by hand, a stretch of open pavement where nothing is stored and no one is passing. The sparsity is not neglect. It is maintained. Everything is in its place, and the places are mostly empty.

Thirty or forty houses, a communal hall, a granary, a smithy, a pottery kiln, a row of weaving sheds. No more. The buildings are of the place: grey schist and fieldstone, dark slate roofs laid heavy, limewash peeling to show the stone beneath. Nothing is new and nothing is falling. That is the settled state of it. Every house is layered. A thick ground floor of older stone, a second story added when a family grew, a lean-to kitchen tacked on with a different slate, a doorway moved six inches to follow a drain. The layers do not argue. They have worn into one another. Corners are rounded, thresholds are dished in the middle by centuries of feet, steps are concave, the carved notch that marks each house number on its post has been smoothed almost illegible. Erosion here went as far as it was going to go, and then the town learned to stop it. Every roof soaks to its own channel, every lane falls gently to its own stone gutter, every yard drains to the square. The rain, when it comes, runs where it is told and cuts nothing new.

The tech is hand tech, and it shows in the materials. Beams are reused and slightly too short, joined with copper rivets and wooden pegs. Tools are stone hammers, simple iron, bronze where iron would rust too fast. There is a forge with a bellows worked by foot, a loom in each weaving shed, a wheel for pots turned by hand. Carts are two-wheeled and rare; most things are carried. There is no machine here that makes a sound of its own. The town is organized — the granary stands on stone posts against damp and rats, the kiln is shared, the hall in the middle is common to all — but the organization is the kind that takes generations of hands shaping stone, not engines.

Water is a presence, not a landscape. This is a damp place without a river. The air holds moisture most days; in the morning the north-facing walls sweat, and under the arch into the hall, condensation beads and falls from the vault in a slow, regular drip onto the stone floor. Your palm on a wall in shade comes away cool and faintly wet even on a dry day. Rain comes briefly and without drama — a passing shower that darkens the slate, makes the gutters run for an hour, smells of cold mineral, and then is gone, absorbed. There are no puddles that last. The water lives in pockets, carefully kept: a rock basin at the spring just outside the wall, roof-water led to a covered cistern under the square, long shallow troughs along the smithy lane, a row of jugs by every door. Each house is responsible for its own catchment. Nothing is wasted because nothing is abundant.

Because of that, the green is poor and stubborn. Vegetation finds only the wet lines. A single stem of grass in a paving crack. Lichen in orange and black patches on the south walls where sun reaches, moss only directly under a drip line, never spreading farther. A few hardy shrubs in the yards, pruned small. A row of mountain ash by the granary, stunted, kept alive more than grown. The town feeds itself more from storage than from soil — from potatoes in pits, dried meat, grain brought up the ridge in sacks. What grows here grows thin because the ground is stone and the moisture is intermittent and the season, such as it is, stays cool. Woodsmoke hangs thin and cold over the roofs and does not lift far.

Animals are rare in the same managed way. A dog asleep in the lee of the smithy, not tied. A small flock of pigeons in the granary eaves, and two sheep in the one pen by the wall, and very high and far off, a pair of ravens circling without a sound. No flies in the cool. No birdsong in the lanes. A cat on a warm slate when the sun finds it. That is the whole of the living noise.

And it is mostly quiet. The sounds of the place are the sounds of maintenance at rest: a bucket against a cistern rim, rope over a windlass, a shutter closing, the bellows sighing, a hammer striking once and then stopping, condensation dripping in the hall, wind moving over the ridge outside the wall but hardly entering the hollow. The town’s own walls break the wind, so the air inside settles and stays. Light is normal light — no glare, no perpetual dusk, just a clear day moving slowly across slate and stone, making the damp patches look darker, the dry stone pale, the shadows short and ordinary.

In the middle of it all is the one large interior: the hall. From outside it is plain, lower and longer than the houses, with a slate roof like the others. Inside, the vault is high — cathedral-high for a town this size — and cool and echoing. The granary is in its upper part, the meeting benches below, the cistern’s mouth in the floor. It is the only room that feels larger than the town’s scale, and for that reason it holds the town together. Everything else is grove-and-room scale, lane-and-yard scale, hand-and-arm scale.

Time does not pass here so much as repeat. The same light, the same cool, the same damp stone in the morning, the same repair made before decay can outrun it. A slate is replaced with a slate taken from the old quarry and dressed by hand until it fits. A wall is re-pointed in the spring the way it was re-pointed the spring before. Nothing is built to be new and nothing is allowed to become ruin. The balance is exact and a little austere, and it holds because everyone tends the drains, keeps the yards clear, catches the rain, and leaves the empty spaces empty.

From the square, one last time, you can see it all: the scattered stone houses, the network of channels and steps between them, the wall and the ridge beyond it, the bare high country falling away in every direction. A small town, built by hand, high and cool and damp in pockets, quiet, old, and finished — not ended, but settled into its own form, where the water runs where it should, the stone has stopped wearing, and tomorrow will look, carefully and deliberately, exactly like today.
