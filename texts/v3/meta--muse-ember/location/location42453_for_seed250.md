**The High Ocean**

The first thing is not water, exactly. It is distance.

You are already submerged when you understand where you are — not in a sea that has a shore somewhere behind you, but in a sea whose bottom is a world in itself, and whose surface is a far, bright ceiling hundreds of meters overhead. The horizon underwater has its own horizon. The water is clear to a degree that makes emptiness visible as a substance. There is nothing suspended in it, almost nothing at all: no cloud of sediment, no drifting weed, no glint of anything alive. Light comes down in a normal, terrestrial blue-green, the way sunlight should, and travels a long way before it thins. You can see a dark smudge ten kilometers off across the plain, and that smudge is the next thing. The space between presences is the landscape. Low density is not a lack here; it is the main material.

This is a high place that happens to be drowned.

The seabed lies on a vast plateau, high above the true low places of this world — places that have not yet formed, and may never be reached. The air above the surface, when the surface is visible as a rippling white-yellow sheet, is thin and cool. The water itself is cool in the same unspecific, high-altitude way: not glacial, not warm, a steady chill that never quite lets you forget the sun is strong. Because of the altitude and the low pressure of the whole system, the ocean behaves in a manner that is only approximately familiar. It is deep enough to be an ocean, shallow enough in pressure for strange things to happen in the water itself.

And the bottom is not finished.

That is the second thing, and it undermines the first sense of calm. The terrain is embryonic. It is not eroded into valleys and cliffs by age; it is still being put together, and sometimes un-put. Raw ridges push up out of the plain in long, soft spines of unweathered rock — basalt still glassy where water has quenched it, chalkier, crumbly stone where it has not. Some spires rise thirty meters and then, without any storm to blame, slump sideways and dissolve back into silt that never settles because the water will not let it. Others are aborted halfway: a hill that swells, cracks along a bright seam, sighs, and flattens again over the course of a season. Erosion here is not carving. It is editing. Creation and destruction happen at the same rate, in the same places, and the result is a geology that looks like a first draft being continuously revised in the dark.

The scale forces you to recalibrate what a current is. There is weather *inside* the water. Planet-wide fronts move through the ocean in slow collision, bearing their own internal storms — towering internal waves the size of mountain ranges, rolling along density boundaries, breaking and reforming for weeks. On the surface, the chop is not chop at all but swells with wavelengths of kilometers, driven by winds in the thin air above, and because the world is vast those swells have room to grow until the surface ceiling heaves. The world curves away beneath you. A plain can continue for a thousand kilometers without a rise larger than a man, and then everything you thought was flat is gently bending down out of the light.

It is turbulent in a way that never settles into pattern, though pattern is everywhere.

Superimposed on the churn is something that, from a distance, can only be described as engineering. It is not built. There are no structures here, no artifacts, no wreckage, no marker of any kind, not even a straight edge left in the rock. And yet the water is full of machinelike lace: broad vortex streets that lock into coherent lattices, filaments of magnetized water that braid, unbraid, and braid again in geometries that hold their phase across kilometers; sheets where bubbles nucleate in ordered rows and collapse in unison, flashing faintly as they do; great slow gyres that carry information along their rims faster than sound, in phase-coupled ripples that current physics has no clean equation for. The ocean is running processes that look, unmistakably, like post-modern computation — nanoscale coherence at meter scale, self-correcting currents, error-canceling vortices — but no one put them there. They are what cool, low-pressure, strongly magnetized water does when it is left alone long enough and given room enough. The world itself has a technology, and it is not a tool.

The sound is the proof of that. Given the emptiness, you expect silence. Instead it is loud, continuously, in the body.

Water moving at this scale makes a roar that is not noise but layering: a deep planetary hum from the great fronts grinding against each other; the crackle and boom of cavitation where turbulent sheets tear and reseal; the whine of the vortex lattice, rising and falling as a lattice stretches; the occasional concussion — a pressure pulse from a collapsing ridge or a surface storm slamming down — that travels across the whole basin and arrives in every part of it at once, as if the ocean rang. There is no animal sound in it. No song, no call, no clicking. The loudness is entirely abiotic, and that makes it stranger: an empty place, near-void between its few presences, that is deafening for reasons having nothing to do with life.

Because there is no animal life. That must be stated plainly, because everything else tempts you to invent it. No fish turn in the midwater. Nothing darts from the rocks. Nothing grazes, hunts, migrates, or sinks. The ecology stopped, or never started, at the stage of plants.

And the plants are sparse to the point of being a rumor.

On the few rock spires where the light still reaches and the current agrees to pause, there are strands of algae — dark, almost black-red, kelp-like but unbranched and ribbon-thin, anchored in the cracks of newborn stone. They stream in the turbulent flow like flags, then hang limp in a lull, then stream again in a different direction as a gyre passes. Colonies can be a few meters long and then nothing for hundreds of kilometers. In shallower luminous patches on the plain itself, there are low mats, rootless, photosynthesizing in the sun-filtered water and lifting and sinking in slow, independent clouds that never quite land. They are the only green, and even they are not really green; they are a dark, light-drinking color that makes the clear water around them seem brighter by contrast.

Nothing eats them. Nothing hides in them. They grow, they tear loose, they drift far into the empty blue and fade, and that is the entire food web.

The light remains stubbornly normal through all of this. Day comes as day. The sun is the sun. Shadows are cast by the spires in the correct direction and with the correct softness. Night, when the surface ceiling darkens, is simply dark, flecked only by the brief, cold flashes of collapsing bubbles in the lattice — not bioluminescence, not a living glow, just physics discharging. There is no perpetual twilight, no alien aurora in the depths, no glow from below. Weirdness here is not cosmic. It is coefficient-level: water that sometimes flows uphill along a density sheet for a moment, as if remembering a slope that is no longer there; layers that act like glass, refusing to mix for months and then suddenly exchanging in a single overturning; echoes that do not fade in the usual way but persist and interfere, so that a boom from a ridge collapse can be heard, faintly, returning from the far side of the basin days later, altered by every lattice it passed through.

It does not stay still long enough to be called a place, and yet it is a place — vast, high, drowned, cool, and being made. You are not meant to find a coast. You are meant to understand that the bottom is an unfinished highland, the water above it is an unfinished physics, and between the few dark spires and the few dark ribbons of algae there is only the blue, moving, humming, computing, collapsing, reforming emptiness, loud enough to fill a world, and empty enough to hold it.
