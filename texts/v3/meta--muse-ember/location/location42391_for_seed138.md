The first thing you notice is not the town, but the air. It's hot and wet at once, and it doesn't move. It sits on your skin, in your lungs, thick enough that sweat has nowhere to go. You breathe it in and it comes back out as damp. From the moment you cross the low causeway into it, the world feels enclosed, as if you've stepped into a large, shallow room that happens to be outdoors.

The light never clarifies. It's always that dim, yellow-grey of late afternoon, even at what should be noon. The sun is somewhere above, but smothered by a permanent low ceiling of steam, smoke, and condensing moisture. Shadows are soft and vague, never sharp, never moving much. Street lamps stay on under the eaves. Windows glow. The glare you expect from heat is absent — instead the whole place is lit like the interior of a shed with a translucent roof. It is oppressive, but it is nothing exotic. It is just a very warm, very damp day that has decided to stay.

The ground is low and flat, barely above the water table. This is new land. You can tell because nothing has had time to soften. The dredged fill that makes the platform is still banded in fresh layers — pale sand, dark clay, grey ash — cut square at the edges. The drainage canals are clean, angular trenches with sharp banks of unrounded riprap. The concrete retaining walls are still pale and un-stained, their formwork lines crisp, their corners unchipped. There are no old gullies, no weathered slopes, no rounded stones. Everything has the hard, newly-made geometry of a place recently lifted out of a marsh and held there.

And water is everywhere, because it never really leaves. It is not a river, and it is not rain. It is water near the surface, weeping out of the soil, sheeting across the paved yards in shallow mirrors, filling every ditch to the same level. Rocks sweat. Walls sweat. Hot moist air touches cooler metal pipes and condenses and drips, steadily, in the same places, all day long. The water in the canals is warm, still, and faintly filmed with oil and algae. Where a road dips, it becomes a permanent puddle. Where a yard is empty, it becomes a flooded lot that reflects the grey sky and the buildings around it, unbroken by wind.

That is the town, after all. Small and bounded — you can see the far dike, the far fence of corrugated iron, the far water tower from almost anywhere — but spread out thinly. This is urban, but not crowded. A grid of asphalt roads with fresh white lines, a main street of low shops and houses, a pump house with a painted sign, a generator hall, a processing works with overhead steam lines marching between buildings on steel trestles, a small depot, a row of warehouses. They are real, organized, built to a plan with addresses and infrastructure. And between them is mostly emptiness: wide, wet, paved waste grounds, chained-off lots full of standing water, gravel hardstands where nothing is stored, service roads that go nowhere in particular. There is room enough between presences that you hear your own footsteps echo off wet concrete.

It is industrial to its core. Pipes hum. Fans turn. Pressure valves sigh on a cycle. A water tower drips from a joint. The machinery is not frenzied; much of it looks almost idle in the heat-shimmer, but it never stops. It maintains the place. The pumps keep the water at exactly that level — no higher to flood the roads, no lower to expose the mud. The engines make the heat that makes the steam that makes the condensation that makes the drip that feeds the puddles that the pumps lift again. The place is a loop, and it is closed.

Vegetation has not taken. This is the strangest part for such a wet, hot lowland, and the most telling of its newness. There should be a jungle here. Instead there are only sparse, stubborn survivors: thin clumps of reed in the drains, salt grass along the dike crest, a line of stunted street trees whose leaves are dusty and thin, a crust of algae thick in the warm canals. The soil is fresh, saline, trampled, and paved; the air is too hot at the surface for seedlings; the ground saturation is constant. Plants grow as fast as they die, in equilibrium. There is no green, only a sparse wash of it.

Fauna, by contrast, is everywhere, and louder than the people. They do not need the trees. The warm shallow water is full of life and so are the eaves and the walls. Frogs call from every drain canal in a continuous, overlapping chorus. Dragonflies and midges swarm in thick clouds around the lamps and the steam vents, humming audibly. Swallows dart and nest under the factory roofs. Rats run the pipe lines at dusk that never quite comes. Catfish turn in the cooling canals, snails climb the weeping walls, mosquitoes rise in a visible sheet over any flooded lot. It is abundant, busy, mundane life — no predators large enough to check it, no winter to pause it. It feeds on warmth, on algae, on detritus, on the heat leaking from the machines. It simply continues.

And it is loud. Not loudly in bursts, but in a constant, saturated way. From anywhere in this small bound you can hear all of it at once: the low drone of the generator hall, the cicada-like whine of electric motors, the drip-drip of condensation, the pumps thudding in their pits, the frogs, the insects, the occasional shout from a yard, a truck reversing somewhere across the flooded ground. The water and the concrete carry it. It is the sound of a place that is awake and working all the time, with no quiet hour to rest in.

What holds it all together is that it does not change. This is not a place in transition. The hot temperature is not a summer peak; the dim light is not a passing cloud; the water level is not a flood; the sharp edges are not fresh because a storm just cut them. All of it is held. Birth and death among the frogs and midges balance. Growth and die-back among the reeds balance. Evaporation and condensation balance. The machines are tended carefully, not to make them go faster, but to keep them exactly as they are. If you left for a year and came back, you would find the same puddle in the same pothole, the same drip from the same pipe, the same note in the pump's hum, the same grey-yellow light at the same intensity, the same heat that never breaks. Your watch would be useless here. Your body would learn the place by dampness, not by time.

It is not weird. There is nothing magic about it. It is only a small, low, industrial town built on fresh wet ground in a hot climate, where people and machines have arranged things into a perfect, sweaty equilibrium and then decided to keep them there.
