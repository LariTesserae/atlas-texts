From the lip of the basin you can see all of it at once.

Ilthmere does not rise. It sinks. A low, fresh-broken hollow maybe four hundred paces across, lower than the fields around it by the height of a house, its rim not rounded by age but jagged, raw fracture rock still shedding pebbles. No soil has had time to decide itself here. The slopes are bare and angular, pre-formation, as if the earth is still in the act of becoming earth. When you step over the edge, the air changes before anything else does. It gets colder, perceptibly and all at once, colder still the lower you go. Cold pools down here like water. Your breath shows. Your fingers go clumsy on your coat. After a few minutes your shoulders ache from the damp cold settling into them, and it never quite leaves.

It is a town, and it is not. From above it looked like a hamlet compressed. Up close it is a city saturated into the size of a cathedral. There is no empty lot. Streets, alleys, yards, service cuts, roof paths — everything is used, and used more than once. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, some attached by a shared wall, some overlapping at a corner by a meter that should not be possible, some stacked not simply up but *through* each other in layers. A three-storey house in grey concrete leans against a nine-storey slab in wet brick; between them a narrow passage goes down six levels to a street that does not appear on any map from above because it is underneath the later city, still lit by ordinary daylight filtering through gratings, still numbered.

There is a grid, attempted and preserved. Street names are painted on damp plaster. There are bus stops with no buses, traffic lights blinking amber for no traffic, shopfronts with lettering for businesses that have not yet opened, or have already quietly closed a decade ago and left their signs up. The built is layered the way geology is layered: older infrastructure is not removed, it is incorporated. Copper conduits run under newer lattice cable. A tram wire, useless, sags across an alley now used for drying clothes. A lift shaft opens into a kitchen. Foundations are exposed in the middle of inhabited blocks, rebar standing like a thicket of iron trees, floors poured but walls never built, stairs arriving at open air and stopping, patiently.

At first, in normal light, it all looks merely old and cold and wet. The light here is ordinary — a diffuse, northern, mid-morning grey that renders color truly. Wet brick is russet, concrete is pale, puddles reflect a pale sky, shadows fall where they should. That ordinariness is what makes the rest harder to see. If the light were strange you would be warned.

Get close and the materials betray themselves. Nothing is quite dry. Condensation lives on the undersides of awnings, on the insides of window glass, on railings, on the bellies of pipes, beading and crawling and dripping in slow, intermittent lines. There is no river. Water here is dampness made local: pockets of clear water in shallow depressions in the stone, ankle-deep mist that does not move, gutters that run for a day after no rain and then stop, an occasional fine rain that seems to start at roof height rather than from cloud, and lands without sound. Everything smells of damp concrete, cold iron, wet earth that has not yet become soil, and faintly of ozone from below.

The technology is not visible as technology. That is the point of it. Under the streets, woven through the walls, there are crystalline lattices carrying power and signal by means that make the word *current* feel inadequate — matter compilers, phase-locked comparators, vacuum-annealed structures that hold a building's shape the way a field might hold a pattern. To the eye they masquerade as scaffolding, as cable trays, as ordinary ducting. To the hand they are sometimes too warm, sometimes cold in a way that has no source. A door that has been in place for forty years will, if you listen, tick once a month, a sound you feel in your teeth more than hear. A panel will heal a crack by sealing it with glass that was not transparent a moment before. The builders — if there were builders, as we mean them — built beyond what present physics fully describes, and the city has been left to run on that beyond ever since, slowly.

Life is thin on top of all this density. Vegetation is sparse to the point of being a rumor: moss in the north-facing seams where a microclimate leaks heat from the lattice below, a fringe of blackened grass along one wall that never dies back, a handful of stunted, pale shrubs in cracks, seedlings that take a whole season to add a leaf. Animals are rarer. You can walk a whole day and see nothing living but your own breath. Once, near dusk, the pale moths come out of the condensation — blind, translucent, wings crusted with mineral — and settle on the shop windows. Something small and eyeless lives in the puddles, moving them slightly though there is no wind. That is most of the ecology: a few species clinging to the leaks between humidity, heat, and the places where reality is fraying.

And it is fraying. That is the other density here, a density of inconsistency packed into the same small volume as the buildings. It is not dramatic. It does not explode. It persists.

A staircase goes down to a landing and arrives at the same landing, but the room beyond it is different on Tuesdays than it was last week, and will be that different room for the rest of the season. An alley has a reflection in a window of a building that is not opposite it. A street, walked twice, is a little longer the second time, long enough that your pace changes without your noticing, and then remains that length for years. In some doorways gravity pulls a fraction stronger; you lean without meaning to. In a pocket of air near the old market, sounds arrive a half-second late, so you see a glass fall before you hear it. Condensation forms in straight lines across empty air where a seam runs, like a crack in glass, and if you cross it your shadow, for three steps, is cast the other way.

The seams are everywhere if you look. They are what the lattice is trying to stitch. Coherent zones — a block where physics holds, a building where time runs in the right order — butt up against zones where it does not, and the border is patient. Nothing here hurries to resolve. Changes happen at the speed of lichen, of frost, of the slow extrusion of a new wall, millimeters a year. A sign is being obscured by moss. A puddle is taking a year to fill. A building that was half-finished when you arrived will be half-finished, in a slightly different way, when you leave, and its ghost — the version of it that was planned, or that was last century and is still leaking through — will still be visible at the edge of sight, layered over the brick.

That is really what this place is: embryonic, twice over. The geology has not finished forming, and the city built into its hollow has not finished forming either, and perhaps they were never meant to be separate processes. The rock splits, the concrete sets and unsettles, the streets add a layer, the lattice adds a correction, the damp adds a pocket of water, the moss adds a green, the seam adds a line. Erosion here is not wearing things away into softness. It is building them up, raw and unfinished, into a shape that continues to be decided.

It is mostly quiet in a way a dense place should not be. No birdsong, no traffic, no market noise. The damp walls swallow footsteps. The machines are silent to the ear at the frequencies they use. What sound there is arrives as drips, as the distant tick of cooling metal, as your own breathing, as — occasionally, from a closed doorway — a conversation in a language that might be yours, spoken very slowly, and unfinished.

You leave by the same raw rim you entered. From above, the whole thing is again visible, small and complete and bordered by a line you can almost see: on this side the cold, the wet, the close-packed layers; on the other side, warmer air, dry fields, ordinary physics resuming without apology. Ilthmere does not extend. It does not need to. In its small, low, cold hollow, a city and a landscape are still in the middle of becoming themselves, and reality, left to itself in normal light, is still in the middle of coming apart — slowly, quietly, and in far more places than you first thought.
