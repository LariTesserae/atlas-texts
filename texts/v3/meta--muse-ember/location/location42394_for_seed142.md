You are very high up, and it is very dark, and you are warm.

That is the first contradiction the place gives you, and it does not resolve. Your ears ache with altitude, the air is thin enough that each breath feels unrewarded, dry enough that your throat scrapes and any sweat on your skin vanishes before you feel it. And yet the stone under your boots is warm through the soles. The air, when it moves at all, moves like it has been held against a stove. There is no wind to speak of at this height, no weather coming over a ridge, no change from hour to hour. The warmth does not come from above.

Nothing up there gives light. The sky is not a night sky so much as an absence of sky, a black that is even and total, with no moon, no rising, no stars close enough to make a pattern you could follow. If there are stars they do not move. They do not help. All the light in this world is made down here, on the ground, and most of it is very far away.

You stand on a shelf that has been in the process of becoming flat for longer than there is a word for. It is a high plateau, regional in its extent — it goes on past every horizon you can imagine, to clusters of lights ten, twenty kilometers off in one direction and then another dark gap, then more lights. From here it looks almost level, which is the trick of it. The erosion is so ancient, so complete, that the mountains that must have been here have been planed down to broad, low domes and truncated mesas, the craters that must have stamped it have been filled and polished out, the old lava flows have been ground into a single continuous skin. Near-flattened, the descriptions say, and that is exactly what it is: not flat like a table, but settled into its final form. Nothing loose is left to be taken. Every pebble is a disc, worn smooth on both sides. Every edge, natural or otherwise, has been rounded to the tolerance of a river stone.

The ground is that stone, exposed everywhere. Black basalt, obsidian glass worked into the surface until it glints like wet — though there is no wet here, no water at all, no puddle, no mud, no dew, no rain, no river that ever carved this. The dry channels you can trace from above are mineral flows, pale scars of ancient melt, not water. Between the black glass are expanses of pale grey dust, crushed feldspar so fine it coats your boots and does not clump, and rust-brown iron streaks, and white crusts that look like salt but are dry mineral efflorescence, crumbling if you touch them. There is no green anywhere — not in the cracks, not in the shelter of walls, not even the faintest film of lichen. No plant life has ever found a foothold, and none will. The cracks sit empty. The dust sits where it fell, undisturbed for a very long time. You can see footprints in it that are not yours, crisp as the day they were made, because there is no rain to soften them and no wind that changes its mind.

That stillness is everywhere. Warmth is constant. Light is constant. The low industrial hum you can hear from the nearest district is constant. This place is not young and violent. It is old and finished, an equilibrium reached so long ago that it has become the rule of the world.

The city is spread across this high shelf like dice thrown and left where they fell, sparse, balanced with emptiness. It is unmistakably urban — organized, layered, built on a scale that took engines and factories to make — but it is not dense. A district of housing blocks, a mill, a smelter, a transformer yard, then two kilometers of open black plain, then another district. You walk in darkness between them and the only evidence is the sound arriving too late and too muffled, and the lights ahead: small amber squares in stone towers, a steady red point, a green arc that does not flicker.

The buildings are made of the plateau itself. Basalt block, cut and laid, eight or ten stories in the older clusters, their corners worn down by the same final erosion that smoothed the mesas. Lower floors are half-sunk in accumulated dust, forgotten, their windows blind. Upper floors are polished. Newer construction, if you can call it newer, is built directly on top in the same stone, so there is no contrast of style, only strata — like geology, like the city has been weathering into its final shape at the same rate as the land beneath it. From a distance a factory with its rounded sheds and smoothed chimney looks natural. Up close a natural dome with its thermal cracks looks built. That confusion is everywhere here, and it is strange in a way that takes time to notice. Where does the plateau end and the manufacture begin? The pipes, at least, are honest.

Infrastructure crosses the emptiness because there is so much of it to cross. Raised pipe causeways on stone stilts carry crushed rock and molten slag from the mills to the smelters, straight lines that vanish into the dark in both directions. Single rail lines, no branches, run level for kilometers. Conduit towers march across the plain, their wires humming a low note you feel in your molars when you pass underneath. Everything is electrified and mechanized, scaled production on an industrial rhythm, but the rhythm never varies. A belt conveyor grinds in a closed loop, carrying the same stone from a pit that never deepens to a mill that never fills and back again. A smelter exhales a slow, heat-shimmering breath through a vent, the same breath, at the same interval, forever. The glow from its door is a slit of dull orange, the only color that is not amber or white or black. No smoke rises to be blown away, because air does not move to blow it. It hangs and dissipates.

Inside the districts the quiet is even more complete. If you stand between buildings you hear mostly your own steps crunching on grit, then the long silence that follows, then a distant thump from a press, then silence again. No birds, no insects, no creak of vegetation, no water dripping. Machines make a soft, subdued noise — a ventilator sigh, a bearing turning, a transformer’s hum — sounds that seem to have been padded by the stone and the dust. It is not a dead silence. It is a quiet that has been tended to, as if loudness would be a kind of erosion, and erosion has finished its work.

Life, of the kind that moves on its own, is rare to the point of rumor. Vegetation is zero; fauna is barely more. Once, after a long walk along a warm conduit, you may see one of the rare crawlers. They are low, elongated, blind things armored in overlapping plates of the same basalt-grey mineral they live on, moving with a slowness that makes you doubt at first that it is moving at all. They graze not on plants — there are none — but on the mineral dust and on the faint metallic condensation that forms around warm electrical equipment. They gather, very few at a time, near pipes where the air is a degree warmer and the hum is loudest, and sit. It is hard to tell an animal from a rock that has decided to be in a different place. That, too, is part of the strangeness: the fauna have been eroded into the landscape, and the landscape has been built into the shape of fauna, and you learn to tell them apart only by the patience of watching.

Even footprints, tools left on a ledge, a door left ajar in a housing block — they stay. You could leave this plateau for a season, if there were seasons, and come back and the dust pattern around a boot would be identical, the light in the same window the same steady amber, the conveyor in the same place in its loop. Nothing grows, nothing rots, nothing rusts the way it would in a wet world. Without water, metal does not know how to decay. Without upheaval, stone does not know how to fall. The factories do not produce so much as they maintain a state that was reached long ago, grinding and heating and carrying in a circle that has no beginning you can find.

At the edge of a district, where the built stone gives way again to open plain, the view opens out. Even in darkness you can count the distance by the lights: here a dim cluster, there another, farther ones reduced to stars that have fallen to the ground. Between them, kilometers of warm black glass under a black sky, utterly still, utterly ancient. The plateau holds its height without drama. The city holds its shape without effort. The warmth holds in the stone. And the whole vast, open, high place remains exactly as it was when you arrived, as it was long before you, as it will be — finished, sparse, dark, and quietly industrial in its final, unchanging form.
