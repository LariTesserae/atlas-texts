It is low, warm, and green past any distance you can quite judge.

The valley does not rise. It sinks, gently, flat, and stays sunk — a basin holding heat under a normal sky. The light is ordinary daylight, white-yellow and clear, but almost none of it reaches the ground unbroken. It comes down in moving lace, leaf-shadow and stem-shadow and quivering bars, and the shadows move wrong, sometimes against the wind, sometimes after a delay, so that at noon the shade under a tree may be the shape of a tree that will grow there tomorrow.

The ground itself is young. It was not eroded into this shape over ages; it has erupted into it. Everywhere the surface is fresh-split, sharp, recently lifted. Cracks run in clean lines where roots have pried through pulp and bark-soil. Fresh hills of compressed leaf-mould stand up with steep, unweathered edges, still faintly steaming with sap and fermentation. There are no old boulders, no smooth stones, no deep river cuts. What looks like stone at a distance is a cliff of hardened, layered trunk, for the country has turned its own substance over, and over again, until inorganic things are few and quickly taken. When a ridge slumps, it does not wash away — it rots in place into loam, and new growth tears it apart from inside.

Vegetation here is not *on* the place. It *is* the place.

Soil is a mat of roots, root-hairs, fallen bark, pulped leaf. You walk on bark that gives and springs, on woven rhizomes braided by pressure, on a thick carpet of grass that is not grass but a low, dense forest flattened by its own weight. Above ankle-height is the understory, wall upon wall of stems, ferns unfurling like hands, reeds, broad herbaceous stalks. Above that, climbing as far as light will let them, the midstory, and above that the canopy, which is also a ceiling. In the depths, the canopy closes entirely and day becomes green twilight, then opens again onto clearings that are not clearings but plazas of bare, living ground where a tower has just collapsed.

Because it competes, it builds.

This is a city made without builders, tools, or hands. In one district, a species of bundled trunk grows in straight ranks, trunks lashed together at the base by root-cords, splitting at the top to hold leaves high — towers, eight, ten, twenty storeys of leaves, with hanging balconies of epiphytes and squashed curtains of vine for walls. In another, fern-fronds layer themselves window over window in flats. In another, broad-leaf plants spread their crowns into a continuous roof, and beneath it streets form because nothing can grow in the deep shade except the pale, thread-like stems that learn to crawl along the ground. Avenues are where a vine, seeking light, has stretched and hardened, died back at its core to leave a hollow tube, and then grown again around it. Bridges of living tendril cross between towers and swing in the warm air. Plumbing is roots: thick aqueduct-roots that run under the streets, storing damp and distributing it, swelling after a shower and thinning in drought.

There are districts of thorn, sharp and defensive, where the young erosion of the ground and the sharpness of the plants reinforce each other. There are slow, mossy quarters where growth has been dense so long that air itself grows through seep and vein. There are markets of bloom, sudden concentrations of flowers opening and closing in minutes, pistils thrusting, stamens exploding in pollen-winds because there is nothing else to carry them.

No animal crosses it. No bird, no insect, no thing with a mouth moves through the air between the leaves. The silence of that absence is immediately filled — it is not silent at all.

The sound is deafening, and it is the sound of the place growing. It begins as a rustle, millions of leaves rubbing, and deepens until it is a roar with a rhythm. Stomata open with a click like rain on a roof that never stops. Sap surges, and trunks creak and boom under pressure. Branches snap and re-strap themselves. Seeds burst from pods with a crack like knuckles, or sigh out on the wind in clouds of fluff. Roots tick against each other underground, forcing through bark-soil, splitting fresh soil with pops you feel in your teeth. On warm afternoons, when transpiration is at its peak, the whole valley breathes at once in a wave that travels for miles, a rising and falling hiss that drowns any thought you try to hold. You do not hear it so much as stand inside a vibration. The ground is constantly making noise under your feet.

And it will not stay still while you listen. This is a turbulent place, Solaris-like in its habit of creation and destruction in the same breath. Sprouting is visible. A tower may push up ten feet in an hour, its leaves expanding wet and furrowed, then at the next growth-wave, a strangler vine climbing its neighbor will tighten, starve it, and the tower will sag, fold, dissolve into mulch with a sigh of steam and spores. A plaza that was open in the morning is a thicket by evening; a thicket that was choked in the morning is open by evening, burned down to short shoots by a fungus — the only decomposer allowed here — and the fire of decay that eats without heat. Leaf-fall comes in storms, spiraling up as well as down. Branches trade places. The city migrates. Over weeks, whole quarters crawl, root by root, towards the light on the basin's edge, leaving behind fresh, sharp young ground where they have been.

Water is present the way breath is present. It is damp, not wet. In the early morning the leaves are beaded so heavily that water runs from their tips in a constant, soft dripping, and the air is cool on the skin despite the warmth. Cupped leaves hold small, perfect pools that last minutes before the plant drinks them back. Then the day warms, the beads vanish, the ground becomes dusty and papery, and the valley seems dry. A rain may come, but locally — a single street receives a sudden, heavy shower as warm air releases the moisture the canopy has been exhaling for hours. It drums on the roof-leaves, steams where it touches sun-warm bark, and is gone within ten minutes, reabsorbed. No rivers run. No lake stays. The only standing water is inside the plants themselves, in trunks, in succulent leaves, in the aqueduct-roots, moving from place to place in pulses. You can drink nothing here except by biting a leaf or a fruit, and the taste is always green and sweet and sharp.

That is the ecology. Plants are substrate, predator, prey, soil, sky, building, road, and weather. One plant feeds on another by shading it, by strangling it, by sending roots into its trunk to steal its stored water. Pollen is thrown like seed. Seeds travel by explosion, by wind, by being dropped from a height they have grown for the purpose. A single root-system may be a whole district, and a whole district may be a single individual that has translated itself from one end of the valley to the other, leaf by leaf, root-step by root-step, leaving a copy of itself in the shape of the where-for it has been. Fungi — part fungus, part plant in everyone's shorthand here — eat the dead and return them to green. Nothing else dies.

And then the rules cease to be quite rules.

Reality breaks in the manner of growth. Time is not even across the valley. A seed in one pocket will sprout, flower, and seed again in a minute, while its twin a foot away takes a full season, and the two plants are forever mismatched, reaching for light at different speeds. Fallen leaves are sometimes green, sometimes already rotted, sometimes still on the branch they have fallen from, and no one can say which is first. Shadows lie on the ground that do not correspond to anything above, because the canopy above them is not the canopy that cast them — it is a later canopy, grown a moment later, copying the first. Roots pass under a street and emerge in a clearing a mile away without passing through the intervening ground. A vine avenue that you follow straight will, if you do not hurry, loop back to where you started, not because it curves, but because the vine has grown longer behind you.

Gravity pools around the thickest trunks. Damp runs upward along certain stems. In deep root-tunnels, you can sometimes see a smear of sunlight on the wall, though there is no sky above you — it is light remembered by the bark. A flower may open mouth-like and deep, and inside it, impossibly, is a miniature of the whole valley, with its own tiny weather. A plant can be both its own ancestor and its own descendant, because its leaves, fallen, become new plants in its own image, in its own place, while it is still standing there pretending to be one.

There is no tool here because there is no need for a tool to be separate. A thorn thickens until it can cut a rival stem. A leaf hardens until it pries. A trunk hollows itself to be a channel. Everything that might be made is first grown.

 By late afternoon the warmth peaks and the valley is at its loudest, its most turbulent, its most itself. New growth tears through old in waves you can watch cross the basin, a visible tide of green. The light, still normal, is broken and rebn'ted and broken again. The city of plants keeps building and unbuilding itself, street by street, tower by tower, district migrating slowly across the low, young, damp-and-dry ground, filling the air with its deafening, continuous work of making and unmaking — and the valley, having no animals to name it or map it, simply goes on, larger than the sight that tries to take it in, region succeeding region, layer on layer, never finished, never still.
