From the outside, there is almost nothing to find.

The land is low and flat — a cold, bright plain of damp earth and stone, spreading so evenly that the horizon seems to have been pressed into a single plane. The light is that hard, pale light of a far northern day, strong enough to make snow and wet stone glare, but it carries no warmth. The air tastes of metal and rain that never quite falls. Mist condenses on your skin, on the tops of low plants, in the dark soil between scatter stones. Everything is damp to the touch, nothing is wet enough to run.

Across this openness rises a single hummock, no higher than a man is tall, perhaps thirty paces wide. From a distance it is only green: moss and low shrubs and white saxifrage flowers crowded over a pale dome of packed earth and old, clear ice. It looks like a place where the ground simply swelled. In truth, it is a roof.

You have to part a hanging curtain of moss and roots to find the way in, and then stoop down two rounded steps and the world turns inside out. Instead of darkness, there is more light. The inside of the dome is the pocket: a single chamber, wholly contained, no larger than a village square, yet so layered you cannot take it in at once.

The dome is snow and ice that has long ago settled into its final form. Its inner surface is smooth, polished in places by centuries of feet and hands, fluted and veined in others where melt-water once found its course and stopped. Pale blue ice shows through white, and through the thinner crown of the roof the bright day outside pours in, filtered and diffused. The whole interior glows as if lit from within paper. There are no strong shadows, only soft pools of deeper green and blue. Every green thing looks almost luminous against it — moss, fern, leaf, algae, lichen — saturated and juicy despite the cold, and everything made of wood is silvered, everything of stone is rounded by touch.

At the centre is the reason the place holds together. A single dwarf birch, older than any sign of building, grows from a shallow pool in the packed earth floor. Its trunk is twisted and pale as bone, its roots spread across the floor like a river delta, and its crown presses up against the glowing roof, held in place by generations of pruning. Around it, the town has grown.

For this is a town. Not a camp, not a single house, but an urban place, densely and deliberately made, with all the layered order of a much larger city compressed into a room. Houses are cut as alcoves into the dome's wall and built out from it in stacked terraces: frames of hand-split driftwood lashed with root-fiber, walls of turf, woven mats, and clay daubed thick, roofs of moss and overlapping mats that shed the slow drip from above. They rise three and four deep in places, shoulder to shoulder, with no blank wall between them. Streets are the narrow gaps left for passing, worn concave in the earth. Where there is no room on the floor, there are root-bridges at the height of a man's chest, and above those, ledges on the dome where gardens and nests cling to the curve. Below, in cellars scooped under a house, are stores of dried berries, roots, pottery, and mats. Above, laundry and weaving and bundles of herbs hang drying in the bright still air. Even the ceiling is occupied.

Every material is old in the way of things that have reached equilibrium. Nothing is new and sharp. The driftwood is grey as drifted stone, the clay is dark and smooth as river mud, the ice has forgotten any edge it might once have had. Doorways are rounded where shoulders have passed for a hundred years. Steps are hollowed. Cobbles are set into the paths where feet fall, polished to pebbles. The erosion here is not destruction but settlement: everything has slumped, weathered, and come to rest exactly where it belongs, and stays there.

The people who live here build only with stone, bone, wood, and fiber. Their tools are flint knives and bone needles and adzes hewn to fit the hand. Their pottery is shaped by hand and low-fired in compost-warm pits. Their rope is twisted root, their baskets woven so tight they hold damp grain. There is no metal to speak of, no wheel — goods move on small sledges and in baskets carried between the close terraces. And yet the infrastructure is complete: grooves carved in the ice wall carry water, mats are laid to keep floors from mud, compost beds under the houses make heat enough to keep the air a few degrees above the killing cold outside, and paths of woven sledge-mats connect all of it.

Water is the town's one river, and it never flows. It arrives as condensation. In the bright, still air, breath and leaves give up their moisture, and it beads on the coldest part of the dome — the upper ice, the north-facing curve. There it gathers into fat, slow drops that roll down in thin, bright threads, following carved channels in the wall. The channels lead to the central pool, to garden beds, to clay-lined cisterns tucked under houses. The soil stays dark and damp, the moss stays spongy, the algae on the wet walls stays green. But there is no flood, no stream, no rain. Only this closed breathing: water rises invisibly, gathers, falls a little, is drunk by roots, and rises again. When you stand under a drip, it falls on your neck with a cool, patient weight, one drop in a long time, and is gone.

Life is abundant here, and it is silent.

Moss carpets every surface that is not a path — floors, roofs, stone, the outside dome itself — thick enough to deaden a step. Ferns hang down from the terraces and from the birch's lower branches, their fronds turning toward the reflected light from the pool below, so that gardens grow sideways and upside-down where the light reaches them. Cloudberry shrubs are planted in soil pockets in the wall. Saxifrage and other cold flowers bloom everywhere, and because nothing here passes through seasons in any clean way, a single shrub will show white blossom, green fruit, and orange ripe berries all at once, side by side. Algae makes the wet lower wall a soft, living green skin.

The fauna share in the silence. Pale, cold-bodied flies hover over the flowers without buzzing — their wings are too chilled to hum. Snails with translucent shells graze the algae on the ice. Worms work the damp soil under the houses. Frogs sit half-submerged in the pool and the smaller pocket pools, slow and fat, and do not croak. Tiny stickleback fish turn in the clear water and make their homes among the birch roots. Birds nest in the birch crown and in the eaves of the houses, watching with bright eyes, and do not call. Beetles move in the silvered wood. Nothing here makes the sounds its kind would make elsewhere. The cold, the still air, the thick moss and snow of the roof swallow vibration as fast as it is born.

People have adapted their manner to it. They speak in breath, too quiet to carry, and mostly in hands — a language of quick, precise movements in the bright air, made visible by the shadowless light. Hammers are struck on moss pads. Pots are set down, not dropped. Doors are mats let fall, not shut. The one regular sound is the drip from the dome, and even that is muffled into a cup or a channel before it can strike. You can hear your own step as a dull thud on moss, the soft brush of cloth, the faint click of bone against bone. Then silence again, complete and not empty. It feels like a held breath that has been held for a very long time.

The temperature inside is the temperature of deep, damp earth in spring, though it is never spring. Your breath fogs. Frost persists year-round in the north corners of the dome, in a thin, white lace on the ice, and the bright air has no heat to melt it. The inhabitants wear layers of woven pale fiber and moss, and they cluster around the compost warmth and the communal long-house, which smells of cold clay, crushed green leaf, peat, and wet stone. The cold is not a hardship to be conquered; it is the condition in which everything has learned to be lush.

And nothing changes. The light does not rise or fall. It is always the same bright, diffuse morning, filtered through ice. The town does not grow or decay. The birch does not grow taller; it is cut back as fast as it reaches. Harvests equal growth. The pool does not rise or fall. A tool left on a mat today will be there, in the same soft light, with the same dusting of frost on its handle, season after season. Moss will grow over a path and the path will be cleared again to exactly the same shape. Outside, the plain is the same bright cold, the same damp hummock; inside, the same quiet, the same green, the same packed city glowing under its single roof.

That is the strangeness of it: a place perfectly in balance, where youth and age, bloom and ripeness, building and moss, belong at once, where a whole town is hidden under a bump in the ground and can be seen in one glance, yet never fully seen because every surface is covered by another surface, another life. If you leave by the moss curtain, stepping up into the flat, glaring cold, you may look back and see only the hummock, green and unremarkable, with a few white flowers trembling — although there is no wind to tremble them — and you would pass it again, never knowing the bright, silent, crowded world breathing damp and green beneath it, patiently holding still.
