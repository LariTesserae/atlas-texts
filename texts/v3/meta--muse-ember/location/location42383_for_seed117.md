It is too small and too wet to surprise you for long.

You can cross it in two minutes, and from the one low ridge of slag and broken concrete that makes its boundary you can see all of it at once — a shallow bowl perhaps sixty paces across, sunk a little below the flat black gravel plain around it. But you cannot take it in at once, because everything is crowded. Water crowds water, machine crowds machine, living thing crowds living thing, layer upon layer until the eye has to learn to separate them. And the light is dim, pressed down by a permanent low sky, so that for the first minute it seems empty. It is not empty. It is saturated.

The ground is never dry. That is the first rule. Water stands between the paving slabs, runs in thin rivulets along the cracks, sweats out of the walls, weeps from the undersides of pipes, condenses in beads on every cold surface and runs again. A footprint fills before the next step lands. The pools are not lakes — there is no deep water here — but a single water table so near the surface that it joins itself into a mirror, pitted by drips, disturbed by your ankles. In the low places it is ankle-deep, in the high places it is a film over black glassy stone. It is always cool. Not freezing, but a deep, northern, stone cool that gets into your hands and stays there. Your breath shows even at midday. The iron never warms.

There is nothing green in it. That is the second thing you learn, once your eyes adjust. No grass, no weed, no seed has ever taken here, or none can survive long enough to be noticed. The wetness is too constant, the light too dim, the water too iron- and chemical-laden, the ground too mineral. The plain outside the bowl is the same — wet gravel, shale, weathered concrete, slag — stretching grey to a grey horizon. Inside, where a lush place would be, the ground is instead felted and crusted.

A grey fungal felt covers the damp concrete in soft, breathing mats. Where it is undisturbed it lifts in pale ridges like dried foam. Black yeast films slip over the stagnant pools, iridescent at the edges. Cream and mustard mycelial cords run like shallow roots under the stones, but they are not roots; they quest for rust, for oil, for dead insects, for the iron itself. White mineral bloom feathers across the brick, salt and calcite crystallising in dendrites that look, at a glance, like tiny ferns — but they are rock, growing slowly outward in the condensation. Orange bacterial slime glistens in the pipe joints, pulsing faintly when the pumps cycle. Everything that grows here eats stone, metal, or decay. Nothing photosynthesises. A few fungi fruit in the dim: blunt, damp caps on thin stalks beside the generator shed, exhaling a cloud of spores every time the air moves. Their glow is almost nothing — a greenish phosphorescence in the darkest corners, just enough for other things to gather by.

The place is old in the way of things that have reached an equilibrium. Nothing is sharp. The bricks at the edges of the yard have softened and rounded. The steel has feathered out into stable rust, that particular pocked orange-brown that no longer spreads. The concrete troughs have sagged into a posture, as if they have settled into sleep. Water erosion has not carved anything new for a very long time; it has simply polished what is already there, smoothed it, taken the harshness off all the angles until the whole bowl has one dull, wet sheen. It looks inhabited not by use but by long accommodation. The ruins are not falling down any further. They have negotiated with the rain.

At the centre, crowded to near-touching, is the reason for it all. A washing plant, a small industrial yard for sorting and cleaning aggregate and shale. The machines are still here and they are packed so tightly you have to walk sideways between them.

A long open shed, its corrugated roof half-collapsed, shelters a row of shaker screens and troughs like narrow canal locks full of black water. Overhead, a gantry and three conveyor belts cross and recross, sagging under their own weight, one of them still moving — very slowly, very quietly — carrying a sparse load of wet stones up to a chute, where they fall with a soft plink back into a pool to be carried up again. Pipes loop everywhere, bolted to walls, disappearing into the ground, re-emerging elsewhere. Valves are frozen open. Gauges stare with cracked glass. A painted number — 3 — is still legible on a gearbox in faded white. A diesel generator in a separate low shed idles with a patience that has gone beyond duty; it coughs every twenty seconds, charges a bank of batteries, and drives a single set of electric lamps and the pump.

For this is the loop that keeps the place alive. The pump draws from the lowest pool, pushes black water up through the troughs to the top of the gantry, where it rains down in a fine, cold spray over the sorting screens. It collects, it churns, it is drawn up again. The water has been going round in this closed, useless circuit for years — washing nothing, sorting the same stones by size and dropping them back to be sorted again. The spray is why the air here is always condensing, why mist hangs knee-high and does not quite lift, why every surface beads and runs. In one corner that spray creates its own small weather: a perpetual drizzle over a few square metres, hissing faintly on hot-er metal, mushrooming into mist.

The built things are few and scattered around that dense machine-heart. The shed, the generator hut with its single dirty window, a small office with a broken chair still inside and a kettle long rusted to a brown shell, three low concrete bunkers filled with silt, a culvert where a stream — spring-fed, constant — enters under a fence and leaves the same way. A fence of bent posts and sagging wire marks the boundary, and a single lamp-post at the gate still burns orange in the dimness, though no one comes to read by it anymore. Beyond the gate the black gravel plain is empty.

And yet the place is full of animals. Abundant, and mostly quiet. The quiet is the third quality you notice after the wet and the absence of green. The machines are slow and muffled by water and thick, spore-laden air. The animals do not call. They move.

Under the black film of the pools, pale blind eels — no, not fish in any ordinary sense — slide through the pipes, feeling their way by the faint electric field leaking from the submerged cables. Translucent amphibians, like salamanders that never grew eyes, cling to the undersides of troughs in dozens, their gills fluttering in the cold. They do not feed on plants, for there are none; they graze the fungal felt and the orange bacterial mats, in slow, rhythmic bites. Beneath them, in the silt, armies of grey isopods and blind worms work the mycelial cords, turning fungal growth into humus that the mushrooms will use again.

Around the lamps the air is dense with fungus gnats and pale moths, a living dust that thickens when the generator coughs and the fans turn. They are food. Dippers — small, dark birds — nest in the gearbox housings and walk, bobbing, along the conveyor belts, picking insects from the trough edges with a dart of the beak. When they dive, they vanish into the black water and emerge shaking, and the mushrooms beside the shed seem to listen. In the flooded office, a colony of something like naked, soft-bodied slugs climbs the walls in a continuous, silent procession, following the condensation upward and laying down a faint, glistening trail that other slugs follow.

There are animals that live entirely on the machines. Tiny, armoured mites populate the warmest pipes. Beetles with glass-bead eyes, dulled by the perpetual damp, harvest flakes of rust. In the darkest bunker, a dense raft of translucent, six-legged larvae hangs from the ceiling by threads, swaying together like a curtain, filtering spores from the wet air. Everything is near-touching. A fungal mat touches a pipe touches a nest of larvae touches a pool of tadpoles touches the leg of a bird. The density is not chaos; it is layering, a high saturation of presences in a very small volume, each using the one above and below it.

The strangeness is not violent. It accumulates when you watch. The stones on the conveyor never accumulate; they are endlessly re-sorted, and occasionally a stone will be carried up, dropped, and found an hour later in a different pool, moved by an eel or a current. The condensation sometimes forms on the cold iron in brief, clear patterns — fingerprints, rings — before running. The fungal fruiting happens not by day or night but by the electrical cycle: when the generator kicks, the caps in the shed exhale together, a visible puff of spores that the gnats rise into. Some of the animals have no eyes at all and navigate, unerringly, by the taste of the water and the faint hum in the pipes. A current of water that should flow downhill has been trained, by pumps, to flow uphill, and the eels have learned to ride it like a road.

It is active, despite the quiet. Several processes run at once and none ever stops. The water level rises a fingertip with the distant rain and falls a fingertip with the pump's cycle. The conveyor knocks softly every few minutes as a stone falls. The fungal mats shift their edges by a millimetre a day, reclaiming a new section of rust. The isopods graze and are eaten by the amphibians, who are hunted in turn by the eels in the dark pipes, who are left alone by the dippers. A fine spray falls on the screens and hisses and evaporates a little and condenses again. Even the light moves, that dim, grey light, sliding across the pools but never warming them.

At dusk — if you can call the dimming of an already dim place dusk — the lamps become more important. The orange lamp at the gate, two bare bulbs in the shed, one light under water in the lowest pool, still burning after all these years, casting a smudged glow up through the black film. The fungi brighten a fraction. The gnats thicken. The generator settles into its slower part of the cycle. The sound returns to its baseline: a drip that lands every four seconds in the culvert, the faint churn of the pump, the cooling tick of metal, the soft plink of stones, your own breathing, fogged.

Nothing here will become a meadow. Nothing here will dry out, or be rebuilt, or be truly abandoned. The water has taken the yard into itself and the animals have taken the water, and the machines, useless now, have become cliffs and caves and feeding grounds. It is established, in balance, saturated, and waiting — not for an end, but for the next cycle of its own loop.
