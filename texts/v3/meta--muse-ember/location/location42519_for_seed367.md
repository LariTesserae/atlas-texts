It is high, and it is dry, and for a long walk that is all the world there is.

You cross a plateau of bone-pale limestone, worn so long by wind and sun that it has no sharpness left in it. Slopes slump at the angle they have slumped for a thousand years. Edges are rounded as pottery. White salt crusts in the cracks, red dust in the hollows, fossil shells spiral out of the stone where a sea once lay so far back the memory of it is only in the rock. No stream crosses here. No puddle. The ground drinks everything that falls on it the moment it lands. Your boots kick up fine flour-dust that hangs a half-second longer than it should in the thin, high air, and your breath comes short.

Then the plateau dips — not a canyon, not a cliff — and you are in a notch worn through the lip of a single, shallow bowl.

That is the whole place. From the notch, with one turn of the head, you can see all of it.

Three hundred paces across, perhaps sixty deep, ringed wall to wall. A pocket dented into the high country, hidden from every direction except directly overhead, and even then only as a dark smudge that could be shadow. Inside it, time has settled. The bowl is ancient in the way a very old room is ancient: nothing here is new, nothing is falling down in a fresh way. The walls have long ago finished crumbling. They are smooth, banded, washed strata curving down like the inside of a cupped hand. Deep steps, cut and then eroded and then cut again by feet, are concave in the middle. Old walls have slumped into soil. Old pots have slumped into soil. A footpath that has been walked for perhaps a hundred generations is a shallow trough in stone.

Because the rim stands between it and the sun, the bowl lives in dimness even at noon. Light arrives late, slides in over the lip as a small, hard disc, and crawls across the floor all day like a slow clock hand. For two hours in the middle of the day the sun patch is on the market square and the central tree and the hottest stones, and everywhere else is blue shade. Everything not in that patch glows by reflected light — warm stone bouncing light back, green leaves bouncing it, dust in the air. In the shadow it is cool enough for dew to survive until ten. In the sun patch the stone under your bare feet is almost too hot to stand on, and the warm air rises straight up and out.

There is no water on the surface, and yet there is green everywhere.

That is the bowl’s trick, and its rule. Rain, when it comes — a warm, violent, all-at-once summer rain — vanishes through the porous limestone into a deep, invisible aquifer far below. The floor never pools. The floor is dusty to the touch at noon. But the roots go down. They are the oldest inhabitants here, even older than the stone steps.

In every pocket of black soil that has gathered in a crack, a plant has taken hold and never let go. The outer rim is dry scrub, grey and wiry. The inner bowl, falling into shade, is lush to the point of overcrowding. Broad-leaved evergreens with waxy, cool leaves that never quite wilt. Rock ferns uncurling from wall crevices. Vines as thick as your wrist climbing houses, climbing the central fig, climbing each other, crossing lanes at head height so you duck under living roofs. Flowering shrubs packed so tight their blossoms touch. Fruit trees — dust-figs, small hard plums, a grove of resin-pines — planted in terraces where a hand's breadth of soil has been coaxed into existence.

At dawn every leaf is wet. The dew hangs heavy even though the dust a hand's breadth below is dry. Larks and lizards and children lick it off. The plants drink from below and breathe out the excess above, so the bowl always has its own small weather: a low mist in the mornings that does not rise, but hangs between the houses and smells of crushed green leaf and warm stone, then burns off as the sun disc reaches the floor.

A town fills the bowl, dense as bees in a hive.

It is primitive, entirely hand-made. No thing here was made by anything but hands, stone tools, fire, and patience. Houses of mud-brick and fieldstone climb the lower slopes in tiers, flat-roofed, leaning into each other for support, walls sharing walls. Roofs are gardens, goat pens, drying racks for fruit and meat and clay pots. Lanes are not planned so much as worn between them — steep, stepped, too narrow in places for a loaded donkey, scented with dung, woodsmoke, roasting resin, sweat, and ripe fruit. In the center is the clearing and the fig. The fig is wider than the clearing. Its roots go into the cracks and never come back, lifting slabs, making cool hollows under the soil where cats sleep and stores are kept. The town was built around it because the town had to be built around shade, and the shade just never moved.

Everything is close. A nest in the eaves. Ants in a highway line across a doorstep to a fallen plum in the market. A lizard flattened on a sun-warmed wall, motionless, while three feet away a woman pounds grain in a stone mortar in a steady, loud rhythm. Workshops are open-fronted: a loom clacking, a potter turning a base by hand in a pit, a smith — if the word is not too grand — working a single piece of traded copper with hammer and fire. Granaries are sealed clay jars sunk into cool earth. A low, thick wall runs around the usable floor, not for war but for goats, who would otherwise eat the gardens to the root in a day.

There are deep shafts, only three, sunk by hand-drilled stone and rope into that hidden aquifer. Water comes up one bucket at a time, is carried in covered jars, is rationed in a way no one now remembers inventing but everyone obeys without thought. It is for drinking and for the hammock of young plants. The rest of the town lives, as the plants do, off what is given from below and what falls from the sky and is immediately lost.

And the animals are overwhelming.

You hear them before you properly see them, and once inside you never stop hearing them. Cicadas sawing in layers from every tree at once, so loud in the still shade that conversation is raised-voice work. Parrots arguing in the fig canopy. Swifts screaming as they shear around the rim. Bees and large, slow flies working the flowers in a constant hum. Underfoot, ants, beetles, centipedes trading places in the warm dust. On the walls, lizards and small geckos by the hundred, tails flicking. In the crevices, mice and rats going about their business in daylight because there are too many of them to be shy. Cats padded between houses. Dogs barking at nothing on the rim. Goats tethered everywhere, bells clanking, stripping bark from any root they can reach.

At dusk the bowl changes its population. The day birds settle screaming into the trees, and something stirs in the stone. Bats pour in a thin, rapid stream out of wall cracks to hunt the insect plume that rises over the fig like smoke. A hawk comes over the lip twice a day, very high, and for a minute the whole bowl goes quieter — lizards flatten, birds freeze, goats bunch — then the hawk passes and the noise rushes back louder, as if relieved. The dynamics here are fast and close and endless: pollination, predation, scavenging, decay. Nothing is allowed to lie still. A fallen leaf is eaten by ants before evening, a dead bird is found by the rats by dark, a dropped plum is a war by morning. The green that looks so settled is being chewed, eaten, hunted, and rebuilt every hour.

The air moves in its own daily cycle. Cold, dense air pools in the bowl overnight — you can feel it as a chill at ankle height at dawn. As the sun disc touches the floor, the pool warms, lifts, and begins to spiral up, drawing the cooking smoke, the dust, seeds, feathers and midges with it, out through the notch and away over the empty plateau. In the afternoon a pleasant, hot wind comes down the other way, bringing news from outside — a scrap of dry paper, a seed from a plant that does not grow here, the smell of salt and distance. The leaves shiver all together. Then, near evening, the updraft dies, the cold pool sinks back, and the bowl seals itself again like a room at night.

At night the sky is only a small circle, far above the rim, intensely blue-black and crowded with stars. The town's lamps — oil in clay dishes — make the lanes amber. The sounds change to night crickets, soft wings, goats shifting, a distant dog, the occasional crack of cooling stone. The stone gives back its stored heat slowly. It is warm, always warm, even high up. The dust settles. The plants close their breathing.

Nothing here is strange. That is part of its intensity. Stone, soil, smoke, leaves, bells, sweat, excrement, fruit. The strangest thing is how complete it is in itself, how much life can be packed into one dim, dry, ancient hollow, how a whole working town and a whole forest and a thousand small hungry lives can share three hundred paces of floor and never once step outside the rim for weeks at a time.

You could walk its entire length in five minutes. You could spend a lifetime learning its lanes.
