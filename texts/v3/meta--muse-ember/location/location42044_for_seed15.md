You hear it first, and it is louder than anything that small should be.

The tunnel comes in low and dog-legged, brick on brick, the ceiling so close you have to turn sideways around a jutting sluice-beam. The brick is still sharp. Not rounded by centuries, not softened. You can feel the thumbprints in the lime mortar, the chisel marks where a mason hurried, the fingerprint ridges in a tile that was laid while the clay was still damp. A brick at your shoulder is cracked clean in half - the break is pale and unweathered, as if it happened last week. Someone finished this place recently and then left it to fill.

Then the tunnel opens, and the noise opens with it.

It is one room. A pocket, sunken. Twenty paces across at most if you could walk it, but you can't walk it, because most of it isn't floor anymore. It's water.

Warm water, to the middle of the vault. Waist-deep on the entrance steps, where the brick treads go down into a a clear, turquoise green. Chest-deep in the channel that runs along the far wall. In the corner by the spring-mouth it goes up past your chin, almost to the spring of the arch, and there is only a curved lung of hot air left above it. 3/4 of the room, by volume, is liquid.

The light shouldn't be there. You're below the surface - above you, through the brick crown, you can hear dull thumps and the scrape of a cart on a courtyard floor. No earth is visible anywhere. Not on the floor, not between bricks, not underwater. Even where you'd expect silt or mud or a natural bank, there is pavement: the same warm, sun-baked brick, laid in uneven hand-made courses, continuing down under the water to the far wall. The walls are brick. The ceiling is brick, vaulted in a single hand-laid dome. The "rocks" in the water are brick. The steps, the ledges, the collars around the pipes - all built, all made, all warm to the hand even when submerged.

And yet it is blazing bright. Midday sun falls through three round vents in the crown, no wider than a dinner plate, and the whitewashed lime inside throws it back on itself until there are almost no shadows. The water turns that flat white-gold into a moving, rippling glare. Underwater, looking down, you can see the far wall as clearly as the near one. The bricks down there are bleached almost white, fired looking, though they're wet. Overexposed is the word.

It is hot. Not furnace hot. Bath hot. The air is 30-something degrees and thick with steam, smelling of wet lime, hot clay, copper from a few nails, and the faint mineral tang of the spring. The bricks themselves hold the heat. You put a palm flat on the wall above the waterline and it feels like a sun-warmed stone in summer. Underwater it feels the same. There is no cold anywhere.

You wade, and the water is not still. That's the other loud thing. This place is active, cycling.

Water never stops moving through it. At the deep corner the spring comes in through a clay pipe-mouth set in a brick collar - a single, mouth-sized bore, built not found. It pushes in cloudy with tiny bubbles that burst along the floor cracks. At the opposite end a wooden sluice, lifted and dropped by a stone counterweight on a rope, chatters and bangs every few minutes against its frame. When it drops, the roar changes pitch, the whole room echoes, and the level rises a hand's breadth in a rush that slaps the bricks. Then it falls again, with a sucking gurgle through the overflow channel by the entrance tunnel. The water is constantly on its way somewhere else. Vents of steam rise where the hot inflow meets the sun-heated surface. Light shafts in the water sway with the current.

Because it's loud, it feels bigger than it is. The vault throws every drip back at you like a hammer on a drum. A single drop from a ceiling joint - a little lime stalactite built up in weeks where water seeps through mortar - lands and sounds like a stone thrown into a well. The sluice, the inflow, the slosh of your own legs, the creak of wet wood: they stack up until you have to shout to be heard across a room you could throw a ball across. And for all that noise, it is empty.

Sparse is the best word for it. Sparse in objects, sparse in life, sparse in everything except water, light, and sound.

On the dry ledge above the main waterline - a narrow brick shelf that runs halfway around, for sitting or for setting things down - there is only the scatter of work left behind: three wooden paddles leaning at different angles, their ends dark with use; a stone hammer sunk half in the shallows where someone dropped it; a coil of wet rope on the second step; a row of unglazed clay jars, most of them half-submerged and bobbing gently in a line like buoys; the broken frame of something, maybe a loom or a hide-stretcher, its legs poking out of the green water. Nothing else. No benches, no decoration, no storage. Just a great deal of open water and open air between presences.

Life, where it exists, is the same. There are no animals at all. That is the first uncanny thing, and then you realize it's just practical. The water is too warm, too lime-rich, too fast-moving, and too regularly scoured for fish to live, for snails to cling, for insects to land. You look and look through that perfect clarity and there is no flicker, no dart, no web, no track on the brick. The emptiness in the water is total.

Plants manage, barely. Sparse vegetation, clinging where it can. A thin film of green algae paints the north side of the vault where the light is a little less brutal, in streaks down from the joints. Elsewhere the walls are bare white. In the mortar cracks above the waterline there are pinched tufts of salt-grass and a single determined fern, its roots finding a joint and splitting it by a hair. From one vent, a fig root has come down through the brickwork and hangs in the air, pale and searching, not yet reaching the water. On the surface, in the slack corner by the steps, a drifting mat of duckweed-like green appears and is torn apart again by the current. An iridescent bacterial skin forms and breaks on the slower water by the jars. That's the ecology: minerals, algae, bacteria, and a few plants holding on at the edge of an environment that is trying, with lime and heat, to sterilize itself.

The rules are simple and man-made, because everything here is. The plug controls it. A heavy wooden plug sealed with clay in the drain channel near the entrance: pull it, and the whole chamber will empty in minutes through the tunnel, leaving the bricks steaming and sharp and dripping. Leave it, and the spring fills it again by evening. The counterweight sluice is set to cycle - a primitive timer of stone and rope and wood, no metal beyond a few copper pins. The workers - whoever built this on hand tools, with hammers and baskets and a wooden lever - came down the ladder by day to soak wool, hides, laundry, whatever needed hot mineral water, and left at night. The chamber was built to be used and then, perhaps, not quite finished. The replastering is still pale. The erosion is only what water and hands have done in the first weeks: bright waterline tide-marks in green and ochre, a silt-free floor, bricks still too sharp to be old.

You float for a moment in the deep channel, because the water holds you up warm and buoyant, and the ceiling is so bright above you it feels like sky, not brick. The sound of the inflow is in your ears as much as in the air. All around you, below, the built floor recedes, perfectly paved, perfectly empty, perfectly clear. Above, the three small suns in the vault move minutely with the hour. The steam gets thicker. The sluice bangs again.

It is a hidden room inside a built world, under the ground, under water, full of light, full of noise, and almost entirely empty of everything that would normally live in it.
