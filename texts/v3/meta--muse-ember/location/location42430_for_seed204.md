It is wet first, before it is anything else.

A black stone under your hand, cold enough to ache, and beaded all over with water that has not fallen from the sky. It weeps. The rock is young — split, not worn — its edges still sharp enough to cut the palm, its faces faceted like fresh broken glass. Where the stone has split most recently the inside is pale and clean, almost white, before damp and air have had time to stain it. Water runs in thin, unhurried threads down those faces, gathers in the grain, finds a lower crack, and runs again. It never quite dries. In shaded joints it beads and freezes in the night and clicks as it melts in the morning sun.

You are inside something. That is the first scale of the place. From where you stand you can see all of it at once: a bowl, a pocket, a hollow crown set high in a mountain that has not finished rising. The rim goes all the way around, close enough to read its texture — layered basalt, spalling shards, fresh scree at the base of each cliff face — and far enough above the world that the clouds are below you in the morning, like a sea that has forgotten the shore. Your ears pop when you came up. The air is thin and still inside the bowl, for all that the rim is narrow. There is no road in. There is only a slot between two slabs, just wide enough for a person, and a membrane there that is not a door, that lets you through and closes behind you as if it had never been.

Light here is ordinary. Normal daylight, high and clear, blue-white, hard-shadowed. For an hour at midday the sun finds the gap in the rim and lays a bright stripe across the floor of the bowl, and the stripe travels slowly, the way a sundial intends. Nothing glows. Nothing flickers. The weirdness of the place is not in the sky.

The floor is not soil. There is no soil. It is mineral all the way down: polished black flats, rough pale crusts where water has left salts behind, shallow pans of clear water that reflect the rim. No green anywhere. No plant has taken hold, and none will yet — the young rock is too wet and too cold and too new, and the city does not leave room for it. In the damp cracks there is a thin grey film, fungal, simpler than lichen, that eats stone-sweat and mineral dust and leaves no leaf. That is the entire flora. The rest of the living surface is construction.

The city is sparse. That is its second paradox. It is a settlement, organized and layered and unmistakably urban, and yet most of the bowl is empty. You could walk from one end to the other in ten minutes, yet between the buildings there are hundreds of meters of open, wet stone with nothing on it. The towers stand far apart, like chess pieces left on a board after a game has mostly been cleared.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, no more. Tapered, jointed, ribbed in layers of ceramic and metal and something clear that is not glass. Some are dwellings, their windows fogged from within, lit in the day by low interior light. Some are water towers, vast and slow-breathing. Some are heat exchangers, their flanks finned to shed cold. Some are stores, some are empty and sealed, growing a millimeter a year for no reason anyone can now remember. Between them run the streets, which are not streets at all but cleared strips of polished stone and the buried lines of ducts that carry heat and water and power from tower to tower without loss, a circulatory system laid in plain sight in shallow trenches, steaming faintly in the cold. Freight sleds move along the strips without wheels or tracks, a hand's breadth above the ground, silent, unhurried, carrying cisterns and crates and once in a while a person who does not look up.

Everything is active and almost everything is quiet.

At dusk the temperature falls — it is always cold here, a mineral cold that radiates up through your boots even when the sun is on your neck — and the bowl fills from the bottom with fog. Not mist from outside. Condensation. The air inside the pocket saturates, the towers go pale and weep harder, their skins turning to a fine, rainless rain that runs down into channels cut in the stone. The fog rises to the height of a man's shoulder, then to the second stories, then submerges the lower towers entirely, so they stand as islands in a white sea. Water is the city's harvest. It drinks the fog.

In the morning the rim catches the light first, and the fog begins to burn away from the top down, in layers. Meltwater from the shaded joints feeds the trickles. Ice formed overnight in a tower's expansion seam ticks and pops as it thaws. A tower exhales a plume of steam, a duct sighs and equalizes, a shutter opens in a wall six floors up and closes again. The ground itself, still settling from a formation that is geologically yesterday, lifts by fractions. The whole city, patiently, jacks itself upward a few millimeters a year, and no one remarks on it. The young mountain is still deciding its shape, and the city has learned to decide with it.

The materials do not behave as materials ought to, and that is treated as ordinary. A slab cracked by contraction in the night will bead over with a clear resin by noon and be stronger than before. A window is not a pane but a phase-sheet; when the fog rises it goes opaque, when the sun clears it goes clear again, faster than any shutter. A tower is two meters taller in late summer than in spring, from simple thermal breathing, and the stairwells inside have been built to accommodate the change without comment. Gravity here is normal. Time is normal. The physics is stretched thin in places — heat moves without pipes, water moves without pumps, a single duct can be warm on one end and cold on the other for reasons the maintenance manual explains in a single sentence — but no one here is astonished by it. It is infrastructure. It is plumbing.

Life, besides the people who are rarely seen and rarely heard, is rarer still. In the warm culverts under the main ducts live blind grazers, six-legged, smooth and pale as tallow, that scrape the mineral film off the pipe walls and are content to go weeks without light. At night, high up, cleaner things move between the towers — small, winged, mechanical or half-mechanical, you cannot tell — sweeping the weeping skins and leaving them dry. There are not many. You can spend days in the bowl and see none of them. The ecology of the place is not an ecology of growth but of cycling: water up as fog, down as drip; heat out, cold in; stone splitting, stone healing; the towers taking in the wet and giving off the dry.

Mostly it is quiet. The high air does not carry sound far, the wet stone swallows what is made, and the machines were designed — or have evolved — to make almost none. What you hear is the drip of the rocks into the channels, regular as a pulse. A soft click as a tower contracts. The faint, distant hum of a duct equalizing, which stops being noticeable after an hour and is only noticed again when it stops. Wind only at the rim, where it keens a little over the sharp edges, and even that does not reach the floor.

Sit for a while on one of the black flats, where the sun has warmed the stone just enough to be bearable through your coat. The fog is coming up early now, because it is cold. The towers emerge from it one by one, beading, breathing, lit from within against the white. The water keeps running: off the rim, off the towers, off the stone under your hand, into the pans, into the ducts, back up into the air at dusk to start again. Nothing here grows green and nothing rots. The city holds its empty spaces, holds its water, holds its shape against a young and sharp and rising world — and is, for all its cleverness, mostly silence and damp and cold stone, waiting for the next fog to fill the bowl.
