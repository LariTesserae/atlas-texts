From the plain, you do not see a town so much as a table.

The plain goes on flat and wet to the horizon in every direction, a sheet of grey mud and shallow water that has not yet decided whether to be land or sea. It is low, almost level. Nothing rises on it except a line of mist that hangs knee-high and does not quite burn off. The light is ordinary daylight, bright and thin and colourless, and it shines hard off the water even at noon, so everything looks varnished, desaturated, and cold. The wind comes straight off that water with nothing to break it, and it carries salt and a smell of wet stone and rotting kelp from far out.

Then the table breaks the flat.

About ten minutes' walk across — you can see the far edge from the near edge, and the whole of it at once if you stand on a roof — a single slab of rock lifts only a few metres above the plain, and stops. Its sides are not cliffs in the old, eroded sense. They are fresh. The rock has been sheared, not worn. Blue-grey slate and mudstone split into plates with edges still sharp enough to cut a boot, barnacles still crusted high up on the vertical faces where the sea used to reach, fossil shells still lying in their seabed patterns in the stone. This ground is young. It came up within living memory, when the sea floor slipped and tilted, and nothing has had time to soften it. There is no soil. Rain does not soak in; it runs, and cuts a new rill every time.

A town has been built on the table. Not beside it, not around it. On it, and only on it.

Because the plain around is tidal and soft and useless for building, and the table is the one hard, relatively high place for miles, the town has nowhere to go. It is packed in a way that looks planned and accidental at once. The streets follow the cracks between the rock plates, because those were the first drains. Each crack is a lane, a gutter, and a property boundary. Houses stand wall to wall with no yards, no gardens, no alleys left between them; one house's wall is the next house's wall, its roof is the next house's floor. When a family needed more room, they did not spread outward. They built on top. Look closely and the town is stratified. Lower doors open onto the roof of a neighbour below, reached by a notched driftwood ladder. Older mudbrick is half-buried under newer stone, and under that, under that. In places the lowest rooms have been given up entirely to damp and are now used as cisterns, filled with shell rubble to keep the floors above from washing out.

The building is urban, in its way — there is a grid, a perimeter wall no higher than a man's knee to keep a child from the drop, a communal kiln, a great central cistern sunk into the rock, lanes named by their crack. But the tech that made it is primitive and hand-made. Mudbrick dried slowly in the cool air, slate split with stone wedges, shell lime burnt in the kiln and plastered white over everything so the rain will run off. Timber is driftwood, pale and salt-bleached, every lintel different. Rope, bone needles, flint and basalt adzes, clay pipes and wooden buckets on pulleys. You might see a handful of hammered nails in a whole street. Everything is thick because it must be — the walls are damp, and damp swallows.

Water is the real architect here. It is not a marsh, and it is not lush. The ground never dries. Water lives near the surface and in the air more than in the earth. The rock weeps. In the cool of morning the cold slabs condense the wet air and drip steadily, plate by plate, into the cracks. Inside the houses, the same thing happens on the inside walls, so each house has an interior trough along its lowest wall, and a clay pipe that carries that slow sweat to the cistern. The people live as much on condensation as on rain. When it does rain, it sheets off the slate and mud roofs in minutes, runs down the lanes ankle-deep, and pours off the edge of the table in a dozen thin waterfalls onto the plain below. The lanes are never really dry. Your feet are always cold.

That is why there is so little green. The water is sea-water and salt, and the rock is new and bare. Only lichens crust the slate in pale maps. Only salt-tufts and one stubborn halophyte grow in the cracks where fresh seep gathers, and clumps of reed hold on at the base of the cliff where a spring finds the edge. The strangest vegetation is where you would not look for it: on the roofs. Up there the rain washes the salt away, soot and leak feed it, and moss and small tough weeds grow thickly and creep down the north walls in green beards. The town looks, from above, as if it is wearing a thin, ragged thatch of its own hair.

Life is rare. There are no birdsong, no livestock noises, no market shouting — and that is part of why the place feels so quiet despite being so full. The thick, wet walls muffle footfall. People move barefoot or in soft shoes on the rubbery mud of the lanes, and they speak low, partly from habit, partly because the constant drip has made loudness unnecessary. What you hear instead is water in motion everywhere: dripping into troughs, trickling in cracks, buckets clunking into the cistern, the faint fluting of wind through a lane. After rain, the waterfalls roar for an hour and then are gone.

The animals that are here have adapted to the same narrow terms. Grey moths drown themselves happily in the condensation on the cistern rim. Eels thread the drains and appear, slick and black, in the lanes after a tide. In the dark cistern itself live pale, blind fish, no bigger than a thumb, that the town feeds on crumbs and watches as a sign of clean water. At the foot of the table, in the pools that never empty, crabs work the fresh rock. Little else. No trees for birds to nest in, no soil for insects to breed in. The emptiness in the air is as noticeable as the pressure of the houses.

And the place is never still. The table is still settling, a fraction at a time. On a cold night you can hear the plates contract and tick, like a bowl cooling, a soft counting going across the town. By day the tide advances and retreats against the base, undermining a corner, leaving a new crack across a lane that someone must fill with stone by evening. Condensation comes at dawn and runs out by midday; the seep returns at dusk. Mudbrick slumps a little each winter and must be replastered each late summer with fresh shell lime, a communal, repetitive work that is the town's calendar. Houses are not built once. They are continuously lifted, propped, re-faced, re-roofed, built over.

There are rules that follow from this, unspoken and strict. A leak is not a nuisance; left alone it will become a channel and take a wall. A house left empty will weep itself to ruin in a season. No one throws water away. No one builds on the newest splinter of rock at the highest corner, because that piece still tilts after rain and nothing will sit square on it — the children play there instead, on the only open ground, surrounded on all sides by the packed roofs.

Stand in the middle, by the cistern, and you can take the whole world in. The wall-to-wall roofs all around you, each weighted with stones against the wind, each chimneyless kitchen sending up a thin curl of driftwood smoke that dissolves instantly in the cool air. The lanes dropping away in wet lines. The four sharp edges of the table. And beyond that, nothing but the wet, shining plain, going flat to the sea, where the water that the town has caught, used, and sweated out again will eventually find its way back.
