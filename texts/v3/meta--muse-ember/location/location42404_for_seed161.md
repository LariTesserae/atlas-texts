It is not a peak. That is the first surprise, after three days of switchbacks.

You climb and climb out of the warm lowlands, through forest that gets denser and then strange and then smaller, through cloud that thickens until the sun goes out like a lamp turned down. The path is old — worn into steps by feet and hooves and finally by sledges, cut into rock that is no longer sharp. The mountain has finished. They call it the Worn One, and geologically they are right. It has been standing so long, and being worn so steadily, that its top is no longer a point at all but a broad, rounded dome, like a shoulder, like a polished stone at the bottom of a river. The edges have slumped and softened and been caught by soil and forest. There are no fresh cliffs. No scree sliding. Everything that could fall, fell millions of years ago, and what was left has had time to knit together with roots.

The soil on top is deep and black and ancient. Not thin mountain soil over rock, but true accumulated earth, decades of leaves rotting into it, generations of the dead buried in it and turned into it. Ferns grow waist-high in the lanes. Moss grows on the north side of every stone, which is every side, because the light never comes from one direction long enough to dry anything.

The town sits in the middle of that dome, and you can walk around its entire edge in twenty minutes. That is what stays with you. It is small enough to see whole, and yet it is a city — a whole city crammed into that smallness.

A low wall, built of round river-stones and flat slate and re-laid every spring, runs around the rim of the plateau. It is not there for war. It is there to mark where the soil ends. Beyond it, the ground drops away steeply into forest that falls for a thousand meters before it finds a valley, and birds are flying *below* you, and clouds are pooling below you in the valleys, while you stand in a different cloud that never leaves.

Inside the wall, the town is dense to the point of touching.

Houses are not detached. They lean on each other, share walls, stack two and three stories high over shops that open directly into gardens that open directly into lanes that are more like stairs. A house will be stone on the ground floor, timber and wattle above it, a roof of slate shingles weighed down with moss, and then, on the roof, a garden — potatoes and tubers and onions in shallow, damp earth, their roots shallow because there is nowhere deep to go. The next house's terrace is the next house's ceiling. Vines run from one balcony to the next, so that in summer the whole quarter looks like one continuous green mass with doors cut in it.

Every surface is vegetated. Orchids grow from doorframes and from the cracks in market stalls. Bromeliads big as buckets sit in gutters and on walls, each one holding a perfect cup of dew and, inside that cup, a frog no bigger than a thumbnail, which peeps at night loud enough to wake the street. Tree ferns spread their fronds over the roofs so that the slate is always in shade. Fungus softens fence posts. Lichen makes the stone steps green and slick.

It is warm here, improbably warm for this height. The mountain is near the equator, and the cloud acts like a greenhouse. The air is thick and humid but thin to breathe — you get winded carrying a bucket up twelve steps, your heart loud in your ears — and your skin sweats and never quite dries. There is no glare. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a flat, green-grey luminosity, as if you were under water or under a canopy that covers the sky. At midday the lamps are lit in the market hall, not from ceremony but because it is simply dimmer inside. Shadows have no edges. You could live here a year and never throw a sharp shadow.

And there is — this is the rule that shapes everything — no water. Not a river. Not a lake. Not a spring. Not even a puddle after rain, because rain, in the way it falls elsewhere, does not happen here. The cloud that sits on the summit is caught by the trees first. The trees are the rain.

Everything is wet, and nothing is ever wet in a pool. The air drips. Leaves drip for hours after the mist has passed. Dust is never dusty; it is damp and smells of wet stone and smoke and leaf-mold. People and animals drink by licking leaves, by eating dew-soaked greens, by wringing cloths hung overnight on tall poles between the houses. Those poles are everywhere — great woven nets of fiber, like sails furled, that comb the mist out of the air and drip it, drop by drop, into clay jars set underneath. A roof is a funnel. A lane is a channel. Every stone street is slightly dished so its dew runs to a closed cistern under the market square, a reservoir built by ancestors whose names are gone, sealed with clay, opened only in the driest weeks — which are not dry, only less misty.

Water lives inside things. Inside leaves, inside frogs, inside jars with lids tied down with string, inside people's bodies. A jar left open is a fine. To spill a jar is to spill the town's rain. Children are taught before they can read: you do not wash, you do not bathe, you do not let a leaf be torn except by the gardener who needs it. You are not thirsty, most days, because the mist is in your lungs. You are only, always, a little damp.

The fauna make up for the silence the lack of rivers might imply. It is loud here. Not a quiet mountain hermitage. At dawn the birds start — hummingbirds that hover and scold at the orchids, and larger birds that nest in the eaves and scream on schedules you can set a clock by. All day there is hammering — slate being snapped with a stone hammer, wood being pegged, because they have no iron nails to speak of, only a little copper worked by hand into needles and pins, and tools of stone, bone, and hardwood polished by use. Market women call. Goats bleat from terraces above your head. Guinea pigs squeak from under floors. Dogs bark at the fog. Insects make a continuous low roar in the warm air. At night the frogs in the bromeliads take over, hundreds of them, and the dripping into jars makes a soft, irregular percussion underneath it all. The sound of a place with nowhere to spread out.

Its technology is all hand and patience. They build slowly because they must, and because there is no reason to hurry. A house takes years — the stone must be gathered round from the ancient slopes, the timber cut high on the mountain so it will be strong, the roof laid shingle by shingle so the moss will root and hold them. A terrace takes a lifetime; its wall must settle. The town does not expand. It cannot. The wall is the wall, the soil is the soil, and every stone used in a new house is a stone taken from a ruined one, cleaned and re-laid by a grandchild. Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks ruined. It has settled. The mountain settled first, and the town has settled in imitation of it, into a final form that will outlast everyone who lives in it and change only imperceptibly — a millimeter of erosion a century, a root thickening a mortar joint, a garden shifting one hand's breadth from one roof to the next.

There is nothing magical about it. The people are ordinary, tired, funny, busy. They keep their tools oiled with plant fat. They bury their dead shallow in the garden soil so the potatoes will be strong, and they plant a tree fern over each grave, so the dead literally shade the living. They mend nets, they salt their damp greens, they light oil lamps at noon and blow them out at dusk when the frogs start.

At evening, the light does not change so much as thin further, until the whole town is in a green twilight that could be noon or midnight. You stand on a terrace, and from here you can see the whole of Tawaq: the wall, the roofs, the nets, the gardens climbing over everything, the paths dropping away at the rim. Below the rim, the older, wilder forest falls away into cloud, and you can hear, faintly, the town still going — hammers, birds settling, a bell rung slowly, not for time but to tell the net-keepers the mist is thickening again.

Everything here is ancient, and everything here is alive, and nothing here ever moves quickly.
