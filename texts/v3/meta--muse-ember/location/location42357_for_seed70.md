It is green before it is anything else.

Not a clearing in the green, not a path through it — green pressing at shoulder height, at knee height, overhead and underfoot, layered so there is no single surface to call ground. Leaves overlap leaves. Stems braid into stems. Vines have forgotten where they end and the plant beneath them begins. The air is warm and full in the lungs, like breathing through a damp cloth laid over a warm stone. Sunlight comes through normally, high and clear and white-gold, and it falls the way it would on an ordinary afternoon. It is only slowly that you understand the light is falling *through* something.

Your feet are wet. They have been wet for some time. 

The water is not a river. It does not run. It does not lap. It stands. Everywhere, between the green and the rock, in the hollows and across the flats, a still sheet of warm water holds itself at a level — ankle-deep here, waist-deep there, neck-deep in the places between the young ridges where the land dips. It is clear, startlingly clear for all the lushness. Sunlight goes straight down through it. Under the surface the green continues, unchanged. Ferns uncurl under water as they do in air. Broad leaves spread flat against the current that never comes. Woody trunks go down and keep going, their bark softened, their roots spreading into a mat that is at once soil and floor.

You are not on a shore. There is no shore that stays. There is land that has recently come up through water and has not yet decided where it ends.

This is a high place. That is the second strangeness, after the light underwater. The air is thin enough at the top of your breath that the sun feels hotter on your back, the sky is larger, the horizon repeats. The land is a plateau lifted up into the warm belt of the world — high ground, equatorial sun, no cold in the water, no season to dry anything out. Around you the water stretches past the immediate slope into a whole region of basins and interlocking lakes, because the uplift is young and the drainage has not learned its way out yet.

The erosion is young. That is visible. The ridges between the flooded valleys are not rounded by age. They are sharp, shattered, almost freshly broken — black stone with glassy faces, red earth still raw where it has been torn open, gravel that has not yet been sorted into sand. You can cut your hand on a crest if you are careless. A few peaks punch up bare and knife-edged above the water and the treetops; most of the world is the softer country below them, where the water has settled and plants have taken the settling as permission.

And they have taken it densely. Density here is not crowdedness in the animal sense. There are almost no animals to crowd. A nibble taken out of a leaf every so often, a single blind frog-slow shape easing through the weeds far below the surface, once, no birdsong, no chatter of insects. The quiet is not empty, it is muffled. Water takes sound and makes it slow. A drip falls from a leaf and takes a long time to reach the surface it was already almost touching. Elsewhere water sighs over a submerged log, once in a while, and is still again. The place makes noise the way a plant makes noise: by growth.

The growth is what fills the volume. Above water, canopy touches canopy until you cannot see sky except in patches. At the waterline, a second canopy spreads — leaves that live half in, half out, their upper faces waxy and bright, their undersides pale and translucent. Below that, a third and fourth layer: stems, roots, hanging moss, algae-soft mats that move only if you move them. Near-touching is the normal condition. A seed does not fall to earth; it falls onto a leaf, onto a root-mat, onto water that will carry it an inch before it snags. The world is high-saturation green, near-touching green, green all the way down.

Because the water is shallow and standing, the rules are different.

Water does not flow downhill here, not really. Rain arrives as a fine, patient wall at times — warm rain, without wind — and it adds to the sheet, and the sheet rises by a fraction, and stays. Nothing runs off. Nothing dries. Cut wood does not rot quickly in the calm; it silts over and becomes a planter. Stone does not wear smooth. The water is almost too still to erode, so the sharpness of the new land persists under it, preserved like something under glass.

Plants do not need a boundary. A vine can root in air, cross the surface, and root again underwater, and both ends thrive equally. Flowers open at the surface and have their faces reflected exactly below them. Oxygen made by submerged leaves does not escape as a fizz of bubbles racing up; it collects in slow silver films under leaves, hangs there, trembles, and is reabsorbed. Leaves grow in spirals and rosettes that make sense only in still water. Some grow flat as lily pads but never rise; they carpet the bottom between the tree roots. The underwater forest looks like the forest above it, seen through a lens of itself.

There is nothing built. No track, no boat hauled up, no trap, no hut, no cairn. Even the dead do not make architecture. A fallen trunk becomes ground for thirty kinds of green, and then ground for water to stand on, and then ground again. The only tools are biological: air bladders hidden in stems, waxy coatings, roots that weave into a basket strong enough to walk on, seeds that germinate in mid-air and finish opening under water. If something lives here, it lives by being plant, or by being almost plant — slow, anchored, patient.

Time moves at the speed of that patience. Dynamic is a weak word. A ridge that is emergent today will be an island for a generation of trees, then a reef of crowns, then a suggestion of crowns under bright water. A lake that is knee-deep will be chest-deep in a hundred wet seasons and the same in the next hundred in any way you could feel. The plateau itself is still settling, still lifting by amounts you will never notice, its fresh edges slowly — very slowly — forgetting their sharpness. Everything else accumulates. Humidity. Silt. Leaf by leaf, the floor rises.

That is the strangeness, mild but thorough: a high, warm, sunlit land, newly broken and still sharp, drowned not under a sea but under its own standing water, where lushness has filled every volume and animals have mostly left it alone, where silence is the default and movement is a rumor, where you can walk for miles in what looks like forest and never once step onto dry ground, and where the water, holding the sky and the green in perfect clarity, does not seem to be covering the world so much as continuing it, downward, in a slower and brighter register.

At midday the light is ordinary. That is what makes you stay.
