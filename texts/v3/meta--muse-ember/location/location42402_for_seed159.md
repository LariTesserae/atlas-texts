The first surprise is the light.

You are below the surface — unmistakably, by a few tens of metres of rock — and yet the light is ordinary. White, warm, cast by a sun you cannot see. It comes through punctures in the ceiling: not a single cave mouth, but a broken roof, a plateau of stone honeycombed by sinkholes and collapsed domes. Some shafts are wide as streets, some are no more than cracks through which a thin blade of noon falls and moves, very slowly, across the floor. In their paths the rock is the colour of wet clay and ochre; out of them, in shadow, it is dark grey and damp and warm to the touch. Your own shadow is sharp. Dust hangs in the sunbeams. If there were not the ceiling high above — irregular, vaulted, partly held up by pillars that are themselves still growing — you might think you had walked into a dry valley between hills.

It is a valley of a sort. The floor opens out for kilometres, town-wide, chamber joining chamber by broad, low arches, wider than a river canyons, so wide that the far wall dissolves into haze and into movement. There is no single horizon. The ground undulates, heaves, splits, and rises again. This is not finished country. It is raw geology, caught in the act of becoming.

Errosion here runs the other way. You can watch formation happen. In the drier corners, soda straws of calcite drip and lengthen, a finger's breadth in a season, drop by drop building stalactites that will, in a human lifetime, meet the stalagmites below and make a pillar where there was only air. In other places the floor is still clay, unfired and unhardened, cracking in sun-shafts, where a boot leaves a print that a gust of falling dust will half-fill before you turn around. Salt flowers up in feathery white where water has evaporated — a frost, though the air is warm, 25, 28 degrees, close and liquid. Thin plates of stone peel and curl. Vents sigh steam. A slab the size of a door will announce itself with a long, groaning creak, then let go, thunder, and be rubble before the echo has died. And then, in a week, vines of fungal felt will be sewing the rubble back together.

Water is everywhere and nowhere for long. This is a damp world, but an intermittent one. Condensation is the chief rain: on the coolest rock faces, and after the daily sweep of heat through the shafts, moisture beads and runs in a thousand slow threads, dripping from the ceiling in irregular pulses that travel across a chamber like a wave — a sudden, brief, clattering rain from stone, lasting two minutes, soaking one patch of floor, missing the next by a metre. Rivulets braid themselves across the low ground, brown and clear and orange with microbial film, then slacken, sink into porous travertine, and vanish, only to reappear a hundred metres on, foaming white over a lip. Pockets catch and hold: shallow, warm pools still as glass except where something swims through them, leaving a wake for a long time. Even the driest stone is never wholly dry. Run your palm over it and it leaves a damp print. It sweats.

There is not a plant in it. No grass, no root, no leaf, no moss as you know it. The green that would clothe such a place in the open world is replaced by other things, grey and black and sulphur-yellow. Vast fungal mats spread where the damp is constant — not trees, but felt. They skin the walls from floor to halfway up the ceiling in blue-grey velvet, they hang in curtains from ledges to catch the condensation, they run underground in pale cords that surface as sudden, fleshy mushrooms with no cap, only a folded, brain-like fruiting body that pulses and releases a puff of spores when disturbed. In the rivulets, bacteria paint the stones in rust and ink and a startling, almost metallic orange. Where calcite grows fast, it grows like coral without being alive: mineral flowers, branching urchins of stone, white and glassy and brittle, that snap underfoot and then regrow.

And over, under, through all of that, there is life in overwhelming quantity — not plant life, but animal life, dense to the point of pressure.

You do not so much walk through it as negotiate with it. The floor is rarely empty for more than a few steps. On the fungal mats, herds of grazers move: segmented, armoured, dog-sized things with kneeling legs and scraping mouths, pink-grey, half-blind, that chew the fungus down to mineral and leave a clean, wet path behind them. Where they have passed, the mat regrows in days, and they return, and the whole basin seems to breathe in their slow, synchronized advance.

The walls are inhabited layer upon layer. Clinging in clusters so tight they read at a distance as a change in the colour of the rock are thumb-length amphibians with sucker feet and flickering tongues. They cover the sun-warmed faces of pillars in tens of thousands, near-touching, a living wallpaper. Every few minutes a section ripples: they detach, drop a short way, land on a lower ledge, reattach, and the ripple runs across the wall like wind across a field. Their clicking is a constant, dry rain of sound.

In the shafts, the air itself is textured. Flocks of swift-like fliers — leathery-winged, sparrow to hawk-sized, loud with a tearing rush of wings — spiral up and down the light, nesting in the cracks of the ceiling in screaming colonies. When they take off together, it is a weather event: a vortex of bodies, a roar, a momentary eclipse. Below, in the rivulets and pools, eel-like swimmers work the shallows in shoals, and some of them, the strangest of the common things here, climb — haul themselves up wet walls on fin-limbs, out of water entirely for hours, hunting the wall-clingers, leaving glistening tracks on vertical stone.

Burrowers are the engineers. Blind, pale, mole-like but bigger through the shoulders, they push up the clay floor in endless, intersecting tunnels. The ground is never still under your feet; it bulges, subsides, is riven by a crack that parallel-walks you for ten metres and then closes as the animal beneath passes. Their spoil is laid in ridges that are instantly colonized by things with too many legs.

And rarely, and felt more than seen, there are the large ones. Solitary, low, long-legged predators that the grazers fear as a change in the air. You will find their tracks in the wet clay — three-toed, human-hand-sized — fresher than the rubble, older than the fungal regrowth. When one passes through a roost, the wall-clingers ripple all at once and the fliers go up in one confused thunder and the grazers bolt in a single, dust-raising surge, and for thirty seconds the whole chamber is nothing but noise and motion, and then it settles, not quieter, but differently loud.

It is loud everywhere, almost constantly. The dripping amplified by the vaults. The chitter and scrape of the walls. The hissing of the vents. The splash and scramble in the pools. The low booms of settling stone. The wings. The grazers coughing and snuffling as they chew. It is not a silence you can find by waiting. It is a bazaar, an anthill, a hive at full work, only spread over a landscape.

In the midst of it, scattered small, are signs of something that makes and uses things. Not much of it, and easily lost among the animal abundance. A windbreak, half-collapsed, made of large, flat stones and lashed together with gut cord — no plant fibre here, no wood, cordage is made of sinew and hair. A shallow fire-pit, blackened, its ash long since washed into a rivulet. A flaked stone scraper, still hafted to a bone handle, left on a ledge. A needle of polished bone. Low, domed huts of interwoven shed exoskeletons and haematite, roofed with fungal felt, big enough for a crouching family of whatever lives here now, outlined in a little clearing where the grazers have deliberately kept the mat cropped. The tool-making is hand-tool primitive: stone knapped, bone worked, things mended and remended. And the place reclaims them fast. Leave a camp for a season of storms and burrowers will have run a tunnel under the windbreak; the wall-clingers will be piston-crawling on the hut roof; a new shaft of calcite will be growing through the fire-pit.

The rules here are not stable because the place is not finished becoming itself. 

Water appears, does a little work, and is taken back into the stone.
Stone grows up from drips, and from below, and then fails and falls.
Fungus eats the dead and the mineral stain, and is eaten in its turn by the thousand-footed herds.
The herds are eaten, or outbreed their food and crash, and the walls thin, and then flare again.
The animals do not behave as individuals so much as as currents. A migration of grazers is a river on land. A flight of the shaft birds is a storm cell, complete with wind and shadow and noise. A wall of clingers shedding at once is a landslide that then climbs back up.

If you stand still long enough in a shaft of light, you can watch the turbulence logic play out in miniature: a steam vent opens with a sigh, the warm air rises, the fliers are lifted and spin a little faster, the condensation at the top of the pillar breaks loose and rains down in a fine, brief shower, the rivulet below swells and carries in a leaf — no leaf, a fungal leaf — and a shoal of swimmers turns to follow it, and somewhere to your left a slab cracks, warns, falls, and the dust from its fall settles on the fungal curtain, which will, within the day, have shot new threads across the rubble.

Nothing here is unusual in the way of impossible physics. It is unusual the way a place mid-birth is unusual: the floor too young, the noise too animal, the air too warm and too wet and too full of wings, the absence of green so complete you feel its shape as a hunger your eyes keep trying to fill. It smells of iron and ammonia and wet stone and mushroom.

Walk out — if you can find a sloping gallery that leads up and out to air that is not breathed by the place — and the memory that stays is not of a cave. It is of a country still being made, and being made, largely, by its fauna. They are its weather, its erosion, its builders and its demolition crew. The stone is trying, slowly, to be a landscape. The animals have already taken it over and are running it, turbulently, densely, loudly, while it finishes.
