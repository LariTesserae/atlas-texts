You do not arrive so much as subside.

The lift goes down until the word down loses its meaning, and then keeps going, and the walls close in to a width you could span with a few raised arms, and then open again, and again, until you are in a place that has no top you can see and no bottom you can stand on that is not alive.

It is cool here all the time. Not cold. A cool that gets into your fingertips and stays, that makes breath come out in a small, brief cloud that dries before it can fall. The air is dry to the point of soreness. It tastes of dust, resin, and warm metal at a distance. There is no moisture in it to carry a smell of rain, because there is no rain, and there has never been a river, a puddle, a lake, a spring. Water as a free thing does not exist in this place. The plants do not drink it. They eat rock.

That is the first rule, and everything else follows from it.

This is a rift, not yet finished being a cavern. The ceiling, when you can make it out, is a hundred meters and more above the floor in the high chambers, low and tightening in the necks between them, and it is still moving, very slowly, with a groan you feel in your teeth once, twice in an hour. Fresh faces of basalt shine black where the stone has split in the last decades — raw, unweathered, uncolonised — still sharp enough to cut. A footstep away, that same stone is already furred and held and softened by plants. The geology is embryonic. So is the city.

The light is the second wrong thing, and it is right. It is normal light. Daylight in colour and in strength, the kind that makes green look green and skin look pink and eyes work without strain. But there is no sun. There are no windows to it. The light comes down in broad, slow shafts through translucent root-tubes and light-wells drilled and grown from a surface impossibly far overhead, spread and bounced and diffused by mirror-plant panels and by the pale, layered leaves of the canopy that lives under the ceiling. In the big hollow, midday has a direction and a softness, and shadows fall the way they should. At night the wells dim to a dusk colour, then to a functional dark shot through with work-lamps. The plants keep a fourteen-hour day. Everything else keeps time by them.

The floor is not ground. Or, it is, but only a hand's breadth down. What you stand on is a mat, meters thick in the settled places, centimeters thin in the empty ones, of interwoven rhizome and root and compressed leaf-fall and moss-that-is-not-moss but a dry, velvety, dust-green plant skin. When you step, it gives a fraction and then holds, springy as a packed trail but alive. It breathes, slowly, in and out with the light cycle. It is warm in patches where factory heat has soaked into it, cool in others. It clicks faintly when the beetles move through it. If you kick it, you do not uncover soil. You uncover more root, and under that more root, and under that, eventually, the cold mineral dust and salt the plants are slowly eating their way through.

Vegetation is not in this place the way vegetation is in a forest. There is no substrate that is not plant. The walls of the rift are clad in draping lichen-plants, in curtain vines, in low, tough mats that climb until they meet the light and then stop, turned back by the hot breath of a factory chimney. The buildings are plant. The roads are plant — living causeways cut and re-cut through the mat, banked with woven roots, repaired by seeding them and letting them swell shut. The cables are plant, twined around electric wire. The clothing people wear is plant fibre, the food is plant, the fuel is plant resin distilled to a thick amber oil. Even the machines, which are truly machines — presses, looms, battery stacks, extrusion frames, the electric tram that runs on a vine-wrapped third rail — even they are half-swallowed within a season if the crews let them be. A factory here is not built; it is planted around a metal heart and then trained. Its chimneys are hollow trunks. Its conveyors are living belts that grow their own grip. Its waste is not effluent. It is dry powder, spore, and chips, swept up by vacuum brushes and fed back to the mat.

And in spite of all that life, it is sparse.

The scale is open, regional, larger than any one view. What you think of as a city is not a city in the dense sense. It is a scattering of districts across a valley that is a mile wide in places and runs on beyond sight, connected by those plant causeways and by tramlines that hum a long way between stops. Between the First Hollow and the Pressworks there is perhaps an hour's walk across a salt pan — a flat, pale, cracked expanse of dry mineral where the plant skin is only a film, a few desert rosettes, a scatter of salt-tolerant shrubs with fleshy, oil-filled leaves. You can see the lights of the next district there the whole way, small and steady in the cool air. Each district is a town grown around a light-well or a factory cluster: a handful of streets, multi-storey hollow trees woven into houses and schools and workshops, layers of older plant construction slumped over newer, like a village that has been replanted over itself three times in thirty years. Between them, emptiness that is not empty — just plant in its most elementary form, breathing and waiting, unbuilt.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest part for a place so industrial and so alive. The factories make noise, but the noise is muffled by layers of living material; it arrives as a distant, regular clunk, a pneumatic sigh, the whirr of a loom through a wall of root. There is no drip of water, no rush of wind with anything in it but dust, no birdsong from a sky. Instead there are the small sounds the fauna make, abundant as they are: a continuous, almost subliminal clicking and skittering in the floor mat, the soft thudding of grazers, the papery flutter of the pollinators that go from light-well to light-well in the day cycle. And then long stretches where you hear only your own feet on the mat and the faint tick of cooling metal.

The fauna are everywhere because the plants are everywhere, and because nothing here can afford to waste a body. There are dust-beetles in thousands of kinds, blind and armoured, who tunnel the mat and aerate it and eat the dead bits. There are herd grazers — low, long-backed things with shovel mouths — who clip the open mats in the salt pans in slow, moving herds, cropping and fertilising as they go. There are root-worms the thickness of a wrist that turn over the rhizome. There are stalkers, few, patient predators who have learned the causeways. Above them all, in the warmer, brighter air near the wells, the flutterers carry spores and pollen and, in the factory districts, carry heat and dust from one press to another. Nothing drinks water. They all live on resin, on plant fluids, on the oil that the great leaves exude in the heat of the light cycle and that beads, dry to the touch, on their edges. The animals that die are not buried. They are opened by beetles by night and their minerals are taken back into the mat by morning.

Movement is everywhere, even in the quiet. The place is active in cycles. At dawn of the light, the whole mat seems to inhale — leaves uncurl, pores open, the air changes scent. By midday the factories are in full, mechanised swing: the press-plants chew, the looms weave fibre-board and cloth in closed loops, the resin stills are topped up with shredded root, electric carts carry dry bales along the causeways. In the evening, the grazers settle, the stalkers begin their patrol, and the construction crews come out — for construction here is mostly replanting. A new ward is not assembled. It is seeded. Engineers lay a scaffold of dead wood and wire, plant the house-seeds in rows, and for two months the walls are translucent, tender, humming with growth, before they thicken and harden and can be walked through. Young districts look like that all the time: half-houses, half-plant, scaffolding merging into growing walls, streets that are still root-wide. Old districts slump and are reclaimed in the same way, the mat taking back a factory floor by a centimeter a season.

The weather, such as it is, is a weather of dust and light and air pressure. No rain ever falls. Instead, in the dry season of the light, spores come off the canopy in a slow amber snow that settles on everything and is eaten by the beetles. In the cold season of the cycle, the light-wells dim a little and the whole rift cools a fraction more, and the growth slows, and the factories swell with stored resin. Air currents move down the long valley when a new chamber opens or an old neck is cut through — a sudden, cool draught that carries the smell of fresh basalt and makes the curtain vines shiver.

It is strange, but not in the way of wonders. It is strange in the way of a place that has solved its problems by a completely different chemistry and then kept going. Plants that build factories. Factories that are plants. Animals that farm the floor they walk on. A city that is never finished because it is still being grown, in a cavern that is still being born, under a light that is borrowed from a world it has never seen. You can walk for a day and not reach the end of it, and you can stand in the middle of a district at noon, in perfect daylight, with resin under your boots, beetles clicking under your feet, a press humming through a living wall ten meters away, and feel the cool of the stone through the mat below you — and know it is all, still, emerging.
