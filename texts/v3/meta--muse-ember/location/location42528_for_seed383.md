You do not arrive at the city. You descend into it, and the descent is so gradual you do not notice it until the rim has closed behind you.

For miles the land only tilts, a fraction at a time, toward the middle. The high ground far behind you is not a cliff or a ridge you climbed down — it is a shoulder of old, old stone that has been wearing down for an age without mountains, without rivers to hurry it. What remains is a broad, shallow bowl settled into the surface, perhaps eighty paces deep at its center, its slopes made of the same pale silt and broken slab as the floor. It is near-flattened. Everything here has been flattened, eventually: the hills to dust, the dust to a skin over the rock, the rock to a polish where feet and herds and weather have passed over it again and again.

That is why the light is so hard here. There is no haze to soften it, no leaf-shadow, no green anywhere — not a blade, not a twig. The sky is white-hot and high. It falls straight down and bounces up off the pale floor, up into eyes and under chins and into the undersides of walls, so that even shadow is bright. The builders wear veils not against dust alone, but against this reflected glare. And yet the air is cool.

The stone is cold. Put a palm flat on any slab at noon and it will feel as if it has been kept in a cellar. The basin is a cold trap. Cold rock, cold groundwater not far below, cold air that pools here and does not quite disperse. The sweat of the stone is the local weather.

At dawn, before the glare fully comes, the whole plain sweats. Fine beads gather on the upturned faces of flat rocks, on walls, on the domed shells of the beasts that sleep against them. In shaded seams under a slab, in the undersides of low lintels, moisture gathers thicker, runs in thin threads, finds a crack and drips. It never makes a river. It never pools except where hands have taught it to. By mid-morning most of it is gone, drunk up by sun and by thirsty mouths. Once in a long season, rain comes — a sudden sheet that hisses where it strikes the cold stone, darkens the dust for an hour, fills the shallow pans on the plain to a finger's depth, and vanishes into the ground as if the ground had simply inhaled. Between those events, damp is a habit of pockets: under a stone, in a cistern's mouth, in the folds of a fungal mat where the air cannot move.

There are no plants to drink it. There never were, or if there were, the age of this place has taken them and left only their absence like a color taken out of a painting. The world here is mineral and fungal and animal, and that is enough.

In every crack and damp seam there is a pelt. It grows in grey, in rust, in bone-white, in a dull blue-grey that is almost black when wet. The mat is not moss — it has no leaf, no root to hold. It is a woven skin of filaments that eats stone. It secretes acids that loosen the silt, it binds the dust into a felt, it softens and dies and softens again in a matter of days. When it is wet it is supple and smells faintly of wet rock and old smoke. When it dries it folds into papery sheets that crackle. The builders call it loom. They strip it in broad blankets, twist it into cord, sew it into cloaks that shed the reflected light, and spread the rot-fed leavings on prepared beds of crushed stone to coax new growth where they need it.

The animals live on the loom, and on each other, and on the stone itself, and they are abundant beyond any proportion you would expect from so bare a place.

The rasp-hogs are the first you learn. They are low, jointed, six-legged things with domed shells mottled white and rust, blind under a lip of shell, with a tongue like a file. They move in loose herds across the plain from colony to colony, shoulder to shoulder, utterly silent on padded feet. Where they settle, they work. They rasp at a wall's base, at a fallen slab, at a fungal bed, lowering their heads until the shell is almost a roof. A wall left alone will be eaten at its foot over a season, smoothly, until it slumps. The builders do not fight them so much as farm the relationship: a hog's dung is rich in dissolved stone and fungal matter, and a bed that has been dunged and scraped will bloom thick for a week.

Above them, at dawn and dusk, hang the veil-swarms. They are not birds — nothing here flies with bone — but pale, translucent sheet-creatures that fold and unfold on membranes, drinking the condensation off the air itself wherever a surface sweats. They make no call. A thousand of them can pass overhead and you will feel only a faint moving of air, cooler for the passing, and the faintest tick as they settle again on the warm side of a wall.

And under the slabs, everywhere, are the seam-worms, pink-grey and blind, burrowing the fine silt into tunnels that undermine foundations with a patience no human builder can match. They surface at night in stringy knots to eat the loom disturbed by their digging. The builders' children hunt them with bone hooks for the fireless hearths — there is almost no fire here, only slow, smoky dung burns in sunk pits, a grey thread of smoke — and the worms' castings go back to the beds.

This is the food web: stone to fungus to hog to builder to dung to stone again. Closed, and moving.

And there *is* a city, though you can walk for an hour and see only one wall.

It announces itself not with density but with intention. A single rectangular cell, sunk halfway into the ground, roofed with two massive slabs, its doorway facing the dawn. A low pen of standing stones. A stretch of wall that goes nowhere in particular, then stops. Then emptiness again — a scraped yard, a patch of flat dew-slabs laid out like a field of pale tiles tilted a degree toward a channel, a heap of rubble already being sorted into building stone. Then, far off, another cluster.

District by district the settlement spreads across the whole bowl, town-or-valley sized, more area than any dense city could hold, held together by roads that are not so much built as worn: broad causeways of compacted silt and crushed shell-stone, crossing the emptiness and linking the sparse precincts. Granaries are squat, windowless, thick-walled to keep the cool. Workshops are open-sided shelters where hammerstones tap and scrape without ceasing. Cisterns are the city's heart and its pride — deep, stone-lined pits covered with overlapping slabs to hold the night's damp, with channels cut in the rock feeding them from the dew-fields and from the sweat-lines along the basin's slope. In a dry place, water is not found; it is coaxed, shadow by shadow.

No wood is used. There is none to use. Lintels are stone. Beams are stone. Cord is loom twisted, or hair, or sinew. Tools are hammerstones, flint scrapers, bone awls and needles, a little simple copper for awls when a traveler brings it down from the rim. The builders work with their hands, patiently, in a way that looks primitive until you see how much of it there is.

For urban is the right word, in its layered sense. Almost every standing wall is on top of an older wall. A house thickens by one course of stone each generation. A courtyard fills and is roofed over. A cistern is re-lined inside its own lining. You can read the place in cross-section: the same footprint rebuilt until the mortar — which is only wet silt and dung, packed — has squeezed out, and the stones have been handled so often, by so many hands, that their edges are soft as river pebbles. In the oldest precincts, the walls are no longer clearly walls at all. They have slumped and been re-stacked and eaten by hogs and worn by weather until they are long, low ridges in the plain, indistinguishable from the natural stone waves except that a hog has rasped a groove in them and a loom-mat grows straight and purposeful along their northern face.

This is where the turbulence lives. Nothing about the place is still, though almost nothing about it is loud.

The ground moves — not in earthquakes, but in the small, relentless settling and heaving of a basin floor fat with moisture and hollowed by worms. A slab that was level in spring has tilted by autumn. A wall leans a hand's breadth and is taken down and rebuilt two paces over, using its own stones. The herds migrate in pulses, and where a herd settles, a district is effectively besieged until it moves on. Fungal beds bloom overnight after a rain and are eaten to dust in three days. Cisterns fill, crack, are relined. Rubble heaps shift as builders sort them. There is a constant anthill energy, a business of hands and jaws and feet and membranes, a bazaar of small labors that never completes itself. Creation and destruction are not events here; they are the same act, done at different hours. The city is not ruins. It is a work in progress that has been in progress for longer than anyone can count, and perhaps it has always been so — the flattened plain around it may be nothing more than cities that finished, at last, being built, and were allowed to become ground.

And through all that motion, the silence.

Sound does not carry in this bowl. The open distances swallow it, the soft dust and felted loom absorb it, the beasts are built to move without it. What you hear, close by, is almost nothing: the tick and tap of a hammerstone on soft limestone, the wet scrape of a hog's tongue, the patter of a veil-swarm settling, a single drip falling from a lintel into a cistern and echoing once, smothered. When the stone warms under the noon sun it sometimes cracks with a faint pop, like a knuckle. When the ground settles, there is a sigh of dust. The builders speak in low voices because there is no reason not to, and because a shout would be startlingly, obscenely loud in all this quiet. You become aware of your own breathing, and of theirs, and of the slow breathing of the herds asleep against the walls, throats working.

At evening the glare withdraws a fraction. The cool deepens. The dew-slabs begin their work again, beading. The veil-swarms rise. The hogs lift their heads from the walls they have been eating all day and shuffle, in their silent herds, toward the fresh beds. In the workshops the tapping stops one by one. The builders walk the long roads home between the far-spaced houses, cloaks of woven loom catching the last reflected light, and somewhere a cistern lid is shifted, and a drip sounds, and the basin — ancient, sunken, sweating, rebuilt and eaten and rebuilt again — settles into its night, waiting for its stones to be cool enough to make water out of air, and for morning to begin the work all over.
