It is not obvious, at first, that you are inside anything at all.

You come down by a service stair that was cut, a long time ago, for people who needed to get to the machines below them. The stair is worn in the middle, each step dished and polished, and the handrail is no longer iron-colored but a warm, dark brown where hands and then roots and then animal bodies have burnished it. The air gets warmer as you descend, not stuffy, but steadily warm, as if the rock itself is breathing out a low body heat. When you reach the bottom the stair opens, and you are in a room.

That is the first true thing about it. It is a room, at hamlet scale. A single, high, irregular chamber far below the surface — far enough that the pressures in your ears have changed, that there is no weather from above and no sky to check against. You can walk from one wall to the other in perhaps eight minutes, briskly. You can shout, and hear the shout come back broken from the far side. It has boundaries. Outside this room there is only more rock, more dark, more damp stone. Inside, it has settled into itself for a very long time.

The light is dim. Not darkness — darkness would be simpler, and quieter. Dim as a shade pulled too low. The original ceiling lights are long gone except for a sparse string of industrial lamps, amber and already yellowing, that hang between living beams. They do not make the room bright; they make pools. Between the pools the shadows are deep and green-black. In those shadows, other light moves: dull fungal glows where colonies have established on wet timber, a pale wash from bioluminescent moss on the north wall that brightens when the engines idle, and the occasional green blink of a gauge through algae. Your eyes adjust, then adjust again. You learn to read shapes by their absence against the lamps.

The floor is not stone, although stone is under it. The floor is plant.

That is the second thing, and then the third, and the rest of the place follows from it. Vegetation here is not a covering. It is the substrate. What you stand on is a mat of roots, packed loam, fallen leaf, fungal felt, and flattened rhizome, woven so tightly over centuries of growth that it gives a little underfoot, like a mattress over rock. In the more traveled lanes between structures the mat is worn down to a firm, dark earth, dusted with spores. Off the lanes it is spongy, fern-soft, pocked with hollow basins where water collects and evaporates a finger at a time.

The walls are plant too. Where the cavern rock curves in, roots have laced across it like cables, bearing weight. Where men built, plants have finished the job. The houses — for lack of a better word — are hollow trunks and grown towers, walls of living wood that thicken and thin with the seasons of the machines. Their roofs are overlapping leaves, broad and rubbery and waxy, shingled over and over so that very little of the outer damp ever truly gets in, and very little of the inner warmth ever gets out. Inside a house, if you go in, the walls are green with condensation and the air smells of wet soil and hot oil together. Vines have taken the place of wiring, or rather grown alongside it: thick lianas carry water and sugar where copper once carried current, and they pulse faintly when the engines fire.

Around the outside of the houses the industrial past is still legible, because it never actually left. It was not reclaimed in a dramatic way. It was settled into. This was a plant, a factory floor — presses, a boiler, conveyor runs, a packing shed, storage silos — and the plants grew through it until the distinction between apparatus and architecture became polite. Pipes run along the walls like a second set of vines, rusted to a soft orange, their joints sealed now with root and moss. Fat water-lines cross the ceiling, beaded with condensation. A conveyor still moves, very slowly, carrying a single-file line of hard fruits from a central tree to a press that thumps in a steady, sleepy rhythm. Gauges are fogged. Motors hum under blankets of fern. The whole thing is electrified still, in a desultory way: when the central engine cycles up, lamps brighten, the bioluminescence dims as if embarrassed, and a hundred small things in the walls shiver.

It is warm everywhere, 28, 30 degrees when the boilers are working, a little cooler between cycles. The warmth comes from below — geothermal seep through the floor seams — and from the engines themselves, and from the sheer mass of living, respiring tissue. It is damp, but not wet. That matters. There is no river here, no flood. Water arrives in intermittent, miserly ways: a slow rain of drips from a crack high in the ceiling in the far corner, each drop falling for a second or two every twenty seconds, ringing on a metal tray and pitting into the root mat; condensation that forms on the coldest pipes in the morning of the machine cycle and runs down in bright beads; dew that weeps from the rock walls when the warm air hits them and then is taken up again by leaves. You are never dry, exactly. Your sleeves are always slightly tacky. The leaves overhead never quite dry. But there are pockets, small hollows where a fern-cup holds a spoonful of water for days, where frogs sit half-submerged, where the floor stays darker and softer. The place lives on that thin budget of moisture, and it has learned to count it.

The city — and it is a city, scaled down to hamlet size — is sparse. That is its strangest comfort. Between the clusters of grown buildings there is a lot of nothing. A lane of root-earth will run eighty meters with no door, no window, just the wall of the cavern growing ferns out of its cracks and the occasional abandoned cart, its wheels now planters. A plaza, if you can call it that, is an empty oval of packed mat around the central engine house, kept clear by traffic and heat, where only dust, spores, and condensation move. In a normal town, emptiness would feel like abandonment. Here emptiness feels like respiration — space left between presences so the whole room can breathe warm air through it.

And then, in those empty spaces, the fauna arrives, overwhelmingly.

If the plants are the architecture, the animals are the population, and they are everywhere in numbers that make the sparse buildings feel like an afterthought. They occupy every layer at once. High in the dark near the ceiling, flocking birds that are not quite birds — legless, blind, with paddle wings — nest in the pipework and go off in chattering bursts whenever a lamp flickers. Lizards, warm-bodied and slow, sun themselves in the lamp pools on the roofs and thaw their tails. In the mid-air, in the vines and between leaves, there is a constant, swarming migration of moths and beetles and flies the size of your thumbnail, drawn to the amber lamps, to fermentation, to each other. On the ground, it is louder and stranger. Woodlice the length of a forearm move in slow processions along the damp lanes, lifting their armored backs. Frogs of a dozen pale species sit in the water pockets and croak in overlapping waves, so many that individual calls merge into a thick, wet chord. Geckos dart across the walls. A colony of social rodents — mole-rats with no eyes and powerful teeth — worries at the roots of the central tree, carrying cuttings back to burrows that never quite close. And under all of that, a micro-fauna of mites, springtails, glittering rotifers in the condensation, that makes the leaves themselves seem to tremble.

You hear the place before you see most of it. The sound is loud, 2.5 in the way a living room can be loud when everyone is talking at once and a machine is running in the kitchen. A low drone from the boilers, a thump-thump from the press, the hiss of steam escaping through a root-sealed valve, the tick of cooling metal. Over that, the chorus: drips, croaks, clicks, wingbeats, the scrabble of claws on stone, the squeak of the mole-rats in their tunnels. When the engine cycles up, the whole soundscape rises a key. Plants open — you can almost hear them, a soft creaking as leaves unfold and roots shift in their sockets. Fauna surge. When it cycles down, the bioluminescent moss brightens, the animals settle a little, the drips become distinct again.

The erosion is everywhere, because everything has been here long enough to reach equilibrium. No edge is sharp. The stalactites have been snapped off and polished by animals using them as scratching posts. The brick walls of the oldest shed have bloated and rounded with moisture, their mortar gone, their bricks now held together by rootlets. A steel stair has rusted into a lace of plant-shaped lace. The machines do not look broken so much as retired within their own bodies — settled, weathered, in-balance. Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks ruined. It looks like it has decided to stay exactly as it is, growing and decaying in the same gesture.

That is the rule of the world, if you stay long enough to notice one. Nothing inorganic is allowed to remain exposed, and nothing organic is allowed to waste. The presses crush fruit that the birds have left. The boilers heat water that condenses on the pipes and feeds the ferns. The fungus eats the dead animals and the dead machines' paint. The animals eat the fungus and the plants and each other, in a slow, active rotation that never quite speeds up or stops. Waste heat is the sun. Sound is the weather. The plants grow toward the lamps and the warm vents as surely as other plants grow toward the sky, pale and reaching, their leaves flattened into the shape of whatever reinforcement they had to climb. Some of them have grown into perfect mimicry: a fern that unfurls exactly like a gauge face, a vine that knots itself into a valve handle, moss that spreads in the precise pattern of a wiring diagram on the wall. At first you think you are seeing factories with plants on them. After a while you understand it is the other way around — a plant system that once learned how to use factories, and kept the habit.

There is a strangeness to it that is not decoration. It is structural. Light is rationed, so sight is rationed, so the inhabitants are mostly blind and almost entirely communicative by vibration and smell and song. Water is rationed, so everything is a little thirsty, a little damp, never sated. Warmth is abundant, so bacteria bloom on everything, so fermentation is the chief smell along with soil and hot iron. In the dimness, colors have gone muted and then, in places, hallucinatory: the orange of rust under green algae, the cream of fungal flowers in the unlit corners, the sudden white flare of a moth's wing crossing a lamp. The place feels old not in years but in gestures — the way an old hand repeats the same motion until the motion itself becomes the anatomy.

You can walk its whole extent before your tea cools. You can also get lost in it for days, because small is not simple. A single grown house opens into three rooms and a chimney hollow full of nesting lizards. The back of the engine house is a grove of saplings identical in height, grown from seeds that fell into oil. A corridor between two silos is carpeted so thickly with moss that it bounces. Everywhere the same principles: warm, dim, damp, living, loud, settled.

And when you come back up the stair, the place does not follow you. The warmth fades from your clothes in minutes. But for a little while, everything you touch seems faintly tacky, as if you have been coated in a fine, invisible spore, and you hear, in the quiet of the shaft, the faint thump of the press still going, and a frog, somewhere behind you in the dark, beginning its call again.
