It is small enough that you never lose the edge of it.

Stand anywhere in it and you can see all the way across — a bowl, a dent in a very high place, a room of broken rock roughly a kilometer in each direction. Beyond the rim there is air and far below, a hazy drop to a world you cannot name from here. Inside, the walls close around you in a complete, irregular circle, too fresh to be rounded. This place has not finished being born.

That is the first strangeness, and it governs the rest. The stone is young. It was lifted, or vented, or upthrust very recently on a geological scale, and nothing has had time to soften it. The cliffs are knife-edged. Basalt comes up in black glass shards that cut boots and hands. Pale sinter — white, chalky, hot to the touch — has crusted over vents in plates and ribs. There are no smooth pebbles in the streams, no wide alluvial flats, no deep soil. Where water runs it falls in steps down uncarved ledges. Rain has only just begun to make its first thin gullies, and they look like scratches in plaster. When you put your palm to the rock it smells of iron and sulfur and fresh breakage, as if the mountain were still warm from splitting.

It is high — high enough that your breath comes shorter on the climb of the rim, high enough that the air is thin and carries sound badly — and it is hot. Unreasonably, oppressively hot. The sun is ordinary, a normal white-yellow midday sun with normal hard shadows, light 1.9 out of 3 in any honest measure, but the bowl traps it. Black glass drinks it. The earth below breathes it up. Shade, when you find it, is not cool, only slightly less fierce. By ten in the morning the ground is too hot to sit on. By noon your shirt is damp between your shoulder blades and the air shimmers above the white crusts.

And yet it is wet.

Water is everywhere, but it does not behave as water should in a landscape this steep and this new. It is near the surface everywhere, held up. The ground squelches in places where it looks dry, a sponge over hollow stone. Rocks weep — literally, a slow seep along fracture lines that beads, runs, beads again. Puddles perch on slopes of thirty degrees and do not slide. In the lowest dent, a marsh of perhaps a grove's size spreads warm and shallow over mineral white, steaming faintly even at noon. Water condenses on every cool surface at dawn and drips from leaf-edges and from your own hair until mid-morning. Then it evaporates so fast you can watch the marsh lose a finger's width before lunch, then the afternoon builds its own small weather: a cloud forms only over the bowl, for no reason you can see from outside it, grows dark-bottomed for twenty minutes, and rains warm and straight down, but only inside the rim. The rain hisses on hot stone, does not run off, and is drawn back up as steam. By evening the steam thickens again, and the whole bowl fills with a low, hot fog that has no cold in it.

That cycle — condense, burn off, storm, steam — repeats daily, and under it other things are busy. Vents tick and then sigh open. A new crack will appear overnight, white-rimed, expelling a thin jet you can feel on your cheek from two meters away. Ferns unfurl in audible bursts in the heat. Vines heave in tiny, visible increments during the noon rain. The landscape is active, multiple processes at once, but it is not loud. The thin high air muffles everything. The frogs that ought to croak in the marsh are silent. You hear mostly your own feet on sharp stone, an occasional drip from a weeping wall, the soft tick of cooling mineral, your breathing. The quiet is a physical pressure, like being underwater at altitude.

The vegetation is lush, but it does not cover. It occurs in islands, with a great deal of emptiness between them — sparse, scattered, low saturation of green until you come upon one and it overwhelms.

A single plant can be the size of a door. Leaves the breadth of a table, fern fronds arching over bright black pools, moss so vividly green it looks dyed, orchid-like flowers that open only for the hour of the storm. They do not grow in soil so much as in the wet skin of the rock, roots threading into fresh cracks, feeding, apparently, on steam and dissolved mineral. Between islands: bare sharp stone, white salt, rust-red algal films, patches of ash-pale ground hot enough to blister. You walk stone, stone, stone, then suddenly you are inside a sudden cool green room, dripping, breathing the smell of crushed sap, and then you step out again into white glare and emptiness. The lushness is intense because it is so concentrated and so out of place.

Animals are correspondingly rare. In a full day you may see none at all, only traces: a single pale newt in the warmest pool, nearly translucent, that vanishes when you look directly at it; a line of tiny tracks in wet sinter that end abruptly at a vent; vapor-moths at dusk, slow and blind, blundering into steam. There is no chorus, no birdsong. One species of thermal lizard, if you sit very still in the right island of shade for an hour, might sun itself on a black shard, its skin the exact grey of cooling stone, motionless except for its throat pulsing. That it exists at all in heat and altitude and near-boiling water seems like a decision the place made recently and has not yet revised.

And then, in the emptiness between the green islands, there are the built things. Scattered. Partial. Not many. You could count them on one hand — and that is why they unsettle more than a ruin would.

There is a single shelter, a low half-dome sunk into a terrace on the western side, about the size of a small room. Its material is matte, grey-white, seamless, with no fasteners and no door you can find until you approach, when a section simply becomes less present. Inside, the temperature is a steady, mild 21 degrees, though outside it is over forty and the dome's skin is hot enough to burn. There is no power source, no vent, no cable. It has been there long enough for moss to colonize its base and for a vine to grow across its hairline crack — and the crack healed overnight, the vine still crossing it, as if the material had closed around the living thing without harming it.

Fifty meters away, a line of ankle-high stelae crosses a dry ridge, perhaps seven of them, perhaps a path marker. They are not stone. They are cool to the touch even at noon, bone-cold in hot sun, and water beads on them from air that is not beading on anything else. A flat, mirrored plate the size of a dinner table lies half-buried near the marsh, tilted slightly toward the sky. Pools near it do not drain; water gathers *toward* it from uphill, in a thin, trembling sheet that current physics, as taught down below, does not quite account for. Under the marsh, if you probe with a stick, you find a taut, flexible lattice just under the mineral crust, humming at a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear. It is holding the water up. It is not a dam. It is a skin with rules of its own, maintaining surface tension at a scale where water should have broken and run off the fresh fractures long ago.

That is the level of the technology here: beyond modern, but not grand. No machines roar. No towers. Only a few artifacts, self-repairing, self-cleaning, quietly maintaining microclimates, purifying temperature, coaxing water to linger where it wants it. They are not built in the way a building is built down below. They seem grown, or grown *into* — mineral and lattice and plant root fused at a nanoscale we have no name for. They do not age in the normal way. They do not rust. A piece broken off by a falling shard will, over a few days, smooth itself back into the whole, as if remembering its shape.

The weirdness is not one trick but the accumulation. Shadows fall normally, and yet the shadow of the afternoon cloud appears on the white crust a full minute before the cloud itself condenses overhead. Steam rises and sometimes, at the rim, falls back down as fine, warm rain on the *outside* of the bowl, where no cloud is. Some stones are perpetually cold — those near the stelae — in a landscape that should be uniformly hot. Some pools reflect a sky that is not over them, a slightly deeper blue, as if reflecting a moment an hour earlier. The plants photosynthesize in steam, in near-dark under their own leaves, and unfurl toward heat rather than light. Your footprints in wet sinter fill with water from below before your foot lifts, as if the ground anticipates you.

None of this is violent. The place is not trying to be hostile. It is simply new, and hot, and high, and wet, and unfinished, with a few impossible objects left in it like punctuation. The processes keep moving — vents opening, water climbing, vapor moths dying, ferns heaving — but the overall feeling is of a held breath.

At dusk, if you stay, the fog comes in and the light, still normal, turns honeyed and then grey. The built dome glows faintly from within, though no one is inside and there is no light source to glow with. The marsh steams more heavily, as if exhaling the day's heat. Somewhere out of sight, that one lizard returns to its warm shard. The stelae tick once, softly, like cooling teeth, and are quiet again.

You can walk the entire bowl before dark. You can see, from any point, all the way to the far rim, and beyond it to emptiness. You will leave it, presumably, with damp boots, a sunburned neck despite the altitude, and no clear understanding of whether the water is held up by stone, by heat, or by the lattice under the marsh — only that it is held, and that the place, still young, still sharp, still breathing steam in the ordinary light, is holding itself together in a way that has not yet been described.
