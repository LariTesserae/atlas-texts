You don't arrive at Wickhollow. You descend into it, and the descent doesn't end in a cham-ber so much as a thickening.

The stair is cut - recently cut, by the look of it, the basalt still glassy and sharp under your hand - out of a service shaft that was not a shaft a generation ago. The rock here is young. It has not learned to be smooth yet. Faults are fresh teeth, blast marks are still pale, old tunnels end in raw faces where men are still picking away, and at the far end of the town new fingers of tunnel are being pushed into blackness and abandoned again when the vein of heat runs thin. Above you, somewhere far off, is a surface you will not see for days. Below you the air gets hotter with every turn, not the dry heat of a furnace room but a wet, close heat that condenses on your spectacles and makes your shirt stick in a slow, patient way.

Wickhollow is small. You could walk it, end to end, in twenty unhurried minutes if the streets let you. That is the first strangeness of it: it should feel cramped, and it does, but the crampedness is vertical and layered, so it feels larger than it is. One main cavern, cathedral-big in height if not in length — two hundred paces long, perhaps forty high at the crown — and a half-dozen lesser grottoes opening off it like rooms off a corridor. The boundary is always near. You can hear, or rather *not* hear, the end of it. The rock swallows everything.

And the town has filled the hollow to its skin.

There is no empty wall. No blank stretch of stone left unclaimed. Houses are wedged into the natural scallops of the cavern, then wedged into each other. A brick front of old, sweating brown brick, three stories where the rock allows one, with a corrugated iron lean-to grafted onto its side, with a timber balcony nailed over the lane, with a copper pipe running diagonally across all of it to somewhere higher. Laundry hangs from pipes and drips onto awnings below. Signboards - painted, stencilled, hand-carved - overlap and interrupt each other. Power cables sag in catenaries between chimneys, heavy with condensation. A second street runs on a bridge over a pool, a third on the roofs themselves, where people have built small sheds for sleeping in the warm updraft.

It is a town, not a mine camp. There are districts in the way towns have them: the engine ward at the lower, hotter end; the vat terraces in the middle where the air is green; the kettle-stone houses near the far, cooler grotto where the damp is constant and the poorest live because the stone is wettest there. Market awnings. A clinic with a tiled front. A hall for meetings with a door too heavy to close all the way. Everything is built, and built again over what was built before.

The light is dim and almost entirely made. No daylight ever reaches. Amber lamps burn in pools along the lanes, electric and steadier than flame, but they hum low and their light goes brown at the edges, eaten by haze. In the grottoes between lamps the dark is not black but deep green-black. On damp walls, on the undersides of pipes, on the cool north faces of vats, moss and algae give off a very faint, living glow - not enough to read by, but enough to tell you where the wettest places are. Your eyes adjust slowly, and then slowly again. Shadows have no sharpness here; they are soft, warm, and layered.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. That is the rule of the place, and you learn it by standing still: Wickhollow lives in a closed breath. At the lowest end is the Kettle, a broad, shallow pool of mineral water too hot to touch, ringed with a white crust of precipitated stone. Steam rises from it in a slow, unbroken sheet. It rises to the cool upper rock, to the pipes and roofs and the bare ceiling, and it condenses. It beads. It runs in thin, silver threads down stalactites - some of them no longer than your finger, still growing, still sharp - and down the sides of cold engines, and down the walls behind the houses. It drips, at long intervals, into gutters, into cisterns, into the planters. Then it runs downhill in warm rills along the streets, steaming faintly, and finds its way back toward the Kettle. What is lost is almost nothing.

When it rains on the surface - you know it has rained because a day later, miles of thought away, the fresh cracks in the young rock weep - then new water arrives all at once in a muddy flush. A lane floods ankle-deep with silt. A recently blasted tunnel collapses a little more. The terraces drink. For a week afterwards the ferns are brighter and the algae in the gutters is thicker. Then the loop closes again, and the town goes back to sweating and breathing its own moisture.

Because of that, the vegetation is lush in a way that has nothing to do with forests. Everything that can live on heat, damp, mineral runoff and bad light has come and stayed. The vat terraces are the most deliberate of it: great shallow tanks and stacked troughs where algae mats grow thick as felt, in greens and rust-browns, harvested for food and for the slow industrial processes of the engine ward. Around them, in every gutter and planter and crack in the paving, wild things have rooted: ferns the size of umbrellas unfurling in the drip-lines, their fronds beaded with water; vines that have bridged the gap between two houses, crossing a lane twenty feet up, heavy with leaves; small, twisted trees in courtyard pots whose roots have found the warm pipes and wrapped them like fingers. On the ceiling, gardens hang in stalactite clusters, growing *downward* - away from the lamps, toward the heat below, their roots pale and blind and their leaves turned up toward a light that is too dim to matter. They flower when a machine is shut down for its long rest and the air cools a degree, and no one can quite explain the timing.

The town's work is industrial, but industrial at the pace of the place. At the hot end, the engines breathe. Two huge, slow steam turbines turn on bearings polished by years of use, their flywheels making one ponderous revolution every few seconds, driving the lamps and the pumps and the belts that go off into the workshops. Furnaces glow low - not in violent orange but in a steady, dull red that pulses with a kiln's long cycle. Brick and tile are fired here, and glass, and the mineral crust from the Kettle is ground and re-fired into cement. Conveyor belts carry stone at a walk. Everything is mechanized and heavy, and everything is slow because the rock is hot, the air is thick, and men working in it learn not to hurry. The machinery, for all its scale, is quiet. The rock takes the sound. A piston sighs rather than bangs. A belt hisses. A gear turns with a muffled, oiled clunk that you feel in the floor of your boots more than in your ears. The loudest moments in Wickhollow are the drips: a thread of condensation falling from a high stalactite, striking a tin roof, striking a puddle, each sound arriving a little delayed, spread out across the cavern in no particular rhythm.

People move in that same quiet. They speak low, not from politeness but because heat makes speech expensive and the cavern makes it unnecessary. Children run short, bright bursts and then sit, flushed, in the shade of a doorway. Work shifts are measured not by hours but by the condensation cycle - by how long it takes for the upper rock to bead and run again, by the slow rise and fall of the steam, by the temperature of the Kettle's crust. A clock in the hall is a drip-clock: water filling a copper vessel grain by grain.

Animals are rare enough that a sighting is noted. No birds, obviously. No swarms of insects - the heat and the dry-hot lanes take care of that. In the coolest, wettest grotto at the far end, in pools that stay only damp-warm, there are pale, blind fish, no longer than your hand, that appear in the gutters after a surface rain and vanish again into the cracks. In the mineral crust around the Kettle, blind beetles work the edge of the heat, eating algae, moving slowly, their bodies translucent. And in the walls, in the damp behind the houses, live a few salamanders - grey, slow, ancient-looking - that come out only when a tunnel is newly opened and the rock is still raw. To see one is considered an omen of a new street. Most residents will live in Wickhollow for years without seeing any animal but each other.

That is part of the strangeness, too. The town feels alive everywhere except for the living things that are supposed to be alive. The pipes grow moss like skin. The vats breathe. A wall that was blasted last year is already softened with a green bloom that will, in ten years, be a fern. The stalactites in the engine ward grow faster than elsewhere, fed by the steam, and some of them grow not down but sideways, following a pipe, as if the rock were listening for warmth. The water in the Kettle, on very still rest-cycles, seems to run uphill where it condenses on the underside of a low ledge - a trick of capillary and heat, the old men say, but they watch it for a long time anyway, patiently, as if it might decide to do something else.

Erosion is happening all the time, but at this pace it looks like stillness. The young rock is still settling. A street that was level last year leans a fraction. A fresh cutting throws off a sharp flake that becomes a step, then a planter. The new tunnels are left with their teeth showing, un-blasted smooth, because smoothing would take heat and labor the town does not waste. When you put your palm flat on the wall in the quietest lane, away from the engines, you can feel it: warm, damp, faintly trembling with the slow work of water finding its way through, and the slight, irregular tick of stone cooling and cracking, one sharp note at a time.

At night - and Wickhollow has a night only because the engines rest for an hour each cycle and the lamps drop to half - the amber light dims, the moss glow strengthens a little, the steam thins, and the whole packed town seems to exhale. The hanging gardens turn their leaves a fraction toward the remaining heat. The Kettle ticks as it cools. Somewhere a drip falls, and then another, and no one gets up to investigate. The place has been here a very long time in human terms and an instant in geological ones, and it knows the difference. It will go on filling its small hollow, brick by brick, fern by fern, drip by drip, until the rock and the town are indistinguishable - which, if you watch the walls long enough, they already are.
