It is not a cave.

That is the first thing you understand, getting down. The shaft is not cut into rock. It is built, all the way down, in concrete rings and steel collars, and then it opens, and then it keeps opening, until the air changes and you realize you are not in a tunnel at all but in a valley that has been turned inside out and pushed below the world.

The Low Basin, they call it, on the ration cards and the shift rosters, as if it were low ground instead of a made thing. It has no sky above it. It has a ceiling, very high, and ribbed, and that ceiling sweats.

You descend into cold first. Not cave-cold, which is a steady, mineral cold. This is industrial cold. The ventilation coming down from the surface still carries winter in it, and the Basin has been built so large and so open to the air that the engines cannot warm it. Your breath fogs. Your fingers go numb on iron railings. In the shadow between the lamps, puddles have a skin of ice that cracks when a machine rolls too close, and the frost on the inside of a pipe will melt at noon, drip, and freeze again by night shift without the sun ever being involved.

The light is dim and it never changes. Sodium orange over the terraces, a sick green-white from the long tubes in the service corridors, and between them a darkness that is not darkness but just the absence of reaching. You can stand on one terrace and see the next terrace, and the next, five or six steps down, fading into fog, and you cannot see the far wall at all. It is town-size, valley-size, a whole regional hollow made for holding things, but it is not crowded. The density here is a kind of emptiness. A factory stands alone in a wide yard of wet concrete. A crane stands alone over nothing. A single lit window in a block a hundred meters long. The loudness makes it feel more empty, not less — the sound travels, bounces off the ceiling and the far rings, so you hear the press stamping seven seconds away and the pump sighing forty meters off, and you think there must be people near, and there is no one near.

Everything is made. That is the rule, and it holds even where it looks most natural. The ground under your boots is not earth. It is packed slag and crushed tile and concrete aggregate, graded and tamped and laid in lifts, so level it looks like it was poured, because it was. The "hills" in the middle distance are not hills; they are piles of scrap under a skin of shotcrete, and the "cliffs" between terraces are retaining walls faced in black enamelled steel, and the "river" at the bottom is a reservoir, and the "stalactites" hanging from the ceiling are ice and mineral scale on pipes. There is no substrate that was here before. If you kick the ground, it rings faintly. Water never soaks in. It runs.

And it runs everywhere, because it is wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain from outside. The Basin is a closed weather system. The reservoir at the lowest ring — a vast, black, slow-moving sump that the engineers farm for coolant and the gates farm for nothing — breathes up vapor. The cold ceiling condenses it. The pipes weep where a joint has loosened. The concrete sweats after a machine has passed and left heat, and the sweat finds the cold again and drips. In the center, over the reservoir, it actually rains, a fine, interior rain, falling from the underside of high bridges and ducting, warmish where steam crosses it, cold where the winter air cuts through. You can stand in a dry ring, then step under a pipe bridge and be in a shower that has no cloud. The fog rolls in low banks across the terraces, thicker than breath, and breaks and reforms as the fans cycle. Everything glistens. Even the dry places look recently rinsed.

Because it is new — not old-new, but newly made and still being made — the erosion is young. Nothing has softened. The edges are sharp. Water cuts bright channels through steel grating in weeks, leaving polished, silvered gouges you can see at a glance. A concrete wall that was poured last season still shows the formwork lines, still cures, its surface pale and unweathered. At the perimeter, beyond the last finished ring, the expansion is chewing forward: a raw frontier of fresh pillars, exposed rebar like roots, a front of spoil — that same made earth — waiting to be tamped. A channel that was meant to be straight has already been re-cut by a flood gate, and its banks are a fresh cliff of layered slag, sharp as a quarry face. It feels recently emerged, as if the whole Basin was lifted out of something else yesterday and set down, still dripping.

The industry does not fill it, it scatters across it. In one ring, through open doors, the heat blast of a rolling mill and a half-finished line of presses, idle now, parked like sleeping quadrupeds with their arms folded. In another, a row of vats, lids sealed, steam sighing from pressure valves in a regular rhythm. Conveyor belts move empty, patiently, loop after loop, because the schedule says they should. Overhead cranes cross the dim on rails, carrying a single beam, groaning and electric, the light from their work-lamps swinging across fog. The smell is the smell of wet concrete and hot oil and ozone and, underneath, mildew, and cold metal — an engine trying to start. The sound is constant and layered and loud enough that your teeth know it: the fifty-cycle hum of the grid, the drip-drip in the high vaults amplified by the ceiling, a hiss, a clang, water sloshing against a gate far below you, a distant stamping that lands in your chest before it reaches your ears. Silence here is a machine stopping, and that is brief and startling.

Life is rare and it is adapted, or it is placed. There is almost no green, and what green there is has been put there. Planters along the service corridors hold engineered grasses and hardy ferns, pale from the dim light, tended for air more than for food, sparse and yellowing at the tips where the cold touches them. Where water drips steadily onto dusty concrete, a pale lichen-moss colonizes in soft, felted patches, eating nothing but cement dust and light from a lamp. It is the only thing that looks like it grew on its own, and even it was probably seeded in the mix.

The fauna, if you can call it that, are mostly the workers themselves, few and far between, in heavy coats, crossing a terrace that takes an hour to walk. And the things that follow the workers: in the reservoir, pale fish that never surface, blind and slow, that the outflow pipes keep warm enough to live; in the coolant tanks, eyeless eels that knot themselves around heating elements; a rat, occasionally, in the grease traps; the mold that eats the rubber seals, spreading in black tides. Machines behave more like animals than the animals do — the little maintenance crawlers patrolling a channel, the cranes turning their heads toward a sound. Predation here is not between tooth and claw. It is between water and steel, between frost and a motor, between a schedule and a body that needs to sleep. The Basin eats its own made things: rust starts bright and quick, ice pries a joint, condensation finds a seam. It is early in that eating, so the wounds are clean.

That's the strangeness of it, arriving slowly, after the cold and the wet and the noise have settled into you. This was all built to hold water out, or to hold it in, or to hold heat. It does the opposite, in a cycle. The reservoir makes the rain that makes the reservoir. The heat from the engines makes the steam that makes the fog that makes the ice on the far wall. Nothing natural is allowed to be a foundation, but the made foundation can no longer tell what's made and what has worn into place. The made earth is channeled by its own water. The pipes have grown icicle trees. The lamp tubes have a blush of moss under them. At the edges, new construction is being built out of old construction — crushed concrete from a demolished ring becoming the floor of the next.

There is no season. There is a cycle of gates: gates open, the reservoir rises a hand's breadth, sending a low flood across the lowest ring, then gates close and pumps haul it back up in a roar that shakes the railings. Frost forms, fans clear it, frost forms again. A shift bell rings, muffled by fog, and somewhere an engine warms itself into a cough and then into a steadier, louder shout.

You stand for a while on a terrace, wet on your shoulders from a rain that fell from a bridge, looking down through orange light and green-white light and fog toward the black water that reflects nothing but the lamps. The Basin is active in every layer at once — construction at the rim, production in the middle rings, storage and drowning at the bottom — and empty in every layer at once. The whole thing is sharp, and cold, and echoing, and made, and still, unmistakably, wet.
