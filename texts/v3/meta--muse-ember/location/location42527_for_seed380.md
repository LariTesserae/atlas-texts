You don't arrive over a horizon. You descend.

There is a hedge, a wire fence, a gate giving onto wet stone steps, and then the ground falls away from you faster than a road has any right to. The world above — brighter, drier, ordinary fields — closes behind you, and the air drops a few degrees in your lungs and stays there. What opens below is not a valley. It is a bowl, a worked-out hollow cut into limestone, its benches terraced by old quarrying, its walls weeping in dark vertical bands where groundwater finds the seams. The rim is somewhere above you, indistinct in mist. The town lies wholly beneath it. Even the tallest chimney does not break the line of the edge. Its smoke, such as it is, has nowhere to go.

From the steps you can almost take in the whole of it. Two or three hundred paces across, bounded on every side by rock. A main street curves through the middle like a shallow S, bringing you past a square, a pump house, rows of houses, sheds and factory blocks, and out to the marshy margins where the floor rises again toward the walls. It should feel intimate, and in a way it is: you could walk end to end in ten minutes. But the fog sits low and thick, and in that fog distances deceive, and there is so much empty wet ground between the buildings that the place feels larger and lonelier than its size allows.

The floor is not dry anywhere. It never really has been, not in living memory of the place. Under the fog it has the gloss of a thing permanently just rained on. Puddles are not isolated; they bleed into one another across cracked concrete, across cobbles, across the old tram rails that lie in the street like canal edging. A shallow sheet of water stands in the square, reflecting the lamps and the grey wedge of sky, so still that ducks can land on it and leave no ripple for long seconds. The canals between the warehouses are brown and slow, full to the brim. Seepage threads down the quarry walls in black-green lines and collects at their feet in marsh. Indoors, condensation drips from pipes, from beams, from the undersides of factory roofs, in an irregular, patient percussion. Nothing the water has touched is ever quite dry — not brick, not timber, not paper left in a drawer, not the hem of a coat hung by the door. The cool keeps it from rotting quickly. Everything here is preserved by damp, like a specimen kept in a jar.

That is the settled state of it. This is not a ruin in the dramatic sense. Nothing is dramatically collapsing. The erosion happened a long time ago and has come to rest. The cliff faces have slumped to a stable angle, softened at the edges, furred with moss. Silt has filled the right angles where wall meets floor. Buildings have sunk a hand's breadth or two into the mud and leaned against their neighbours, and lean in equilibrium now, held up as much by each other as by their own foundations. Rust has bloomed and bloomed until it has become a kind of skin, a reddish-brown patina that protects the iron beneath it rather than eating through. Corners are rounded. Details have been taken by water and given back smoothed. The town is weathered not because it is being destroyed, but because it has been there, wet and cool, long enough to find its own shape.

It was built in layers, and you can read them as you walk. The oldest cottages at the low end are stone, their ground floors given up to the water, their windowsills now at the level of the street. Above them stand brick terraces, blackened at the chimney ends, with small yards that have become ponds. Later comes a long, low row of shops and workshops with corrugated roofs, then the great sheds and silos of the works: concrete stained green to the height of a man, iron cladding streaked and salting white, gantry cranes frozen mid-gesture over yards full of crushed stone and slow water. Pipes cross the streets at head height, jointed, patched, sweating. Power lines sag between poles in long catenaries, heavy with wet. The infrastructure of a town is all here — sewers, drains, a substation humming in a fenced yard, the rails, the signs faded to ghosts — but spread out, as if planned for a population three times the size and at three times the confidence. Whole lots between the factories are nothing but shallow marsh, or packed earth where a building was taken down years ago and the ground has simply remembered water.

The works are still, intermittently, works. That is the strangest part of the everyday, and the easiest to miss. Almost nothing is loud. A flywheel in the pump house turns so slowly you could wait a minute for a spoke to cross a chalk mark, and it is turning, you realize, because in the time you waited it has moved. A conveyor creeps along its track carrying one bucket of aggregate an hour toward a silo that may already be full. A furnace in the far shed breathes at a low, banked heat, exhaling not smoke but vapour that immediately condenses and falls back as a fine, warm rain on the roof. This is industry at the speed of silt. It produces, but on a timescale of seasons and years, and no one hurries it. The machines are electrified, motorized, mechanized in every way a factory should be, and they run as if they have all agreed, without speaking, to move at the pace the damp allows. In a week you would notice the level of the square has risen by the width of a fingernail. In a year, the crane has lowered by a finger's length. Lichen advances. A doorway fills with sediment and must be dug out again, slowly, like a mouth.

The light never really arrives. At midday the fog above the rim brightens, and a diffused, colourless light filters down and touches the tops of the chimneys, and that is all the sun this place gets. The rest is a permanent dusk, grey-green and dim, softened further by the mist that the chimneys themselves help to make. The lamps are on. They have, so far as anyone can say, always been on — low amber points along the street, greenish lights in the factory windows, reflected and doubled in every standing puddle — but the voltage is old and the light they give is brown and tired. You can see your breath. Cold water comes up through the soles of your shoes. Metal is cold to the touch even where the sun touches it. Summer, above the rim, must be a different country. Down here the temperature is one long cool exhale.

Against that light, the plants are few and hardy. Algae films the still water in the canals, greening most thickly around the lamps. Moss climbs the north faces of walls like a tide mark, and reedy tufts and pale, stunted willows lean over the marsh edges, their leaves always wet and heavy. Nettles grow in the cracks of the square. There is life, but it is thin, a sparse green punctuation on so much stone and silt, as if the place has decided that vegetation is optional.

The animals have decided otherwise.

Life here is abundant in a way that surprises you precisely because the plants are so reluctant. The canals and the flooded factory floors are full of fish — carp turning slowly in the brown water, eels sliding between the legs of presses and across a flooded office floor in a slick, unhurried procession, small bright perch in the clear seepage at the walls. Frogs and toads are everywhere in the wet margins, and at dusk their calling rises from the ditches and the flooded lots in a chorus that is the loudest conventional sound the town makes, and even that is muffled, as if the fog and the water are absorbing half of it. Swallows and pigeons nest in the chimneys and under the gantry roofs by the hundred, wheeling in the narrow strip of open air and coming to rest on pipes and ledges. Bats hang in the dark of the kiln mouths. Moths crowd the lamps. Spiders string their webs between the pipes, and snails and mussels and tiny barnacles crust the submerged rails, the lower walls, the ironwork of the machines themselves, as if this inland, dim, freshwater place were a harbour. Rats run the drains. Feral cats sit on dry doorsteps, unperturbed. The fauna has not merely tolerated the town; it has adopted it. The factories have become cliffs. The cooling pools have become ponds. The engines, with their sheltered warmth and their dark, humid interiors, have become burrows and nests. A your feet, walking through the flooded shed, you have to step around a shoal of fish crossing the concrete in two centimetres of water, undisturbed by the machinery around them.

That is the rule here, if there is one: water will come to a thing, and life will follow the water, and the machines will keep turning at whatever speed is left to them. Sound travels badly — a shout off the quarry wall comes back softened and damp, as if heard through cloth. Light does not penetrate far. Nothing dries. Nothing hurries. A building given up will be inhabited by swallows within a spring and by frogs within the summer, and by a thin skin of algae within the year after that, all of it proceeding at the same unruffled tempo as the conveyor and the rising square and the weeping walls.

Stand in the square at evening, when the lamps are doubled in the water and the frogs begin, and the rim has disappeared entirely into the mist, and the place comes to its final impression — not dead, not alive in any busy sense, but in balance. A small, sunken, industrial town held in cool, dim, perpetual wetness, its edges settled, its empty spaces full of animals, its machines patient, its rain made locally out of its own breath. It does not look like a place that is waiting to become something else. It looks like a place that has already become what it is, and intends, very slowly, to go on being it.
