It is not a place you enter so much as a place that condenses around you.

The first thing is the light — or the lack of it. There is no true noon here. The sky, such as it is, is a low, continuous ceiling of damp grey and greenish-black, pressed down on a plateau so high that you should be above weather, and instead are inside it entirely. The sun is a rumor somewhere behind that ceiling, diffused until it loses direction. Shadows do not fall. They pool, detached, in the hollows between stones, or smear across cliff faces at odd angles, as if light were being poured from below. And yet it is warm. Not sun-warm. Deep warm. The air on your skin is thirty degrees and rising and falling with a slow, industrial breathing, as if the whole landmass were exhaling through its cracks. The warmth comes up through the soles of your feet, up through the wet basalt, up in invisible columns where the ground is still young.

This is a high country that has not finished happening.

The terrain is sharp in a way that takes time to get used to. Erosion has barely begun its work. Ridges are knives, not hills. Fault scarps stand up in fresh, unsoftened steps, black obsidian still glassy where it was most recently lifted, red ironstone crumbling in your hand like baked blood, pale quartz veins running through everything like wiring in a wall. Canyons are not carved so much as torn — narrow, vertical, still steaming in places, their walls layered in strata that are out of order, ancient bones wearing a skin only hours old. If you stand on one peak you can see the skin of the whole vastness: ridge after ridge after ridge, continent-scale, lifting and dropping for hundreds of kilometers, each range generating its own small season. A storm no larger than your hand at home is, here, a slow-moving system with its own eye, turning above a valley you will never reach, shedding rain that evaporates before it can be seen to fall.

That is the scale of it. You could walk forward for a year and the plateau would not end; it would only repeat itself with variations, weather systems crawling below horizons that curve. Distance does not flatten here. The far mountains are as crisp as the near ones, because the air — thin at this altitude despite its dampness — carries almost no haze. Everything is near and far at once.

Water is a miser here, and a trickster. There are no lakes. There are no proper rivers. The dampness is in the air, in the sweat of stones, in the way every surface beadingly collects moisture and then gives it up again. In the mornings, if mornings can be distinguished from the long dim afternoon, condensation forms in thick wool on the northern faces of rocks, soaking moss forests that cling like wet felt. Occasional rain comes not from clouds so much as from the air deciding, all at once, to become rain for three minutes, then reconsidering. It falls upward sometimes, in sheets low over a ridge, and collects somewhere impossible, in a pocket on the underside of an overhang, in the cupped leaves of plants, in a braided thread of water under a mat of ferns that vanishes into warm gravel a meter further on. The ground is never dry and never wet. It is damp the way a lung is damp. Everything tastes of metal and rain and warm earth.

And in that damp, in that dim, green is obscene in its abundance.

Vegetation does not grow in patches here. It grows in layers, stacked upon itself until saturation. In the sheltered troughs between the young peaks, where water can be caught by condensation, there are forests without trees — vast mats of fern, of broad-leaved things with leaves like wet black paper, of hanging moss that falls in curtains from every ledge for thirty meters and then, where it ends, another kind begins. Roots interlace into a single continuous fabric you can walk on, springy and humming faintly with contained moisture. The plants are dark, a deep bottled green, to drink what little light there is. They open at night as much as day. Some feed on the dim light. Many feed on the heat. Their flowers, when they have them, are not colored for pollinators to see, but for the warm updrafts to carry their spores upward into the engines of the sky.

Because the world *is* industrial, in a way that has nothing to do with builders.

There are no buildings. No roads, no ruins, no artifacts of any kind — not because nothing was ever made, but because anything made would be eaten. The technology of this place is not constructed. It is grown, secreted, and inherited by the geology itself.

Along the great thermal seams that run the length of the plateau there are piston-fields: columns of vesicular basalt that rise and sink in a slow, hot rhythm, a kilometer of rock breathing in and out by centimeters, pumping cold air up and warm air down, lifting moisture to where it condenses on the high ridges. These are boilers. Further out, where the wind from the updrafts is constant, there are stands of crystal-turbine plants — each one a lattice of iron-moss and quartz spar — that turn in the warm currents, not to generate electricity for a grid, but to grind. Their turning edges rasp against cliff faces, day and night in soundless motion, and the rock dust they produce falls as fertile soil on the fern-mats below. Conveyor-lichen crawls across whole mountain flanks in bands, moving nutrients at a stately, mechanized pace, depositing them in fan-shaped deltas where the bands meet. Fungal mills unzip fallen plant matter into sugars, into acids, into more lichen, at a scaled, relentless rate that keeps the entire green saturation fed.

It is a factory that produces only land. Soil, slope, new ridge, new valley. That is its product, and it is produced in excess.

The fauna are part of the production line, and they are everywhere. Abundant is a weak word. The air at dusk — if dusk is a dimming of the dim — is thick with blind, translucent swarmers that filter the damp air for spores and metal ions, hanging in curtains so dense they blot what little light remains. On the fern-mats, herds of low, many-legged grazers move in near-touching ranks, their bodies already half-mineral, their teeth millstones, their stomachs chambers of grinding rock and acid and stone-eating bacteria. One species does nothing but walk, ploddingly, across the industrial bands, its hooves tamping soil into place as it goes. Another, vast as a house, translucent and slow, drifts on the updrafts and rains down a fine, rust-red dust that is both waste and seed. Predators exist, but they are connectors on the same line: they take an animal whose mill is worn, dissolve it, and return its minerals to the updrafts in their own bodies. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is built. Everything is recycled into motion.

And for all this — the herds, the mills, the pistons turning in the rock, the whole turbulent, crowded, near-touching density of life layered upon life — it is almost completely silent.

The thin, saturated air swallows sound. The fern-mats and moss-curtains absorb what is made. The industrial motions are felt more than heard, a vibration in the teeth and in the soles of your feet when you stand on the piston-fields, a low pressure that makes your ears pop and unpop on a plateau that should not change elevation. Occasionally you hear the drip of condensation, a single tick of cooling crystal, the soft boom of a valley deciding to become a mountain somewhere far away, arriving long after the dust of it. But the silence is not empty. It is dense. It is the silence of a system running at full capacity in a vacuum-sealed room.

Reality, here, is not so much broken as overworked. It tears where the production is fastest.

Shadows fall upward on the west faces at midday. A peak will cast its shadow onto itself before the sun — the distant, diffused sun — has risen. Tracks in the damp soil are sometimes older than the feet that made them ; you find the print, fully formed, in mud that has not yet been rained on. Rivers — the thin braided threads — flow uphill for a stretch, gather in an air pocket, and vanish. The strata in a fresh cliff can show a layer of fern-fossils above a layer of basalt that, by any sane geology, must be a million years younger than the plants it contains. Time does not flow in a line here. It loops around the thermal seams, pools in the valleys, and is carried up on the updrafts to be re-deposited as soil. Age is local. A stone can be both newborn and ancient because the land constantly makes itself new over the top of its own old bones.

That is the turbulence, and it never settles. This is not a landscape in a storm. It is a landscape that *is* storm-state, Solaris-like, in its endless cycle of creation and destruction. On a scale of hours, a fern-forest can grow to maturity, collapse under its own lush weight, and be ground by the mill-animals into soil that lifts into a new terrace. On a scale of days, a fault can sigh upward a hand's breadth, sending a slow avalanche of fresh rock down a slope that will be green within the week. Weather systems are born over a ridge, grow continent-wide, tear themselves apart, and in tearing apart seed the condensation that feeds the next forest. There is no climax, no equilibrium. The anthill-energy is constant, but it is not frantic. It is the frantic energy of a factory at the moment of a total, perpetual meltdown — building and breaking the same things, faster and faster, without ever stopping to rest.

You learn the rules by living in it for a little while, and they are not rules so much as habits.

Nothing built will survive, because the industry will take it as feedstock. So nothing is built. The land remains untouched in the human sense and utterly worked in every other sense.

Water will not be where you last saw it. It will be where it is needed, which is to say, where the heat is rising. Follow the warmth and you will find damp.

Sound will disappoint you. Silence will not. If you listen with your body, you will hear the plateau working.

And light — dim as it is — is enough. It is not the main energy here. The main energy is heat from below and motion from within, and the chemical fire of things eating stone. The plants are green not because of the sun, but because the whole world is one vast, warm, damp metabolism, and green is the color of things that are fed.

Stand on the highest young ridge, where the basalt is still warm enough to make the air shimmer, and look out. Below you, the fern-mats are a sea, the swarmers are a weather system, the piston-fields are rising and falling, the crystal turbines turn in silence, a ridge to the north is in the process of becoming something else entirely, and the dim sky presses down, warm and wet, on all of it. Behind you, the mountain you just climbed has already softened its fresh edges with moss. Ahead of you, a valley is being unmade by the same hands that made it.

It does not want to be understood. It wants to be continued.
