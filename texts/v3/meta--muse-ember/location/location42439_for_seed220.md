**Marrowhold**

You hear it long before you see it, and by the time the lift has finished its slow pressure-change descent — not really going down so much as *submerging* — you realize the sound was never coming from people.

Marrowhold sits one-point-eight kilometres below any sky there is. Not in a mine shaft, not in a tunnel built by hands, but in a single vast hollow in the rock, a chamber roughly six hundred metres across and tall enough that the ceiling disappears into darkness above the towers. The floor is fractured basalt, worn flat by a city. The walls curve in like the inside of a ribcage, limestone and ironstone rising in smooth, water-polished arcs. That is the whole of it. The whole city. You could walk from one boundary gate to the other in twenty minutes if the avenues weren't so absurdly wide.

That is the first contradiction the place teaches you: it is built like a metropolis, but at hamlet scale, and it is mostly empty.

Towers rise in layered ranks — some of them thirty, forty storeys — but they are set far apart, with plazas between them large enough for a flock of birds that don't exist. Avenues are thirty metres wide and carry no traffic except the occasional freight pod humming on its pressure-cushion a metre above the street. Apartment blocks stand dark, facades scaled in ceramic tiles like overturned pinecones, ten windows lit in amber to a hundred that are blind. Inside those dark towers are often no rooms at all, just service voids where heat-exchange fields breathe slowly in and out. The city was built, or grown, for a population that never arrived. Those who live here now — eight hundred, maybe a thousand, permanently — simply rattle around in the infrastructure.

And the infrastructure never sleeps, and it is loud. A low, house-deep roar from the geothermal pumps under the main bowl. A constant whine from the air recyclers, like cicadas made of metal, rising and falling with the ventilation cycles. Water-hammers in the copper-green pipes overhead. The freight ring sighing past every seven minutes on the elevated curve, a whoosh of displaced air that rattles market awnings and sets dust hanging in layers. Drips. Everywhere, drips. In Marrowhold the sound of human voices, when you encounter them, is almost an interruption — soft, echoing, swallowed by those longer mechanical echoes bouncing wall to wall to wall.

The warmth is the second thing you learn. It is not hot air blowing in; it is the rock itself, and the city itself, exhaling. Thirty-one degrees in the main streets, humid enough that your shirt sticks within minutes and the sweat on stone never quite dries. The builders — who were not the people who live here now — laid heat pipes in veins through the walls, and the walls themselves are part of the system. Touch a facade and it is warm as skin. Some of them, if you press your palm flat and stay still, will glow very faintly under your hand and then fade, a slow phosphorescence in the sintered stone. They are not painted. They are annealed in place, field by field, by constructor rigs that still crawl facades at night like pale spiders, sealing hairline cracks with a bead of white-hot ceramic that cools to the colour of wet bone. The residents don't understand the physics. They just keep out of the rigs' way and sweep up the glassy spatter.

That is the technology of the place: beyond modern, and older than the town. Nothing here was made by current science. The lifts don't travel on cables; they ride a pressure nudge and your ears pop in a different way, and sometimes in the deep shaft the dust hangs for a full minute because gravity is a fraction thinner there. Doors open before you touch them. Lights, where they exist, are not lamps so much as patches of wall that brighten when someone is near, amber and economical, and then dim to nothing. Transit pods navigate without drivers. The big central vault — a domed cistern as large as a cathedral nave, dominating the middle of town — generates its own weather. At the turn of each condenser cycle, cold air is pushed across warm moist air and it *rains*, indoors, a fine, warm, mineral rain that falls for eleven minutes and beads on every surface. No one outside can explain it fully. Inside, they just open buckets.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. At 0.9, as some old surveyor might have put it if they still surveyed. There are no rivers down here. There are pockets: a chain of still cisterns in the transit troughs, knee-deep puddles in the sunken service streets that reflect the amber windows perfectly, condensation streaking down the north wall in slow, dark tracks that never dry. The air itself is damp. Stone sweats. Metal blooms with rust in soft, established spots. You learn to drink only from the filtered taps, because the cistern water, for all its clarity, is warm and slightly sweet and full of pipe-mites.

There is almost no light. Darkness is the default state, not an absence. 0.3, if you want a number. There are no windows to the sky, because there is no sky. The windows that do exist look out onto other towers, other dark voids, the curve of the cavern wall. People keep their amber rectangles shuttered if they can afford to lose the glow. The permanent night is broken only by task-lights over market stalls, by the green-white pulse of the constructor rigs, by the slow parade of freight pods, and by bioluminescence that is not natural at all — the fungal curtains.

Those curtains are the closest thing Marrowhold has to wilderness, and they explain the sparse green. Vegetation here would die in a day under a real sun, which it will never see. Instead, in the flooded atrium of the Old Exchange, there are pale, palm-like plants grown hydroponically under discs of artificial light, their leaves translucent and slow. In the cracks between basalt plates, in the seams where newer houses have been bolted into older arches, a sparse felt of moss and iron-lichen creeps — eaten, in turn, by the city. The lichen isn't decoration. It scrubs the air, eats the damp, and makes a faint, musty perfume that mixes with ozone and warm stone. It is everywhere you look closely and nowhere you could call a forest. A handful of gardens, tended obsessively. Everything else is stone that has learned to be alive enough.

Fauna is rarer still. You may live here a month and see none. At dusk-cycle, when the lights dim for the simulated night, blind flitters — animals the size of bats, with no eyes and wings like wet paper — drift down from the ceiling voids and hunt the swarms of repair-mites, the silver dust that moves in coherent spirals along the pipes doing maintenance no human sees. In the cisterns there are translucent, eel-ish things that live in the warm outflow and are considered good luck, mostly because they indicate the filters are working. In the ventilation ducts, something with too many legs makes a scratching sound behind the walls at three in the morning. The residents leave out bowls of mineral paste for it. That is the whole ecology: a closed loop of machines, fungi, mites, and the rare creature that has learned to live off all three. Nothing is wild. Everything is adapted.

This is also why erosion here looks different from erosion above. Marrowhold is not crumbling. At 2.4, established, settled-in-its-own-state, it has had time — tens of thousands of years by the strata counts — to come to a balance with the rock. Sharp edges have been polished away by humidity and by feet and by pods. Streets have sunk a finger's width where they settled. Stalagmites have grown *onto* statues, onto abandoned kiosks, onto the rims of the big bowl, in perfect mineral lace. Mortar blooms white. Ceramic tiles have gone the colour of old teeth. But nothing collapses. The cavern is stable. The buildings are stable. The city has exhaled into its final form and now simply maintains it, forever, with its humming, self-repairing skin.

There are rules that follow from all this, though no one wrote them down.

The air is recycled, so it smells of last week's rain. Time is not measured by sun but by light-cycle and condenser-rain; when the rain starts, you know it's time to close the stalls. Sound carries weirdly in the hollow — a shout in an empty avenue will arrive back at you a half-second later, layered over itself, so people speak quietly even in a loud city, because the city is loud for them. Once a week the heat exchangers purge, and a warm wind rushes through the lower streets carrying spores, and everyone with a cough stays in. You cannot see the surface from anywhere here, and the people born Downstairs feel a vague, physical discomfort after a few days above, in real sunlight and unrecycled air, as if their lungs have forgotten a larger volume.

It is a small place, a cathedral, a hamlet, a single bounded feature in the deep rock, and yet the empty avenues, the dark towers, the rain that falls where no cloud could be, make it feel enormous by volume. The density is a near-void: vast space between presences. You can stand in the central plaza at night and hear the pumps, the drips, the freight ring coming, and see perhaps four other lit windows in the whole skyline. It is урban in every way that matters — pipes, wires, layered construction, markets, zones, infrastructure-density piled on infrastructure — and empty in every way that matters too. A city built first, and people second, and nature a distant, sparse afterthought that learned to grow in the cracks.

That is Marrowhold. Warm, dark, damp, loud, hollow, old, and still, slowly, actively, breathing.
