You go down, and the down does not end in a single step or a single door. It ends in wetness.

The stair is cut — not worn. That is the first thing. The walls of the passage are fresh fracture planes, black and glittering, still sharp enough to catch skin. No old smoothing, no dust laid down over years. White silt has settled only in the corners, a thin first skin. Stalactites overhead are needles, a few centimeters long, still deciding whether to become anything. This hollow has recently emerged, geologically speaking. It has been opened, or it has opened itself, and it has not yet learned to be old.

It is warm as soon as you are below the throat of the entrance. Not outside warm. A heavy, enclosed warm that sits on your lungs. The air is already saturated. Droplets cling to your forearms and bead and run, not because it is raining here yet, but because the atmosphere is condensing on everything cooler than itself — on rock, on metal, on you. The stone weeps in a way that is less like a spring and more like a sponge that has been squeezed from below. Water finds the surface everywhere, uniformly, and gives up.

Then the passage widens, and the sound arrives before the place does.

It is loud, and it is almost all water. Not a river, exactly. A chorus. Drips from the ceiling a hundred feet above in irregular, echoing ranks, each one amplified by the hard, wet bowl of the place. A low, continuous rush somewhere to the left, as if a waterfall is being poured through a throat and cut off. Pumps changing pitch. The faint tick and pop of things growing. It reverberates so much in the hollow that you cannot tell if a sound is near or is only arriving late from far away. In such a space your own breathing comes back to you.

The hollow itself is small. That is the strange comfort of it. From the center you can see the bounds. It is a single feature, a cathedral dropped underground: perhaps four hundred meters long, a little more than a hundred across, its vaulted roof lost in dim mist. There is no horizon. Whatever direction you look, the wall is there, close enough to touch in an hour's walk, far enough that the opposite side is always a little ambiguous. There is nothing beyond this. It is a whole world with edges.

And inside it, squeezed — and not squeezed — is a city.

It is urban in the way a forest is urban: layered, organized, infrastructural, without ever being crowded. The built things cling to the margins. Against the east and west walls, tier upon tier have been raised: habitation shells, reservoirs, exchange towers, transit arteries, catwalks, pipes within pipes. Lower levels sit in shallow marsh-water, their feet in black reflective pools, reeds and ferns growing up through their service joints. Middle levels are hung from impossibly thin lattice masts that do not seem thick enough to hold them in any ordinary stress. Upper levels are narrow galleries, lit sparsely, where cables run like vines from one module to the next and vines run back.

Between these clusters the volume is almost entirely empty. That is the second strangeness. You stand on a wet platform and look across and there is just — space. Falling mist. A long, slow sheet of water coming down from the ceiling, breaking into rain halfway. The far wall, dim. The faint green-amber of a corridor light, alone. The city is dense with construction and sparse with presence. Streets are canals. Plazas are basins. Many modules are dark and shuttered, their doors sealed with a clear, beaded film. The infrastructure hums and works and maintains itself richly, a city built far in excess of anyone needing to live in it.

Closer, the building is not built the way buildings are built above. It is precipitated.

You can see a new edge forming on the end of a gallery: a straight, razor segment of white-grey ceramic, still damp, its surface beading as mineral leaves the water that is being passed through it. The field that shapes it is invisible — a slight shimmer in the air where gravity feels a fraction off, where water mist hangs a moment too long before falling. Buildings here grow by drinking their own rain. Water is drawn from the condensing air, from the pools, from the weeping rock, run through a lattice of controlled fields that lay down silica and iron and polymer in atom-precise order, and left as structure. When a tower needs to extend, it seeds itself and the rain does the work. Everything therefore looks new in the same way the cave looks new. No facade has faded. No edge has been softened by weather that is not already, deliberately, its own.

Thin things hold great weight because they are not only material. Some beams are hollow and full of pressurized mist, balanced. Some platforms float a centimeter above their mounts, held in a quiet, continuous correction you feel more than hear — a subsonic pressure in your teeth. It is engineering that has stopped pretending to respect the usual limits. The city does not fight the wet and the heat and the dark. It metabolizes them.

Temperature lives in the floor. Under the black, glossy slabs, under the canals, the rock gives up geothermal heat, and the machines add their own, until the whole hollow maintains a thick, tropical warmth that never quite gets comfortable. Fog comes and goes in tides. At bottom it is a shallow sea, ankle-deep, obscuring the marsh level entirely, smelling of wet stone and warm soil and a faint ozone from the fields. Then a pressure change — a slow sigh from the pumps — and the fog lifts, climbs the walls, and gathers on the ceiling until it is heavy enough to fall again in patches. Weather, entire, in a room. You can watch a rain cell form over the west tiers, drift east, dissolve. The plants drink it. The buildings drink it. The pools rise a fraction, and the canals reroute themselves around new shallows with a soft, arguing rush.

The green is not decoration. It is part of the layering. Vegetation is lush here in the specific way of a place with no animals to eat it. Ferns have colonized the vents. Algae has turned the lower canals a deep, translucent green, clean and lightless. Moss coats the undersides of catwalks. Vines have threaded themselves through transit tubes and out the other side, flowering in the dim with small, pallid blooms that open and close with the fog cycle. Trees — real trees, with trunks as thick as masts — grow up through the hollow center of a reservoir tower, their canopies catching what light there is. Roots are infrastructure too; they stabilize fresh sediment, they lace the living ceramic, they filter the air.

Light is scarce and deliberate. A 0.7 kind of dim: what filters down from panels set into the ceiling like distant, cloudy stars, what glows from within water-filled tubes along the corridors, what catches in the wet surfaces and doubles itself. It is amber in the lower city, green in the gardens, and almost nonexistent in the high vault where the needles of rock wait. Shadows here behave unreliably in the dimmest zones; they lag a fraction behind the thing that casts them, as if the air is a little too thick to let go. Plants lean, too, toward the loudest sources — toward the pumps, toward the falling water — as if they are listening and growing toward sound in the absence of sun. On certain walls, when the fog thickens, water runs upward, a thin film climbing glass-smooth ceramic against the pull, for a minute, then gives up and falls.

It is noisy with process. The growth pops like warm ice cracking. Pipes knock. Somewhere a valve opens and a whole district sighs with steam. Rain ticks for a long time on metal roofs that do not need roofs. It is active in the way a body is active: breathing, condensing, precipitating, photosynthesizing, circulating. Nothing is still. A new gallery edge is a centimeter longer than an hour ago. A canal has changed its mind. A garden has closed its flowers against the rising mist.

But there is no animal life at all.

That absence becomes more and more insistent the longer you are here. The pools, though they look perfect for fish, are empty of fish. The light panels, though they are warm, are empty of insects. No bird crosses the vault. No scurry crosses the catwalk. No voice, no footprint in the fresh silt except yours, and even those fill slowly with water and seal over. Perhaps animals cannot tolerate the fields that hold the city up — the staggered gravity, the pressurized mist, the chemistry of the rain that builds. Perhaps they were never seeded here. Whatever the reason, the ecology has simplified to plant, fungus, mineral, machine, and water, cycling endlessly among themselves, and the plants thrive with a terrible, unchecked abundance because there is nothing to prune them, nothing to eat them, nothing to carry their seeds except the wind the machines make.

The effect is a place that feels inhabited and uninhabited at once. A hamlet that could hold thousands and holds almost no one. A city that is working frantically — precipitating, pumping, condensing, growing — not for residents, but for the maintenance of its own possibility. Its people, if they ever come, will find the streets already laid with canals, the towers already half-grown toward them, the gardens already waiting, leaning into the noise, drinking the rain that falls upward and then down.

You stand for a while on one of the middle platforms, where the air is warmest and the dripping is loudest. Beneath you the marsh level disappears into fog. Across the void, a greenhouse tower glows faintly green, its vines swaying though there is no wind except the one the city makes for itself. Above you the ceiling disappears into fog. Behind you the wall is close and freshly cut and weeping. In front of you is everything there is: small, bounded, wet, dim, loud, new, and growing — a whole subterranean town learning, in real time, how to exist.
