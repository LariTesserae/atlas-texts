The light arrives first, before the cold. It comes in white, without haze or softening, as if the air itself were too thin to filter it. At this height the sun is not a disc in the sky but a presence on the ground — it strikes a pale slab and throws it back so hard the eyes close. Shadows are not grey. They are blue-black, knife-edged, and absolute. A stone in full sun can be hot to the palm while the air two feet away in shade is still below freezing, and frost persists at noon in every crack that turns its back to the sun.

You are at the top. Above you, only more sky, an intense, darkening blue at the zenith that fades paler toward the horizon. Below you, a sea of cloud and the tops of other peaks like islands, far smaller and softer than they should be. The ascent has taken the softness out of everything. Up here the world is still young.

This is not a broad territory. It is one place: a bowl, a hollow in the summit, perhaps three hundred metres from rim to rim, hamlet-size, cathedral-size if you count only its floor. You can stand in its centre and see the whole boundary at once. The rim is a broken ring of teeth — fresh, angular rock that has not yet had time to be rounded by weather. It lifts in sharp schist blades and black basalt blocks, in newly exposed granite still pale where a rockfall has sheared it clean a season or two ago. No lichen has colonized that scar yet. The scree at its foot is shards, not pebbles. At night frost wedges in and splits off new edges, and you can hear, very faintly, the tick and pop of stone breaking by cold. Erosion here is recent work; the mountain has only just risen and is still settling into shape.

Between presences, there is almost nothing. That is the first surprise of the interior. Walk from one green patch to the next and you cross twenty, thirty paces of bare stone — clean faces, loose grit, thin dust blown into ripples — hollow and empty as a held breath. The density of things is low. The volume is mostly sky and rock and air. And yet, once you learn where to look, life is not scarce at all. It is concentrated, and it is small, and it is fast.

Water does not run here. There is no stream, no lake, no spring. What water there is arrives as dampness from the air. At dawn every underside of an overhang, every night-facing surface, is beaded. Condensation gathers on cold rock through the night and drips, very slowly, into moss. A late squall may boil over the rim in the afternoon — a sudden rain of ice grains that hiss where they strike dark stone and vanish, darkening the rock for a minute before the sun takes it back. The soil stays cool and faintly peaty under the cushions, never dry, never wet. Drinkable water is a pocket, a sponge, a film on a leaf-hair at first light. Animals drink dew. Plants drink dew. The hollow lives on moisture it inhales at night and spends by day.

Where that damp and that soil collect — in the lee of the rim, in old windbreaks, in cracks where grit has settled — the vegetation is startlingly lush. Not tall. Nothing here has the luxury of height against the wind. But in those pockets the growth is dense, thick, and in high summer almost urgently green. Cushion plants pack themselves into domes as tight as upholstery, grasses weave a turf that springs underfoot, moss forms velvet troughs, and for a few short weeks the whole bowl is speckled with flowers: yellow composites, deep purple spikes, white stars no bigger than a thumbnail, opening at sunrise and closing when the shadow reaches them. Lichens add their own colours — sulphur, rust, pale green — on the sunward faces of stone only. The lushness is localised and therefore fierce. Step off a cushion and you are back on stone. Step onto one and the air smells, suddenly and coldly, of wet earth and crushed herbs.

That patchiness is the rule of the world. Heat comes not from the air but from the sun on dark stone, so life follows light. On a warm rock slab at midday you can put your hand down and feel a crowded micro-world stir: mites, tiny spiders, flies that live their whole lives within a metre of that slab, beetles the colour of wet basalt, all moving in the thin, silent air. Down the slope in the turf, pika-like mammals dart between burrow mouths under the cushions, emerging in bursts, sitting upright to take the sun, disappearing the instant a falcon’s shadow crosses the bowl. Snow finches nest in the rim and hive in the air above the flowers, their calls thin and bell-like and immediately swallowed by the altitude. There are more animals here than the emptiness suggests — not in numbers you can count from the centre, but in constant small motions everywhere a pocket of warmth and damp exists. Predator and prey are both present and both busy, because the warm window is so brief. Everything must feed, mate, and hide between the morning frost and the evening freeze.

Human presence is scattered and primitive, in keeping with the place. No settlement could hold here. There are faint marks instead: a half-collapsed windbreak of hand-laid stone, shoulders high, its roof long gone, once sheltering a fire-pit blackened only lightly. A cairn on the highest tooth of the rim, built stone by stone without mortar. Single flakes of knapped obsidian left where someone sat to work them, a bone needle, a twist of woven grass rope snagged on a rock and frayed to fibres. A few low stone fences that run a little way across the turf and then stop, as if the builder lost interest or the seasons reclaimed them. Nothing here was built to last, and the mountain is already taking it back, shard by shard.

Sound is the other governing oddity. For all this activity, it is quiet. The air is too thin and too cold to carry much. Footsteps crunch on frost-shattered grit and then stop. Your own breathing is loud in your ears. Wind, when it comes over the rim, does not roar — it whistles only in the narrowest crack, or combs the turf in a brief, combing sigh, or drops suddenly into the bowl and fills the ears with pressure and then is gone. Insects make no hum you can hear unless you put your head to the warm stone. Birds call, infrequently, across long intervals of silence that can last minutes. Even the afternoon squall is mostly visual: cloud spilling over the teeth, light going white, shadow racing, and then the hiss of ice grains. The silence feels not empty but held, as if the place is listening.

And yet nothing here is still. The dynamic is constant. In a single day the bowl passes through seasons: dawn frost on every cushion and a rim of rime on the windward stones; a bright, hard midday when flowers open, insects swarm the sun-warmed slabs, and mammals are out in force; a sudden afternoon build-up when cloud wraps the rim and snow grains fall in a driven veil, melting where the rock is dark, chilling the turf to steam; a clearing in which the light returns even brighter because it is diffused and reflected; then evening, when shadows lengthen blue and the temperature falls off a cliff and moisture begins again to condense out of the air onto cooling stone. Plants open and close. Animals emerge and vanish. Ice needles grow in soil cracks during the night, pushed up by invisible water, and melt by ten o’clock.

The strangeness is low and quiet, the kind you notice only after you have been here a while. In the dampest hollows, under a slab, a small garden of moss stays green all winter by feeding solely on condensation, and thin stalagmites of clear ice grow upward season after season though no water ever visibly drips. Some cushion plants are furred with hairs that catch the light and look silvered from one angle, though the sun is behind them. The obsidian flakes, left in the sun, give off the faintest warmth long after the stone around them has cooled. From the rim at a certain hour, the far cloud sea below reflects a glare so pure that the bowl itself seems to float in white.

At night, when the light withdraws, you understand the boundaries. The rim is absolute; beyond it is a drop to cloud and cold. Inside there is a small, fierce, bright, cold world that fits within sight, where rock is still sharp, where water is mostly air, where green is sudden and animal life is abundant in miniature, where human things are reduced to a few stones and flakes, and where silence is the loudest sound of all.
