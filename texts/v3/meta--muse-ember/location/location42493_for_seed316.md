First, there is no horizon.

Not a line where land stops, but a thinning. The plain goes on until the dim light cannot carry it any further, and then it seems to continue anyway, softer, whiter, folded back on itself in the mist. It is low — lower than any place has a right to be while still being land — a basin so wide and so shallow that you cannot find its rim. Stand here and you are in a weather system. Clouds are not passing over the plain; they are being manufactured inside it, born in one quarter, circling, dying in another, leaving their rain behind in long, slow sheets that never quite touch the ground, or touch it everywhere at once.

The light never clarifies. It is a warm, pearl kind of gloom, as if the sun were on the other side of a wet blanket, or under water. No hard shadows. Edges dissolve at fifty paces. Colors come back muted and slightly wrong: stone the color of wet ash, mud the color of weak tea, the occasional algae slick that throws a brief, iridescent green-brown before the light swallows it again. There is no dawn to speak of, no dusk. The brightness shifts, sometimes thickening to a yellow-grey, sometimes thinning to something almost blue, but it never arrives. Reflections from the ground do as much work as the sky. On a plain this flat and this wet, the world is lit from below.

## The Ground

The ground is packed.

Not packed as earth is packed in a field, but saturated, occupied, no room left between presences. Pebble on pebble on pebble, overlapping like scales, like roof tiles, like the crushed shells of something that was once alive and has been reworked a million times. This is the oldest surface you have ever stood on, and it shows it not by being sharp or monumental but by being finished. Erosion has had time to finish here. Every edge has been worried down. Every stone is round, or half-round, or polished flat on the side that faces the water. Cracks exist, but they open fresh and then heal, their lips soft. Hills do not exist. There is a suggestion of a rise a kilometer away, a very gentle swelling in the gravel, but you could walk toward it for an hour and never climb it — it moves, or the water moves, or the distance does.

Beneath the pebbles is silt, and beneath the silt is more pebble. Scrape with your hand — and your hand comes away cold, though the air is warm — and a thin film of water wells up instantly. You have not dug a hole; you have disturbed a surface. The water table is the surface. The plain is a skin, and the skin is weeping.

Weeping is the right word. The stones sweat even when it has not rained, because it is always raining in the way that matters: the air is so full that water condenses on everything, gathers in beads on the upturned faces of the gravel, runs in threads down the sides, drips — perpetually, everywhere — onto the stones below. Small trickles braid between the pebbles, altering course every few seconds, finding a new channel, abandoning the old one, which remains darker and colder for a little while, as if remembering. Puddles merge and separate. Mud boils up in slow, silent domes, exhales, subsides, is immediately trampled by water again. The ground crawls. Not fast. Relentlessly. A gravel bar can build itself in front of you, centimeter by centimeter, all its stones trembling, and then, without warning, slump sideways in a sigh, dissolving into the general wet.

Nothing here dries. Nothing here is allowed to. A footprint fills. A disturbed patch darkens and stays dark. Warmth — for the air is warm, a body-heat warmth that makes sweat bead and never evaporate — only feeds the cycle: evaporation from the open water, condensation on the stone, the two exchanging places so quickly that the plain seems to breathe in place.

## The Cover

For all that wet, there is almost no green.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of rumor. Here and there, in a backwater that has been still for maybe a minute, a grey-green thread of algae stretches a meter or two, catching the dim light. A clump of pale reeds — hollow-stemmed, jointed, colorless as bone — stands in a small hollow, every stem humming faintly in the wind, every root clinging to nothing more substantial than a film of mud over stone. A lichen crusts a slab, soft as felt. A moss hangs, not from a tree — there are no trees — but from the lip of a crack, a curtain of tiny damp fronds.

It does not spread. The turbulence will not let it. Roots cannot find purchase in a ground that shifts, floods uphill for a moment, boils, re-forms. Light is too dim for anything ambitious. And the water, though everywhere thin, is not clean; it carries iron, and salt after a long stillness, and the slow leach of stone. Plants that can live here are plants that can detach and start again, and so most of what you see is in the act of starting again.

Life, of any kind, is rare. That is the second surprise, after the absence of green. The plain is packed — you cannot put your foot down without displacing a dozen other things — but it is packed with matter, with sound, with water and stone and air, not with animals. Once in a long while you see one: a blind, eel-pale thing knifing through a shallow channel, gone before you are sure it moved. Or a cluster of small, clicking burrowers in wet sand, armor plates of polished fleck, that vanish by burrowing in two directions at once. Or something with too many legs under a slab, quick as a thought. They are not a population. They are incidents. The plain does not support a world; it tolerates occasional visitors who have learned to be mostly water themselves.

## The Scattered Things

And yet, in that packed, empty vastness, there are things that look made.

A line of ankle-high stones, one every two paces, running straight — unnaturally straight — for four hundred meters across the gravel, then stopping, then resuming a little to the left, as if the middle section had been taken away by water. A shallow pit, square, its corners still faintly right-angled, filled to the brim with clear water that reflects a sky different from the one above you. A mound, stepped on one side, melted on the other, wide as a house. A single upright slab, pierced near the top with a hole worn smooth, standing in the middle of a flat where nothing else stands.

Are they built? Erosion has answered that question by erasing it. If they were built, they were built by something that only ever wanted them faint. If they were not built, then water and wind here have a talent for pattern that is almost cruel. You will find yourself arranging them in your mind — path, door, marker, wall — and then the ground will shift under one of them, the line will break, a puddle will cover the square pit, and the pattern will go back to being gravel. They are scattered the way driftwood is scattered: occasionally, meaninglessly, proof that something once stood still long enough to be noticed.

There is nothing here with a light, or a smoke, or a road, or a tool. No metal. No glass. No sound that was made on purpose. The artifacts, if they are artifacts, are pre-everything: stone on stone, older than the final form of the plain, which has since decided to keep them as faint suggestions.

## The Sound

You will hear the plain before you understand it.

It is deafening, but not in the way of a crowd or a storm overhead. It is the sound of everywhere at once: the hiss of condensation forming on a million stone faces, the patter of drips landing on pebbles with a spatial randomness that makes it impossible to locate — it is inside your ears. Water trickling through gravel makes a continuous, tearing shush, like paper being pulled. Wind, with no hill to break it, moves across the vast flat in long pulls, scouring, and the pebbles clack against each other in it, a dry, wet clatter beneath the hiss. Underneath all of that, subsonic, you feel it in your sternum: the groan of the ground settling, a stone shifting its weight, a crack opening somewhere a kilometer away and traveling toward you as pressure.

Your own voice does not survive. Shout, and the sound is taken apart by the air, by the water, by the echoes bouncing off the mist, and returned to you a fraction of a second late, from the wrong direction, thinner. Footsteps are swallowed and also amplified; the suction of mud, the click of gravel, are louder than they should be and then gone. There is no silence to fall into, ever. Speech, if you try to sustain it, becomes a kind of pointing — you gesture, you stop talking, you listen to the drip that has not yet fallen. Because sometimes you hear it first.

## The Breaking

Reality here does not break all at once. It frays.

Walk a straight line — as straight as the ground will allow, which is not very — and after a while you will see the same configuration of stones on the horizon, in three directions. Turn around; it is behind you too. The mist is not one mist but many layered walls, each repeating the last, and the plain folds them. A puddle at your feet reflects not the sky above you but a brighter sky, a sky with edges, and in that reflection your own figure is standing a little to the side of where you are, as if the water has kept a slightly different version of you.

Water runs uphill in patches, for seconds at a time, where the gravel has packed itself into a temporary slope and then un-packed. Rain — the real rain, when the local weather decides to commit — falls for a bit and then, in one sector, begins to fall upward, beading on the undersides of stones, climbing back into the air as mist. A sound arrives before its cause: you hear the slump of a mud bank, and then, half a breath later, you see it slump. A channel you stepped over is still flowing in your memory after the water has left it, and a new channel, exactly where it was, fills while you watch, as if the plain is rehearsing.

Time is not broken into days. It is broken into moments that repeat with slight variation. The same drip, the same clack, the same gust, return — not identical, but recognizable, the way a face seen in a crowd is recognizable. You cannot get lost by distance, because distance misbehaves; you cannot get lost by direction, because north will not hold still under your feet. What you can do is keep walking. The plain permits walking. It does not permit arriving.

## The Final Form

This is an end-state, and it is also a beginning-state, and after a while you stop feeling the difference.

The plain has been eroded for so long that it has reached the shape erosion wanted: flat, wet, packed, low. It will not become a mountain. It will not become a desert. It will become this, again and again, with variations. Islands of mud rise fully formed from the water, patterned with pebbles as if they had been laid out, sit for a minute in the dim light, and collapse back into the general slurry. Cracks split the gravel in branching, lightning patterns, fill, seal. Storms hatch overhead, mature, and die of their own density, depositing their water on ground that was already wet. Gravel crawls from nowhere to somewhere and back. Everything is in turbulent motion, and the motion, taken as a whole, does not move. It is stable the way a waterfall is stable.

That is the rule, if the place has one: nothing dries, nothing stays put, nothing is quiet, nothing is far away for long, and nothing is new. The stones are older than the water that polishes them and younger than the moment they were last turned over. The faint stone lines are older than any builder you can imagine and will outlast your attention. The rare animals are accidents in a system that does not need them. The plants are hanging on.

Stand here long enough — hours, which here feel both longer and shorter than hours — and your body starts to acclimate in ways you did not consent to. Your breathing syncs, a little, with the hiss of the air. You stop trying to hear a single drip and hear the drip-field. You stop trying to see the horizon and see the surface, which is enough, which is endless: pebble, water, pebble, water, mud breathing up, stone sweating, wind worrying, light failing to arrive, sound filling the space where thought would be.

And the plain, vast, warm, dim, ancient, packed, goes on doing it. Creating and destroying its own small forms. Weeping and absorbing. Repeating its configurations at the edge of sight. It has no need of you to witness it, and it has no end in sight — only the thinning, where land and mist and reflection give up distinguishing themselves, and the wet flat continues, under a sky that sweats back.
