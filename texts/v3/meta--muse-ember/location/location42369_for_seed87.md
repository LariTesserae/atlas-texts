There is no air in it, and no end to the water.

The water is cold enough to ache through everything, near the point where it would turn to ice if it were left still, and it is everywhere — above, below, and in between. It does not lap. It presses. It holds every surface wet and gives every movement a slow, viscous resistance, so that to move is to part something thick. Beneath it all, twice below: under a lake, and under that lake’s sealed lid of ice, the bed is soft with silt. The light that reaches here has already been thinned by ice and by dark water and by distance. It comes as a flat, blue-grey dimness, never clear, never night. There is no sun, no shore, no sky to orient by. There is only a dimness that gets a fraction paler toward one narrow seam, and darker toward another.

And the place is not the lake. The lake is far away in the way a country is far away. This place is *inside* something.

It is a single hollow, half-sunk in the silt, a shell left open a finger’s width and never quite closed. From outside it would look like nothing much — one more pale, empty shell on the bed, rimed with silt, its seam a black line. From inside, it is a world with a domed roof and a curved floor, a whole country with an entrance that breathes.

That scale is the first strangeness of it. The entire chamber is only as wide as a dinner plate. For the things that live here, it is a metropolis. A street that for us would be a ripple in the silt is a ridge-road miles long in their measures, and the white nacre wall that arches overhead is a sky you will never reach except by climbing your whole life.

The terrain is made of that nacre. The upper shell and the lower shell meet at a hinge at one end, thickened and ridged like the spine of a book. From the hinge, concentric ribs run out across floor and roof in tightening arcs — not quite steps, not quite terraces, but the city follows them as if they were laid out for it. Between the ribs, silt has drifted and been trampled into crescents and hollows. The floor is not solid ground. It is shell-grit and crushed old shells and fine mud held together by mucus and the bodies of the dead, soft in places, springy in others, everywhere being reworked. At the seam — the mouth of the city — the silt is deepest, and the current comes in there, pushing aside the fringe of drifting grains.

Much of the nacre is still bare. That is the other strangeness: this is a city that is still coming into being. Wide polished districts of it gleam unoccupied, smooth as ice, reflecting the dim light from the seam. Their edges are frayed. In one place a patch of first settlers, a fuzz of tiny tube-builders waving hair-thin tentacles, has taken hold on the clean surface and will in time harden into a block. In another, a low mound of sponge tissue is spreading like a suburb of soft, pale flesh, only a few cells thick at its frontier. There are no ruins here. There is no erosion carving it away. It is pre-formation, raw and embryonic — the geology of the place is still its biology, depositing itself cell by cell while the bare shell waits ahead of it like unbuilt land.

Where the city is old, around the hinge where the shells are thickest, it is astonishingly dense. It is layered. It is difficult to find a surface that is only one thing thick. Mussels cement themselves in rows that become walls. Tube-worms stack their translucent tubes in leaning spires, five and six stories high, with alleys left between them where the water has to pass. Byssus threads, fine as spider silk, sling in bridges across the gullies between ribs. Crawlways of hardened mucus run as raised roads. On the roof, the city hangs inverted, the same builders fastened to the dome and growing down toward the floor, so that for a creature born there, up and down are less important than toward and away from the current. Ceilings are floors for someone else. Terraces overhang terraces. Silt and pellet and shed shell make the mortar. Nothing is brought in from outside to build with, and there are no tools, no machines, no devices at all — only mouths, feet, cilia, secreting glands. It is pure biological construction, and it is continuous: the city filters the water that passes through its streets, and in filtering, feeds.

Because the builders are so many, the density is a physical pressure. The fauna are abundant beyond counting in any human census — blind white amphipods and isopods scuttling in endless slow processions, translucent shrimp with empty-looking eyes, rotifers turning in the water like living dust, small eyeless fish that slip through the seam now and then and circle the outer districts like weather. They are packed near-touching, crawling over one another’s houses, over one another’s backs, through streets that are only the gaps that flow has left unfilled. The water between them seems to hold them up as much as the ground. When they feed, thousands of tiny appendages beat in loose, asynchronous waves, and the whole chamber seems to pulse — not loudly, but as a pressure against the shell.

The green things are few. Vegetation is sparse here, a concession to the dimness. Along the seam where the light is strongest, a fringe of olive algae sways, sparse and stringy, each filament isolated like hair. In darker corners, bacterial mats spread in black-green films over the silt, and there are a few low sponge gardens, soft mounds whose pores open and close. That is almost all the plant life there is; the rest of the food arrives as rain — larvae, spores, and organic snow falling from the lake through the seam, sifted and eaten by the city on its way through.

And for all this activity, it is silent.

Sound is 0 here, or so close to it that silence is a kind of material. The cold water is heavy, the silt absorbs, the shell is thick, and the ice far above does not speak down into this pocket. Almost nothing that lives here has a voice. The great activities — feeding, hunting, settling, dying — are done without struggle or cry. The loudest event in a week might be a single empty shell, loosened from a wall, settling a fraction downward with a faint *tick*, like a distant clock. Or the soft sigh of silt when the current shifts. When a hunting shrimp, huge by local standards, pushes in through the mouth, its passage is not heard at all; it is felt, as a sudden steepening of the current, a bulge of pressure rolling through the streets. The residents respond silently too: a wave of retraction runs through the city, tube-worms pulling in, mussels snapping shut, a shimmer that passes from district to district like a stadium wave, and then, after the hunter has gone, a slow re-opening, tentacle by tentacle.

Yet nothing is still. The dynamic is constant, only slow, because it is cold. The seam breathes in and out with the flexing of the ice above, every few hours pushing a tide of fresh water through the city and carrying the settled silt and larvae out again. Larvae are always raining down to found new frontier blocks on the bare nacre. Old shells empty and become the foundation and the upper stories of new ones; the dead are literally built upon, and the chamber, very slowly, is filling in from its walls inward. Bodies molt, feed, mate, are eaten. In the old core the accretion is so layered that some streets are roofed over entirely and have become tunnels, dark even by this place’s standards, lit only when a resident flashes — a pinprick of cold blue bioluminescence, a window going on and off in a room no larger than a grain of sand.

No one here knows there is an outside. No one has a plan. A district is not founded; it accumulates where a current happened to drop larvae, where a mussel happened to anchor, where a sponge happened to find a foothold on the rib. Traffic jams form where a fallen spire blocks an alley; new bridges are slung the next season. The city makes itself, and unmakes itself only by being overgrown.

That is its rule: to grow by deposition, to live by filtering what passes, to build only from what dies there or washes in. In time — in a few hundred slow, cold years — the accretion will meet itself, the dome and floor will be joined by a solid mass of shell and tube and silt, and the pocket will close and fossilize into a single stone made of a city. But that has not happened. Now it is still embryonic, still mostly empty in its bright centre, still busy at its edges, still descending into itself in layers.

Hold it to what little light there is and it would gleam, a dull pearl with a seam. Put it down again in the silt under the ice, and the water closes over it, and the dimness settles, and the city inside goes on feeding, building, hunting, and being hunted — silently, densely, and entirely in the wet dark that is its whole sky.
