First there is the height, and the wetness with it.

You are on top of a plateau so large that it makes its own weather, and the weather never leaves. Below you, in every direction, the lower world is filled with a white sea of cloud. It does not drift or thin. It stands, level and silent except for the noise from up here, and it makes the edge of the plateau look like a shoreline. The plateau itself is green to the horizon, but green that shines. It is flooded.

Water is not in a river here, or in lakes set into land. Water is the condition of the air and the ground. A shallow, cold sheet of it lies over everything, clear enough to see down into roots, deep enough to cover your ankles and, in the low places, your knees. Rain falls in a fixed veil over one end of the plateau and does not fall over the other, and the same veil has always been there. Mist does not roll in. It is resident. Even where the sun strikes, the air is saturated, your lungs fill with it, your clothes never dry, and water beads and runs off every surface only to be replaced from underneath.

For there is no ground here in the ordinary sense. What you stand on is alive and plant all the way down.

At ankle depth the surface is a continuous felt of mosses, liverworts, small rosette plants, algae, and root hairs knotted together into a sponge. It gives under your weight like a wet mattress, then springs back, pushing water up around your boots. You sink a hand in and it is fibrous, cold, and full of slow motion; you can feel it moving without going anywhere. Underneath that is more of the same, thickened over a time no one can guess, layers of tuber, peat-making stem, and decaying leaf that have never quite had a chance to become inert soil because they are always being eaten from below and grown from above at exactly the same rate. Stones exist — a plateau has to rest on rock — but they are so far below the plant mass, so smoothed and mantled, that you almost never see them clean. On the rare step where the mat has worn thin, a boulder shows, rounded, dark, entirely upholstered in green, as if the world had decided not to be bare.

This is what has made the shape of things. Nothing here is fresh. There are no sharp cliffs, no new scars, no banks freshly cut by a flood. Everything has been worked over by water for so long that it has settled. The channels that carry the sheet-flow are broad and braided and worn into gentle terraces, step down to step, each tread soft with plants. The mounds are low and symmetrical. Even the dead — and there is death, continuously — is worn smooth before it is absorbed. If you look for age, you find it everywhere and nowhere in particular. The plateau has reached its own form and stays in it.

And because it is so vast, most of it is empty.

Vegetation is total, but presences are sparse. For a hundred paces at a time there is only the waterlit mat, a few emergent reeds, the reflection of a normal, pale sun, and the sound. Then, far off, a solitary column of plant rises out of the flood — not a tree as in a forest, but a hollow, ribbed stalk the height of a house, banded, venting a slow stream of oxygen bubbles from its crown. Then another, and another, spaced like markers across the emptiness. They are the only vertical architecture, and they are not built. They grow where the mat is thickest, and they stay.

Under them, and everywhere under the surface, the place works.

It would be wrong to call it wilderness if wilderness means idle. This is industrial in the way a forest can be industrial when it is left to run long enough: mass production without machines. Algae bloom in long, straight rows along the channels, unit after identical unit, processing light. Fungi run like conveyor lines through the root felt, carrying sugar one way and minerals the other. The vent-stalks pulse at intervals you can feel through your soles, a low thump transmitted through water and plant, pumping, circulating, exhaling. If you put your hand in a deep pool, you may feel a faint tingling on your skin, a small electric discharge where bioelectric roots cross, welding nutrients together in the dark. There is a metallic taste to the air after one of these pulses. No factories stand here, no engines are bolted down, no artifacts wash up. The industry is the bodies themselves, scaled up until a whole continent of them forms a single, humming system. It is efficient not because anyone designed it, but because anything inefficient would have long since been balanced out.

The light is ordinary. That is part of the strangeness. It is mid-morning light, cool and white, arriving from a sun that does not seem to move significantly in its arc. Shadows are short and soft and constant. Colors are true: the deep green of the mat, the grey-blue of water, the pale cream of the cloud sea below. Nothing is twilit, nothing glows in the dark, nothing is hidden by gloom. You see farther here than you expect, because you are high and the air, though wet, is clear. It is the clarity that makes the emptiness feel so pronounced. You can pick out a bird, or a back, or a vent-stalk, a mile away, and then nothing again for another mile.

The sound is not ordinary. It is loud, and it does not vary.

There is the rush of water off the plateau edge — not one waterfall but a continuous curtain along the whole rim, falling unbroken into the cloud sea, making a white noise that you feel in your teeth. There is wind through the sparse reeds, always the same pitch. There is dripping from the vent-stalks, chiming, and the bubbling up through the mat, and underneath it all the low pulse of the root pumps, a heartbeat at the scale of a landscape. Distant animal calls add to it, but do not change its volume. There is no quiet place to rest your ears. When you step, your boots splash, and the splash is taken up by the general roar and lost.

Life, for all that sparseness, is abundant.

Because the world is so large, abundance does not mean crowd. It means you never go far without entering a different niche being used. In the open sheet water, long flat-backed grazers wade — shoulder-high, moss-furred, moving with the slow deliberation of things that have nowhere to hurry to — cropping the mat in wide swaths which heal behind them. In the channels, eel-like swimmers loop in the current, silver and blindish, filtering. On the surface, rafts of floating bloom carry small, bright, hard-shelled creatures that look like walking seeds. High up, sparse flocks of glider birds circle the vent-stalks on wings that are more membrane than feather, calling in chorus that has been going on, at the same volume, for longer than you can measure. Under the mat, invisible except for the bubbles they send up, are the filterers and root-eaters who make the machinery run. Dozens of kinds, hundreds of kinds, each with its patch, none overlapping in a way that ever gets crowded.

Nothing built has ever been seen here. No path, no cairn, no shelter, no nest of sticks that is not just the mat folded over, no bone arranged, no tool dropped. Even the animals do not alter their ground enough to leave a mark; a grazer's track fills with water and the plants close over it within the minute, at the same rate they open. If a living thing dies, it is not left to rot into a new shape. It is unmade at the rate it is made, returning its substance to the sponge without a ripple.

That is the rule that holds all the rest together.

This place does not change. Not in the small way we mean by stable, and not in the large way we mean by seasons or growth. Water flows but the level never rises or falls. Plants grow but never outgrow their space; an equal amount dies, loosens, feeds the fungi, and is taken up again. The grazers move but the mat they crop is always cropped to the same height. The rain falls but the pools never deepen. The sun gives light but neither warms the water nor dries the air. The pulses beat but send no net energy anywhere. It is a frozen equilibrium at continental scale — a system so perfectly in balance that time passes through it without leaving a trace.

You could mark a waterline with a cut in a reed, leave for a year, and return to find the cut exactly as you left it, the reed exactly as tall. You could listen for an hour and hear no sound begin or end. You could watch a vent-stalk breathe and notice that after each breath the air is exactly as it was before.

And so it will go on. High, cold, flooded, green, loud, and working. A vast, submerged plateau made of plant, making itself over and over so precisely that it never needs to become anything else.
