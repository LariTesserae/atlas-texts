It is loud before it is visible.

Not a sudden noise, but a pressure you feel in your teeth and in the soft place behind your ears — a deep, continuous roar, as if something enormous is breathing very slowly and has been breathing for a very long time. Within a few steps of the sources, conversation is impossible. Across the empty spaces it carries and collects, bouncing off metal until it seems to come from everywhere at once. And yet nothing here hurries. A piston sighs, a gear turns, a cable hums, water drips. The loudness is in the volume, not the tempo. This is a place that makes a great deal of sound while doing almost everything at a glacial pace.

When your eyes adjust, the whole of it can be taken in at once. It is small. A single bounded feature, a hamlet's worth of ground, a cathedral's footprint. A high wall, a gate, a paved yard of poured concrete and steel plate maybe sixty paces across. Then, in the middle of that yard, rising straight out of it, a tower. It is built like a stepped mountain and a factory at the same time: terraced rings of concrete and riveted boiler-iron, glass clerestories, copper pipe runs, ceramic insulators, smokestacks gone quiet, a gantry crane frozen mid-swing. Its top is open to the sky.

And it is impossibly high. The yard itself sits at an extreme elevation, on a constructed summit. Beyond the railing there is no country, no slope, no valley floor you could walk down to. There is only a sheer built drop and, far below, a slow sea of cloud moving through which the land looks like a rumor. The air at the edge is thin and clear and cold enough to sting your nose, and when you look down your inner ear protests. The sun is ordinary — white, high, normal noon light, casting hard ordinary shadows — but at this height it warms the metal quickly, and the tower holds that warmth.

Everything is made. That is the first rule you learn by touching anything. The ground is concrete. The cliff beneath the yard is steel and concrete. The ledge under a clump of moss is concrete. The soil a tree is rooted in is a flattened accumulation of rust-dust, leaf-rot, grit blown in and caught in a blocked drain, slowly cemented into something soil-like on top of a machine housing. There is no rock, no sand, no natural substrate anywhere. Even the weathering is built upon: concrete cracked and re-cracked and softened at the edges by two centuries of wind and intermittent rain until the steps are worn concave in the middle, paint weathered to rust and grey, iron gone to a flaking red-brown patina that itself has become a planting medium. It is not new and it is not falling down. It is established. Decay and repair have reached a settled balance. A crack will open, moss will fill it, water will widen it a fraction over twenty years, a root will hold it. Nothing is pristine. Nothing is in collapse. It has become itself, form and all.

Between the things, there is mostly nothing. The density is near-void. The yard is a vast, empty paved expanse. The tower is hollow — a vertical shaft of air at its center, perhaps forty meters across and a hundred and fifty high, with only sparse platforms and catwalks hung far apart in it, connected by ladders and a slow lift that takes an age to travel one level. On those platforms sit the few presences: an engine shed, a row of humming transformers, a greenhouse cage of twisted glass, a single water tank, a clump of trees. Walking from one to the next, you cross long stretches where there is only wind, the roar, and the view over the void. It feels emptier than a desert because everything here was intended to be filled with machines and work, and the emptiness echoes.

Then, suddenly, at those sparse presences, it is lush.

Vegetation at 2.5 out of any scale — thick, unapologetic, layered green pushed into every seam of the industrial structure. Ficus vines as thick as your wrist drape from the terraces and climb the smokestacks, their roots in pipe joints. Ferns erupt from bolt heads and gutter cracks in great shaggy rosettes. Grasses grow in the expansion joints of the concrete, holding rain in their blades. From the upper catwalks hang inverted gardens, growing downward toward the drips below, leaves drinking condensation as it falls. On the roof ring, where the normal sun hits unfiltered, there are small trees — real trees, with trunks, branches, dappled shade — rooted in planter-engine housings, their canopies interlaced with electrical cable, their blossoms visited by insects that live their whole lives in the warm light around the lamps. The green is not in spite of the steel. It is on it, in it, because of it. The engines make heat. The pipes make drips. The metal makes ledges that catch dust. The place feeds its jungle from its own waste.

It is warm inside the shaft, even with the thin cold at the rim. Sun on iron, engine breath, the enclosed air slowing as it rises. Your skin in the hollow feels damp and warm at once. The water here never arrives in quantity. There is no river, no lake, no pool worth the name. Rain comes sideways at this height, torn by wind, and scarcely wets the yard before it sheets off the concrete into drains. What there is, is moisture in pockets. At dawn the cold steel sweats condensation in beads that run in dark, slow lines down the walls. Warm air from the lower engine floor rises, meets the colder air at the top opening, and condenses — a closed, slow breath of weather the tower makes for itself. It drips, almost always somewhere, from pipe to leaf to bolt head, collecting in the cup of a leaf, in the hollow of a gear tooth, in the saucer of a cable clamp, in a footprint-shaped hollow in the concrete. In those teacup pockets, dark and warm and still, life breeds. The whole structure is streaked with damp: efflorescence blooming white on concrete, algae green on copper, black damp-tears beneath every joint. Damp, not wet. Intermittent, patient, endless.

The fauna are abundant, but concentrated, like the plants, on those few warm, wet, lit points in the emptiness. Swifts by the hundreds nest in vent holes and screaming holes, tearing around the shaft in tight, deafening loops, their calls sharpening the engine roar. Moths and midges swarm thick around the electric lights of the lower floors. Spiders string webs across idle gears and conveyor belts, so fine they vibrate with the hum. Lizards bask on sun-hot transformer boxes, motionless for hours. And from the condensation pockets comes a chorus of frogs — thousands of them, improbably, at this altitude, in this machine — their evening calls amplified by the hollow steel until they are part of the deafening soundscape, a wet percussion under the industrial drone. Rodents run the cable trays. Wasps paper the undersides of the catwalks. Every warm pipe has its insects farming its warmth. A small mammal may live its entire life on one platform, never descending, because there is food, water, and shelter in a single engine housing, and the void between platforms is for swifts alone.

The strangeness is not monstrous. It is the odd, settled interdependence of a thing that has been left to itself long enough. Plants grow in exact synchrony with the slow machine cycle — a great wheel below that completes one revolution in nine days ventilates the shaft with a sigh, and the hanging ferns lean, almost imperceptibly, into that slow wind. Vines have grown over the dials of gauges until the dials are only found by following the roots. Birds pollinate the roof trees and carry seed to the transformers, where new saplings sprout from the oily grit. Frogs, which should need a pond, have made a pond system out of a teacup, a bolt head, a leaf axil, and move between them in the rain of drips. Everything alive seems to have negotiated terms with the machinery: it will not be hurried, it will not be moved, so life will use its heat, its water, its pauses.

Time here is not measured in days. A vine advances a handspan in a year. A fallen leaf takes three seasons to rot into the thin soil on a ledge. The great production line on the lowest floor, still electrified, still warm, moves one part increment over the course of a month. The erosion that matters happens over decades: a rust crust thickening, a concrete bowl deepening under a drip, a tree trunk slowly splitting a housing seam. Visitors, if any come, see a snapshot of a place in equilibrium — not static, but in such slow flux that change is only visible if you return after years and find the green a little farther along the pipe, the patina a little deeper, the shaft a little greener at the rim.

So the landscape resolves, as you stay, into its contradictions held together: a small, single, made mountain at extreme height, mostly empty air and concrete, deafening with slow machinery, warm with engine heat and ordinary sun, damp with its own breath, lush with trees growing out of engines, and alive everywhere a drip can be caught. It does not feel like a ruin, or a garden, or a factory. It feels like a place that was built for industry, abandoned to weather and time, and then claimed — leaf by leaf, frog by frog, swift by swift — until the industry and the ecology became the same thing, humming together, very slowly, at the top of the world.
