From the plateau you would not know it was there at all.

The land up there is flat and hard and thinly covered — sparse scrub, wind-bent grass in the cracks, a pale soil no deeper than a hand. It is cool even in the middle of the day, and the light is plain and unfiltered. Nothing much grows, nothing much lives. A few goats, tethered far apart, crop what they can. The air moves freely and carries dust a long way.

Then the ground simply stops.

The town is a sink, a broad bowl cut down into the earth below the level of the plateau, wide enough that from the rim you can take in the whole of it at once. This is not an old valley worn smooth by water. It is new. The rock has been opened within living memory, and it still shows it: sharp faces, bright bands of limestone and shale exposed in section, corners too clean to be natural, spoil heaped and re-hoped and still being carried up in baskets to make the outer ring walls. There is no soil to speak of inside, no rounded pebbles, no softened slopes. The geology is raw, unfinished, and the settlement matches it — this is a city still in the act of being dug, house by house, street by street.

Most of it lies below the original surface. The rim is the old ground; everything that matters is down in the cut, terraced in rough steps to the floor. The oldest dwellings are at the bottom, gouged directly into the floor and low walls, their roofs at the level of a newer neighbour's second floor. Newer buildings climb the terraces above them, each generation building hard against the last, sharing walls, sharing roofs, stepping up toward the light. And at the edges — all around the edges, where the face is still bare — the digging goes on.

You descend by ramps and stairways cut from the same stone, narrow and packed. There is no empty space anywhere. Lanes run between houses with perhaps a shoulder's width to spare, and even that is used: washing hung overhead, baskets tucked into niches, firewood stacked into hollows chipped for the purpose. Workshops open straight onto the lane — the stone-cutters at the fresh faces, the weavers and leather-workers a terrace up, the bakers with their shared ovens at the cool low ground where the heat is welcome and the smoke can rise unobstructed. Storage rooms, cisterns, and sleeping rooms are dug sideways into the walls, one behind another, so that from outside a house may look like a single blank front and go deep and branching within. It is fully occupied, saturated in the way a hive is. When a family leaves, the room is taken before the stone dust settles.

The building is organized, even if it is primitive. The town has districts by trade and by age of digging, a rough market on the flat floor where grain and salt and wool come down from the plateau and cut stone goes up. There are drains carved along every lane, covered with split slabs, so that the occasional rain — a short, cool downpour that comes in late summer and vanishes — runs off in sheets down the faces and is led away instead of flooding the lower houses. There are timber props that begin as scaffolding and are left to become floors and beams, and rope-and-pulley winches, hand-cranked, for lifting stone and water. The tools are simple and made for hands: picks and sledges of iron, wooden wedges to split a face, baskets, ladles, oil lamps for the deep cut rooms where daylight does not reach. Everything is repaired, re-hafted, and used to the last.

Everything is also damp, but never wet in any generous way. Water does not run through this place as a river. It gathers in pockets. At the lowest level a seep line weeps slowly from the shale — the town's main source — and is caught in a shallow cistern and ladled out by the bucket, house by house, according to an old rota. Condensation beads on the north-facing walls in the morning and trickles into the channels. Puddles in the lanes after rain take a full day to dry in the shade. Down in the newest tunnels, the air is perpetually moist, cool on the skin, smelling of mineral and wet stone. The stone itself is cool to the touch, and the whole sink holds that coolness. Even at midday, when the sun at last drops straight down and the floor is lit normally and brightly for a few hours, the walls keep most of the town in a soft, moving shadow, and a breeze slides down the ramps from the plateau like something poured.

Sound behaves strangely, and quietly. In a place this dense and this active you expect noise, but the stone takes it. Voices stay low without any effort. The work — a rhythm of picks at the digging faces, hammer on wedge, the clink of a mason dressing a block, carts rumbled down the ramps on packed stone — is muffled within a few houses. The dominant sounds are small and close: sandals scuffing on steps, a bucket set down, water dripping somewhere in the deep, the soft thud of a basket, a child calling once and quieting. At night it falls to almost nothing, just the drip and the wind at the rim far above.

Life here is thin outside the human kind. Vegetation is sparse to the point of tokenism. On the rim, the scrub continues. Inside the sink, the only green is what people have deliberately defended: a line of moss and lichen where the seep keeps a north wall damp, a few potted beans and a stunted fig in a courtyard where the sun lingers, some hardy vines trained up a south face for shade. Animals are rarer still. Pigeons nest in the holes left by blasting and in the eaves. A few dogs sleep in doorways. Rats live by the cistern. At dusk, swallows come down from the rim and cut low across the bowl. Nothing wild finds much room when every crack is a house, a store, or a drain.

That is, in a way, the rule of the place. No one may leave a gap. A new wall must meet an old one. A tunnel may not break into a neighbour's room; it must turn, and the turning is marked and remembered. The digging front moves outward at the speed of hand labour — a hand's breadth a day on a good face — and the masons follow directly behind it, and the roofers behind them, and the painters of plaster — lime wash, white and pink — behind them. The town is therefore never finished and never idle. It is always a few years old and always in the process of becoming something else.

It is a perfectly ordinary place, for all that. People buy bread, argue about water turns, mend sandals, marry, move a terrace up when they can afford to. They go up to the plateau on market days to see the flat horizon and come back down into the cool, packed, quiet bowl, where the light falls straight for a little while at noon, the walls sweat faintly in the morning, and somewhere at the edge, stone rings once, twice, picks at work, opening a little more room for somebody who has not yet arrived.
