### Iskara, Inside the Seed

There is only one object on the plain that is not plain.

The plain itself is low, bright, and warm to the point of punishment. It stretches flat for as far as you can see, a floor of pale cracked mineral, salt-white where the sun touches it, grey-brown where it does not. The cracks are wide and empty. There is no water in them, no damp, no river-cut, no green thread following a hidden seep. Even in the shade of a stone the dust is dry as flour and fine as flour, and it coats your mouth. A wind comes and goes, hot and arrhythmic, raising a thin glitter of dust that hangs for a moment and disappears.

Sunk half into this floor, as if it had settled there a very long time ago and had never thought of moving again, is a seed.

It is not a small seed. It is a single, enormous, hollow pod, perhaps sixty paces across, amber and translucent, polished by wind-blown grit until its surface is smooth as river stone. The erosion has had time to finish with it. There are no sharp edges left. Where the shell meets the ground, it has melted into a soft rim. Where it meets the sky, the top has paled to a milky, almost white crown. Long ago a slit opened on the northern side, and the slit has been worn into a low, resinous keyhole, just large enough to crawl through.

From outside, dosed with heat and glare, you would think it empty. That is the first of its tricks.

There is a note coming from it. You feel it before you hear it, a low, steady hum in your teeth when the sun is high. Put your palm flat on the shell and the hum strengthens, running up your arm like a string pulled in the sternum. The pod is a drum that nobody built, and the sun plays it all day.

### The Throat

You duck into the keyhole. The shell is thick here — a hand’s breadth or more of amber flesh, layered resin, and mineral — and it is warm, faintly sticky, with a smell like hot honey, hot bark, and old oil. Beads of clear amber sweat on the inner wall and harden before they fall.

Just inside is a small throat-chamber, deliberately made, not grown. The builders — whoever they were, long before anyone now living — cut niches into the thick wall and plastered them with mud and pounded seed-fiber. Jars sit in the niches. A rope ladder hangs. A copper awl, a stone hammer, a loom of knotted vine lie ready. Everything is handmade and weathered and rounded by handling. Nothing here is new, and nothing looks as if it has recently broken. The city inside has settled into its form like sediment.

This first room is the quietest place in Iskara. The shell’s hum is muffled behind its own thickness. Light comes not in a shaft but everywhere, filtered through amber, so that there are no true shadows, only softer and brighter ambers. Dust and resin motes hang glittering. It is bright in a way that makes your eyes water, like being inside a lantern.

Then the floor drops, very slightly — this whole world is a shallow bowl — and the throat opens into the main hollow.

### The Main Hollow

It is, impossibly, a country.

You are standing inside one seed, and you could walk the entire city in half a day, and yet the space above you is tall enough to lose the dome in glare, and the floor in front of you is empty for a hundred paces in every direction. Almost nothing stands per volume here. That is the second trick.

The pod’s interior is ribbed, like the inside of a gourd. Great curved ribs rise from the floor to the crown, and between the ribs run the streets. There are three main rings, terraced into the wall like steps cut into the curve of a bowl:

The Low Ring, around the floor. The Reed Terraces, halfway up. The Bell Rim, just beneath the crown.

Between ring and ring there is no wall, only a steep, vegetated slope of resinous flesh, too slick to climb, hung with green. Between cluster and cluster on the same ring there is often nothing at all — broad, empty ledges of packed shell-pulp and mud-brick, polished by feet and by the slow daily expansion of the shell, where a shout will answer you from far away, a minute later, thinner and higher than you sent it.

The buildings are isolated islands in this emptiness. A house here. A kiln there, beehive-shaped, with a hand-bellows beside it. A row of storage hollows carved straight into the rib, their doors made of woven reed. A stall with no roof, a loom under a shell-shaving awning, a stack of unfired bricks drying in the amber light. For every inhabited cube, there are ten empty cubes. The city was planned, or perhaps it grew in fits, for a population that never came, or that thinned, or that learned to live far apart. A few hundred people live in Iskara now, the Resin-people, and most days you could walk a whole ring without meeting one.

Their construction is primitive and layered and patient. Houses are thick-walled — a meter of mud, fiber, and ground shell, laid in courses like baskets — with small dark openings, deep-set against the glare and the sound. Roofs are domes of beaten shell. Floors are mats of woven reed over resin. Pipes run everywhere: not for water, for there is none, but for air and sound. Clay flutes are set into walls, hollow ribs are left open to the rising air, bells of fired ceramic and beaten gourd hang from rope bridges and from the upper ledges. Ladders, pulley-ropes, spiral ramps of brick follow the curve. Kilns breathe heat in pulses. It is a town, unmistakably, organized and layered and inhabited — and it is also mostly empty, like a city holding its breath between houses.

### The Sound

At dawn, Iskara is almost quiet. The shell has contracted in the cool of the night, ticking and creaking as it settles, resin popping under the surface, and for an hour you can hear your own footsteps and speak loudly enough to be understood across a room.

Then the sun climbs, the amber begins to glow from outside, and the shell begins to expand.

It does so slowly, and continuously, and it sings as it does. The fundamental note is so low you feel it in your ribs and in the soles of your feet. On top of it, the hollow ribs act as organ pipes. Warm air is always rising in a lazy central column — the pod breathes, slowly, in and out with the heat — and as it rises through the ribs and through the city’s clay pipes, it whistles harmonics, reed-tones, overtone after overtone, stacking until your head is full. The vegetation adds its own layer: hollow ribbon-ferns vibrate, bell-vines slap their trumpets against each other, dry pods burst with a sound like knuckles cracking, thousands of times a day.

By midday it is deafening.

Not loud like a market or a waterfall. Deafening like standing inside a bell that has just been struck and will not be allowed to stop. Your teeth buzz. Loose resin beads dance off ledges. The people stop work, or work by touch. Children learn early to cover their ears with wax-and-fiber plugs and to speak with their hands. Even in the thickest-walled houses, on the Low Ring, behind two doors, the drone is present, softened to a pressure. Up on the Bell Rim, where the shell is thinnest and brightest, it is almost too much to bear, and the Resin-people go there only to tend the great resonators or to look out through pinholes worn by erosion, at the white plain and the empty sky.

The city has built *with* the sound, not against it. The resonators in the walls are not decoration. They are tuned to the pod’s note.

### The Green

That is because in Iskara, the green lives on sound and on the shell itself.

There is no water anywhere in this place. No rain can enter, no spring rises. The floor is dry and cracked in the same way as the plain outside, only darker, and stained with resin. People go thirsty in a way you would not expect in a jungle, and they meet it by harvesting the green: the clear beads that condense on bell-vine lips, the sweet amber sap that sweats from cushion moss when the shell expands, cut and chewed and stored in jars. It is their drink and their oil and their glue. It tastes of warm resin and green stems, and it is not water.

The vegetation is nevertheless lush, overwhelmingly so. It does not grow primarily from the floor. It grows from the walls.

Curtains of hook-fern hang from the slopes between rings, rooting in fissures in the resin layer, their fronds each a hollow tube open at both ends. When the pod sings, they sing back, and the Resin-people swear, and the botanists among them will tell you it is true in physiological fact, that the fronds’ cilia catch the fine silicate mist that the warm shell flesh exhales all day, and that the vibration strips nutrients from that mist more efficiently than still air. A garden that is not piped, not resonated, not allowed to hear the drone, will languish and die, however much light it gets. A garden hung beside a tuned flute will double in a season.

Cushion moss carpets every ledge that is not built on, velvet green a hand thick, crunching underfoot when dry, exuding in the heat. Bell-vines sprawl across the Reed Terraces, their trumpet flowers opening at noon to the light and the noise, closing at dusk. Drum-leaves, broad and taut as skins, grow in clusters around the low kilns and fold and unfold with the temperature. Spore-dust is always in the air, carried up the central updraft, falling slowly, constantly, like a very fine, green snow that never melts. The whole interior smells of hot resin, fermenting sap, baked mud, and green growth.

That is the strange rule on which everything else depends: the pod must sing for the jungle to live, and the jungle, by catching and rerouting the air, helps the pod sing. The people, in their primitive way, farm both.

### The Center

In the middle of the floor, empty, is the one other feature of the place: a shallow circular pit, perhaps thirty paces across, over which the builders have stretched a single membrane of dried pod-skin, scraped and resined and tuned until it is taut as a drumhead. This is the Great Drum.

At midday, it vibrates visibly. Dust jumps on it in patterns. No one crosses it then. At dawn and dusk, when the drone softens, people walk out onto it to listen with their feet, to test its tension, to tighten its lashings with hand tools. It is not an altar and it is not a well. It is simply the largest, most resonant surface in a hollow place, and the city is arranged around it like listeners.

Around it, life proceeds in its sparse, active way. Geckos, deaf and pale, live in the wall cracks and hunt the moths. The moths — glass-winged, blind, navigating by the feel of vibration in the air — rise in small clouds at dusk and are eaten, or not. A single species of small glider nests in the eaves of the Bell Rim and occasionally slips out through the keyhole to wheel over the white plain, the only animal in Iskara that ever leaves. No predator larger than a person has ever lived here. The Resin-people are the largest thing that moves on the floor, and they are rare enough that a new face is an event.

Processes never really stop. Morning contraction, midday expansion, evening cooling. Leaves opening, pods popping, kilns firing in their slow bellows-rhythm, ropes creaking, the updraft sighing through the ribs, spores rising and falling. Weather, of a kind, moves inside the seed as a slow tide of heat. The city and the jungle and the shell age together, weathered and settled and in balance, nothing sharp, nothing new for a very long time.

### At Night

When the sun drops, the amber walls go from bright gold to a deep, dusky brown. The shell contracts, and the great note thins, thins, and finally — for a brief, astonishing interval — ceases.

In that silence, Iskara becomes a different place. You can hear a loom three rings away. You can hear resin bead by bead. You can speak, in a normal voice, and be answered, and the answer is not distorted. The Resin-people emerge from their thick houses, take out their plugs, and do the work that needs language: mending, harvesting, arguing, teaching. The green folds its bells, the moss stops sweating, the moths come out.

It does not last. The pod is already beginning, imperceptibly, to warm for the next day, and the first low creak of expansion runs through the ribs like a held breath being released.

You understand, standing there in the sudden quiet, that this is not a ruin and not a refuge and not a desert and not a jungle, though it contains all of those things in miniature. It is a pocket. A single seed, hollowed by time, filled by green that does not drink water, by a city that does not need streets, by people who live far apart because the world they live in is mostly empty air and mostly deafening song — and who have learned, primitives as they are, to live in the intervals, and to keep the music going.
