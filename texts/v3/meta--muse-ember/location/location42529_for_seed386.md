It is high enough that your breath comes short, and hot enough that you do not expect it to.

That contradiction is the place. A broad volcanic shelf on the equator, perhaps three thousand meters above a sea you cannot see, left over from an eruption so old the rim has forgotten its sharpness. From the lip, the country falls away in steps — terraced bogs, forested gullies, a second plateau beyond a cloud-filled gap, then another. The scale is regional. You are not in a single valley; you are in a system of valleys, all perched, all connected by water that never quite decides whether it is rain, stream, or vapor.

The sun is ordinary. White-yellow noon light, hard shadows, true colors. Nothing alien in the sky. But the air is thin and direct, and under it the ground itself is warm. Pumice and dark basalt hold the day’s heat under your palms. At the edges of the marshes, shallow pools steam without boiling to the eye — at this pressure water gives up at little more than body heat — and yellow-white crusts of sulphur bloom in rings where the steam condenses. The mountain is a furnace that has settled into its work. It is not erupting. It is running.

Long-existed is written everywhere in the form of things. The ridges are not jagged. They have slumped into amphitheaters and soft swells, healed landslip scars now draped in moss, gullies cut and re-cut until the water has found its permanent courses and settled there. Red peat has built up layer on layer in the flat places. Talus has weathered into rounded blocks that sink into lichen. Erosion here is not violence anymore. It is balance — a slow, finished shaping, still going at the margins, but held.

And it is wet, without pause. Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it. This is marsh-edge country lifted to a summit. Orographic rain builds in the afternoon, pours for an hour, and vanishes into vapor. In the morning the whole slope condenses. Rocks weep. Cliff faces are black with seepage, dripping steadily long after rain. Mosses hang in curtains from tree ferns and drip until the ground squelches underfoot. Little springs emerge sideways from under stones, from the sides of mounds, from the roots themselves — the shelf is so saturated that water does not need to travel far downhill to be visible. It appears.

Vegetation answers that wet and heat with a density that is almost total. It is layered so closely that from above the plateau looks like a solid green skin. At the bottom, thigh-high moss and clubmosses and creeping ferns. Above them, a tangled shrub layer of wet-leaved plants whose flowers are waxy and brief. Above them, tree ferns unfold, trunks furred, fronds making their own small weather. And on everything, more life: bromeliads filling with rainwater in the crooks of branches, orchids in white clusters, lianas, vines, epiphytes on epiphytes until a single limb may carry five kinds of plant in its length. In the bog terraces the plants are lower and stranger, cushion plants thick as mattresses, and the water between them is green-brown and still.

There is nothing built. No path holds. A footstep sinks and the moss closes behind it in a day. No stone wall, no hut, no artifact, no rust, no cut timber. It is untouched in the sense that nothing has been added, and yet it is far from unworked. The work is being done at industrial scale, by the inhabitants themselves.

Under the plateau, the geothermal engine runs hot and regular, pumping steam in pulses — some vents hiss on a cycle of minutes, sigh, pause, hiss again, like pistons in another room. The rumble is felt more than heard, a low thump through the soles of the feet at the warmest pools. At the surface, production is organized. Leafcutter ants run highways in columns a thousand long, carrying green shards overhead in endless conveyor lines to fungal farms inside their mounds. Termite cities rise in clay towers with ventilation shafts and chambers, kept at precise temperature by the movement of air, cooling towers in miniature. Microbial mats in the hot springs lay down mineral travertine day after day, building terraces like factory floors, yellow to orange to brown in bands. Even the bogs are productive, fixing and storing, turning mist into peat, peat into soil, at a pace that would look mechanized if it were not so green.

Fauna is abundant, but it does not announce itself the way a forest low in the heat might. You find it in tracks and signs and sudden presences: frogs in every pool, glass frogs clinging under leaves, toads the color of wet bark. Hummingbirds work the flowers in a constant, irritable whir. Giant spiders string webs between ferns across the damp gullies. Insects of every size — too many to count, and most of them silent as they go. Small mammals move at night through the undergrowth, leaving neat runways in the moss. A hawk rides the lift from the warm updrafts, patient, circling. A frog chorus erupts for ten minutes at dusk and then stops as if switched off.

That is the other dominant quality: quiet. For all the density and activity, the place is mostly still to the ear. The heat thickens the midday. Moss and wet leaves absorb sound. Many of the animals communicate chemically, or in subsonic tremors through the ground, or with a single plip that does not carry. What you hear, mostly, is water in different moods — a drip from a fern frond, a trickle finding its way through roots, the soft hiss of a vent — and, far below the rim, thunder from weather happening under the shelf. The air on the plateau itself is muffled. It makes the abundance feel secretive rather than loud.

Life is in motion, nonetheless. Growth is fast in the wet warmth; a fallen tree fern is overtaken in weeks, its trunk becoming a nursery of seedlings. Geysers cycle. Afternoon storms fill the streams brown and swift by evening, clear again by morning. Predator and prey keep the usual pace — snakes take the frogs, hawks take the snakes, ants farm and are farmed in turn. Lichens creep over stone, then are carried away in a small slide. It is an active, working system, mid-cycle, not growing over or dying back.

One small unnaturalness to remember, because the place is otherwise familiar: the normal rules slip sideways at the edges. Water drums on your skin in the morning not as rain but as condensation forming on warm stone and falling upward off it in mist. Sun-bleached rock can be cold to a hand extended into shade, and burning hot an inch away where the earth warms it from below. Steam rises from ground that is also sprouting orchids. You put a metal cup down on the moss to fill it and it fills itself from the air. Nothing dramatic, nothing alien — just a high, hot, wet mountain that has run long enough to perfect its own processes, and is doing so without us, at full production, in near silence, under ordinary light.

In the late afternoon the clouds rise from below to meet the rim, and the whole shelf disappears into white. You stand in cool vapor for a moment, surprisingly cool, water beading on everything, and then the sun breaks again and the ground warms under your feet as if the engine below had turned up, just slightly, for the evening shift.
