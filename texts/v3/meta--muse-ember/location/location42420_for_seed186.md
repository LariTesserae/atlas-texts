It is higher than a place should be to have ground at all.

You do not arrive at it so much as you emerge onto it, step by step up a slope that never really ends. Below, far below, is weather. Clouds go past at knee height and break against the stone. Above, there is no weather to break. The sky is a thin, black lid, bruised at one edge by a permanent twilight where the sun touches and does not rise. Stars are visible at noon. The only light that moves is the aurora — slow curtains of green-grey and violet unrolling and refolding, not bright enough to read by, bright enough to see the shape of the world by. It makes everything monochrome and then, shifts.

The scale is open and merciless. Range after range falls away into that dark, each ridge a little darker than the last, until the world is only silhouette. The plateau itself is mostly empty: a vast, wind-polished field of black and iron-grey stone, rounded by a very old patience. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is crumbling either. The erosion has finished its argument. Basalt columns stand worn to pillars, granite slabs have been softened at the edges until they look like they are thinking about melting, scree has sorted itself into size by size, patiently, over an age of freeze and thaw. It is a settled place, in equilibrium with itself.

Density, per volume, is almost nothing. You can walk for an hour — or for what feels like an hour, time misjudges at this height — and see no sign of life except your own breath freezing in front of you. The cold is not sharp, it is absorbed. It soaks into your boots and your teeth. The air is too thin for sound to carry far. The silence is not an absence; it is a thick, insulating thing. Snow does not crunch so much as sigh. When ice cracks somewhere impossibly far off, you feel it in your sternum before you hear it, as a single muted *tok*.

And yet it is damp.

There is no river. There is no lake. There will never be rain that reaches the ground — what falls from the twilight sky evaporates, or turns to needle ice, while it is still falling. The moisture lives in other places: in the undersides of stones, where condensation beads and runs inward, never dripping; in hollows where fog settles and stays, and the moss drinks it directly, leaf by leaf; in the pores of the rock, where a black water hides and is taken up by roots that tap, patiently, like straws. Your skin is clammy and your mouth is dry. Everything alive is taking water the way a factory takes power — secretly, through contacts, in small, constant draws.

Then, miles apart, the emptiness breaks.

A hollow, no bigger than a courtyard, no bigger than a house, where the stone dips and the wind cannot quite enter. And inside it, lush. Impossible green. Moss as thick as a mattress, layered and springy, ferns unfurled in overlapping rosettes, low shrubs with leaves like burnished copper, lichen in apricot and cream you can peel off in sheets. It is lush the way a jungle is lush, dense to the point of suffocation, and it is steaming, very slightly, in the cold. If you put your hand in it, it is warmer than the air.

These patches are the whole world inverted. The vast plain is empty. The pockets are overfull. Between them — nothing. Within them — everything.

Look closer and the lushness stops looking natural in any familiar sense. It works.

Every patch is a self-contained engine running at industrial scale, without a builder. The root-mats are conveyor belts, constantly trafficking minerals up from the stone and sugars down to the fungi. The fungi, white and intricate under the moss, are smelters and chemists: they break basalt, they fix nitrogen from the thin air, they produce heat by fermentation — that steam you felt — and they sort the results into chemical packets, piped through hyphae at a pace that would shame a factory. The leaves do not so much photosynthesize as harvest. They open toward the aurora, their undersides faintly conductive, gathering the charged light and the ozone that comes with it. When the aurora shifts, the whole patch shifts with it, thousands of fronds angling in concert, a slow wave across the mat.

Reproduction is mass production. At the edges of each patch, spore-columns rise and burst on a schedule set by the cold. Seeds are not scattered; they are launched, in tight, ballistic clusters, on the rare updrafts, to land in another hollow miles away and found a new factory, identical, instant, fully staffed. Growth happens not in spring but in the deepest dark, when the cold is most absolute, because the plants have learned to use cold as a catalyst. They grow downward into warmth and upward into light, simultaneously, an engine straddling two worlds.

And they are not alone in their efficiency. The fauna is abundant, but it lives where the plant lives, and it is almost silent about it.

Under the moss, there are thousands. Piston-legged mites that click into mineral seams and pump detritus backward to the fungi. Blind, translucent grazers that move across the leaf surfaces in herds, eating the factory's excess and fertilizing it with their passage. They have no need to call. The air is too thin and too cold for waste; sound would be waste. Predator and prey are not dramatic here. They are chemical and constant: some ferns are carnivorous, absorbing their grazers by the foot when they linger; some fungi exude a poison in winter that kills off the overgrazers, then recycles the bodies; the mites, in turn, shelter in casings that are themselves made of leaf-fibre and mineral, little armored houses they abandon and rebuild by the hour. Life is in motion at every scale, just not at yours. It is a food web turning over minute by minute inside a square meter of moss, while the plateau outside does nothing at all.

There are larger things, too, if you wait. High birds — or things that have decided to be birds — ride the aurora's edge in silence, wings beating so slowly they seem to row through the air. Toward evening-that-never-ends they descend, not to land, but to hook one leg into the moss and hang, upside down, while the patch feeds them static from its leaves. Nothing here was made for them. Everything here has learned to use everything else.

That is the strangeness that settles, slowly, in place of the first image of emptiness. Nothing is built. There is no road, no artifact, no door, no tool. Pick up any object and it is stone, or ice, or bone, or seed. And yet all of it works like something built: sorted, piped, powered, mass-produced, recycled with almost no loss. The glaciers far below grind for weeks at a time, presses the size of valleys, lifting nutrient dust and sifting it upward for the wind to carry. Geothermal cracks tick and exhale under the plain, punctuating the silence with a soft, internal rhythm, warming the undersides of patches from below. Freeze and thaw tap the stone in time. Aurora, glacier, root, fungus — it is one system running an industrial process on a planetary scale, with no engineer in it.

And it is active, relentlessly, despite the quiet. The aurora never holds still. The ice never stops grinding. The moss never stops fermenting, steaming faintly into a sky that will not let it rain. A plant at one edge of a patch will die, be absorbed, be resurrected as spores in the next burst. The whole patch, over a season, migrates a hand's width toward a new seam of damp stone, root by root, factory by factory, leaving a ghost-green trail behind it that will take a century to fade.

You leave the way you came, across the empty miles. The lushness does not follow you. The silence closes behind you like water. Above you, the black sky is still black, the twilight still not arriving, and behind you the patches continue to work in the dark, lush and cold and damp and utterly, industrially alive — a world that achieved everything we associate with machines and scale and production, long before anyone thought to build a machine, and without ever building one at all.
