The first thing is not land, but the wetness under it.

You are standing — ankle-deep, knee-deep, sometimes only toe-deep, depending on the season — in a plain so low and so old that water is never more than a step away. It does not arrive in rivers that rush. It is simply *here*: in the ground, under a film of clay, rising in hollows, beading along the edges of low stones and running off them in thin, patient threads. The rocks weep. The mud sweats. After rain, which comes often and usually without violence, the whole surface stays saturated for days, as if the earth itself cannot dry out. At dawn a low mist condenses and rolls across the flats, settles in the shallowest pans, and lifts again by mid-morning, but the air never becomes truly dry. It stays warm, close, and full of vapour. Your skin feels it. The water feels it. Everything is held in a warm dampness that softens all edges.

This is a landscape that has finished becoming.

Erosion here is not an event. It is the oldest condition. Hills that might once have stood have been worn down over spans too long to picture to mere swells — rises of a metre or two that you feel in your ankles more than you see with your eyes, extending for hundreds of paces before they subside again. There are no sharp ridges, no cliffs, no angular stones. Every pebble is a rounded thing, polished by water and grit until it looks healed. The sand is fine and leached pale. The clay is grey-brown and cracks in old hexagonal patterns when it dries, only to soften again and close the cracks. Black peat has built up in the lowest hollows, layer upon slow layer, like concentrated age. Even the channels are ancient and settled: lazy, braided sloughs perhaps twenty centimetres deep where they are deepest, meandering through the flat in wide loops, joining and dividing through sheets of water you can wade across without knowing you have crossed a watershed.

Because it is so flat, it is open in a way that goes beyond the immediate. The horizon is not a line but a distance — you can see for kilometres on a clear day, the light hazy and ordinary, the normal white-yellow of a sun filtered through humidity. Shadows are soft and brief. The scale is regional, multi-featured, but all the features are low-amplitude variations on the same theme. To the west the water stands more permanently, a chain of warm, shallow lagoons linked by films. To the east the clay pans dominate, bare for great stretches, patterned with old ripples and the faint trails of animals. In the north, peat hollows hold darker, stiller water where floating plants gather. In the south, those low stone outcrops rise just enough to be dry at the top, weeping at the sides, and turtles haul themselves onto them to warm in the sun. You could walk for a day and the world would not climb, would not change its character, would only repeat itself with subtle difference — another pan, another slough, another tuft — and that repetition is the point. It has settled into its final form.

And for all that openness, much of it is empty.

Vegetation is sparse, and deliberately so. The soil is too old, too leached, too constantly drowned for dense growth. Nutrients do not stay in the ground here; they wash out into the water. So plants grow in scattered clumps, only where the ground lifts a little higher or the water is a little less permanent: tufts of sedge and reed, low salt-tolerant shrubs with thick, waxy leaves, small rushes that tremble in the warm air. Between them — for metres at a time — there is nothing but wet mud, sand, and shallow water reflecting the sky. A few solitary trees stand far apart, gnarled and hollow, their roots in peat, their canopies thin. Most dead wood does not stand long; it falls, softens, and becomes part of the black ground again. There are no forests, no thickets to hide in. The density is balanced with emptiness. Your eye travels across bare ground to the next small green presence, and to the next, and to the next, with great silences in between — visual silences, at least.

Because what seems empty is not.

Life is abundant, but it lives mostly in and on the water, not in the plants. If the land is sparse, the shallows are rich. The warm, sunlit sheet water grows a green sheen of algae and biofilm across the mud, and that invisible pasture supports everything else. Small freshwater shrimp flick through the weeds in clouds. Tadpoles darken some pools so densely the bottom disappears. Minnows and mudfish dart and surface with a soft plip. Frogs and toads sit half-submerged, throats pulsing. Crabs work the mudflats sideways, burrowing in almost imperceptible movements. Terrapins paddle slowly in procession along the channels. Dragonflies patrol arm's length above the water, iridescent and tireless, and twice as many biting midges hang in the air where you stand, whining. Wading birds — long-legged, grey and white, with cries like torn cloth — pick their way far out on the pans, spaced widely, each one alone in its own stretch of water. Ducks and geese come in loose flocks to the lagoons. The fauna is not concentrated; it is spread out, as the sparse land requires, but in total it is everywhere.

And it is loud. Over a flat, wet plain, sound carries. At first light the frogs begin singly, a note here and there, and within minutes the entire basin joins them, layer upon layer, until the air vibrates with croaking. Birds answer — honking, cackling, whistling — and the insects sustain a steady high hum beneath it all. Add the constant small music of water: drips from sedge heads, splashes as fish turn, the squelch of a crab entering its burrow, rain drumming on the surface in a steady afternoon patter that turns the whole plain into a skin. Even the silences between animal groups are filled with sizzle and buzz. It is a loud world, not because any single creature is loud, but because so many small lives are simultaneously making themselves known.

Much of this happens slowly. Nothing here hurries. The dynamic is patient and weak-gradient: water rises an inch in a season, migrates across a pan over years, recedes and leaves a new sheen of algae. A footprint in the mud is not washed away in a day; it softens, fills at the edges, and may persist long enough to be fossilised in the peat. A channel may take a thousand years to shift by its own width. Plants grow, are cropped, are flooded, grow again in the same place. Animals move with the water's breath, not against it. The warm temperature accelerates decay and growth in the water, but on land the overall impression is of minimal flux — a system that has reached equilibrium and now simply continues, recycling its own detritus and leached minerals from mud to algae to animal to mud again.

There is no building here, and no sign of a tool. No path holds its shape for long. No artifact resists the damp. Nothing made stands against the water, because the water is the maker. The only structures are the ones animals build for themselves and abandon as quickly: burrows in the bank, nests of reeds trampled flat, mud walls that slump. Everything else is undeveloped, untouched, and biological in its maintenance — the plain keeping itself.

It feels, in the end, primordial not because anything is strange, but because nothing is new. The light is normal, the animals are ordinary kinds of frog and bird and fish, the rain is just rain. What is extraordinary is the age written into every surface: the rounded stones, the flattened contours, the black peat, the bare pans that have been bare for longer than any living thing can remember. It is a warm, wet, low world at the very end of its life as a changing landscape — not dying, but settled. A place where water lives near the surface forever, where plants are few because the earth is tired, and where, in compensation, the shallow water teems and sings without cease.
