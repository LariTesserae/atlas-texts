From far away it does not look like a city at all.

It looks like a plateau. High, broad, and cool, rising out of lower country in long, weather-softened steps. The sky above it is ordinary daylight — flat white-grey most of the time, breaking at midday into a clear, normal sun with normal shadows — but the air never quite dries. Even when it hasn't rained for hours it looks as if it has just finished raining. Every stone glistens. Every hollow holds a black, peat-stained pool that does not evaporate so much as slowly sink. The ground weeps.

This is a wet high place. Water is not in rivers here, mainly, though braided streams run everywhere once you are on top of it, threading between rocks, filling the grooves of streets, spilling over terrace edges in thin, persistent threads. Dig down half a metre anywhere outside the built ground and you find water. The water table sits just under the soil, and the soil sits just under the stone. At dawn the whole plateau sweats. Cold stone meets slightly warmer air and condenses a fine film across every wall and lintel and boulder. By noon the sun — ordinary and thin at this altitude — warms the upper surfaces and the condensation runs off in slow drips. By evening, as the temperature falls, it forms again. The place breathes damp in and damp out, every day, and no surface is ever fully dry for long.

It is vast enough to make its own weather. You can stand in one ward of the city in sun while, ten kilometres across the same plateau, another ward is inside a low cloud that has settled and will not lift until late afternoon. Rain here is often local: uplift from the plateau edge wrings mist out of the air, and the mist, once made, stays trapped in the stonework, drifting street by street rather than blowing away. Winter brings snow to the hollows, but it does not stay white long; it melts into the seep and becomes more damp.

The ground itself is old in a settled way. Nothing is sharp. Basalt and slate and pale limestone have been rounded by a long balance of rain, frost, and creep. Retaining walls lean a degree or two into the soil that holds them and then stop. Steps are hollowed concave in the middle by centuries of feet. Older buildings are not in ruin — they are low, slumped, moss-capped, lichen-coloured, as if the plateau has accepted them and they have accepted the plateau. Erosion here is not an event. It is the established state: stone wearing at the rate of a fingernail a year, soil moving millimetre by millimetre downhill, frost lifting a pavestone in January and October rain laying it back sleek.

Vegetation is sparse, and that surprises you at first, given all the water. There is no forest, no lush moor. What grows is tough and small and dark from saturation: cushion plants in scree, rosettes in wall cracks, tufts of stiff grass that bend more than sway, dwarf heather no higher than your ankle, lichen and algae painting wet rock green, black, and dull rust. The soil is thin over stone, the wind is cool even in high summer and cuts across the plateau unbroken, and much of the ground is covered — not by plants, but by construction. Where there is soil, it is usually held in a terrace, wet and black and carefully tended.

Only when you walk into it do the buildings declare themselves, and then they are not separate from the rock. This is a city spread across the entire plateau like a crust. It has no centre you can point to, only districts separated by miles of open, stony moor that are still, geologically, the same urban layer. Houses are made of the plateau it sits on: roughly coursed stone, cut stone for thresholds and channels, earth and stone packed thick against the cold. Roofs are usually the next street. Ground floors are sunk half below the level outside. A house's upper storey is another family's doorway. Chimneys, where they exist, are low and rare and give off almost no smoke; there is no glass to gleam, no wire, no lamp, no machine of any kind. Everything here — every channel, every stair, every aqueduct — was cut by hand and is kept by hand, moved by gravity alone. It is organized, layered, and overwhelmingly dense.

In the middle districts the density is near-touching. Shoulders of roofs almost meet over alleys so narrow you walk sideways with a pack. Courtyards are wells of stone capturing rain. Covered passages run for hundreds of metres under other buildings, damp and cool and completely silent except for water. Streets do not follow a grid; they follow the ancient lines of drainage. The lowest line in any area is always a channel, open, a finger-wide stream running in a groove cut into the stone, carrying roof-water and spring-water to a cistern. The highest points are dry by design: granaries, shrines, lookouts, places where goods are stored above the seep. Between them, terraces hold the plateau together — wall upon wall upon wall — so that walking up through the city is like walking up through the cross-section of the hill itself.

You can walk for days inside this extent without leaving the built layer. From a high terrace the city looks less like buildings and more like a pattern in the rock, pale walls darkened to near-black by wetness, threaded with the bright glint of a hundred small waters. Up close, individual houses are modest: one or two rooms, a low doorway, a shutter of stone slab, a small inner court. But they are stacked, abutted, filled in, extended, sunk, and rebuilt on foundations that are far older than the walls above them. The saturation is not of people. It is of presences — of stone faces, doorways, steps, channels, niches, hollows for jars, hollows for lamps that are never lit, handprints worn smooth in the plaster of a wall.

For that reason the place is very quiet. Sound, at 0.2 on any honest scale, dies quickly here. Mist absorbs it, wet stone absorbs it, thick walls absorb it. There is no traffic, no market cry, no machinery, no chorus of birds. You hear, if you listen: a single drip falling every few seconds from a spout somewhere above, landing in a cistern and echoing under a vault; the soft tick of a stone cooling after sun; cloth rustling on a distant figure in heavy woven wool; wind sighing when it finds a channel or a tunnel and goes through it like breath through a flute. The silence is not empty. It is a high, damp hush.

Life, other than stone and water, is rare. That is the second surprise. The city is dense, the water is everywhere, the light is normal — and yet you may walk a whole morning and see no animal larger than a lizard. A raptor will cross high and silent once in a while. Alpine choughs nest in empty niches. In the cisterns, in the still pools, there is pond life invisible unless you look closely. On the moor between districts, sparse sheep or shaggy goats move slowly, far apart, in dark wool. A few inhabitants move through the city — you will see someone at a channel, someone carrying stone, someone in a doorway keeping out of the mist — but they are slow, low-voiced, and they seem to be tending more than living here. Most doors are closed. Most houses are soaked and cold and only seasonally used. The city persists as architecture and hydrology more than as bustle.

And yet, in that quiet, everything is in motion. That is the rule here. Water is always moving: seeping up through a floor in spring, condensing at night, running off at noon, pooling, sinking, re-emerging two terraces down. Mist rolls through the streets at walking pace, clearing a courtyard and then filling it again. Frost heaves the pavements. Lichen grows over a doorway in a decade. A channel cleared by hand in the morning carries silt by afternoon. The plateau itself creeps, almost imperceptibly, toward its edges, carrying walls with it, leaning them and then bracing them with soil. Plants along a crack grow an inch in a cool summer and are cropped by wind in an hour. It is active the way a wet stone is active — not fast, not loud, but never still.

By evening the temperature drops another few degrees and the condensation returns, first on the high west-facing walls, then everywhere. The streams in the streets sound slightly louder in the cold. The city, which in daylight looked pale and patterned, becomes uniformly dark as its stone re-wets. There are no lights to mark it. From the moor above, it would disappear again into the plateau, except for the thin lines of water catching the last normal light, and the faint, low cloud that hangs over it because the city, vast and wet and high, makes and keeps its own weather —

— and will do so, patiently, for as long as the rain keeps falling on stone that has learned to hold it.
