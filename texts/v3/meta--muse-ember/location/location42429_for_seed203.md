There is no sky. There is a ceiling.

Far above you — a kilometer, maybe more — is a roof of ice. It is not a cavern roof of rock. It is the underside of a frozen crust, clear and fractured and thick as a mountain range laid flat, and daylight comes through it as if through a pane of sea-ice left out in a polar sun. The light falls straight down in broad, cold shafts. It is bright to the point of hurt. It bleaches everything pale that it touches, makes sharp black-edged shadows, and gives no warmth at all.

You are well below the surface of the world. The air is cold enough that your breath fogs and then freezes into a fine rime on your eyelashes, and stays there. The light is constant and merciless. The cold is constant and merciless. Between them, they kill any softness the place might have had.

And yet the ground is alive.

Not the ground *under* the plants, or the ground *between* them. There is no other ground. The whole floor of this hollow, for as far as you can see in any direction — and you can see for a very long way, because there is almost nothing to block the view — is plant.

It is a mat, inches to meters thick, of compacted fiber, shed leaves, root-hair and pulp, all pressed and frozen into a white, spongy peat. It gives under your feet with a sigh, and everywhere it is cut or worn it smells faintly of sap and fermentation and cold iron. Where it is undisturbed it is covered in a lace of frost-crystals that grow overnight from the damp air, sharp as broken glass, and do not quite melt even under the bright light. They just sink, glisten, and reform.

This is the soil, the rock, the sandstone, the architecture. Where the mat thickens into hills, those hills are made of interwoven roots. Where it arches, it is a root. Where it rots, it still feeds the next plant. There is no stone ecosystem here, no mineral crust to speak of. It is a pure-plant world that has long since buried its own geology.

And it is empty.

That is the second thing you learn, once your eyes adjust to the glare. For all that vegetation is total, it is spread out across a vastness that makes it feel sparse. A single plant here is not a tree. It is a continent.

The nearest one is an hour's walk away, and when you reach it, it is still rising. It is a low, broad dome, fifteen kilometers across, ridged like a frozen lily pad or the cap of a giant fungus, its surface covered in overlapping leaves the size of houses, pale green-white and hoarfrosted at the edges. From its rim hang curtains of roots and tendrils, some as thick as bridges, arching down to the mat and fusing with it. Inside the dome, if you go in under the arch, is a hollow as dry and quiet as a cathedral nave, lit only by the ice-ceiling far above leaking down through a crack in the leaves. The air inside is damp in a different way — condensed on the cold, curved walls of living tissue, beading and running slowly down in dark, sweet streaks.

There are thousands of these domes, discs, bulbs, and sprawling rhizome-islands, but they are so far apart. Between them are kilometers of empty mat-floor, flat and blank and bright, where nothing grows but frost and the fallen, translucent leaves that have drifted there and frozen into lenses. You can walk for a day between two plants and your horizon does not change, except that the two domes get minutely larger. The emptiness has its own physics here. Distances are not measured in steps but in the small weather systems each plant generates around itself.

The water is like that: present, but never abundant. There are no rivers, no lakes, no wet ground. There is only damp. Condensation forms on every cold leaf and drips, very occasionally, from the far ceiling in isolated drops that take a long time to fall. Once in a while, across one dome, a brief, fine rain shivers through — not from clouds, but from the plants themselves exhaling vapor as they warm under the light. Puddles gather only in the cupped centers of leaves, shallow and mirror-bright and already skinning with ice at the edges. The plants live largely on stored moisture, on mist, on the slow melt of frost. They are cactus-cold, desert-thrifty things, though the whole world smells damp.

The landscape is young. Everything is sharp.

Because the mat shifts, because the iceroof creaks and splits and heals, because the plants themselves are constantly cracking the floor open, new features are always appearing fresh, unweathered, unrounded. A fissure opens and its walls are a brilliant, unclouded blue, baruely covered in green. A dome buds off a daughter and the daughter slides out across the empty floor with a slow scrape, leaving a fresh furrow of white fiber behind it, edges still upturned. Shards of frozen sap litter the paths — clear, hard, sharp as pottery — that break and glitter underfoot. Nothing has had time to be worn smooth. Even ruins are sharp.

And it will not stay still.

That is the turbulence, and it is terrible in its quietness. Because the sound is almost nothing. The cold thickens the air and the spongy plant floor swallows it. You hear your own steps muffled, the faint tick-tick of sap freezing in a leaf seam, a groan from the ceiling ice once an hour like a continent settling. But the change is continuous.

A plant across the plain, too far to hear, unfurls. A hundred sail-sized leaves open in a ripple that travels across its dome like a wave over water, displacing air ahead of it. Another dome collapses inward — not explosively, but with a slow, exhausted folding, leaf over leaf, until it is a low, steaming mound, and then, over hours, it begins to push up again from the center, a green spear stabbing through the old tissue. Farther away, a spore eruption: a white plume rises from a fissure, catches the shafts of light, and expands into a slow snowstorm of seeds. In an hour it will whiteout the spaces between two domes, depositing a centimeter of future plant across kilometers of emptiness. The weather here is not moved by wind. It is moved by growth.

The plants breathe. Whole domes inflate in the damp cold of the "night" — there is no night, only a dimming in the ceiling light when the surface sun shifts — and deflate under full light, raising and lowering the ground by a meter over a region. Roots pulse. Tendrils lash and retract. To live here long, you would learn not to trust any slope, any arch, any path. A shelter you built last week might be half-swallowed this week by a root growing through its walls, still living, still warm from inside.

Life, other than plant, is rare to the point of being rumor.

In the root arches and in the hollows under the domes, in the damp mat where it is thickest, there are small things. Blind, pale crawlers with too many legs, no bigger than your thumb, that live entirely inside the fibers and rarely surface. A broad, flat grazer that moves like a slug and is the same color as the leaf it eats, so you only notice it when it moves. Something with wings that makes a sound like a sigh and never lands where you can see it. They are not an ecosystem. They are guests in one.

The builders — if there were builders — have left the same kind of sparse evidence as the fauna. Scattered across a few of the domes, far apart, are small structures. A hut lashed together from living tendrils, woven and tied with fiber cord, its roof a single large leaf stitched to the frame. A windbreak of upright root-stumps. A fire-pit that is cold and rimmed with ash the color of bone. On the floor between two plants, a faint mark: a line of stacked stones, or a carved signpost of hardened stem, pointing nowhere you can tell. Half of them are already being reabsorbed; the plant grows up through the floorboards, the leaf-roof seals shut, the tool left behind — a hand-axe of flaked bone, a knife of sharpened chitin, a needle of fish-bone — freezes into the mat and becomes indistinguishable from a shard of sap.

The technology is primitive because there is nothing else to make it from. No metal. No stone that is not borrowed from the rare shard. Everything made is made of plant, or of the rare animal that died in the plant. Everything made is temporary. The structures do not age. They are either fresh, or they are gone back into the substrate.

At midday — if you can call it that, when the light is at its most direct — the bright shafts come down at an angle through the cracks in the ice ceiling and turn the falling spore-snow into a slow, glittering storm that hangs in the air for a long time before it settles. The domes catch the light on their frosted rims and throw it back. Shadows are deep indigo-black and reach for miles. Sound, when it comes, is a soft exhalation of air displaced by a leaf turning over somewhere beyond sight.

You are standing on a living floor, in a sealed, cold, brilliantly lit hollow beneath a frozen world, surrounded by emptiness so large that each plant is its own horizon, each growth-spurt its own weather, each silence its own event. Nothing is old. Nothing is crowded. Nothing stays.

The place is not hostile. It is indifferent in the way a plant is indifferent — busy with a turbulence so vast and so quiet that it takes you hours to realize the landscape has entirely changed shape behind you.
