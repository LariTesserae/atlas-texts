It is wet first, before it is anything else.

You realize it through your feet. The ground is flat for longer than flat should be able to last, low and level to a horizon that never quite arrives, and everywhere underfoot is a hard, made surface that holds water instead of drinking it. Puddles lie in the expansion joints between slabs. Thin sheets of it run along gutters and curb-edges and straight, canal-straight channels. It weeps from brick retaining walls, beads and runs down the cool sides of pipes, drips steadily from tunnel ceilings and awnings, condenses on metal in the warm air and falls again. After an hour your shoes are dark. After an hour more, so are the hems of your trousers. The air itself feels about to rain, though the light overhead is ordinary daylight — a normal sun, a normal angle, normal shadows falling in normal directions — diffused a little by a low, warm, wet haze.

This is the Flatworks, because it had to be given a name, and because everything here is work of some kind.

There is no natural substrate. Dig, if you could dig, and you would not find soil. You would find layer upon layer of the made: poured concrete, slag and asphalt, tar, iron plate, glazed brick, copper pipe, ceramic tile, polymer sheeting, all in fresh, sharp strata — grey over red over black over white — like a geology made in a few decades and cut open with a knife. The distant “hills” are not hills. They are spoil heaps, stacked containers, smokestack clusters, roofscapes and viaducts softened only by distance and heat shimmer. The “cliffs” are trench walls and canal banks and the vertical sides of foundations, their edges still burr-sharp, unrounded. Nothing here has had time to soften, and in a way nothing here will.

That is the first rule, and the strangest thing once you stop noticing it: the place is both brand new and unchanging. It looks as if it were built last year. It will look the same next year. The water cannot erode it because it is impermeable by design; it runs off, it pools, it is pumped and piped and returned. Paint does not fade past a certain point. Rust blooms and stops. Concrete does not crack into soil because there is no soil to fall into. What would decay is repaired at exactly the rate it would decay, or else was never allowed to begin. Young, sharp, recently-emerged features persist forever.

There is no plant life. Not a tree, not a blade of grass, not moss in the joints, not ivy on a wall. The absence is total and has to be learned, the way you learn not to look for green. Tree-like things branch overhead — lamp standards, pylons, gantry cranes, cable runs — but they are iron. The damp corners are not green either. They are black with fungal mats in the vents and inside the pipes, iridescent with bacterial films in the warm water, whitened by mineral bloom where water evaporates off salty concrete. Life here is not photosynthetic. It is fed, heated, and housed by industry, and it does not grow outward into the world. It stays where it was put.

And yet life is overwhelming.

For all its emptiness of plants, the Flatworks is crowded to near-touching. You cannot set a hand down without it nearly landing on something made or alive: a bollard, a rail, a cable, a sleeping dog in a doorway, a cat stretched along a warm duct, a pigeon so thick on a ledge that the stone looks speckled. The density is layered, vertical as well as horizontal. Below the main level, canals and culverts carry fish in such numbers that the surface breaks and breaks again. At the main level, in pens and yards and loading bays that have become habitats, are herds and flocks of domestic animals gone semi-feral and more-than-feral — cattle and sheep and pigs and goats packed in motionless masses that shift like slow weather, poultry in their thousands, dogs and cats by the streetful. Above, the wires are black with birds, and the air above the wires is blacker still at intervals with flocks that wheel in one silent body and turn as one, as if on a mechanism. In the walls and under the eaves: spiders’ webs strung between artificial structures, beetles in the cavities, midges in clouds over every warm pool.

They are not hungry in any dramatic way, and they are not wild in any dramatic way. They are fed from the same industrial loop that feeds everything else: feed mills making pellets without pause, rendering warmth and waste water that feeds the bacterial films, that feeds the midges, that feeds the birds. No populations rise or crash. The herds breathe and shift a few inches and settle. The flocks circle the same blocks. The fish swim their straight reaches back and forth. It is abundance at equilibrium — overwhelming, and completely calm.

The industry is everywhere, but it is not the industry of sparks and shouting you might expect. It is industrial in the older, plainer sense: engines, factories, mechanized production, scaled, electrified, heavy. Diesel shunters move slowly along their set lines. Overhead gantries carry loads across wide yards. Tram and freight loops run on schedules that have evidently run forever. Smokestacks release mostly steam and warm, condensed vapour into the wet air, which is why the sky is never quite clear and why there is weather that belongs only to this place. Because the scale is vast — continental in feeling, a city without an edge, weather of its own — heat rises off the roofs and engines in one district, condenses under the high damp layer, and rains back in another, while a third district a day’s walk away stays in flat, close sun. The rain does not travel across the landscape. It stays in its district and falls and falls, a permanent local shower over far-off warm roofs.

You walk for hours and the horizon does not close. You walk for hours and you meet the same junction, or one so like it — the same slab pattern, the same lamp branching, the same weeping wall — that you cannot tell whether you have progressed or looped. Because in a sense, nothing progresses. Things move, constantly, but they move in circles, back and forth, up and down and back again. A conveyor feeds a hopper feeds a line feeds a store feeds a conveyor. Water flows down a gutter, is collected, is pumped back to the roof tank, flows down the gutter again. Exhaust rises, condenses, becomes water, becomes exhaust. Animals are born in the warm lower levels, live their allotted span in the traffic of the place, die and are removed by the same mechanisms without a trace. The sun, for its part, keeps a normal light but does not appreciably move its angle while you watch. It is always late morning. The same light that fell when you arrived falls when you have been walking long enough to forget when you arrived.

That is why it is mostly quiet. A steady state does not bang or crash or build to a crescendo. The great halls are insulated. The engines are designed for continuous running, not for effort. The birds at a distance make no sound at all, like fish. What you hear, once the initial dripping and soft footfalls on wet tile have taught your ear, is the close, low music of equilibrium: water ticking from a pipe, a distant electric hum at the edge of hearing, the soft shuffle of a herd settling, wings overhead, the periodic, undemanding clank of a shunt coupling far away. Sudden sounds would be an event, and events do not happen here.

The warmth is of the same kind. Not desert heat, not summer heat that breaks at evening, but a persistent, damp, greenhouse warmth, 2, 3 degrees beyond comfortable at all times, even in shade, even under water-dripping awnings. Metal is warm to the touch. Astone wall gives back heat. Animals pant lightly and do not move to cool themselves, because there is nowhere cooler to move to. Your own skin is always slightly damp from the condensing air. The place breathes warm moisture in and out and in again.

And so it holds together, all the contradictory pieces of it: wet but never washed away, warm but never hot, young but never aging, empty of plants but overfull of life, industrial but hushed, vast but close-packed, natural in its light and utterly unnatural in everything under it. If you stand long enough, the strangeness is not in any single thing — the straight canals, the plantless greenless streets, the silent flocks, the factories making feed to make animals to make waste to make heat to make rain to make water to run the factories — but in the small, constant sense that you are standing inside a moment that has been made to last. A newly built world, finished at last, and then held, perfectly, at the instant of its completion.

You will leave your footprints in the shallow water between the slabs. They will fill, and level, and be exactly as they were before.
