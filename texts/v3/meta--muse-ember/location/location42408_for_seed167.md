You are already below. Not arriving so much as sinking, until the sinking stops and you realize you have been still for a long time.

There is no surface here. There is no shore, and no sky to be under. Above you is only a slightly different blackness, then more blackness, the water going up as far as pressure can measure. Below you is the same water, thick enough to hold your weight, warm enough that your skin never cools. It is not a bath and not air. It is a substance. You breathe it through the membranes you were born with and it tastes on the tongue of iron, salt, and something sweetly rotten underneath.

The place is low. That is the first true thing about it. The ground doesn't rise to meet you in hills or ridges. It falls away in a shallow, immense bowl and then flattens, leveling, leveling, until the bottom of the world is a plain that goes on past any edge you could name. You could walk — swim, crawl, drift — for a day and still be in the same undulation, the same broad sway of silt. Only later, when the plain grows crowded, do you understand how far it extends.

The water is always moving, even when it seems not to. It comes in slow, warm pulses from below and slides away sideways in broad sheets no thicker than a forearm, carrying clouds of fine motes with a current that never completely stops. In those pulses the temperature changes against your skin: here a degree warmer, there a faint chill that vanishes. The warmest places are the cracks.

The cracks are not dramatic. There is no erupting fire here, no light to see it by — there is almost no light at all. The dark is so complete that your eyes, which in any case are small and sealed under skin because no use has ever been found for seeing, do not matter. You know the world by touch, by the pressure of water on all sides, by chemistry. A trail of mucus on the silt tells you a lane is used. A sudden sourness in the water tells you a vent is breathing. A soft, rapid vibration tells you something large has passed within arm's reach and is gone.

And things do pass. Constantly. The quiet is not emptiness.

You begin, at first, to think the quiet is emptiness because your ears, your one open sense still working in the dark, hear so little. Sound does not carry here the way it would in air. It is muffled, swallowed, turned into a low pressure against the skull. Most of the time there is only your own pulse, the faint creak of your own joints, the whisper of silt shifting under something unseen. Then, far off, a thump as regular as a sleeping heart. A click, crab-like, once, twice, again a little nearer. The sigh of a vent letting out a slow bubble into the current, though you cannot see the bubble. The faint rasp of a million tiny mouths at work on the mud.

That rasp is everywhere when you let yourself drift a little further in.

Because the plain is not empty. On the second day — if days mean anything where there is no sun to mark them, only the slower and faster pulses of the vents — you begin to brush things that do not move away.

They are low, too. Nothing here climbs high. Everything has settled into the shape of the basin. The ground itself forms soft mounds and shallow terraces, not rock outcrops but accumulations: years and centuries of what died, shed, excreted, and cemented itself in place. Shell gravel. Bone. Dull grey mineral crust from the vents, layered like bark. Amber resin secreted and hardened. Translucent membranes stretched between pillars of living tissue. It is all rounded. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is new, and nothing is old enough to be called a ruin. That is the strange consistency of it: every form has been weathered down to the same softness, sagged to the same angle, draped in the same fine sediment until the built and the natural can no longer be told apart except by a practiced hand. A stranger might lie down on what is, to inhabitants, a ten-generation-old wall and think he was sleeping on a hill.

This is a city. But there is no work of hands here, no metal, no fire, no tool but a tooth or a claw that grows from a body for a purpose already given. No one has ever made a light to see by, because no one has ever needed it. No one has ever made a road, because the lanes are worn into being by the passage of bodies. The inhabitants build the way a reef builds, the way a coral — though there is almost no true coral here, and no green growing thing worth naming — the way a colony of sponges and barnacles and tube-dwellers and soft, blind grazers build by simply living in one place, adding a little of their own substance to the mound beneath them, and dying and leaving that substance for the next.

The houses are domes. Some are the size of a room, hollowed inside, with an opening just wide enough for three bodies to pass shoulder to shoulder. Some are stacked in tiers, chamber on chamber, the lower chambers cooler and darker and half-filled with silt where the old ones sleep, the upper chambers warmer and kept clean by the constant filtering of the residents. There are long, low arcades where the current is strongest: walls of interlocked shells forming a sort of colonnade, through which water rushes and is cleaned, and in that rush hundreds of small, feathery mouths open and close in a rhythm you feel in your fingertips even if you cannot hear it.

There are bridges, too, but they are not built across anything. They are living ropes of muscle and membrane strung between two mounds, used to cross the vent-cracks where the water is too hot and too chemical to linger. They sway, constantly, slightly. They are repaired by the animals that live in them, little pale worms that weave new fibers as old ones fray.

And there are towers. Not high, but higher than anything else in this flat country — chimneys of mineral and shell, perhaps three, four, five meters, narrowing and widening irregularly, belching in pulses. At their tops they are soft and fragile, crusted with bacterial mats that look, in the dark, like nothing at all — a slickness under the hand, a faintly green-black film. These are the only green things. Vegetation, if you want to call it that, is sparse to the point of absence. No forests. No meadows. A few stringy, whip-like fronds near the vents, tasting of sulfur, rising and falling with the current. A few pale fans that might be plants and might be colonies of animals pretending. The rest of life has given up on making leaves.

This was, presumably, negotiated long ago. In the total dark, green is a waste.

What does not give up is animal life. It is abundant to a degree that becomes a physical fact. You cannot move through the central districts without touching something alive.

A blind, eel-like filterer slides past your leg, its body the thickness of your wrist, skin like wet corduroy. A crowd of translucent shrimp, no bigger than your thumb, hover in a cloud over a vent terrace, their legs beating so fast you feel it as a breeze. A crab the size of a plate walks delicately over a dome, testing each step on the shell for weakness, and then, finding none, settles to feed on the film growing there. Below, in the silt lanes, a train of molluscs moves in single file, each following the chemical trail of the one ahead, hundreds long, a slow river of soft bodies. Overhead — if overhead means anything in a place where up is just less bottom — a school of fish turns as one, blindingly white in the faint chemical sense, then turns back.

Everything is close. Layered. The domes touch each other. The arcades open into other arcades. The living bridges cross the open lanes. The filterers swim through the houses as readily as through the open water, and the house-dwellers, soft and slug-like and eyeless, come out at intervals to graze the outer walls, their mouths rasping at algae-slicks and bacteria and the fallen snow of the dead.

There is, always, a snowfall. Particles sifting down from the upper blackness, from places far above where no light has ever reached but where things die all the same. You taste it when the current turns. The inhabitants taste it too, and open. That is the economy here, and the law.

Nothing stays hard if it is not constantly lived in. Anything abandoned begins, within a generation, to melt. The silt takes it. The bacteria take it. The little rasping mouths take it. Walls slump. Domes lose their roofs and become open bowls, then mounds, then indistinguishable from the natural terraces of the plain. This is not decay — decay implies an end. Here it is balance. The city is never finished and never falling apart. It is in its own state, settled, weathered, because occupation is the only mortar. Where there are bodies, there is architecture. Where there are no bodies, there is only the plain again, a little higher now.

The bodies are the architecture in a more literal sense, too. When a large builder dies, its shell is not removed. It is incorporated. A whole row of older houses in the south district is made of the interlocked carapaces of a single species of armored grazer, each carapace curved like a shield, keyed into the next by the animal's own living descendants who cement themselves to the bone of their ancestors and add their own shells on top. To walk there is to walk under a ceiling that is also a graveyard that is also a home.

The dynamic of the place is in its pulses. The vents breathe in cycles, perhaps daily, perhaps not. When they breathe out, the water above them warms, the chemical scent sharpens, and a great many small things rise to feed on what is carried up. When they rest, the great many small things settle, the predators come out in force, and a different, larger choreography begins: the eel-filterers patrol the lanes; the crabs dispute territories on the domes, tapping signals through the shell; the fish-school turns and turns, herding the shrimp toward the mouths they are not, in the moment, trying to escape from.

There is predation, of course. Loud in its own quiet way, because a strike is a sudden displacement of water, a shock you feel in your chest. Then quiet again. The remains are eaten. The shells remain.

That is the city, extending. Because it never really ends. The dense center, where you cannot move without brushing a neighbor and the domes are three and four thick, loosens gradually outward into districts of single domes, then into fields of chimneys with no houses between them, then into the open plain where only the vent-cracks and the wandering herds make a mark. It is a town the size of a valley, made by no one in particular, and it goes on past the valley.

It is warm, it is dark, it is close, and it is silent — except for the work. The rasp of mouths. The click of crabs. The slow thump far away, which might be a vent, and might be something larger, sleeping under the plain and dreaming in pressure waves.

You could stay here. Many have. They do not leave, because there is nowhere to leave to that is not more water, more dark, more plain. They add their own substance to the mound. Their house will be soft. Their house will be round. In a few hundred years it will be indistinguishable from the hills, which were never hills at all, but only the place itself, settled into being.
