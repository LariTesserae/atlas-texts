The light comes first, and it does not warm you.

It is a bright, cool, white light — not the yellow of a sun seen through air, but the clean glare of a place with almost no air to carry heat away. It is noon everywhere, and morning everywhere. It bleaches the high places, bounces up from the low ones, and is filtered, flickered, and thrown back again by leaves until the whole vast plain seems to be glowing faintly from inside. You squint without ever quite needing to. Even in the deepest shadow under a factory-tree the air stays luminous, as if light has learned to travel through the green the way current travels through a wire.

Your coat is still necessary. The temperature never rises out of that cool, dry range. Sweat never quite forms. Your mouth is always a little dusty. That is the first rule of this place: there is no water.

Not a little water. No water. No rivers, no lakes, no rain, no dew, no rot. The low places that look from a distance like frozen ponds are basins of white silica powder, smooth and blinding, that crunch dryly underfoot and puff up like flour when a wind crosses them. Plants do not sweat. They exhale a fine glassy dust — a dry perspiration of mineral — and that dust settles, gathers in the hollows, is swept into drifts, and is gathered again by the veined leaves that drink it back. When a plant dies it does not soften or smell. It desiccates, stiffens, shatters into nutrient ash. That ash is the weather.

This is the Verdfoundry, and it is vast in a way that has nothing to do with mountains. The land is low and essentially flat, a continent built a few meters at a time on top of itself. You can stand on one of the loom-towers and see no edge. Green-industrial green to the bright haze, layered with pale strips of road, white salt pans, and the slow dark smudge of a spore-storm moving forty kilometers away — a weather system that will never leave the district where it was born, because the district is permanent enough to have its own climate.

Almost nothing here is natural, and almost nothing here is merely made. The distinction has decomposed long ago, which is perhaps the most accurate way to describe its erosion. This place is old. Not ruined — settled. Its steel has softened at the edges where roots have held it for three hundred years. Its brick roads have sunk into the root-mat and re-emerged a street over. Factory halls have been half swallowed, their lower floors turned into root cellars and storage for dry husk, their upper floors become dwellings. Corners are rounded. Rust has been composted into a green patina and that patina has been woven back into wall. Nothing collapses; everything is metabolized.

The ground is not earth. It is a pavement of everything that has ever been grown or built here, pressed and re-pressed: root-tile, ceramic leaf, ligno-fiber board, dry mineral brick, pollen wool. It gives a little under the heel, then holds. In the dense districts it is warm-cool, never damp, and everywhere veined with living green. Moss-like mats creep over it, not to decorate it but to repair it. Walk a factory floor and the floor is alive. Walk a street and the street is tended, pruned back from the drainage channels — dry channels, lined with electrolyte vines — in a continuous, silent process.

Vegetation here is not a cover. It is the substrate, the architecture, and the population. By volume, by mass, by living presence, the place is plant. Not trees *in* the city. Trees *as* the city.

Houses are grown hollow trunks, bolted at the seams with industrial ribs, their windows membranes of leaf-glass that open and close with the light. Towers are aerial forests wired together by vine-bridges, their canopies raked into flat solar sheets that drink light and turn it, by a dry chemistry of silicates and current, into sap that is not sap but a conductive fluid. Roots are not only for anchoring; they are cable, they are rail, they are conveyor belt. A root-rail tram passes an inch above a woven track, utterly silent on magnetic lift, its sides covered in lichen that is really a thin factory. Below it, another level down, are the root-wires, threading between foundation stones, humming at a frequency you feel more than hear.

The industry is not hidden inside all this. It *is* the metabolism. In the guild-halls of the loom-districts, vines are fed through frame-looms and woven into cable, into insulation, into clothing, into load-bearing cloth. In the hollow trunks of the old kiln-trees — kilns that have never given off heat, because heat is a waste the green cannot afford — dry husk is pressed into bricks. When a thing is harvested, it is not cut down and removed. It is un-woven, un-bolted, un-grown in stages and fed back into the next cycle as ash, as dust, as current. The pruners are the great machines, and also the great animals-that-aren't: slow, jointed rigs that move through the canopy on branch-rails, trimming, thinning, grafting. The plants, in turn, eat the machines — slowly corroding their joints with acid-dry secretions, absorbing their metal into leaf and trunk, rebuilding them as part of a larger, slower body.

It is dense beyond any natural forest. Seven, nine levels deep. Root level, ground level, factory level, dwelling level, canopy level, sky-loom level, and the twilight level of vines that have bridged tower to tower so high they are silhouetted against the white. Near-touching is the normal condition. You brush past a wall of living panel, past a pipe that is also a stem, past a window that is watching you with a leaf that happens to be folded like an eye. The air is still because everything that might stir it is dampened: leaves, felted husk walls, root earth. No water to drip, no birds to call, no engines to roar.

That is why it is so quiet, in a place that never stops moving.

Sound here is a scarce, almost heroic thing. The factories run on charged sap and magnetic fields. The trains glide. The looms tick, once, like a distant clock, and then are swallowed by the hush. What you hear, mostly, is close: the dry rub of leaf on leaf, the faint ticking of a cooling trunk, your own footstep on root-tile, your own breathing, which is drier and louder than it should be. A worker will sometimes carry a small bell, not for signaling, but because after weeks in the Foundry a human ear begins to ache for proof that the world is still physical. The bell rings, startlingly clear, and then the green takes the sound back and finishes it.

And yet, in that silence, everything is active. The flowering wave is a tide. Once each cycle — a cycle that has nothing to do with a sun and everything to do with current and pruning — a band of blossom moves across the continent at walking speed, district by district. Leaf-panels turn luminous. Pollen, which is also nutrient dust, also ash, rises in pale clouds that darken the light an hour before they fall. A spore-storm is not rain. It is a blizzard of dry potential. It can bury a road overnight and sprout it, by morning, in a new pattern. The patterns are catalogued, photographed, and largely ignored, because no map stays accurate for a week.

Ecology here has only two trophic levels, and they are eating each other politely. The green eats light, current, and mineral dust. The built eats the green. The green eats the built. Fauna — true animal life — is vanishingly rare, a rumor and a kept thing. There are no wild birds. There are no insects in the wild looms. In a sealed vat in one of the old research trunks, a colony of brass-blue pollinator beetles is kept alive in dry amber gel, released for a few hours a year when the machines stutter. On the roof-gardens, the glass-sheep graze — stationary, plant-topped grazers bred small and woolly for the pollen-felt hats of the pruners, known by name to everyone who lives near them. In a window in the Pillar District a cat sleeps on a warm generator housing. Everyone knows the cat. The cat is an event.

That rarity makes the strangeness more legible, once you stay long enough to notice it.

Because the world is all built and all grown, and has been composted and rebuilt in place for millennia, causality has begun to loop and fray. Shadows fall from buildings that have not yet been grown. A street you walked yesterday leading to a loom-hall will today lead to the same hall, but the names of the workers on the door will be different, and they will greet you as if you have always known them. Some plants grow, unmistakably, into the shape of a demolished factory, complete with chimney-stems, and the gardeners leave them standing for a season before harvesting them for parts. The maps woven into the root-tile along the main avenues update themselves, usually in the wrong direction, and erase themselves in the night. Distance contracts when a pruning is done and expands when the flowering wave passes. You can hear your own step a half-second before it lands, but only if you are listening, and the green will take that sound too.

It is not magic, exactly. It is what happens when a closed system has been running perfectly, silently, for longer than anyone kept records. The Foundry has corrected its own errors by growing over them. The errors have become features, and the features have become ancestors.

Stand at dusk — though there is no dusk, only a dimming of the white to a grey-green — and look across the salt-pan. The light is still bright enough to read by, cool on your skin. The root-trains glide past without a sound. Somewhere far off, a loom ticks once. A plant you cannot see is finishing a building that was torn down before you were born. The air smells of ozone, cut leaf, hot dust, and ceramic. No water has ever fallen here, and nothing here has ever needed it.

Everything that is built here will be composted. Everything that is composted here will be built again. And in between, endlessly, quietly, in cool bright air, it grows.
