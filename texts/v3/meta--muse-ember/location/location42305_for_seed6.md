First there is water, and it is deep enough to be a place rather than a weather event. It takes you at the waist as you step down from the entry platform, and it is warm — bath-warm, body-warm — though the light above it is plain, ordinary midday light, white and hard, casting sharp shadows through the clear water and onto the tops of things that break the surface. The water is not a puddle or a flood that happened to a hill. It is held, planned, and everywhere. It laps, muffled and slow, against steel and concrete. Ripples move a little too thickly, as if the water has been sweetened or thickened with nutrients, and they die a few inches from where they begin.

You can see the end of it in every direction. That is the first strangeness, after the warmth. The whole landscape is small — hamlet-size, perhaps two hundred steps across — bounded on all sides by a continuous ring wall, terraced inwards like a flooded amphitheatre. In the centre a stepped spire rises, made of tanks and greenhouse roofs and chimneys, its highest decks dry and warm in the sun. From up there you understand the second strangeness: you are high. Far above the ring wall there is nothing but air, and below that, a slow sea of cloud. The water surface you are wading in is at summit level, perched on a built shelf over the world. A high place that is submerged. A shallow ocean that fits inside a town.

There is no natural substrate here, not at your skin and not anywhere you can look. What appears to be ground is poured concrete decking and resin floors, grooved to drain. What appears to be rock is cast, pale, and unfinished-looking. What appears to be soil is a bed of black ceramic granules in plastic trays. The slopes are steps. The banks are gutters. The walls are welded steel, glazed tile, glass, rubber cable, painted machinery. Even the hill under the shelf is capped and concealed by a platform of piles and slabs and bracing — you never touch a stone that was not poured, cut, or fastened. Everything is made, and everything is new.

That newness is physical. The concrete edges are sharp enough to catch a wrist. The welds are bright silver. The enamel on the machines — teal, safety-yellow, dull red — is still glossy and unchipped. Gear teeth stand exposed and oily but unworn. Leaf edges, where the growth comes close, are as crisp as cut plastic. This is a recently emerged place, recently filled, recently switched on. It has not been rounded or smoothed by weather. It has not had time to fall apart.

And it is packed. There is no room between presences. Each terrace is a factory floor, and each floor is full from wall to wall: rows of vertical tanks, distillation columns, vat after vat, electric motors humming under housings, pipes running like vines along the ground and up the walls, catwalks submerged and re-emerging, conveyors moving at a pace that is almost stillness. Trays are stacked on top of tanks. Cables hang in festoons that have rooted themselves in planters below. A tank lid is a garden bed. A gutter is a planter. A machine housing has split along a seam and put out a spray of green. The industrial scale is in the volume and the repetition — hundreds of identical units, electrified, mechanized, scaled for production — but the tempo is slow. One conveyor advances a new module once an hour. A pump gives a low thud roughly every minute, and then the water takes the sound away. A bubble rises from a submerged valve and takes a long time to reach the light.

The vegetation is lush to the point of excess, and it is not wild. It grows from manufactured crevices, because there are no others to grow from. Under the surface, clear enough to see them, are underwater meadows of feathery fronds waving in the current of the pumps. At the surface, floating mats of broad paddle-leaves drift against the tanks and rot very slowly into the nutrient water. Everywhere else, in trays and pipe-joints and planter-boxes bolted to walls, are dense green growths: fern-like fronds, coil-vines climbing the spire in spirals, pale new shoots, dark mature leaves, things that lean toward the normal daylight and things that lean toward the white work-lights wired into the undersides of decks. The air smells of wet concrete, warm metal, greenhouse leaves, and the faint ozone of electric motors. It is humid and warm long after high places should be cold; the engines and the stored water keep it so.

For all that green, nothing animal lives here. No fish move under the clear water. No insects rise from the leaves. No birds cross the small sky above the ring, though there is sky enough. The lushness is silent in a way that makes itself felt after a while — not empty, but sterile and botanical, a jungle without a single animal motion in it. The only movements are water, leaf, machine, and the slow expansion of growth against metal, a soft ticking brush now and then when a frond presses a housing and slides.

The rules of the place have become tangled because the plant and the machine have become tangled. The same warm water is reservoir, coolant, nutrient solution, and mist. Pipes carry it uphill to planters on the highest terraces, where it is released in a slow, artificial rain that trickles down through the growth and back to the pool. Condensation forms on the glass and steel of the spire and drips steadily, never enough to be called weather. Some of the “plants” are leaf panels, wired into the grid, veined like leaves and drinking light to make power. Some of the machines have begun to photosynthesize, their housings shaded and fed by the growth they were built to tend. The Works makes more of both: vats raise leaf panels, presses stamp root-cables, assemblers bolt new plant-modules together, and then, over months, those modules root in a tray and stop looking manufactured at all. It is a factory whose product is its own overgrowth, and the overgrowth in turn runs the factory.

Time moves slowly here, more slowly than the newness would suggest. The water level changes by centimeters in a year. A flower — a valve-like bloom on a vine grown around a pipe — opens over a season and closes over the next. The bubbles rise slowly. The light stays normal and unchanged from hour to hour, filtered only slightly by water and glass, so that noon looks like noon even when you are waist-deep in a heated reservoir on a mountaintop. Sounds arrive muted and late. Change, when it comes, will be patient: the sharp edges will in decades begin to soften, the bright paint will dull, a tank will be colonized so completely it reads as a tree. For now, it remains fresh, warm, submerged, and full — a small, high, entirely built garden of slow industry, standing in its own warm water under ordinary light, green everywhere, and animal-quiet.
