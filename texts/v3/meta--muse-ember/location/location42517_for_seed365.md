It is not a valley, though it holds itself like one. It is the top of a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain, and the world is the bowl that remains where its peak broke off.

You climb out of cloud to reach it. Below, there is no landscape at all — only a white sea that moves slowly at your feet, up to the horizon, with no edges you can see. The path is switchbacks cut into sharp rock, and by the time you reach the rim your breath is short and shallow and your hands are cold. Then the rim opens, like a door, and you step into a room of air.

The bowl is small. From one side of its rim to the other is perhaps twenty minutes' walk, and you can always see the far wall. That is the whole place: one crater, one grove, one town, bounded on every side by a ring of black teeth. Above it is not sky but the underside of a cloud ceiling that hangs low and does not go away. The light it makes is flat and pearl-grey, a perpetual late afternoon inside a shell. No sun disc ever appears. Now and then the ceil brightens a little, as if something large is turning behind it, and then dims again. Nothing here is ever bright, and nothing is ever quite dark.

The rock is young. That is the first thing you learn with your feet. There is no softening, no rounding, no sand. Every edge is a fresh fracture, glassy in places where the basalt has split and shows its black interior. Pumice crunches underfoot and then gives way to obsidian shards that would cut a bootsole. Cracks hiss faintly when they settle — the mountain is still rising, still cooling, still adjusting by millimetres — and a terrace wall that was straight in the morning may be leaning by evening. The town is not really built *on* the mountain so much as perpetually rebuilt *against* it, stone by stone, by hand.

Because it is high and cool, and because it sits in cloud, it is always damp and never wet.

There is no river. There is no lake. Water does not, here, run downhill. It arrives sideways, as breath. At dawn every surface — stone, leaf, roof, your own forearms — is beaded with condensation, large clear drops that cling to the underside of things and spread slowly inward, uphill, toward the coldest stone. It pools in hollowed lapilli only a palm wide, appears, and vanishes an hour later. In the late afternoon the mist thickens and passes through the bowl like something exhaling, and for a while everything is wetter without any rain ever falling. People collect it the way they collect everything else here: with a primitive, organized patience. Woven nets are stretched on poles in the empty fields, stone troughs are cut into the terraces to draw the clinging drops inward by gravity and by touch, cupped rocks are left with their mouths up on roofs. A household may go weeks without anything that would be called rain, and yet nothing is ever truly dry. Your boots are always damp. Moss is always damp. The air tastes faintly metallic and green.

Vegetation is lush, but it does not crowd. That is the other surprise. The bowl is mostly *empty* in the human sense: broad stretches of bare sharp rock, open moss meadows, wide streets with no one in them. Between those emptinesses, however, life grows with a sudden, extravagant vigor. A mountain fern may be ten metres across, single and enormous, its fronds silver-green and wet. A tree — pale-barked, cool-climate, slow to branch — will stand alone in a field of stone, its canopy a single vast umbrella, its roots thrusting *upward* through cracks to drink the condensation directly. Then there will be nothing for thirty paces, and then another tree, another fern island, another thick mat of sponge-moss inches deep and springy underfoot. The moss is the soil and the carpet and the roof-thatch and the silence-maker of the place. Where there is cover and moisture, it is fat and soft and endlessly renewed. Where there is wind or fresh rock, there is nothing at all. Lushness here is not a forest. It is a series of intense, isolated fullnesses, with great silences of stone between them.

The town sits in the middle of that, and it is unmistakably a town, despite its size.

It is laid out, not grown. A low wall of uncut black basalt runs the entire rim, pierced by four gates which are always open. Inside, the streets are straight, paved with broken pumice slabs, and they divide the bowl into districts: the weavers' terraces below the granary, the stone-cutters' yards near the sharpest quarry, the herders' houses by the east gate where the animals come through. There is a raised storehouse on stone pillars, a market hollow that is padded with moss and is empty most of the day, a watch cairn of stacked rocks at the highest point from which a flag — because sound does not carry — signals the coming of a migration. Houses are one room, sometimes two, built of stones fitted without mortar, locked together like puzzle pieces, roofed with sewn mats of fern and hide. Chimneys are rare; hearths are small and smoky and hoarded against the cool. A house may be rebuilt three times in a season when the ground shifts.

The technology is hand technology. Obsidian knives, bone needles, cold-worked copper awls, wooden looms, rope bridges of woven moss-fibre slung across the young chasms that keep opening in the rim. There are no wheels — they are useless on fractured rock — and no iron. Goods are carried on backs and on flat sledges. Tally-sticks keep the accounts of water nets and roofs. The people speak low, and often not at all, using hands and flags and tapped stones. It is an urban place, dense with infrastructure — fences, troughs, terraces, granary, walls — but sparsely inhabited. At noon the streets can be entirely empty, the houses widely spaced, the whole settlement feeling like a plan for a larger city that never arrived.

Because in proportion to the people, the animals are overwhelming.

You are never alone here, and you are never, strangely, disturbed. The bowl is full of movement that produces no noise.

Highest and most numerous are the drifters. Translucent, pale ribbons one to three metres long, they hang in the mist like slow kites or deep-sea eels that have learned to swim in air. They have no wings, no eyes, no mouths that matter; their skins drink the condensation. When they are full they exhale it, not as rain but as a fine, deliberate mist of droplets that settles on fern and moss below. Thousands of them may be present at once, drifting up and down with the slow breathing of the cloud, parting silently around a person and closing behind them. In the dim light they are almost invisible until you are inside them, and then the whole bowl seems to be filled with slow, glassy weather.

Below them, on the ground, move the carpet-grazers in slow, living waves. Goat-sized, blind, velvet-grey, with soft sponge feet that make no sound on moss, they eat the moss meadows in broad, straight passes, leaving a clean trail behind them like a mown path. A herd may be hundreds strong, flowing from one fern island to the next, and they will pass through the town's streets, through open gates, under houses, without hurry and without a single call. When they feed, faint patches of light bloom along their flanks — a silent language of touch and glow. The people let them pass. The grazers are the town's other inhabitants, and their routes are as fixed as the streets.

And above and among them, patient and thin, are the hunters: long-legged, long-necked things that descend from the rim rocks on tendril-thin limbs, that have no eyes either, that eat the drifters by snapping with hollow, trumpet-like tongues. They too are silent. Their predation is a kind of slow ballet in the grey light, a neck extending, a ribbon vanishing, no cry, no splash, no sound at all. Smaller things fill the cracks between: moss-crabs the size of a thumb that scuttle sideways through fern thatch, clouds of blind moths that erupt from the moss at dusk and settle again, worms that live inside the rock fractures.

It is an ecosystem in constant motion — condensation and evaporation, grazing and regrowth, migration and hunt, the mountain settling, a roof being re-sewn, a fern unfurling, a grazer sloughing its fat outer skin in the spring to become, some say, another drifter. Nothing stays still for long. But because the moss absorbs sound, because the animals have evolved for a world of fog where a call would be wasted, because their feet and wings and bodies are all soft, the motion is almost perfectly quiet. The loudest things in Neth Hollow are the occasional drip of water from a roof, the soft thud of a stone settling into its crack, the rustle of a fern when a grazer pushes through, and your own breathing, too loud in the thin air.

At night it gets a fraction darker, but not much. The drifters glow very faintly as they sleep in the mist. The grazers' flank-lights pulse slowly. The hearths smoke. The cloud ceil stays. The town settles into its damp, cool quiet, waiting for the condensation to come again at dawn and bead everything.

The rules, if you stay long enough to learn them, are simple, and they hold the place together:

Water does not flow here; it clings and is given back.
Sound does not travel here; it is taken by moss and by soft bodies, and is replaced by light and touch.
And nothing — not rock, not roof, not animal, not tree — stays in one shape or one place for long. Everything is young, and everything is moving, and the whole small city at the top of the world is a brief, carefully built clearing in the middle of that motion.
