First there is the sound, before you see the water.

You are on the rim, and the air is cool and thin in the way it is at altitude, the sun ordinary and bright and a little merciless. The rock under your boots is not rock as you expect rock to be. It is black volcanic glass, freshly broken, still sharp enough to take the skin off a glove. Columns of it rise out of the rim like organ pipes snapped off, and there is no soil between them, no moss, no lichen worth the name, no softening. It looks new. It is new. The mountain has not yet had time to crumble into gentleness.

And through the glass, through your soles, through your teeth, a pounding. Low, regular, enormous for the size of the place. Like an engine hall heard from outside a wall. Like a crowd stamping in a cathedral.

Only when you lean over do you understand why.

The crater is full. Not full of land, not full of rain that will drain. Full to the brim with water, a flat, cold, breathing surface that comes up almost to where you stand, so that there is never a shore. No beach, no reeds, no reeds' roots, no green anywhere. Your eye keeps looking for green, because a bowl of water that size should have something growing at its edges. There is nothing. The glass goes straight down into it, naked. A few pale threads in the cracks, fungal and not plant-life at all, and a sour black film in places on the glass itself. Nothing that photosynthesizes in the way a leaf does. Nothing that would be called vegetation by any creature that has ever seen a tree.

You slip down one of the glass pillars, careful, and the water takes you all at once. It is cool throughout, the temperature of deep shade, and it does not let go. Above you, the light is perfectly normal — a normal day, sun high, shadows falling normally — but once you are under, it is changed. It comes down in bright bars, it shatters on things, and it never gets far before it hits something else.

Because there is almost no empty water here.

That is the first rule of the place, and you learn it with your body. Your hand does not enter water. It pushes through bodies. The place is small — you can see the other side, no more than two hundred paces across, the rim all around you like the wall of a room you cannot leave — but it is saturated. Crowded to the point of touch, layered past what seems possible. There are three layers, and they are all touching the next one.

At the bottom, on the sharp glass, is the carpet. Bivalve things the size of fists, shell to shell, locked flank to flank in rows that follow the contours of the bowl. They look, at a distance, like a crop planted by something careful. Up close they are not plants. They are animals. Each one opens and closes at the gill with a click that is small in itself, but they do not each open when they like. A wave goes around the bowl. One row pumps, then the next, then the next, all the way around, back to the start, like the cylinders of a rotary engine turning very slowly. A hundred thousand pumps, staggered and perfect. That is the pounding you heard from the rim. You feel it in your ribs. The whole pool is breathing as one machine, and the surface, far above, rises and falls by a thumb’s width with each cycle.

Between the carpet and the surface swims another kind of animal, larger, paler, slow beyond belief. They do not dart. They do not hurry. They drift in lanes, mouths gaping, turning their whole bodies with a roll that takes a full minute. You can watch one for as long as you like and see no progress at all, and yet when you look away and look back they are somewhere else, further along the lane, their place taken by another. They are threading the pump-wave, filtering what the carpet has pumped out, and what they pump out is taken up by the next lane. Everything is downstream of something else.

On the surface itself, holding each other up, is the third layer. Flat-bodied floaters, back to the normal light, skin to the air, side to side, making a raft that is not a raft of wood or plant but of living shoulders. They do not swim. They cling. If one slips, two neighbors lean and take its weight. Their backs catch the sun and pass it down through translucent tissue in a way that is animal and not plant, strange and slightly obscene to look at in close-up — light inside a body, being used by a body, without a leaf ever having been involved.

And that is how everything eats here, because there are no plants to eat. The black film on the glass is not algae. It is microbial, chemosynthetic, feeding on iron and sulfur that leach, very slowly, out of the young rock. Grazers scrape it with rasping mouthparts. The scrapings go into gut-factories in the pump-animals and come out the other side as pellets. Perfect round pellets, like ball bearings, thousands upon thousands of them rolling in the hollows of the glass. No stone ever rolls that round by itself. These are made.

Down there on the bottom, in lines head to tail, are the passers. I cannot give them a better name. They take a pellet in their mouths and pass it to the next animal in the line, who turns it, who sucks it, who passes it on. One transfer may take an hour. A pellet may travel the whole circle of the bowl in a month, growing a lining, being refined, being polished by stomach acid and by teeth. It is an assembly line without a factory, production on a scale that would shame a factory, and not one thing in it was built.

Rollers move among the carpet, great armored ovoids that do nothing but roll, very slowly, back and forth over the glass, compacting waste and microbial slime into the hollows. They leave a clear track behind them. Within the hour the carpet leans in and closes it. Nothing stays empty. Nothing stays clean. Nothing stays untouched.

And there are sparkers. Small, quick — quick for this place, which is to say, slow — animals that run a current along their nerves and discharge it in a crackle that makes your hair lift even through water. The discharge stuns a grazer, or welds a dead shell to the living carpet, or splits a shell open that the pumpers cannot open themselves. Their power is faint, but in this closed, metallic place it can be heard as a high ping against the glass, and other animals turn toward it, orient by it. Eyes here are poor things. Sound is the sense. The pump-wave, the grinding of shells, the ping, the deep hydraulic thud that cycles around and around — that is how the world is mapped.

There is no built thing here. No wall, no pier, no cut stone, no rusted tool, no dropped artifact of any kind. The rock has no tool-marks. The pellets have no tool-marks. The rows of the carpet have no human intention behind them. And yet it is industrial in every way that matters — mechanized, electrified, scaled, productive, relentless. Engines without metal. Conveyors without belts. Power lines that are nerves. Waste that is feedstock. Product that accumulates, year after the slow year, in the form of shell and pellet and mucus cable and mineral crust. It is as if life, given a small, high, flooded, plantless box and a very long time, reinvented the factory because it needed to process too much mineral and too many bodies with nowhere for them to go.

The water itself is part of the system. It does not flow downhill. It has nowhere downhill to go. The crater is sealed. What little rain and snowmelt seeps in from the high cold outside is balanced by the slow, slow leakage through the glass, which is still cooling and still settling. The cycle is patient almost to stillness. No current, no tide, no season that changes much, because the water and the altitude hold the temperature cool and even. The animals’ cycles are slower still. A pump-wave around the bowl takes minutes. A generation of floaters holds position for years. A roller crosses its track in a day and seems never to move at all.

That is the strangeness of it, when you stay with it: it is loud and it is industrious and it is utterly slow. Individual things barely move. The whole never stops.

If an animal dies in midwater, it sinks, and it does not rot into softness the way a body would in a soft world. The sparkers crack it, the rollers push it, the carpet incorporates it. Its shell becomes part of the floor, and the floor is alive, and the floor is walking, in its way, and producing. Death is not removal. Death is recruitment.

You come up, finally, gasping a little — the air up here is thin, and your ears ring with the pump-wave long after you are out. The sun is still normal. The glass is still naked and sharp. The water is still full to the brim, flat and cold, crowded to its surface with those clinging backs catching the light. Below them, invisible from the rim, the lanes turn, the carpet pumps, the pellets pass from mouth to mouth, the sparks ping off the walls of the bowl and come back.

It is small enough to take in at a glance. It is high enough that the rest of the world is far below the rim, dry and empty. It is young enough that the rock could cut you and old enough that everything in it has learned to work together like a single, humming, submerged machine. No plant has ever grown here. No hand has ever built here. And it produces, and it produces, and it produces, in the cold, in the normal light, beneath the water that never lets go.
