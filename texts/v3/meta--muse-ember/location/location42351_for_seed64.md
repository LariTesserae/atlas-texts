**The Turbine Garden**

At the top the mountain does not come to a point. It ends in a shelf. Black basalt, pale feldspar, white salt-crusts blooming in the cracks — dry mineral as far as you can lose yourself in it. So high the sky has gone blackish at midday and the air will not carry a breath. There is no water here. No pond in a hollow, no spring under a stone, no thaw, no rain that ever found a way up. Only rock, dust, and the thin pressure of height pressing on everything.

Nothing lives on the shelf in the ordinary way. No birds in that thin sky, no insects over the stones, no tracks in the dust. The mountain has kept its animal life far below. What is scattered here is left from industry, and it is scattered thin: a cable line stapled to the rock and dropping away down the far side, a concrete block poured for an anchor, a small hut with its door gone, a valve wheel half-buried in scree. Small structures, faint marks, occasional artifacts — almost nothing, done at a scale the mountain dwarfs.

That is why the casing is missed at first.

It sits in a jam of boulders just behind the highest face, settled deep into the scree as if it had always been there. It is room-sized, not larger — a turbine housing, a mill casing, something built for a factory floor and then lifted to this altitude for work that never arrived. Its paint has weathered to a settled form: long-existed, smoothed at the corners, rust bloomed in patches and then stopped, rivets worn flush. It is not a ruin in collapse. It is weathered into balance and now forms part of the rock. From outside, it is just another dry, mineral shape.

The entrance is not a door, really. A seam, a split where the casing warped, wide enough to slip sideways. Cold air comes out of it, cool as stone, carrying a sound.

Inside, the sound is loud.

Not a crash, not a roar that rises and falls. A single, immense note held forever — low rumble from the fan deep in the machine, a high whine from the belt, a dull clank that repeats in exact loop, again and again, until the clank becomes part of the note. You feel it in your ribs and in the soles of your feet. And yet nothing here moves. That is the first strangeness of the place: it is deafening and perfectly still.

You are not in a room. You are embedded in a pocket.

Light is dim, and stays dim. What comes through the seam is grey-green and diffuse, filtered until there are no sharp shadows and no day. Somewhere in the gloom a dead instrument panel gives off a faint amber glow, constant. The leaves glow a little in return. It is twilight *in* the machine, at noon on the summit, and it never brightens or fades.

The place is packed. Saturated. There is no empty space between presences.

The turbine's ribs are still there, iron arcs and copper wiring, pistons locked mid-stroke, pipes elbowing into collected dust. But you cannot find where the machine ends. The vegetation has taken every surface and then kept going. It is lush — shamelessly, impossibly lush for a place with no water — layered in a way a forest floor rarely achieves in so small a volume.

There is a canopy overhead, though there is no sky. Dust-ferns have grown across the ceiling of the casing, broad lath-leaves overlapping like shingles, dark green gone almost black in the dim. Below them, a wall of soot-fern and copper-moss climbs the curved iron, and on the floor a mat of grease-vine has woven itself through bolts, gauges, and the dead control box, its stems thick as cables and braided with actual cables, green with age. Roots are not roots at all but holdfasts that grip metal, stone, grease, dust-film. In places the plants have grown *into* the mechanism — a fern unfurled through a fan blade, a vine laced around a gear tooth, leaves pressed flat against a turbine fin as if listening to it.

They do listen, in the only way they can. The weirdness of the garden is that nothing in it grows toward the light, because the light is everywhere and nowhere. They grow toward the vibration. Stems curl toward the loudest joint. Leaflets have lined up in narrow slats that mimic the louvers of the machine, or in spikes that mimic its cooling fins. New growth — if it can be called new — leans into the hum.

They live without water. There is no rain ingress, no drip, no damp on the iron; the air is dry and metallic and cold, and everything to the touch is dry, papery, dusty. These are sealed plants. They fatten their stems with their own internal stores and never spend them. They feed on what the mountain gives in abundance: mineral dust for salts, soot and grease for carbon, the carbon dioxide the machine exhales in its unchanging cycle, and that dim, constant light for the rest. Pollen, if they make it, is shaken free not by a pollinator — there are none — but by the perpetual vibration of the casing itself.

That is the ecology: machine and plant locked in a single metabolism, and no animal life to interrupt it. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. Fallen leaves do not rot away in the usual sense; the oldest underlayer has mineralized, silvered, turned to a sort of stone-litter that is still leaf-shaped. Growth and fall happen at exactly the same rate, because both have stopped.

For this is a place that does not change. Not slowly. Not seasonally. It has reached its limit and holds it. The dust in the air does not drift; it hangs in still strata. You can watch the garden for an hour and not see a leaf tremble, not see a shadow shift, not see the clank vary by a fraction. The machine runs at the same speed it ran when the last leaf filled the last open square, and the plants have filled every square there was to fill. Density is the law here: once packed, nothing can grow further, nothing can decay further, nothing can erode further. The rust outside stopped blooming. The leaves inside stopped reaching.

It feels long-existed, not abandoned. Everything has been weathered to its final form and then allowed, inside this sealed pocket, to remain in that form. The scattered built things — the hut, the cable, the block — are the only story of human intention, and even they seem to have been outlasted by the small machine and what took it over.

When you slip back out through the seam, the sound drops to a faint, constant hum in the rock under your boots. The summit is again what it was: dry, black, mineral, silent except for that one note, above the clouds, in the cool thin air. No water. No animal crossing the stones. Only the shelf, the scattered marks of industry, and behind the boulders a dim, loud, packed garden that will be exactly as you left it — and exactly as it was long before you came.
