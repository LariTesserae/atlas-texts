There is no view.

You stand for a long time before you understand that. The horizon does not end in a line, a cliff, a sky. It rolls away in low, wet undulations of green, and then rolls farther, and farther, until the undulations thin into mist and the mist thins into a pale, unchanging dimness. Somewhere out there, beyond days of walking, the surface must fall away — this is high country, the air is thin in your chest and the world below is a sea of cloud that never quite reaches up here — but from inside the place, the fall is only a rumor. The high plateau has worn itself so old and so flat that altitude is felt only in the breath, not in the eye.

It is warm. Not sunny-warm. A damp, bodily warmth, as if the air itself were exhaling. Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. It does not collect so much as hang. Mist condenses on your skin, on the undersides of leaves, on the north side of every stone that manages to show through the plants. It beads and slowly gathers and runs in threads down bark, down moss, down your sleeve, following the same patient routes it followed a thousand years ago. The rocks weep. The ground weeps. Under your boots the water is near the surface, always, a dark pulse under a skin of roots.

Because the ground is not ground.

What you step on is plant, all the way down as far as your foot can press. A continental mat of interlocked roots, rhizomes, fallen trunks softened to felt, a living peat that gives a little and then holds, that closes behind you as if it had never been dented. In places the mat is a foot thick, in places it is man-high, held up by stilted trees whose roots descend like the ribs of a vault. There is no soil exposed to air. There is no bare stone except where, very occasionally, a smoothed boulder has pushed through and put on a coat of liverwort and lichen. To call it a forest is too small and too separate a word. A forest grows *on* a place. Here, the plants *are* the place, its floor, its walls, its roof, its roads.

The vegetation is layered until layer touches layer. At the top, a broken canopy of broad, dripping leaves and tree ferns, so continuous that the sky is only ever seen as a green-tinted brightness between them. Below that, a second tier of saplings and palms, a third of shrubs and climbing vines no thicker than your wrist, and below that again a dark, warm understory of mosses, fungi, seedlings waiting a century for a gap that may never come. Epiphytes grow on epiphytes. A bromeliad holding an inch of water will hold, on its outer leaves, a smaller bromeliad, and in the water, a frog that will never leave it. Vines hang in curtains that are themselves rooted in mid-air, drinking mist and wringing it out slowly along their length, so that rain often seems to fall from a single branch while the sky above is still.

Light rarely arrives clean. It filters, it is borrowed from leaf to leaf, and what reaches the floor at noon has the quality of evening in a place that has not seen the sun directly in days. Colors go muted and fungal — deep greens, wet browns, grey-green lichens — except for certain flowers, white and waxy, that hold their pale shine in the gloom, and for a faint phosphorescence in the deepest root-work, a teal breath of light you only find if you pull aside a mat of moss with your hands. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Nothing dries.

The place is so vast that it makes its own weather down inside itself. The warm, wet mass transpires without pause, and clouds form not only above the canopy but in the cold hollows between them, thin white weather that drifts a few meters and settles, that rains onto the leaves below and is drunk back by the roots and lifted again. A storm can be born here, travel slowly inland for a week, and die of its own dampness without ever reaching the edge. The edge, when a traveler eventually finds it, is not dramatic. The plateau does not end in a peak. After the last true bedrock has been smoothed for so long that rivers run over it like hands over a shoulder, the mat suddenly thins, the roots hang over emptiness in the thousands in a living fringe, and water pours out through them in veils that break into mist long before they fall to the cloud sea below. Even the waterfall is plant-filtered.

That is the age of it, above all. This is not a young landscape still cutting its shape. The stone beneath is older than any of the trees that stand on it, older than the mat itself, and it has finished moving. Every edge is rounded. Every boulder is a river pebble the size of a hut. Fossil shells in the bedrock have been unmade into soil and eaten and made into leaves again. A fallen giant does not snap and vanish; it settles, it becomes a raised road for a while, then a trench of black water, then a ridge of moss, then the upper floor of a new forest growing out of its own slow rot, a process that can be watched, barely, in a single lifetime, if you stay long enough to learn how to watch. Change here is measured not in seasons — the seasons are all but indistinguishable, a slight quickening of fruit here, a flushing of fungi there — but in generations of animals, in the thickness of a moss that grows a fingernail's width in a year.

Life is abundant, and almost entirely hidden by its own abundance. Once your eyes adjust, you realize the silence is not emptiness. It is muffling. The spongy mat, the wet leaves, the saturated air swallow sound the way water swallows light. What you hear, when you listen, is irregular dripping, a low croak from inside a hollow stem far away, the faint click of an insect in the leaf litter, the slow breathing of the canopy as mist moves through it. The animals are quiet because the place is quiet around them.

There are frogs that live their whole lives in the cup of a single leaf. There are slugs the color of wet bark that travel for years across a single tree. There are small mammals that tunnel the root mat and never surface, their passages opening and closing as the mat creeps. There are birds that glide only between the top layers and never land, trailing their feet in the undersides of wet leaves, and others that nest in the hanging curtains and dart out and back so quickly you think you imagined them. Moths, transparent as mist, spend the day motionless under a leaf and the night moving pollen between flowers that stay open for months. Everything eats the plant mass, or each other, or the rich detritus that never quite becomes litter because something else is already living in it. The food chain is not a ladder here. It is a tangle, near-touching, everything overlapping everything else.

And it creeps. That is the strangeness that finally settles over you. A patch of ferns on the far slope is not the same patch it was a hundred years ago; it has grown at one edge and rotted at the other with such patience that its center has migrated, foot by foot, across the plateau, taking its frogs and its fungi with it like passengers on a very slow ship. Some plants move toward a sound — a leaf stem will angle, over weeks, toward the vibration of dripping water. Some fruit are translucent and green and warm to the touch, and when they drop they do not thud; they sigh into the mat and begin, immediately and imperceptibly, to become floor. Roots meet underfoot with a soft pressure, feeling for each other. If you sit still long enough, your own footprints fill with green.

There are marks in it that are not plant or animal, and they are rare enough to stop you. After days, a low spiral of unmortared stones, half-sunk, each stone worked smooth by water and time until it looks like the others. A single room with no roof, its walls woven of stone and then woven over by moss, its doorway now a tunnel of roots. A step, or a place where a step must have been, leading nowhere. These are scattered, faint, partial — a dwelling, a shrine, a boundary, remade by the mat into something like a natural outcrop. No metal. No glass. No smoke, no mechanical sound, nothing that glints. Whatever built them is long gone, and the place has not mourned them; it has simply absorbed them the way it absorbs everything, turning artifact into substrate. The living things build, too, in their own way: root terraces that hold water on a slope, woven nests that grow stronger as the tree grows, dams of living stems that slow a stream to a pond for a colony of tadpoles. But the stone places remain distinct, because they do not grow. They only wear down, finally, toward the shape of the hills around them.

The rules follow from that. Nothing burns — everything is too wet, too dense, too saturated with green life. Nothing is sharp. Nothing moves fast. A thing that falls becomes a floor for another thing. A thing that dies becomes architecture. Water does not run away; it circulates, condensing and dripping and rising again through leaves, up and down in a small, closed circle, mile after mile. Distance is measured in how long it takes a path to grow shut behind you, which is to say, a generation. You do not cross this place so much as you are temporarily inside it, and it continues, after you, with the same dripping, the same creeping, the same dim warm breathing, toward horizons that are not horizons and edges that are only rumors, settled at last into a final form that is still, endlessly, quietly alive.
