The first thing that is wrong is the emptiness, and the second is the noise.

It is a shallow bowl, low to the ground, warm in a way that settles on your skin rather than rising from it. The light is ordinary — a clear, midday sun with soft shadows — but the air inside the bowl is damp, not wet. There is no river, no lake, no marsh. Moisture appears in pockets and vanishes again. Dew runs in the shallow grooves of the ground and makes them briefly dark and shining. A warm shaft of sun hits the cool undersides of leaves and they suddenly weep, a short rain falling only there, hissing faintly on leaves below. Puddles gather in the hollows of the mat, brown with sap and red dust, and by afternoon they are cracked skins and white rings. Then, for no reason you can point to, one tall plant exhales and its own interior weather rains inside it while the sky outside stays clear.

You never walk on earth. There is no soil to walk on, no stone, no sand. The whole floor is plant, and has been plant for so long that it has forgotten anything else. It is a living mat, thick and resilient, woven of roots and low creeping stems and ground-hugging leaves. It gives underfoot like skin over a ribcage. Where feet and bellies and rain have passed for ages it is polished, rubbery, worn smooth. Where it ages it furls and rots sweetly and is replaced from beneath. In places it lifts into broad root-avenues, raised causeways that branch and rejoin in a strict, old pattern. They are cracked by growth and healed by growth. Nothing sharp remains. Edges have been softened by wet and dry, by rubbing, by shedding and regrowing, until everything has the settled look of a place that has been in balance with itself for a very long time.

And over that mat, there is almost nothing.

That is the city, if it can be called that. A hamlet in a single hollow, small enough that from the center you can see the boundary all the way around — a leaning ring of intertwined stalks that closes the bowl — yet so empty inside that you can walk for a long minute with no other presence in view. Ninety percent of what you see is pale mat, warm air, mist low in the hollows, and sky.

Then, very far apart, a tower.

There are eleven of them, arranged in a loose ring and spokes, each one a repetition of the same impossible module, as if a single plant had decided to build a town and then, season after season, kept growing the same building again. Each tower is thirty, forty meters of hollow, fibrous stem ribbed like a throat, widening and narrowing. They all lean a little inward toward the center, politely, worn into that posture. They are not built. No one cut them, no one joined them. They grew their floors, their walls, their roofs.

Each is layered like a house that has been thinking about being a house. Below ground the roots make cellars, cool and braided. At ground level the stem is a great hollow hall, its inner walls spongy and warm and faintly translucent where the sun touches them. Halfway up, wide leaf-platforms jut in as galleries and verandas, waxy and veined. Higher still, round pods hang like fruit, and those are the rooms — bedrooms, nurseries, storehouses, all defined by a membrane of leaf that can be pushed open and will slowly seal again. At the top the tower ends in a crown of overlapping leaves, a thatch that opens and closes with the heat. Windows are not holes cut in a wall; they are thinned places in the stem where light bleeds through amber.

Between towers the root-avenues are the streets, and where they meet the mat opens into plazas that are enormous and completely empty except for the slow crawl of life across them. You understand the plan not because anyone planned it, but because the plant repeats itself wherever it has room. The same tower, the same plaza, the same branching roads, again and again, until repetition itself becomes order.

Life fills the towers more than people would. Fauna is abundant, not spread evenly but in eruptions. Hollow swallows nest by the thousands in the inner walls, their wings a constant rustle that never quite synchronizes. Veil-beetles ride the warm updraughts between towers in clouds so dense they darken the light for a second at a time. In the puddles, chorus frogs inflate and sing until their throats are clear balloons. Spore-bats pour out of blossoms at dusk, shrieking, and return before dawn. On the ground, small mat-runners dart through the creeping leaves, and above them stalk-grazers stride the avenues on legs longer than the towers are wide, browsing the leaf-galleries, pulling down a platform and eating it in a season. Raptors wheel over the ring. Parasite flowers bloom suddenly on the backs of runners and are eaten by pollinators before the runner has gone ten steps.

Nothing dead keeps its shape here. A fallen leaf is eaten in hours. A dead bird is unmade by beetles by nightfall. A footprint you leave in the damp mat closes behind you, a faint green scar that fades. The only materials that hold form are the living ones, so the town is always being rebuilt quietly from within.

And it is deafening. Not in bursts. At all times.

The emptiness is filled with sound because the towers are hollow and the bowl is round and every surface is taut. Wind moving through the crowns makes a low organ chord that you feel in your teeth. On top of that, always, is the drumming of the mat — animals signaling by stamping, sap pumping audibly in the stems like a slow heartbeat, water dripping inside the hollow trunks from condensation high above, falling a long way to a soft spongy floor. The insects are tuned to the same key as the birds, and the birds answer the frogs, and the frogs are answered by the towers themselves when they breathe. The towers breathe. In the heat they open their pores and inhale, pulling a cool draft across the plazas that carries every scent and stray note with it. At night they exhale, slow warm sighs that roll across the mat and condense into brief, local rains.

Because sound has nowhere to go, it layers. You shout across a plaza and your voice comes back to you three times, each time a little altered, as if someone across the town were trying to imitate you. A call you hear now is made again a minute later from a tower where no animal has yet arrived. The roar is not just loud — it is crowded in time. Your own pulse joins it. Travelers here learn to speak by pressing close, or not to speak at all, and let the noise move through them.

This is where the place stops behaving.

The plants and the animals are not quite separate categories. Some towers, in a wet year, will grow a blossom large as a room, and out of that blossom, in the dry year, bats will emerge and fly away, leaving the tower hollow again. Some ground-plants will, in a slow season, thicken into a runner-animal, legs rooting and unrooting, before they settle back into the mat. It would be wrong to say they transform. It is more that the same living stuff, given enough time and moisture, will take whichever shape the current echo requires.

Because everything is an echo. Each tower is the tower before it, a season delayed. Each generation of swallows nests in the same hollow and adds one layer of soft, warm debris, so the hollow gets smaller and the building gets taller from inside. Inside, the space is consistently more than it should be — a pod that looks like an egg from outside will hold a chamber where you can stand upright and turn around with your arms out, and the air inside will be a few degrees warmer and always faintly raining from its own ceiling. The interiors have their own small weathers, their own dawn and damp, out of step with the sky.

Orientation fails here, gently. The towers are identical. The mat is uniform. The puddles sometimes reflect a tower that is not behind you. You can walk straight for ten minutes and arrive, with complete certainty, at a plaza you have already left. The straight root-avenues are the only honest lines, and they too, after a heavy condensation rain, will have grown a new side root, a new door, a new hollow in a stem where yesterday there was solid fiber. The town re-addresses itself constantly.

And all of it is in motion. This is not a ruin or a finished garden. The mat is growing. The towers are shedding a floor as they grow another. The stalk-grazers are pruning the galleries. The beetle clouds are migrating from tower to tower following the breath. A blossom opens at the crown of one tower, closes, bursts into seeds that drift in the updraft and fall, by the thousands, to thicken the mat in a new plaza. Rain comes and goes in patches no larger than a room. The whole bowl inhales and exhales on a cycle of hours, not days, and with each breath the air cools or warms a finger's width, the leaves turn, the sounds shift pitch.

By late afternoon the light is still normal, the air still warm and damp in patches, the mat still breathing under your feet. The eleven towers stand very far apart in their empty ring, leaning in. The boundary stalks at the edge of the bowl are thick and old and intergrown, a wall that was never built and never needed repair. Outside it, the same plant-thin world continues, but drier, sparser, fading.

Inside, the noise goes on. The swallows rustle. The heart in the stems beats. A distant tower exhales. Somewhere in a pod, it is quietly raining, and no one above will ever know.
