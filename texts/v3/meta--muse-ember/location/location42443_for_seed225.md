You do not go down into dark.

The entrance is a slit under a white plateau — a crack where the salt-crust has slumped, no wider than your shoulders. You slide down a few metres of warm, polished stone, and if you are expecting a cave you get something else entirely: a room, lit.

It is bright. Not a shaft of sun, not a beam. The whole ceiling is light.

Above you is three metres of porous white rock, thin as an eggshell, cracked in a fine, patient network. The sun on the plateau above is pressed through it and diffused, scattered, turned into a permanent high noon. There is no direction to the light, no shadows to walk in. Everything is pale — calcite ribs, silt, the skin of your own hands — and everything is seen at once, as if you were standing inside a paper lantern. It is warm here, the stone warm to the touch, the air still and mineral and damp on your skin, but it is never night in this place. It only brightens and dims, very slowly, over hours.

That is the first strangeness, and it is also the most ordinary thing here.

### The room

It is small, in the way a cathedral or a grove is small. One bounded feature. An oval chamber, perhaps forty paces long and half that wide, domed low enough that you can almost touch the ceiling from the floor. From the entrance you can see the far wall. From the centre you can see all of it.

And most of it is nothing.

The floor is a single sheet of fine pale silt, rippled by air that has not moved quickly in a very long time. The ripples are fossilised — long, low, settled-form, like a beach remembered by stone. Erosion has been at this place until it is in balance with itself. There are no fresh edges, no sharp corners, no newly fallen rock. The walls undulate in scallops and bulbous mammillary hollows, polished by centuries of condensation and slow breathing, as if the chamber had been washed and then left alone for longer than anything bright remembers.

You can walk twenty steps across the middle and pass nothing at all. That is the governing rule of the place: vast space between presences.

A stone, dropped from the ceiling when the crust was younger, lies alone. Ten paces away, another. Each one is worn perfectly smooth, solitary, not in a heap, not in a line. A tuft of vegetation at the base of the wall. Ten paces away, another. The emptiness is not a lack of things. It is a spacing.

### Water, and what grows

It is damp, but it is not wet. There is no stream, no river, no pool that stays. Moisture arrives slowly, as condensation.

In certain hollows in the wall a dark sheen spreads, almost imperceptibly, then recedes over hours. At the lowest point of the floor a patch the size of a hand darkens and, after a long breath of the chamber, becomes a film of water a finger deep — amber at the edge, clear in the middle — and then, without a ripple, it sinks and is gone. A single drip falls from a stubby ceiling dome, one drop every minute or so, into the same place, making a ring that widens and vanishes. The sound of it is mostly felt in your feet, through the stone, because the air here kills sound quickly.

Where that dampness lingers and where the ceiling-cracks line up to give the most light, vegetation takes hold — sparse, stubborn, and not where you would expect.

There are felt-like mats on the walls themselves, pale jade and cream, clinging flat in the sweat-patches, growing no faster than you can watch them not grow. And there are plants that root in the ceiling. From the cracks they hang ribbons down a metre or more into the empty air, translucent, pale green, roots invisible above. Their tips are damp. They do not reach the floor. They have no need to. The world here is not only below.

Nothing else grows in the open. The middle of the floor is too empty, too dry, too bright in the wrong way. Life keeps to the walls and the ceiling, and even there, it keeps its distance.

### The inhabitants

For a long time you will think you are alone, and then you will begin to see how abundant this emptiness is.

It is the animals that are abundant. Not plants — there is far less of them. The animals are everywhere, and each one keeps metres of space to itself.

Pressed flush to the walls in the damp zones are the scrapers. Small, flat, pale hexapods the size of a thumbnail, studding the stone in their hundreds if you count along a whole wall, but never in a cluster. Each one has its own square of wall, its own patch of mat to graze. They move a body-length at a time, then stop for hours. Their bodies are white against the white stone; only their eyes are dark, large for the diffused light.

Each carries a tool.

This is their whole technology, and it is primitive in the oldest sense: a single chipped flake of flintlike stone, held in the forelegs, worn smooth by a lifetime of use. With it they scrape the felt-mats from the wall, slow bite by slow bite. They make one. They carry it through their life. They build nothing with it — no nest, no den, no burrow, no cache. When they die against the wall, flush and solitary, the flake falls. That is why the floor has its solitary stones, far apart. They are not geology alone. They are a generation of tools, dropped.

Higher up, in the volume of the room that seemed so empty, hang the others. They descend from the ceiling on a single filament so fine and translucent it is almost invisible until it catches the light. Thread-bodied, head-down, they hang spaced like stars, each metres from the next, swaying in unison when the chamber breathes. They filter spores and condensation from the air. There are dozens of them, maybe more than a hundred, and the air between them is still, enormous, and unoccupied.

And once in a long while — not every day, not every night, but on a cycle too slow to predict from a single visit — a crosser comes down the entrance slit. It is large, dog-sized, slow as a settling dune, blind and pale and jointed, and it crosses the floor in a straight line from wall to wall, eating the mats as it goes, its footfalls leaving a wide polished track in the silt. It takes an hour to cross. Then it leaves, and for days the track remains, slowly slumping back into the ripples. Its absence is part of the density of the room. When it is gone, the emptiness feels even emptier for knowing how vast it is between footfalls.

That is the ecology: sparse light-fed mats, grazed by solitary tool-carriers, filtered by the hangers, cropped by the occasional crosser. Nothing builds. Nothing crowding. The abundance is in numbers counted over the whole wall, over the whole volume, over years — never in density at any one moment.

### The rules

The chamber breathes. Once over many hours, warm air is drawn in through the cracks in the crust, heavy with smell of salt and warm stone, and then exhaled. The hangers sway. The mats darken as the sweat comes. The bright light thickens, then thins. The drip from the ceiling quickens or slows. Nothing else changes quickly.

It is a place that has been in its own state for a long time. Dust motes that are not dust hang motionless. Your footprints in the silt will stay, then soften, then become ripples again. A stone you move will, over a season, seem to have settled nearer to where it wants to be. Time here is not measured by events but by a patient returning to the same damp patches, the same spacing, the same quiet.

Sound is mostly quiet. Your own breathing seems loud and then is absorbed. Far away, a scraper taps its flake against stone — once. The drip falls. The breath of the room moves through the cracks overhead, a faint sigh in the rock itself.

There are no made things here except the tools the animals carry, and those are made to be carried, not left. No paths, no walls, no homes. It is untouched not because nothing has ever lived here, but because everything that lives here has learned to leave almost no trace except a polished stone, a track, a filament, and a spacing that will not be crossed.

You stand in the centre, in the perpetual noon under the earth, and you can see every wall, every hanging animal, every solitary patch of green. The room holds you, warm and bright and damp and empty, and it does not hurry to fill itself.
