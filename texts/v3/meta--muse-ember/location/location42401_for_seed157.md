It is not a valley, and it is not on the way to anywhere.

It sits high — high enough that the air coming over the shoulder of the mountain is thin and cool and has the flat smell of stone and snowmelt, high enough that outside the rim the trees grow short and wind-bent and the ground is pale with lichen. And then there is a hollow, no wider than a throw of a stone, and in three steps you are inside it, and the world narrows to a room.

The walls are not walls, exactly. They are the mountain curving inward on itself, basalt worn down over a time too long to count into a settled bowl. There is no sharp edge anywhere. Every slope has fallen to the angle it will keep forever, every rock has been smoothed at the corners, every corner has been filled in again with soil. The erosion here is not fresh or violent. Nothing is crumbling, nothing is sliding. The bowl has finished becoming itself, and has been finished for a very long time.

Above you it closes. Not completely — a slit of grey sky might show if you look straight up from the very center, but by then the trees have taken it. Alder and birch and the ghost-trunks of tree ferns lean in from all sides, their branches plaited together by vines, their leaves overlapping until the light that reaches the floor is not light so much as the memory of it. The cloud that catches on the rim never quite leaves. It condenses on the leaves and on the rock and on you. Your elbows bead. Your hair beads. Your breath comes back to you as mist.

It is cool inside in a way that gets past clothing. The water is cold. The stone is cold. The air does not move. It has no reason to.

The water is everywhere, and nowhere is it running. That is the first rule. Rain — if you can call it rain when it never stops and never starts — arrives as this perpetual damp, as fog that becomes film, as film that becomes drip. From the weeping faces of the rock it seeps out in a thousand slow pulses, clear and iron-tasting, and gathers in a skin over everything. It does not make streams. It makes saturation. The floor — peat black as tea, moss a foot thick in places, moss over moss over the rotting bodies of ferns — gives under your boot without a splash. It sighs. It takes the print of you and fills it in again immediately with more water welling up from underneath. The water level is always the same. It has always been the same. It rises as vapor off the warmest things — the animal bodies, the leaf litter — condenses on the undersides of leaves, drips, soaks, and rises again. A closed loop, small as a room, turning inside itself without ever going anywhere.

Because of that, the green is overwhelming in a different way than a jungle is overwhelming. It is lush to the point of pressure. There is no bare rock to see, no bare soil to stand on, no empty branch. Ferns unroll and overlap and are overlapped by liverworts, which are overlapped by mosses in spiral rosettes that will never unroll further. Every twig is a rope of epiphytes. Every leaf underside is a nursery. Roots do not so much grow into earth as weave themselves sideways through the mat of other roots, seeking a wet surface to press against, because the surface is always wet. Nothing here needs light the way things outside do — it cannot have it — so the plants have learned the other cues. They grow toward the drip. They grow toward the damp exhalation of a neighboring plant. A leaf that has rotted away is replaced, at the same season, in the same spot, by a leaf of exactly the same size, because there is nowhere else for it to be.

And the animals are more than the plants. If density could be measured by no room left between presences, this would have no measure left to give.

The ground moves, but quietly. A slow, packed shuffle. Millipedes white as paper, blind, threading through the moss in their hundreds. Slugs the size of a hand, translucent, their organs visible through the skin, leaving a silvered trail that fades before it is finished. Beetles armored like black seeds, too many to follow any one. Snails in clusters, so thick on a fallen log that the log's shape is only borrowed. Frogs and salamanders that look half-made of the water they live in — pale, uneyed or with eyes covered by skin, breathing through their skin, their bellies full of algae so their backs are green as the moss they lie on. Mites in the leaf mold, springtails jumping in arcs you feel more than see. Higher up, in the crooks of branches, in holes in the wet wood, in the thatch of hanging moss, more of them. A warm, soft, damp weight of life, pressed together, each one with its fixed territory, its fixed share, eating and being eaten in a circle so tight and so old that it never changes its numbers.

That is the second rule, and it explains the quiet. There is almost no sound here that is not made by water forgetting to fall. A drip, somewhere, at a regular interval, that might be the same drip it has always been. The soft scuff of a thousand feet that never rush, because there is nowhere to rush to. An occasional click, very faint — stone on shell. The great, tool-using inhabitants, mole-like and blind and the size of a hare, with forepaws almost like hands, have a hammer-stone they keep by the one deep pool in the center. Generation after generation of them has cracked the same mussels on the same anvil, and the anvil is pitted and polished smooth. Scattered through the mat, half sunk, are other worked stones — flakes chipped to an edge, an adze shape no bigger than your thumb, left by something else a long time ago, something that used its hands and then stopped coming. The moss has grown through their hollows. No path leads to them. No structure leads from them. The tools remain because they are useful, and they are the only artifacts the room allows itself.

Because there is no building here, and no clearing. You cannot build a fire because nothing is dry enough to burn and, more importantly, because there is no loose fuel — every stick is either alive, or alive with other things, or already rotted into the mat and feeding it. The room has no doors. It has a boundary of shadow and damp and mosses growing right up the bowl's rim and then, abruptly, stopping where the wind and the drier air begin outside. From inside you would never know the mountain was high. From outside you would never know the room was here. The darkness inside is not the black of night. It is a deep, green-black, luminous in the way wet things are luminous — white fungi blooming in cow-pats of peat no larger than your thumbnail, the pale bellies of salamanders, the sheen on a slug's back at the moment it crosses a drip. Your eyes adjust and adjust and never quite finish. Shapes come out of it by touch and by the difference in wetness. A leaf is wetter than a stone. A stone is colder than a body. A body is warmer than anything else.

The stillness is not the stillness of death. It is stronger. Dead trees stand in this place with their bark intact, spongy, indistinguishable from the living ones except that they do not exude water from their wounds. A leaf that falls — and they fall constantly, one by one, not in a season — lands on a leaf below it and does not lie there to be noticed. It is eaten while it is still falling, by things already waiting under it. Growth and decay are the same event seen from two sides, and so nothing grows. Everything is exactly as tall as it was, exactly as thick, exactly as wet, exactly as crowded. If you stayed a year, the water would be at the same line on the rock. If you stayed a hundred, the moss would be a foot thick, as it is now. If you left footprints, the mat would close over them behind your ankles before you reached the rim.

There is a mild wrongness to it, the kind that takes a while to notice. Plants that grow downward, roots like pale hairs waving in the air drinking condensation. Animals that are camouflaged not with color but with living algae, so that to find them is to uproot a tuft of moss and have it blink. A pool that weeps — not flows, weeps — from a crack that is always the same width. A spiral moss that turns always to the left. No man, no woman, no path, no name carved. Nothing has been improved upon.

And when you climb back out over the rim, the wind hits you as if it had been waiting, thinned and cold and loud with it, and the light is real light, and the ground is hard and sparse and moves under your feet. Behind you, in the hollow, the drip falls again, and the shuffle goes on under the moss, and the water rises and condenses and falls without ever leaving the room.

It will be exactly like that when the next thing, if there ever is a next thing, comes to look.
