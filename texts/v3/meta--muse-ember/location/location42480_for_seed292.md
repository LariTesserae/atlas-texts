You do not so much stand on it as move through it.

The first thing is the water in the air. A rain wall comes across the plateau from the sea — you can see it for miles, a grey curtain leaning sideways, and when it reaches you it does not so much fall as arrive horizontally. It drumming the canopy, needling the back of your neck, filling your hood and boots in minutes. Behind it, another. Between them the mist lifts only a little, enough to show that the ground was already saturated. Everything here has been saturated for a very long time.

Then, in a gap, the scale opens out. This is high ground, unexpectedly high. Behind the boggy summit plain the land falls away in steps — 800 metres, a thousand, two thousand in total relief down to mile-long sea inlets that cut inland like flooded valleys. The peaks on the far side are not peaks in the usual sense. They are domes and shoulders, broad, rounded, smoothed to the curve of a loaf. There are no spires, no sharp ridges. The whole region is a tableland that has been planed and planed again, so long ago that its shape is settled. Water has done most of the work and water is still doing it, but it is working over a form that will not change in any human lifetime. The cliffs are worn, the river boulders are worn, the soil itself is thin and leached and anciently tired, and yet the surface is never still.

That is the paradox the place holds: a final form, endlessly turbulent on top of it.

The low ground is drowned. Not one lake, but hundreds of peat pools, interlocking, black and tea-coloured, reflecting the flat grey sky so perfectly that you step into water believing it is air. Tussocks and sphagnum make a spongy causeway between them, and the sphagnum gives under the knee, cold and springy, and then gives way altogether. Rivers do not run in single channels. They braid, wander, abandon a course overnight and cut a new one across a meadow, leaving behind oval cobbles polished like bread rolls and tree roots exposed and then buried again. When the rain is heaviest, waterfalls appear on slopes that were dry in the morning — thin white threads multiplying on every rock face, all of them joining the roar below.

In the inlets the drowning continues under the surface. The water is cold and clear enough — in the sheltered bays you can count kelp fronds twelve metres down, moving in slow, dense curtains, sheltering pollack and wrasse and crabs in such numbers that the rocks are never visible. The kelp forest continues up the shore into intertidal rockpools, into wet meadow, into the low rainforest, as if vegetation here has no interest in stopping at the waterline. It is lush, but it is cold lush. Dark greens layered over greens: moss a handswidth thick on every north-facing stone, ferns unfurling in the lee of every boulder, shrubs locked branch to branch, and below them a low, wind-trained forest whose canopy is near-touching, no light reaching the floor except in flickering patches. Epiphytes, lichens, fungi crowd the trunks. Peat bogs bloom with cotton grass and sundews. Nothing is sparse except the bare rock, and even that is furred.

Animals are correspondingly everywhere, in an ordinary, unshowy abundance. Shearwaters and gulls and oystercatchers work the inlets in constant, noisy traffic; ravens quarter the plateau and drop screaming into the mist; fish move in visible shoals through the peat pools, trout and char in the high lakes, more trout than seems credible for water so cold. Insects darken the bog air on still days. Seals bark and grunt on the outer rocks, and in winter a whale sometimes blows far out beyond the kelp. On the plateau itself there are hardy sheep and a few half-wild cattle, soaked, patient, eating whatever green is within reach. In summer the whole place is alive with work — not anthill-energy in any human sense, but the ceaseless feeding, spawning, nesting, decomposing that fills a volume this dense. You hear three bird species at once without trying to. You see fish under almost every stone you turn.

The light is perfectly normal daylight, which makes the saturation stranger. A pale, watery sun, diffused by cloud, flattening everything to grey and green, and then a sudden shaft on a waterfall or a white foam line that makes the green look almost black by comparison. The temperature never settles. Even at the height of an imagined summer it is 8 or 10 degrees, the wind stripping heat from wet skin, your breath visible when you climb, your fingers numb enough that you have to stop and warm them inside your jacket. The smell is salt, wet peat, crushed fern, diesel from somewhere below.

Human presence is scattered thinly over all this density, and it is mostly machinery rather than buildings. A single low hydro intake high in a valley, its buried pipe snaking down through the forest to a powerhouse no bigger than a barn at the inlet. A pier, a boathouse, a fish farm with its ring of orange floats and automated feeders clicking out pellets in the sheltered bay. A weather mast on the summit plain with its wind instruments turning frantically. A line of pylons crossing the bog, their bases standing in pools. A rusted conveyor and a dewatering pump still sucking at an old peat or gravel working that has half drowned and half filled with frogs. A track that is really a causeway, disappearing under a lake for two hundred metres and re-emerging on the other side. A couple of trawlers moored stern-to, engines idling, exhaust ticking. That is all. No town, no centre. The buildings that exist are already being reabsorbed — gutters overflowing, roofs green with algae, iron rusting in the constantly wet air at a pace you can almost watch, while anything that falls into peat is preserved, black and intact, for centuries.

And everything moves. The weather systems do not pass so much as collide and pile up against the high ground, unload, retreat a little, and come again from a different quarter. The sea surges up the inlets, lifting kelp and dropping it, rearranging the shingle overnight. A bog that has absorbed a month of rain will suddenly shear along a deep crack and slide, slowly at first and then not slowly, taking fence posts and young trees with it into a pool. Rivers rise two metres in a night and fall again by morning, leaving silt, branches, a plastic buoy, a dead bird, all rearranged. Trees fall though the soil is thin; others root in the rotting trunk of the fallen one. The farm feeds, the pumps run, the generators hum — human industry here is not a steady state either, but a constant repair against water. You learn the rules quickly: nothing dries, nothing stays where it was left, wood rots from the top down, iron from the outside in, paths must be re-found every season, and you walk on the tussocks, not the moss, if you want to stay mostly above the surface.

By the end of a day the place has revealed itself less as a landscape than as a system in balance at its final stage. The mountains have been eroded for so long they have become smooth, low, enduring shapes. The plateau has been flooded so long it has become a shallow sea of peat and pool, lake and cloud, kelp and canopy, all continuous. The cold, the height, the rain, the noise — rain on leaves, wind tearing through branches, waterfalls roaring in stereo, gulls screaming, a pump humming somewhere below the mist — are not separate features. They are one loud, turbulent, ordinary world, densely alive, anciently worn into its last form, and still, every minute, being remade in its details.
