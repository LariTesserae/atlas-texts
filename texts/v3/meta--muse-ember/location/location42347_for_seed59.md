**Tinta**

You hear it first, before the ridge drops away.

Not wind. Water, and living things making noise in the water, and people shouting over both — all of it bouncing back and forth inside a bowl no wider than a good throw. The whole place is small enough to take in at a glance from the pass, and too dense to take in at all once you are inside it.

The pass is at maybe 2,600 meters. The air is thin and cool enough that your breath shows even at noon, and the sun, when it finds you, is hard and white in a way that makes the shade feel colder by contrast. For most of the day the light is normal and grey-green, filtered through cloud and canopy, and then for an hour after midday a shaft opens and everything wet shines.

Tinta sits in the bottom of that bowl, where a side valley has folded in on itself. On three sides the walls rise steeply — dark rock sweating in the cool — and on the fourth the forest closes in. You can walk the edge in twenty minutes. You can hear the edge from anywhere in the middle: a frog chorus from the north drain, a waterwheel from the south, bells from the market square, rain starting somewhere upslope and arriving everywhere within minutes.

It is not new. Nothing here looks new, even the things that were built last week. Walls have slumped to the angle of the wet soil. Stone from the river has been rounded by hands and by flows. Wood is silvered. Paths are sunken channels. Terraces that were abandoned a generation ago are not ruins; they are hummocks of moss and fern that children walk over without noticing, and then a family will clear one for tubers because the soil remembers. The place has been in its own state for a long time: eaten away and built up, in balance, a steady churn rather than a fresh clearing.

The ground is the first thing that changes your idea of ground.

There is very little mineral soil at the surface. What you step on is a felt — inches of moss, liverwort, root mat, fallen leaves in various stages of rot, rhizomes braided by water. It gives underfoot like a raft. In the dry-ish places near the center, people have laid platforms of bound logs and woven bamboo over the sponge so houses do not sink. Between the platforms the felt is alive. Ferns uncurl from it wherever a roof drip falls. Begonias and orchids root in the joints of walls. A house is not *in* vegetation. It is *of* it.

Poles are living trees, pruned and trained as they grow, their roots left in the felt to hold the raft. Walls are woven — willow and bamboo split and bent while green, stuffed with mud and shredded moss, re-woven every few years as the old weave rots into soil. Roofs are thatch, but thatch here is a crop. Thick mats of mountain grass, laid over a bamboo frame, planted at the eaves with small ferns that keep the mat from drying out. When it rains, eleven months a year it rains, the roof plops and drips steadily into planter troughs built into the wall below. The wall drinks it. The plants on the wall drink it. The people inside drink it a day later, filtered through moss.

Water is not a feature. It is the condition.

The rocks weep. All around the bowl, springs bead out of the cliff faces and run in bright threads over black stone before joining a drain. The air itself condenses: at dawn every surface — wool, wood, leaf, your hair — is beaded. Mist comes up from the valley floor and hangs at eye level, then burns off, then a convective cloud builds by early afternoon and unloads for an hour, hard and cold, and then stops as if cut. Afterward dripping continues for hours. Drains that were gutters in the morning become streams by noon. A lane that is a lane in the morning is a shallow canal by four, with fish in it.

The town is built around that. That is its urban logic.

Tinta is organized the way a watershed is organized. From the highest spring, a communal bathhouse and washhouse — a stone-and-wood shed with a waterwheel turning a wooden screw for grinding maize and tubers — splits into bamboo aqueducts lashed to living poles. They run, dripping at every joint, along the main lanes to each terrace cluster. Each cluster has a sluice, a settling pit of gravel and moss, a granary raised on stone feet in the driest corner of the square. Houses are numbered not by street but by flow: the people of the upper drain, the lower drain, the weeping rock.

Lanes are terraces. Terraces are lanes. Steps worn into the felt, reinforced with woven root and split bamboo, run in spirals upward to whether you want a house, a garden, or a view of the whole bowl. Basket-loads of potatoes, oca, squash, beans, and greens come down those steps all day. The market square is not paved. It is a clearing where the felt has been cut away and replaced with packed moss and clay, roofed at the edges by fruit trees and by woven canopies that are taken down and re-hung as needed. Stalls are not permanent. Partitions of woven reed come in on frames, go out again in the evening.

Tech is hand tech, everything is hand tech. Iron hoes, stone axes kept for splitting, wooden looms clacking in doorways, bamboo knives, clay pipes, water-powered saws that jam with wet sawdust. No engines, no wire. What cannot be carried by a person or a mule or a waterwheel does not come in. What breaks is repaired where it lies, with green material worked in beside old material. A roof is never replaced. It is patched, layered, replanted, until the oldest thatch is deep in the middle and has become soil for the newest.

And through all of it, the animals.

Fauna is not a background here. It is overwhelming in the literal sense — there are more wild and tame presences per cubic meter than people by a factor that makes the human population feel like a minority. Every drain has tree frogs, glassy and green, that start at dusk and make conversation impossible. Every wet wall has snails, moving in slow silver lines. Moths crowd the oil lamps in the market at night. Beetles work the rotting logs under houses. Dragonflies patrol the aqueducts. Hummingbirds are constant at the planter troughs, tanagers and mountain birds call from the forest wall and sometimes land on a drying rack. Bats come out of the granary eaves. Shrews and mice run the thatch. Inside the houses, under the living floor, pigs and guinea pigs and chickens are penned in the warm dark; ducks are herded along the lanes to the ponds that form in the lower terraces after rain and eat the mosquito larvae. Fish — small, cold-water, speckled — live in the settlement drains and are netted by children with woven scoops before the afternoon rise.

It is loud because of them, and water, and people. In the market at noon the frogs have not yet started, but the waterwheel, the looms, the bells on the pack animals, the bargaining, the rain on a hundred thatched surfaces at once, and the birds, lift into a single roar that you have to lean into to hear a single voice. It is the anthill energy of a bazaar that is also a farm that is also a forest: always carrying, always rebuilding, always diverting a channel that has silted, always moving a fence because a tree has fallen across it, always replanting a terrace that slipped in the night rain.

That is the turbulence, and it is also normal. Nothing supernatural, nothing strange. A warm shaft of air lifts, a cloud forms, it rains, the felt swells, a retaining weave gives way with a soft sigh, and by evening ten people have woven a new one in its place and the frogs are singing in the new hollow. A tree fruits too heavily, branches break, the fallen limb becomes a bridge for a week until it rots enough to be soil again. Rot is not decay as failure; rot is the rate at which the place makes soil for the next thing. Growth is just as fast.

The rules are simple because the materials enforce them.

Nothing stays dry. So nothing is built to stay dry — everything drains, everything breathes, everything is raised or woven or thatched to shed water sideways.

Nothing stays dead. So dead wood goes into the felt, dead thatch goes onto the gardens, a dead animal's bones go into the settling pit. A wall that is not growing is already a garden.

Nothing stays in place. So Tinta does not have fixed streets or fixed borders. The bowl is the border. Inside, a lane can be a pond in the wettest month and a market stall in the drier weeks. A house can lean a degree more each year as its living poles grow unevenly, and be propped and re-woven until it leans back.

And nothing is wasted in space. Because it is small and dense, the layers stack. A house is two or three storeys: storage and animals below, people in the middle, vines, nests, and a roof garden above. A terrace is a crop, a drain, a path, and a frog habitat in the same step. A wall is structure, insulation, planter, and snail trail.

In the evening the rain usually stops, the mist settles, and the temperature drops fast. People pull damp wool ponchos tighter. Smoke from cookfires — green wood, wet leaves, dung — hangs low and sweet and acrid. The frogs take over the sound. Somewhere a loom is still going. The aqueducts keep dripping onto moss. The whole bowl glows faintly under the lamps, each one a small warm circle in the cool, wet dark, and from above on the ridge it looks less like a town built on a mountain than a patch of particularly busy, particularly alive forest that has learned to build itself into houses.
