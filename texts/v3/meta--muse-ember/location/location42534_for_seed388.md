You hear it before you drop down into it.

A low, constant throat-noise rising out of flat marsh — not a roar exactly, more the sound of a very large chest breathing through metal. Boilers. Water hammer in buried pipe. The slow clank of a chain that turns over and over and over. It comes through the sedges long before the town shows itself, because the town is lower than the land around it.

The causeway is cinder and broken brick laid over peat, and the water on both sides is tea-brown and opaque and already seeping up through the stones under your boots. It is a marsh plain only a few metres above the sea, or above whatever decides this water’s level, and then you crest the slag berm — a long, moss-capped levee of ash and furnace waste, settled and softened by a century of rain — and you step down into Vapourlow.

The whole of it can be walked in minutes. From the north chimney to the south sluice gates is maybe two hundred paces. But it is enclosed, it always has been, a bowl dug or subsided into the marsh and then walled round with its own waste, so that from inside you cannot see out except in slivers of sky between roofs. That is the first rule: it is small, and it is crowded, and it holds.

Inside, the air is warmer than outside, and stays warm. The bowl traps heat the way a greenhouse traps it, and the works makes its own. Wet warmth comes up off standing water and off iron and off brick that has never dried. Your hair dampens in minutes whether it is raining or not. Everything sweats. Cool pipework in the warm humid air beads and runs in clean, deliberate lines — condensation finding the same paths down the same columns day after day — until in the engine hall it falls as a perpetual, localized rain, fine and cold, under one particular bank of steam lines, while ten feet away the floor is merely damp.

Light never arrives properly here. The cloud deck hangs low over the marsh and the town adds its own to it, a permanent thin steam that smears the sun to a green-grey smudge. What light there is bounces up off water and off wet walls, dim and diffuse, so that shadows have no edges. The electric lamps are on in the middle of the day — amber globes on wet insulators, humming in the transformer yard, spitting in the damp — and they make pools, not illumination. Under the interlaced roofs and overhead pipework it is always twilight, always the moment after a storm, lit from below by reflection.

The place is urban the way a beehive is urban. Factories shoulder to shoulder with workers’ cottages, warehouses with sawtooth roofs tucked against a substation, a water tower, a row of chimneys as thick as tree trunks, all of it pressed together so that streets are narrow passages and passages are only ventilators. Above the streets is a second street: steam mains, conveyor bridges, cable trays, catwalks, chute pipes, all crossing and branching and tying into one another like limbs. Below street level is a third: open sluices and culverts and flooded cellars, water moving slowly between buildings at ankle height, sometimes knee height, carrying an orange film of iron bacteria and leaf-mould. You walk at ground level in a sheet of water an inch deep, looking up through moss.

It was built to work the marsh — to pump, distill, dye, tan, press peat and water into something industrial — and the works has outlived its purpose without stopping. The machines still run. They run slowly.

This is the second rule: nothing here hurries. The great turbine in the main hall turns so slowly you can only tell by the painted mark on its rim, which creeps a hand’s breadth in an hour. A conveyor of sodden fibre advances at the pace of a fingernail. The sluices open and close on a three-day cycle of fill, heat, release, cool. Vines advance a centimetre. Rust thickens. The ground itself subsides, is sinking around the drains in shallow, settled hollows, the ground floors of the brick blocks now half-basements, their sills below the water line, their brick leached white and rounded at the corners by water that has been running over them for a very long time. Nothing is freshly broken. Nothing is falling down quickly. Everything has reached its own state and is holding it: paint gone to mineral stains, iron scaled thick as bark, concrete slumped and veined, paths worn into channels. Erosion here is not decay. It is maturity.

And yet it is loud. The slowness makes the noise stranger, not quieter. Water hammer thuds through walls in a slow heartbeat. A relief valve sighs for a full minute. Chains clank, regularly, massively, as if something enormous were being dragged. The drips in the culverts are amplified by the brick vaults and fall in counterpoint. Over it all the low drone of the transformers and the pumps, felt in the soles of the feet as much as heard. A loud place with no voice in it that is alive.

The vegetation has taken the place as completely as the industry ever did, and the two now intersect in ways that are hard to separate. Outside the berm the marsh is reeds and sedge; inside, the lushness is more intimate and more total. Ferns grow waist-high out of gutters. Moss is ankle-deep on roofs and complete on north walls, a velvet that drips when you brush it. Vines have climbed the chimneys and spill from the overhead pipes, leaves clustering thickly at every joint and flange as if cooling the metal. Roots have found the oil leaks and the warm bearings and grow into them; gear teeth push through leaves. In the vats, water hyacinth blooms a pale, indifferent blue. In the streets, a jasmine-like creeper shades the lamp globes. Fungi stipple the timber in soft orange and grey. Nothing eats any of it. The plants are the only life in Vapourlow, and because of the warmth, the standing water, the nutrient-rich runoff, and the total absence of grazing, they are extravagant. They grow in the shapes of the machines that feed them.

No animal life at all. That is the uncanniest thing, and you notice it gradually, the way you notice a missing sound. No birds on the wires, though the wires are a perfect lattice. No flies over the standing water. No frogs in the sluices, no fish in the canals, no rats in the boiler house, no moths at the lamps. The water holds no reflection of wings. The silence is not empty — the machines fill it — but it is a silence without breath. Even the lush plants seem to be growing in a chemically clean, mechanically vibrated, perpetually condensing atmosphere that nothing with a heartbeat could tolerate. The houses still have numbers on their doors and curtains at the windows, grey and rotting, but no one has lived here for a very long time, and the works runs itself in its closed loop: pump marsh water up, heat it, move it slowly through its stages, condense it, let it fall again.

The strangeness is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It is in the pond in the middle of the yard whose surface does not ripple with the vibration all around it, held flat by a skin of algae and mineral film. It is in the way the condensation seems to know where to fall, making rain only under the coldest pipes, only over the warmest engines. It is in the vines following the electrical conduits instead of the sun. It is in the fact that the whole town, for all its industry, manufactures nothing anymore except heat and weather — its own weather, warm, wet, dim, endlessly cycling in a bowl barely lower than the fen around it.

If you stand still long enough your boots fill. If you stand still longer, moss will find the cuff of your trousers. The mark on the turbine will have moved. A plume of steam will have rolled off a roof and hung, unhurried, between the chimneys. The water in the street will have risen by less than the thickness of a coin, as it has been rising, imperceptibly, for decades, and will go on rising, imperceptibly, for decades more. Nothing dries. Nothing hurries. Nothing with a pulse lives here. And the place, settled into its own wet, warm, semi-dark equilibrium, goes on working around you, loud and green and slow, as if it had always been exactly this, and intended to remain so.
