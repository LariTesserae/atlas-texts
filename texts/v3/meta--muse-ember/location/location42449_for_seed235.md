High above everything else, it is still a swamp.

That is the first wrongness, and it never corrects itself. The city sits on a rim so high that the air should be thin and cold — your ears pop on the last causeway, the drop behind you is not a valley but a white sea of cloud far below, and the peaks beyond it are bare as bone. Yet the air that reaches you is heavy, sweet, and hot as breath held too long in a bathhouse. It does not thin out. It thickens. Steam meets whatever thinness is left and condenses before your eyes, beading on your skin, on leaves, on stone, running in clean lines down every vertical face.

The light, at least, is ordinary. A clear, white-yellow, late-morning light that shows colors true and makes no mystery of shadow. It does not change its angle. You check, later, and it is still where it was. No dusk is coming to cool the place, no dawn to start it over.

### The Sound Beneath Everything

You hear the place before you properly see it, and you hear it too much.

There are no birds. There are no insects, no frogs, no fish jumping in the canals, no animals at all in the high basins — not even in the rot. The silence where they ought to be is complete. And yet it is deafening here.

Under the whole city is a roar, low and constant, like a sea shut inside a mountain, boiling against stone. On top of that, a hiss — not from wind, because the air does not move in gusts, but from a thousand vents and wet seams where hot ground exhales through travertine crust. The canals talk, too: water sheeting over worn steps, spilling from aqueduct mouths into pools, dripping from every eave and root and bowed lintel into the pools below. Drips fall so densely they make a percussion, faster in the covered streets, slower on the open terraces, but never stopping and never varying in tempo.

Plants add their own noise. In heat that would curl them if they were not drenched, broad leaves rub and shiver against each other in the still air, a soft endless *shhk-shhk*. Seed pods pop with a sharp crack, somewhere, every few seconds, and new ones are always swelling to replace them. Hot stone ticks as it holds its heat. The sound comes into your chest as much as your ears — a drum-beat from below the city that you feel in your teeth on the grand stair.

It is loud everywhere, and it is the same loudness everywhere. You cannot follow it to a source and leave it behind.

### Water at the Surface

Water is not in rivers here so much as *out* of everything.

The ground is saturated a hand's breadth down. Where your foot lands on a plaza stone, a dark bloom spreads and does not go away. The cliffs weep — literally: black basalt faces sweat in vertical tears that gather into threads, threads into falls no thicker than a wrist, falling six meters, then vanishing sideways into a crack that is exactly the right size for them. The canals are not dug so much as settled into; over a very long time, hot water has worn their beds smooth and white with travertine, and they run at a level that is always ankle-deep, always knee-deep, always just skirting a step, depending on the street.

In the central basin, a lake occupies the lowest terrace and steams in a permanent, low cloud. Its surface is divided. On the west half the water is hot enough to scald; white mineral crust grows in rings where it cools. On the east half it is cold and black and still. The line between them is sharp as a cut and does not waver. Plants grow right up to it from both sides, leaves leaning toward whichever half they prefer, roots intertwined under the line in water that should not be both.

Rain never falls. It does not need to. Condensation is a weather. Every surface is a watershed.

### The City That Grew Into Itself

This is a city — undeniably, settlement-scale and then some. From the rim you can take in its whole order: seven terraced basins stepping down around the central lake, linked by radial causeways, each basin a town in itself with its own square, its own bathhouse, its own granaries and shrine-platforms and covered markets. Aqueducts cross on arches. Streets run in a grid in the lower town and spiral in the upper ones. Houses are roofed, doors are hung, stalls are carved ready for goods that never arrive.

And no one built it with tools that remain, and no one maintains it now.

The materials are all what the mountain gives: basalt in columns and blocks, white sinter soft as chalk that hardens in the sun, red iron clay packed into walls, and living wood — black-green trunks so dense they ring like stone when rained on. These are not ruins. Erosion here is not decay; it is finished. Every edge is rounded to the exact degree it will ever be rounded. The centers of the wide stairs are worn into shallow channels where unimaginable traffic once passed, and those channels are now permanent water-rills. The temples have not crumbled; their stone has settled into green patina, moss in every joint, and stopped. This is a city at equilibrium with its own weight.

Because nothing is new, nothing needs repair. Because nothing changes, nothing breaks further.

You walk the streets and find yourself always near-touching something. This is a dense place, not in the way of crowds, but in the way of layers. A house wall is also a tree trunk, hollowed by growth and then thickened around the hollow. Vines bridge from roof to roof, making green tunnels where the street below is also a canal, so you wade and duck at once. Roots head down through a bathhouse floor and come up through a granary slab, lifting nothing, cracking nothing — they have negotiated their shape here long ago. Hanging gardens fall from every bridge, fronds and orchids and bromeliads trailing ten meters toward the water, never trimmed, never wilting, dripping and steaming in the sun.

Vegetation is lush in strata, because there is no season to thin it. Emergent trees tower over the basin roofs, their crowns merging into a broken canopy. Beneath them, palms and tree ferns, beneath that, shrubs and giant herbaceous leaves, beneath that, a floor of moss so wet it gleams like oil, and everywhere the aquatic mats that skin the canals in green felt. The city and the jungle are not competing. They are the same dense tissue, stone giving the plants something firm to hold in ground that would otherwise be soup, plants giving the stone shade and a reason not to slump.

### Ecology Without Animals

With no animals, the work of eating and being eaten is done by water, heat, and fungi — the pale, thread-like growths that live on the undersides of leaves and in the black mud of the canals, blooming and dissolving in the same steady state. Fallen leaves do not rot into leafmold in a season; they dissolve in hot, mineral water, feeding the mats downstream. Spores drift in the steam, always in the same currents, settling on the same surfaces. A plant can live for as long as the city has been settled, because there is nothing to browse it, no pollinator to hurry it, no winter to end it. It grows at the edges, constantly, and dies at the edges, constantly, and the total green of the place never increases or decreases by so much as a leaf.

Your own footsteps are the first disturbance the place has registered in a very long time, and it registers them by simply closing over. A puddle you step through is a puddle again a breath later. A vine you push aside swings back to the same trembling position.

### The Breakdown, Settled

Reality here is not flickering. That would be a kind of weather. This is worse, and more stable: the impossible things are load-bearing.

One of the main rivers splits at a bridge. The left branch falls, steaming, into the canal system as a river should. The right branch flows *up* the cliff face beside it, in a smooth sheet, against gravity, to a ledge thirty meters above, where it turns and enters the rock — to reappear, presumably, below, having satisfied both directions at once. The plants along its upward course grow sideways, then up, then sideways again at the top, as if agreeing with it.

A grand stair connects the third terrace to the fourth. It rises, visibly and unrelentingly, for two hundred steps. At the top you are on the same level you left. The fourth terrace looks down on the third, and the third looks up at the fourth, and both views are correct from where you stand. A tree in the market square is hollow; inside its trunk is a small courtyard with a well, a bench carved from the wood itself, and a door that opens onto the square again, on the other side of the tree.

Shadows occasionally fall in two directions from the same noon light. Water in a basin will show a reflection of the city with one extra tower, which has no counterpart in the real one and never will. A drip in a covered market is heard a fraction of a second *before* you see it strike the pool. These are not events. They are features, mapped as surely as the canals. The vegetation has grown around them. The buildings were — or grew — around them. They do not move, spread, or heal.

### The Rule of the Place

After a while your sense of time, which came in with you, thins out like the air was supposed to. Nothing here is going anywhere. The sun does not cross. The water level does not rise or fall. The heat does not build toward evening. The roar under the city does not swell or fade. Growth and dissolution balance to the leaf. Erosion has done its work and retired. Even the weirdness is in balance.

It is a hot, wet, high, green, stony, empty city, sounding like a waterfall in a cathedral, built of everything and maintained by nothing, where impossibility is just another material — and it will be exactly this tomorrow, and exactly this a thousand years from your footprints, because tomorrow, in the sense the rest of the world uses, has never arrived here.
