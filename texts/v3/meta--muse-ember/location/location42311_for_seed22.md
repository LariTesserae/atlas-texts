There is more sky than ground here, and more ground than anything on it.

You are standing — if standing is the word for it on this surface — on a low, warm plain that goes on past the point where you can see anything in it. The light is dim, not dark. A high, bruised haze hangs over everything and thickens the sun to a dull coin, so there are no sharp shadows and no blue sky. It is the light of late afternoon underwater, except the air is warm enough to make your skin damp just by breathing. Heat comes up from the ground as much as down from above. The clay under your boots holds it and gives it back slowly.

The land itself is young. That is the first memorably wrong thing. This is not a plain worn smooth by a million years of wind and water. It is a fresh sheet, recently laid down and still sharp. It is cut into polygons by cracks as clean as knives, plates of pale, rusted clay tilted a degree or two against each other, ridges just high enough to trip you and hard enough to cut. Where the sheet has slumped, it has made raw, vertical-walled hollows a few meters deep and a few meters wide, their edges unsoftened, their floors still crumbling in dry flakes. No canyon has had time to form. No river has had time to carve. A few shallow rills streak the surface after a dribble of rain, fresh as scratches on new pottery, and vanish before they become streams.

And yet everything that lives here is lush.

There is no lake in this region. There is no river, no marsh, no standing water you could swim in and only a handful of hip-sized, stagnant pools sunk in the hollows, green-black and warm, where the ground funnels what little falls. If you press your palm to the clay it is cool and damp under the dry crust, a few centimeters down, and it smells of iron, warm earth, and wet stone. The water is not in the landscape in the way you expect water to be in a lush place. It is in the air.

Dawn and dusk the air saturates. A fog that is less weather than habit rolls low over the empty kilometers, beading on every surface, and then condenses and drips for hours in a thin, irregular patter that never quite becomes rain. Occasional rain comes as a warm gray drizzle that wets the crust and is gone. In between, condensation is the climate. It collects on leaves, on stone, on the city, and runs down in slow threads to the soil, where the ground has learned to drink it capillary by capillary. The damp lives in pores, in moss, in the big funnel-leaves of plants, in the capillary lattice of the buildings themselves.

Where moisture gathers, vegetation does not merely grow. It explodes.

It does so in islands, because most of the plain is empty in a way that is total. Between islands there is only the cracked, lichened clay, flat to the horizon in every direction. You can walk for an hour and see nothing but the polygon plates, the dim sky, and another smudge far off that may be a thicket or a building or a herd. This emptiness is not a failure of the ecosystem. It is its spacing. The plain is region-sized — a valley floor that continues for a hundred kilometers and more before it rises, very gently, into land that is not yet fully formed either — and the life on it is spread thin across that expanse the way stars are spread across a sky. Abundant, but rarely close.

At the islands, lush means a wall of green so dense it has weight. Ferns the size of doors, their fronds beaded with fog. Vines that spill from windows and balconies — though there are no windows in the ordinary sense — and run across the ground to link one structure to the next, rooting in damp pots of soil that the buildings themselves make. Moss and lichen bloom in acid greens and oranges on every damp face. Flowers open without any obvious pollinator nearby, or open and close in pulses you can almost see, as if drinking. The plants are not planted in beds. They are growing out of the buildings, out of the cracks, out of the condensation trays, feeding on the water the buildings pull from air. Between the islands, only the most stubborn crust plants survive, and the ground stays bare and damp and beautiful in its emptiness.

The islands are the city.

It is not a city in the contiguous sense. A city here is a constellation: node, then five kilometers of nothing, then node, then ten. Each node is twenty to eighty structures clustered around a central hollow, connected by paths etched into the clay that glow faintly underfoot and then fade, and by cables buried deep where only the fauna ever dig them up. From a distance a node looks like one building that has been folded over on itself. Up close it resolves into a dozen hollow spires and domes and low, curving halls, all made of a material that current physics has no good name for.

It is not stone, not metal, not glass, not plastic. It is translucent amber in places and pearl-white in others, warm to the touch, seamless. No joints. No rivets. It was not assembled; it was grown, or precipitated, or announced into being by swarms at the nanoscale that still live in its walls and maintain it. A structure can be solid from the road and phase-thin when you put your hand near it, as if the wall is deciding whether to be matter. Rooms open where they are needed and close when they are not, by the slow flow of that lattice. The buildings breathe — very slowly, audibly, through pores at their bases — and in breathing they exhale damp, warm air that feeds the vines.

They are hollow, deliberately, extravagantly hollow. You can step inside a spire and find a void the size of a cathedral with a garden growing up its inner walls toward the dim light at the top, and then nothing. You can look up through a dome and see the haze. The density of the place is low even inside its own architecture. Most of the volume of the city is air, garden, and quiet space for the sound to resonate in.

The buildings do not cast proper shadows. They absorb light and give it back as heat and, this is the strange part, as sound. They are also waterworks. Their outer surfaces are a forest of microscopic capillaries that harvest fog and dew, store it, mineralize it, and release it to soil and leaf and root on a schedule no one fully controls. In the driest hour of the warm day they are still dripping. On their roofs, condensation gathers and runs into troughs that feed the pools in the hollows below, where tiny, translucent animals — shrimp-like, blind, made almost entirely of the water they live in — come in their thousands to drink and breed and die in a day.

That is only the smallest fauna. The plain is loud with larger life, because the life is so spread out it must travel, and travel here is at continental scale.

Herds move across the emptiness in lines so long you see only the nearest edge of them. Slow, long-legged grazers with barrel bodies and necks like periscopes, their hides thick with lichen, step from polygon to polygon, testing the clay for damp. They are not fast. They do not need to be. Above them, or far behind them, the predators pace at the same unhurried tempo, matching the herd's speed with an patience that makes the whole system feel like a tide moving sideways. Winged things travel in flocks that are less flocks than weather — a darkening of the haze in one place, then another, kilometers apart but part of the same migration. Burrowers churn the young ground from below, opening and closing new hollows, letting air into the damp layer. In the thickets at the nodes, things dart, nest, sing, pollinate, scavenge. The built and the grown and the animal are not separate layers. The fauna use the nodes as landmarks, water sources, scratching posts, and wintering walls. Their dung and urine enrich the gardens. The gardens feed them. The buildings tune the microclimate around all of it.

And above all of it, under all of it, is the sound.

This is possibly the loudest empty place you will ever stand in. You expect emptiness to be silent. The opposite is true, and the reason is the spacing: sound has nowhere to be absorbed, and everything here is made to resonate.

It begins with the condensation. A constant, irregular dripping on clay, on leaves, on the hollow shells of the buildings — each drip amplified by the hollow it falls through into a soft, hollow *tonk* that seems to come from everywhere at once. The wind, which is warm and slow and almost always present, moves through the hollow spires and turns them into instruments: chords, fifths, long sighs that change as the air temperature changes. Beneath everything is the tech hum. Not a machine noise you can point to, but a pressure in your teeth and sternum, a low infrasonic wash from the lattice of the city, so constant that your own thoughts feel slowed by it.

Then the insects. In the damp thickets and over the bare clay, a static chorus of them — minute, translucent, living in the fog itself — raises a white-noise blanket that swells at dusk until you have to shout to be heard a meter away, and then shouts do not help because the air steals them. The megafauna add their own register: mating calls and contact calls that are not calls so much as foghorns made of resonating throat sacs, traveling ten kilometers across the flat plain without losing power, overlapping and interfering until the horizon itself seems to hum.

Maybe five layers at once, maybe ten, and it never stops. It shifts — fog thickens the high notes, a dry hour thins the dripping, a herd passing raises the low end by degrees as their steps, incredibly, are audible. Active does not begin to describe it. Weather cycles here are measured in hours: saturate, condense, drip, evaporate, saturate again, faster in the warm ground heat. Plants grow in visible pulses during the condensation hours and pause in the dry. Predator and prey waves move in seasonal tides that take weeks to cross the plain. City nodes bloom and contract; a spire that was open in the morning may be closed by night, its garden folded inside itself. The ground still settles, occasionally opening a new sharp crack with a sigh of damp air from below. Everything is in motion, but the motion is slow and vast, like breathing on a scale you have to live inside to notice.

The rules of this place follow from that. Water does not run downhill in rivers; it runs down plant stems and building walls and into soil pores, and in some of the hollow towers it runs upward for a little while, defying the weak gravity inside a phase-thin wall, before it finds its way to a leaf. Plants grow toward sound as much as toward light. Where a spire sings a certain chord, vines thicken to reach it. Fauna learn to navigate by the echoes of the city, and a herd will alter course to pass a node not for food but for the water and the resonance. The dim, tepid light is enough because almost everything here has learned to photosynthesize in it, or to live without true photosynthesis at all, feeding instead on damp, on minerals leached by the buildings, on each other.

You are invited, finally, to understand that the emptiness is not absence. It is the architecture. A city spread so thin that each settlement is surrounded by a country of its own. A lush ecosystem so dependent on air-borne water that it never needed rivers. A warm, dim, young plain still sharp from its recent making, whose very newness lets the buildings and the animals remake it daily. And a deafening quiet that is not quiet at all, but the sum of a thousand distant, hollow, living things all dripping, humming, calling, and breathing across the damp ground, each of them so far apart that the sound is the only thing that ever connects them.
