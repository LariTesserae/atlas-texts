**The Hollow Bazaar**

It is smaller than it sounds.

From its highest edge — and there is no high edge, only a shallow rim worn almost flat — you could walk the whole place in four minutes. A grove, a hamlet, a room. Its horizon stays low and close, pressed down by its own warm breath. Beyond the rim the vegetation thins, the ground dries, the ordinary world resumes. Inside, it stops as if cut. This is a bounded place, a single feature enclosed by its own age.

It does not look ancient in the way ruins look ancient. Nothing is sharp. Everything has settled. Erosion at this depth is not destruction, it is a final form. Stones are smooth as bones left too long in water. Hills have given up. The built things have slumped and melted into mounds that still remember streets. The whole bowl is primordial and finished, worn down to the last soft plane where water and plant can live — and yet it never finishes moving.

You notice first the emptiness between presences.

The density here is sparse. Monumental things stand far apart, scattered like punctuation over a wet page. Between them — thirty, fifty meters of nothing much: low plant mat, a warm pond, a rivulet that goes nowhere, mist rising in a thin sheet. The quiet is brief. Then the emptiness fills with sound, because the air is thick and wet and everything here resonates.

The ground is not soil. It is vegetation, total and unbroken. A living substrate of interwoven roots, moss, creeping stems, and broad, fleshy leaves pressed tight together, spongy underfoot. It depresses like a lung. It breathes. In places your step sinks and water wells up clear and warm around your ankle. In other places the mat is firm as woven timber, and you can follow it like a road. You never see bare earth. You never see a rock uncovered. Even the weeping rocks on the edge — great old boulders at the rim — are clothed in skin of green, and from their cracks water constantly sweats and drips, weeping without pause.

Water lives near the surface everywhere, and sometimes above it. The table is so high that condensation is a weather system of its own. Beads hang mid-air on nothing you can see, trembling on invisible filaments of spore-silk, growing until they fall all at once in a soft, irregular rain that was never cloud. Ponds are not contained by banks; they are held by root-lip, by living leather. Rivulets bubble up through the mat in random places, run a meter, and sink again. The air smells of warm loam, sweet rot, coppery sap, and crushed green leaf. Your skin stays damp. It is warm — not hot, but the warmth of shallow water in sun, of a body held close.

The city is inside this.

It is urban, unmistakably, though no two buildings stand close. The urban here is not a dense stack but an organized spread: districts marked by what they grow. The Nursery Quarter, where tower-thickets are lovingly pruned into lofts. The Weeping Quarter by the rocks, where houses are hollowed stems that drip all day. The Bazaar, central and low, where root-bridges cross and re-cross over a pond that deepens and shallows with the hour.

A house is a plant trained into architecture and then allowed to continue growing past the plan. Trunks thicken into walls. Leaves overlap into roofs that photosynthesize and repair themselves. Living wood is never dead; cut it and it seals with resin-glass, clear and hard as amber. Dead matter is not allowed to linger. It becomes substrate within hours. Layers of earlier construction are everywhere, eroded into the new: a doorway now half-buried in root, a stair now a ramp of fused stem, an old aqueduct of hollow cane now carrying sap instead of water, pumping it upward to the cathedral-tall plants that rise sparsely from the middle of town.

Those tall plants are the towers, the silos, the spore-spires. Six, eight of them, scattered. From their crowns they release fine clouds you can only see against the light. That is the infrastructure. That is also, apparently, the engineering.

There is no visible machine. No wire, no engine, no panel. What there is works by principles current physics describes only in pieces. Nanospore clouds seed the air and stitch damage in a night. Root networks compute in chemical pulses, routing water, signal, and nutrients along the same streets the fauna use. Dew is harvested at the scale of molecules and released on schedule, so the place waters itself though the sky above is merely grey-white and ordinary. Sound is stored in the tension of certain leaves and replayed when the wind finds them — so a path can hum with yesterday’s market. A building can be encouraged to grow a new room in an afternoon, not by force but by tuning the sap-flow, the light, the song of the birds nested in its eaves. It is post-modern tech disguised as gardening, and it is older than any city that would recognize it.

Fauna are abundant in a way that makes the sparseness stranger. The emptiness is not empty. Frogs the size of loaves sit in the doorways of houses, semi-transparent at the belly where you can see the water-core inside them. Fish jump not only in ponds but in the low, condensing air, arching on threads of mist. Insects with wings like wet paper move in slow, deafening choruses. Birds nest in the infrastructure — in the hollow stems, in the spore-spires, in the roof-leaves — and some of them do not fly out; they burrow down through the mat and re-emerge elsewhere, migrating internally. Crocodile-long amphibians lie half sunk in the bazaar pond and are politely stepped around. Everything eats something, pollinates something, repairs something. The animals are not decoration on the city; they are part of its maintenance crew, its messengers, its plumbing.

And it is loud. Not constantly, but in pulses that travel. First the dripping — a rain of drips from the weeping rocks, from leaf-tips, from the hanging beads of condensation, a percussion that never quite settles into rhythm. Then a frog chorus rises from the mat until your ears ring. Then a growth-surge: somewhere a root-street tightens, a tower creaks, a pond burps up a great bubble of warm gas and releases it with a pop you feel in your teeth. The plant surfaces are drums. Humidity carries. A sound made fifty meters away arrives as if made beside you.

Light is normal. A normal afternoon, sun a little diffused by canopy and mist, shadows green-grey and soft-edged. No perpetual twilight, no alien glow from the tech. The weirdness is more intimate for that.

The place is turbulent. That is its stable state. Within its ancient, settled bowl, change is hyperactive. Leaves fall en masse and regrow in a day from the same stems. The bazaar opens like a flower — vendors’ thickets unfurling, fauna swarming from ponds and mat and air to trade spores, sap-sweets, woven root-cloth, songs — and then, in an hour, folds shut and the crowd vanishes back into the substrate as if sucked down. Water bubbles up through one quarter and down through another. A bridge of root will sag, be eaten, and regrow a meter to the left. If you stand still long enough, the ground beneath you adjusts to the shape of your feet, learning your weight, raising a soft callus of moss to keep you from sinking. The city does not stay put. It churns within its boundary like something alive in a very old, very beautiful skull.

This, perhaps, is the rule: erosion and growth have become the same act. The oldest-stable form here is not stone wearing down, but plant wearing down stone, plant wearing down plant, city wearing down into plant, plant wearing up into city again. Death is so thoroughly recycled that there is nothing to decay in the usual sense; there is only transformation, fast and slow at once.

By evening — a normal evening, light cooling through the towering leaves — the Hollow is at its most itself. The sparse towers stand far apart against a low sky. The ponds reflect them imperfectly, rippling with bubbles. The mat sighs under scattered footsteps, some yours, most not. Water weeps from the rocks. Somewhere a spore-cloud blooms and the air itself seems to stitch, repairing a tree, feeding a frog, condensing a future rain.

It is ancient and it is in its final form, and its final form is this churn. A small, wet, warm, plant-filled town the size of a clearing, where a beyond-modern intelligence grows in the open, where everything is alive and therefore everything is architecture, and where you can hear, right up close, the loud, strange, ordinary sound of a world that has long since stopped being made of anything but itself.
