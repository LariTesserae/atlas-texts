It begins with sound, long before it begins with shape.

You are already in the water when you first understand where you are. Warm, thick, and close all around — a bath you cannot stand up out of, filling everything to a vaulted ceiling far below any street. The pressure sits in your ears. The temperature sits on your skin, not lukewarm but body-warm, waxing hotter as you move inward, as if the water itself were being heated from below. You taste copper and hot stone and a faint sulfur tang at the back of your throat. There is no air. There is no surface to break. There is no dry place to land.

The sound is in the water, not in the air. A low, continuous roar that you feel in your sternum and your teeth, layered with a higher shriek that comes and goes, and with the irregular hammering of metal somewhere ahead. It is deafening not because it is simply loud, but because it saturates everything — every wall, pipe, and bone is a speaker. You could not shout. You could not listen for a footstep, a voice, a warning. You navigate by vibration the way you would in a washing machine the size of a hall.

Darkness comes next. Not absolute black, but dim and local. Light does not fall from above; there is no above to fall from, only rough rock and poured concrete closing over the chamber. What light there is, is made here. Amber industrial lamps on far-spaced piers, a few red indicator lights, a cold white rectangle in a control cabin, sparks from a junction box, and the glitter of suspended particles passing through each cone. Between the lamps are long stretches where nothing is visible at all — twenty, thirty metres of empty, warm water with only drifting silt and heat shimmer to suggest there is a room. It is a pocket, a contained hollow no larger than a great warehouse, and yet the emptiness inside it is enormous. Almost nothing per volume. A single lamp can burn for what feels like a mile of dark.

Reach out your hand and eventually you touch something. A rail. A pipe as thick as your thigh. The floor under the silt.

The floor is not natural ground. It is built, in stepped industrial terraces of concrete and steel plate, descending toward the center. Channels are worn into it, not by a river but by the place’s own weather. In the middle the terraces open into a basin where the heat comes from — a cluster of heat exchangers and twin turbines anchored in iron, boiling locally, spilling up clouds of fine bubbles that do not rise straight but spiral and shear. From that basin a permanent convection cell rolls outward for the whole length of the chamber: hot water upwells in the centre, spreads at the top, cools against the walls, and returns along the floor. It is turbulent everywhere, and yet it is old. The whirlpool in the basin never moves. The fast lanes follow the same canals between buildings year after year. The storm is settled.

That is the city, once you can see it in pieces. It is urban in the confined way of a plant town: piers for streets, blocks for halls, layered construction stacked in a narrow vertical space. The lowest level is a grid of sunken canals, three metres deep, between squat factory blocks. Above them run catwalks and gangways, and above those, a second street of overhead pipelines, cable trays, and crane rails — all of it submerged. Against the outer walls are the smaller structures, the housing and storage and office pods, their portholes dark except for one or two that still glow from within. Everything is organized, orthogonal, deliberate. And almost everything is empty.

The machinery is still working. That is what makes the emptiness uncanny rather than simply abandoned. A press in an otherwise dark hall cycles every forty-seven seconds on nothing. A conveyor turns slowly, carrying no load. A pump starts with a surge, settles into its roar, and never stops. A crane hangs mid-swing, swaying a fraction in the current, never completing its arc. Switchboards hum behind clouded glass. The place was built wet — a geothermal brine exchange, a chemical works, a processing town meant to live below ground and below water — and it has converted its flooding into its normal state. It does not need people to keep its rhythms.

Everything has been weathered into its own state. This is not a fresh ruin. Concrete is softened and pitted. Steel is rusted to a deep red-brown and then, where the current is fastest, scoured bright. Paint on stencils survives in sheltered places, white letters under a grey film: LEVEL 3, INTAKE, KEEP CLEAR. Stair edges are worn into shallow dips. Rubber hoses have stiffened and sagged into permanent arcs. Copper wiring, fists thick, runs through the water like slow roots, furred with deposit. Where turbulence is strong, surfaces are clean; where it drops, they are buried under a soft, pale mud of mineral silt. The city has slumped a little, leaned into the flow, and found a balance. Nothing looks new. Nothing looks like it is falling down right now.

There are no plants. That is absolute in a way the silt is not. No kelp, no weeds in the cracks, no green anywhere — there is no light to make it, and the water is too hot and too chemical and too loud for it to want to try. In place of vegetation there are mineral gardens, and they are beautiful in a brittle, inorganic way. Where warm brine meets cooler stone, silica grows in needles and fans, sulfur in pale yellow rosettes, iron oxide in dark, shrubby tufts that snap if you brush them. They grow off pipes and pier feet and the rims of valves, catching the lamp light. In the quietest corners, behind baffles, there are films of fungus and simpler microbial mat — grey-white, slimy, almost alive — but even those are sparse, and they smell faintly of a cellar. The ecology here is mostly precipitation. The place grows stone the way a forest would grow leaves, slowly, in the lee of things.

Animal life is rarer still. You may swim for an hour — if an hour can be measured in roar and pressure pulses — without seeing anything move except the machines and the current. Then, for a second, a pale shape crosses a lamp cone: a blind fish, long and unpigmented, riding the return current along the floor, using the rhythm of the turbines the way a bird uses wind. In the intake screens, translucent shrimp the size of a thumb pick at the fungal film, their gills working in the warm outflow. Once, very rarely, something larger passes through the high, empty water between the central vortex and the wall, too fast and too far to identify, and is gone. They do not live here so much as they pass through the edges of here, in the pockets where the sound drops enough to think.

The rules of the place follow from the turbulence. Heavy things sink into the channels and stay there, sorted by the canals. Light things — a dropped glove, a length of insulation, a plastic crate — catch in the permanent eddies behind piers and circle there for years, slowly coming apart. Heat rises in visible plumes from the basin, so you can see warmer and colder water as different weathers; near the exchangers it scalds, at the outer wall it is a comfortable, steaming bath. Every ninety seconds or so the whole chamber takes a pressure pulse as a pump cycles, and the water contracts and expands around you, making lamps sway and chains tick against pipes. Bubbles race upward like inverted rain to burst on the vaulted ceiling and run back down the walls in bright rivulets. It is chaotic on the scale of seconds, and completely predictable on the scale of years.

And so it persists: a hidden, pocket-sized town under the ground, entirely drowned in its own warm working fluid, dim and deafening and empty, its streets full of water and its factories cycling for no one. The lamps burn in their spaced rows, the turbines turn, the mineral gardens grow a little thicker on the pipes, and the current settles, again and again, into the same old, turbulent shape.
