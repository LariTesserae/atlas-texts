First it seems like weather.

You are high up — not on a peak, exactly, but above the lowlands of the world, where the air has thinned enough to feel cool on wet skin and comes to you already full of water. The light is dim, filtered through something that never quite lets the sun finish arriving. The horizon does not end in sky. It ends in a motion, a rolling, dark-green surface that rises and falls like a sea in slow swell, ridged, furred, steaming faintly at its crests. It goes on farther than sight, farther than weather should allow, and from a distance it could be a mountain range wreathed in cloud, or a forest seen from above, or something else entirely, because from here the distinctions have not yet separated.

Coming closer — and the place comes closer to you, on its own weather — the sea resolves into leaves. Then into too many leaves to count. Then into structure.

There is no ground here in the way ground usually means. What you would stand on is a mat, meters thick, of interlocked root and rhizome, of moss cushions and fern fronds pressed flat by their own weight, of fungal threads like wet silk binding everything to everything else. Above it, filling every vertical inch, are further mats: low creepers, shrubs whose trunks are no thicker than a wrist, canopy trees whose branches spread and then spread again, lianas that loop and re-loop until branch becomes net and net becomes branch. At any height you care to name, there is a leaf in front of a leaf. The density is not lush in the familiar sense of abundant growth with clearings between. It is packed to saturation. Light has to negotiate its way down through seven, ten, twenty layers, each one stealing a little more, until the dim that began at the top becomes a green-grey twilight at the bottom that never fully darkens and never fully brightens. You do not see soil. You do not see sky for more than a second at a time.

This is total vegetation in the most literal sense. Plants are not *in* this landscape; they *are* the landscape. The hills are plants. The valleys are plants sagging between hills. Even the stone — for there is stone, but only just, and only in the newest places — is a temporary condition. Where the high plateau is still emerging, where condensation has outrun colonization by a season or a century, you find bare rock sweating in the cool air, weeping clean water from its pores, black and slick and newly exposed. Within days algae crusts it. Within weeks a spore lands, germinates, sends a hair-thin root into a crack, and the stone begins, very slowly, to be un-stoned. There has been no time, and no mechanism, for erosion to do its usual work. Rain does not cut gullies because rain almost never reaches a bare surface. It is caught, and caught again, and caught again, by successive canopies, and released as drip, as mist, as running water inside hollow stems. Rivers here do not carve channels; they travel *through* the living mat, pulsing along root conduits, braiding themselves through leaf litter that is still alive. A wound in the surface — a trunk snapped by its own weight, a mat torn by a surge of growth — is sealed in hours by cambium flowing like warm wax. The land heals faster than water can harm it. It is, geologically speaking, barely born, and it is being born plant-first. Stone will come later, if it comes at all, precipitated out of all this wet, saturated life.

Water is near the surface everywhere, and also in the air, and also inside everything. The atmosphere is condensing atmosphere: cold air moving over warmer, wet leaves makes fog that beads on your eyelashes, on the hairs of your arms, until you drip for no reason you can see. Trunks weep. Leaves weep. The stone at the frontier weeps. Underfoot the mat gives with a wet, spongy resistance and recovers; pressed water rises around your feet, clear and very cold. The whole vast plateau is a marsh lifted to altitude, a sponge continent holding water at every level, exhaling it as cloud at its edges, inhaling it as dew along its interior. Its weather is its own. Storms are born in the canopy, fed by evaporation from below and condensation above, and crawl across the green sea for days, dropping rain that fuels more growth that feeds more storm. From above, the weather *is* the terrain breathing.

And it is loud. Impossibly loud.

There is no animal life here to make this noise. No birds, no insects, no frogs, no fish flashing in the root-rivers. That expected chorus is absent, and what replaces it is larger and less patterned, and therefore harder to escape. Rain on twenty layers of leaf is not rain; it is surf, continuous, crashing, the hiss of an ocean that surrounds you on all sides and below. Beneath it, the plants themselves work. Trunks expand a millimeter and pop like knuckles. Hollow stems resonate and boom when water strikes them. Lianas under tension creak and groan, deep and slow, like rigging on a ship. Saps rise. Seedpods dry and crack with reports like small fireworks. At night — darker night, under the dim canopy — growth pulses migrate across the plateau in visible waves, a peristaltic tightening of the whole mat, and they make a sound you feel in your teeth: a low grinding, a continental stretching, as if the land itself were inhaling. The sound does not come from a place. It accumulates. In the hollows between canopy hills it builds until your chest vibrates sympathetically, and you understand that deafening is not an event here but a condition, the acoustic equivalent of the packed density. Everything is touching everything else, so everything can transmit.

That transmission is part of what the plants are doing, and what they are doing defies tidy explanation. They are not merely growing; they are engineering at a level our physics only half-describes. Mycelial nets under the mat exchange not just nutrients but structured signals, pulse-coded, error-corrected, across distances of kilometers, solving problems of distribution and repair in real time. Spores ride the condensing air like instruments, their coats bending light and charge to steer themselves toward wet stone and virgin substrate with a precision that looks designed. Certain leaves do not just photosynthesize; they focus and transport light internally along translucent veins, lighting their own deep tissues the way a fiber carries a signal. Aerial roots exude compounds that ionize mist and coax water out of air that should be too thin to yield it, liquefying their own weather. None of this is built. There is not a single made thing here, no tool, no wall, no path, no artifact left behind by a maker. A bridge across a root-river is a grown arch, thickened over decades by pressure and response. A dome is a folded leaf. A sheltered hollow is two canopies grown together. The technology is not separate from the biology. It *is* the biology, operating at nanoscale and continent-scale simultaneously, fabricating matter molecule by molecule from air, water, and dim light — and doing it with no factory, no blueprint outside the living tissue itself.

So the rules are simple, and absolute. Whatever holds still long enough becomes substrate. Whatever falls becomes layer. Light is intercepted. Water is intercepted. Sound is transmitted. Nothing is allowed to die and leave. Decay happens in place, instantly recycled by fungi and bacteria and feeding roots, so litter never accumulates as litter; it becomes the mat, becomes air, becomes next year's leaf. Competition is plant against plant: for a gap in the light, for a centimeter of wet stone at the frontier, for the right to be the trunk that bridges. There is no predator, because there is no animal to predate, and no prey, because everything that moves, moves by growing.

It is active, ceaselessly. Growth fronts advance across the newest stone at a pace you could almost watch if you stayed long enough. Pulses of expansion travel. Rain falls and is re-caught and falls again inside the canopy, a closed loop. Spore snow drifts down through the dim in slow curtains. The sea of leaves heaves, ever so slightly, with water and with its own immense metabolic breathing.

And it is still emerging. That is the strangest part for something so vast and so finished-seeming. The plateau is continental in scale — it has its own interior weather, its own distant horizons, its own physics of packed life — and yet it began recently, and is still, at its edges and its summits, pushing up into bare air and bare stone and claiming them. The high ground is not old rock made habitable. It is habitability made into high ground. Weeping, condensing, saturated, dim, cool, deafening, green beyond counting — a world whose foundation is not underneath the plants, but is the plants, growing themselves into being, filling every space they make, leaving no room for anything else.
