The light gets there before anything else does.

It comes in hard and unsoftened, white-gold and almost vertical, because the air up here is thin. That is the first rule of this place: you are high, and you are far out on the top of a very large thing. It is not a peak. After enough time a peak would have been worn away. This is a continental table, lifted long ago and then left to the wind, and the wind has finished the work. Everything has reached its settled form.

The stone is pale — buff, ash-grey, iron-red in the dust, salt-white where a crust has blown and stuck. Slopes fall at the exact angle stone will fall, and then they keep falling, grain by grain, until they are not slopes at all but long, gentle swells. Cliffs have crumbled into aprons, aprons have flattened into fields of broken pebbles, fields have been polished by grit until each pebble is a ventifact, faceted and humming when the wind finds it. There are no sharp ridges, no fresh scars. Deep, broad swales run for kilometres, carved not by water — there has never been water here to carve — but by deflation, by dust lifted and carried and dropped, by stone creeping a fraction under its own weight in the heat of the day and settling in the cold. The whole surface looks old in the way a hand looks old: everything weathered, everything in balance, nothing young except the light.

And there is no water. Not in the sky, not in the ground, not in any pool or seep or riverbed. Water exists here only as a prisoner — locked in crystal, bound in clay minerals, a trace inside living cells that never dares to be free. Nothing melts. Nothing rains. Thirst, as a word for a liquid, has no meaning. What the living things drink is not water.

From a distance — and distance here is very long, a day's walk does not change the horizon much — the plateau looks barren. Bare stone to the edge of the world, with the sky going dark blue overhead even at noon because there is so little air to scatter it, and the haze at the horizon a thin, pearly dust. The sun makes shadows that are black and absolute. Step from sun to shade and the temperature drops as if you had stepped into a cellar, though the stone under your feet in the sun is only warm, never hot. The air is cool, and always moving a little, and it carries no sound far. A footstep crunches and is swallowed. A shout would be a brief roughness and then nothing. Silence here is not an absence; it is a material, thick enough to lean against.

Walk far enough across the emptiness and you will find it, suddenly: green.

Not everywhere. Between presences there is a great deal of nothing, and that nothing is part of the design. The lushness gathers, dense and almost shocking in its saturation, in shallow hollows and on the lee side of worn boulders and in any place where the night can settle. The groundcover is sun-moss, a mat of filaments finer than hair, springy underfoot, emerald and chartreuse and teal where the light hits it, almost black-green in shadow. It is cool to touch even in full sun. When you push it aside with a hand it gives off a sharp, clean, faintly metallic smell, and your fingers come away damp.

That damp is not water. Inside the moss the cells are sponges of silica, and the sponges hold a clear, viscous ammoniac fluid — the solvent of this world’s life. At night, when the stone radiates its heat away and the plateau cools in a matter of minutes, a faint vapour thickens out of the thin air: ammonia and sulphur compounds exhaled by the ammonium minerals in the sunlit rock, a breath the whole continent makes and takes back. It condenses on the cool moss and on the undersides of stones, and the moss drinks it straight through its skin. By morning the sun lifts it again in a glittering lift of vapour that hangs over a grove for an hour like the grove is smoking, then is gone. That is the rain. That is the dew. That is the river.

The moss colonies are the forest floor, and they are alive in a way that makes you slow down involuntarily. Their edges creep. A front of green will advance over bare stone a few centimetres a day, swallowing the pale dust, leaving behind it a thin, darkened, enriched skin of mineral soil that the wind cannot lift so easily. A mat may take a century to cross a single swale. It never hurries, but it never stops, and over the scale of this place — over the scale on which weather itself moves here — the landscape is the green moving as much as the stone.

Rising out of the mats, where the hollow is deep enough, are the fan-ferns. They have no wood. Each is a colony, not a tree: a rosette of arching silica lace, tubes and fronds branching and re-branching in a fractal that looks soft from afar and is in fact stiff as bone and rings faintly if you brush it. They stand a person’s height, some twice that, and their shade is a place — a cold, black-green pool on the stone, where the moss beneath is lusher and the night vapour lingers latest. Their spores are not flowers. At certain hours they release plumes of pale dust that rise straight up in the still, thin air and then travel, because the wind is always travelling, for tens of kilometres before settling. New green is born far from its parent, a speck in a crack, a smear on a boulder, waiting for a night cold enough.

Everything here is active, even though nothing is loud. The wind is doing its slow abrasion. The stones tick and contract as they cool. The mats are migrating. The dust is falling in a sort of dry snow on some mornings — crystals of salt that land on warm stone and vanish with a faint fizz. And the weather has room to make itself. Because the plateau is so vast, you can watch a whole system be born in the middle of nowhere: a convection column over a dark moss grove, wide and slow and silent, rising until it flattens and spreads and dies, all within your field of view. Or a dust front, a wall a hundred kilometres wide, coming at walking pace with its shadow ahead of it for an hour, darkening the stone in a band before the grit arrives. The sky makes its own cycles here, unhurried and enormous, and they cast the only long shadows the place knows.

Life that moves is rare. That is the second balance.

There are the grazers, and there are very few of them. A single one may be a kilometre from the next. They are large and unhurried, barrel-bodied and long-necked, covered in dull plates of keratin and silica that blend with the stone until they move. They do not eat the fans; they eat the moss, cropping it into broad, pale trails that close behind them in a season. They get their fluid from the moss itself, biting a mat and taking the ammoniac sap straight from the sponge-cells. They walk mostly at dawn, when the light is bright but the air is still cold, placing each foot with a care that makes almost no sound even if you are standing beside them. You hear their breathing before you see them — a slow bellows, in through the nose, out through the mouth, once a minute. They have no predator large enough to bother them, and no need to hurry from one. One grazer’s territory is a grove, and it will return to it for years.

Under the stones, in the cool dark where the fern shade touches the ground, live the burrowers. Small, blind, mole-furred things with foreclaws like spades, they live on fallen spore-dust and the roots of the moss, and they come out only in the brief amber interval before night, when the vapour is thickening and the stone is still warm. They are the prey. Above them, so high and so rarely seen that you might walk for a week without noticing, rides the glider. It is not a bird — nothing here is a bird — but a leathery, bat-winged hunter that uses the convection columns the way sailors use trade winds, circling without a wingbeat for hours, watching for the one small movement in the moss. It takes one burrower, perhaps, and then is gone again over the horizon, a silhouette against a sky that is already dark blue. Predator and prey, grazer and mat, wind and stone: the whole dynamic web is stretched thin across an enormous surface, which is why it can hold together at all.

At night the rules reverse. The light, which seemed absolute, cuts off. The stone gives up its day’s warmth in a rush, the air turns bitterly cold, and the stars arrive all at once, hard and close, because there is so little air between you and them. The moss folds its finest filaments in, conserving the day’s captured energy. The ammoniac vapour comes down thick enough to glitter on every surface, and the whole plateau smells faintly of the groves. Somewhere far off, a grazer sighs in its sleep. The ticking of the stones continues, a slow percussion as the continent cools and settles, and then even that stops, and the silence is complete except for the wind polishing a pebble somewhere you cannot see.

Nothing has been built here. No track, no mark, no tool, no bone-camp that lasts. The grazers’ trails close. The moss covers the stone again. The wind erases the wind’s own work and begins it over, patiently, at the scale of continents.

To be here is to be in a place that is lush and empty at the same time, bright and cold, ancient and still in motion. The green does not need water because it found another way to be wet. The stone does not need youth because it has become, at last, exactly what it is. And the emptiness between the groves is not a lack — it is the distance that allows the whole thing to breathe, one slow, silent, luminous breath at a time.
