**VESSKAR, ON THE HIGH SILL**

From the top of the Sill the whole place lies open below you, and that is the first surprise: for all its height it does not hide.

You come over a basalt pass at mid-morning, in ordinary daylight — flat, grey-white, neither bright nor gloaming, washed out by the cloud that lives in the bowl. The air is warm against your face, damp and close, smelling of wet stone, hot metal, ozone from the overhead wires, and faintly of sulfur. Below, a shallow basin spreads for several miles, rimmed by weathered ridges, and Vesskar occupies it in a loose, regional sprawl.

It is a city, unmistakably. Stone and concrete terraces climb the lower slopes in tiers, factories and sheds and cooling towers stand along a cut river gorge, gantries and conveyor bridges cross the centre, streets are laid out, lit, wired, piped. But it is a city with a great deal of emptiness in it. Between one district and the next there are hardstand lots, gravel flats, reservoir basins, marshy ground where the water table comes up to the surface and the mud goes slick and mineral-bright underfoot. Blocks are widely spaced. Avenues are wide and mostly empty. From the ridge you can see all of it at once — the Spine, the Terraces, the Flats, the Yards — multiple features held in one open view, none of them crowded against the others.

The sound reaches you before you descend. It is not an event. It does not rise and fall in incidents. It is a continuous roar, made of many things merging into one texture: turbines in the gorge, pumps hammering water through pipes under the streets, fans and conveyors and the brakes of incline trains, wind in vents, water dropping and sheeting down everywhere. In the centre of the Spine it is deafening in the old industrial sense — shouting is useless, conversation is a formality done with signs and written notes and shrugs, and you feel it through the soles of your feet and in your teeth. On the residential terraces, a half-mile away and twenty metres higher, it is still loud enough that you carry your ear-plugs around your neck out of habit, like beadwork. People are born here, wear them as children, put them in when they go down to work, take them out at home, and still live their whole lives inside that hum. A lull, when it comes, is only a slight thinning, lasting minutes, and every puddle still shivers.

That shivering is the other strange thing made ordinary. The basin is wet in a way that is not simply rain. Rain comes, often, but the city makes rain of its own. Cooling towers breathe out great slow plumes that do not disperse; they hang, cool, condense, and fall again as a fine warm drizzle over the roofs they rose from. At knee height on winterless mornings the steam lies thick and never quite lifts. Cold surfaces — steel beams, window glass, the undersides of pipes — bead and drip continuously. The basalt road-cuts weep long after any weather, dark streaks of warm water running down the rock face and pooling in the gutter, carrying a faint orange silt. Basements are damp. pavements never fully dry. In the flat low ground at the basin's edge, where the old river spread out, water stands at the surface in sheets, marsh-edge and stagnant, reflecting the towers in ripples that never settle.

Warmth comes from the same source. At this elevation you expect cold. Vesskar is warm because the mountain under it is warm — geothermal springs feeding the plant, hot water in the pipes, waste heat in the walls. People walk in shirtsleeves at midday. You do not get out of breath from the height so much as from the humidity, the air holding moisture until your own breath fogs a little. The buildings sweat it out in summer light that is normal, unremarkable, glinting off wet concrete.

Everything has been here a long time. That is the erosion of it: not ruin, but *established*. The concrete has softened at the edges and darkened where water perpetually runs. Steel has been painted and painted again, layers visible where it chips. Foundations have settled a few careful centimetres; doorways lean into their lintels; streets have worn into shallow bowls that collect water exactly where the water always wanted to go. Pipes run over pipes, over roads, into tunnels, in layers built by different generations but now forming a single weathered skin. Nothing looks new and nothing looks abandoned. The city has reached a balance with the rock it was cut into — retaining walls hold the slopes, the gorge is smoothed and channelled, the flats are levelled and flooded on purpose — and so it changes only slowly. Rust penetrates by millimetres a year. Mineral scale thickens inside a boiler over a season. A tank fills over weeks. A conveyor moves at a pace you could walk beside. There are no abrupt seasons, no storms that rip the place open, no rush hours. Time here is measured in long, patient increments, and the deafening machinery turns as if it has always turned and will turn in the same way for your grandchildren.

Against all that wet, warm, industrial permanence, living things are incidental. Vegetation is sparse to the point of being a rumour. There are no lawns, no parks worth the name. The bowl is mostly stone, cinder, concrete and wet metal. Where water is still and not too chemical, a black-green lichen spreads on north walls in maps no one reads, and orange algae blooms in warm puddles. In gutters, where runoff slows, tufts of salt grass and sedge push up, glossy and stunted. In the marsh flats at the edge, reeds stand in the shallow water, and a few plane trees have been planted in square concrete pits in a terrace plaza — they survive, leaves shining with condensation, never really thriving. Everything else that grows does so in cracks, thin and mineral-stained, tolerated rather than encouraged.

Fauna is rarer still. The noise drives most things out; the water, hot and mineralised in the channels, kills most of the rest. If you watch long enough you will see evidence, rather than animals: a nest of grey finches tucked into the warm ducting of a boiler house, birds that are functionally deaf and build with copper wire and string; a blind newt in a culvert under the Spine, pale in the sluggish flow; small fish in the cooler outflow where it meets the marsh, moving like flecks. At night, moths gather thickly around the sodium lights in the Yards and are eaten by nothing. People keep no pets in the central districts — sound and steam are unkind to them — and pigeons, who might be expected here, are absent. The ecology of Vesskar is mostly the ecology of its materials: bacteria in the warm water, moss in the joints of the paving, the fungi that freckle the underside of the cooling towers.

You descend finally into the Terraces, where people actually live, in six-storey blocks of basalt-faced concrete with small balconies, laundry steaming next to washing steam, window boxes with the meh plantings. The stairs are wet. The handrails are warm. Somewhere below a pump keeps its rhythm. A child crosses the empty avenue between blocks with a paper note tucked in a pocket, because calling across the street would be lost anyway. No one hurries. There is nowhere in the basin that is far from anything else in terms of walking, but distances feel large because of the emptiness between them and the slow way the air hangs.

That is how the place holds together: high and warm, urban and industrial, wet at the surface and condensing in the air, sparse in people and plants and animals, open to the sky it makes its own weather over, and settled — eroded, weathered, in balance — into a state it shows no intention of leaving. It weeps, it hums, it steams, it drips. It does not grow fast. It does not die fast either.
