**The Weeping Bowl**

You see it first from the rim, and you see all of it at once.

It is a small place — a few hundred paces across, no more — tucked into a high plateau well above the weather that makes clouds below. From up here the lower world is a slow sea of white, the peaks are pale teeth, and the air is thin and hard and cold in a way that gets into your teeth. The sun is not bright in the friendly sense. It is white, vertical, merciless. It bounces off stone and ice and comes back at you from below, so there is no shade to hide in that is not also lit, blue-shadowed and glaring. Squinting is involuntary. The people who live here wear slitted visors of bone and smoked hide, and even then they move with their heads down.

The bowl itself is ancient and settled. No fresh scarps, no sharp edges. Glaciers and a thousand melt-seasons have polished the basalt walls into rounded, grey shoulders, grooved them into shallow terraces, worn the floor into smooth hollows and low, pottery-like channels. Everything stone is weathered to its own final form. Against that finished, sleeping base, everything else is in restless motion.

Start with the water, because the water never stops.

This is a wet place that is also perpetually frozen. The floor is not dry ground but saturated slush over an impermeable lens of ice a hand's breadth down. Step anywhere and the ground gives with a soft squelch and a thin sheet of meltwater rises around your boots. Pools form in the lowest hollows and never quite dry, because they cannot drain. The rock walls weep. All day, in bright sun, dark seams in the stone bead and run in cold threads, spilling over lichen and down in dark streaks, freezing into fringes at night that grow inward over the paths. Condensation hangs low even when it has not rained — it beads on eyebrows, on roof-hides, on the insides of houses, on the glassy lids of the pools. Nothing here is ever really dry except the wind-scoured tops of the rim. Try to dry a mitten by the hearth and it will be damp again by morning from the air itself.

And yet there is almost no plant life. That is the first strangeness. For all the wetness, for all the light, the bowl floor is mostly grey stone, white ice, black slush, and the faint green-brown stain of moss in cracks where water lingers longest. Lichen crusts the sunny faces in dull orange and pale green. A few stunted, willow-thin shrubs cling to the channel banks, no taller than a knee, their roots feeling for warmth in the stone. There is no forest, no meadow, no turf to cut for building — what sod they use is scraped thin and precious. The brightness and the cold and the drowning ground between them have kept vegetation to a minimum, a sparse punctuation.

Life compensates, frantically, in animal form.

Where plants are scarce, fauna are layered, near-touching, abundant beyond any reason. Above the town, the rim walls are packed solid with nesting cliff-birds — small, white, tern-like things that crowd shoulder to shoulder in collars so dense the rock disappears, constantly arriving and departing in silent white flickers. Below them, in the town, people in their dozens of houses share walls. Below the people's feet, under the clear ice of the pools, fish move in silver schools so thick the water flashes. They are visible through the ice as if swimming in glass, turning together, never still. In the slush between the channels, translucent worm-eels, blind and pale as tallow, wriggle through the saturated soil, eating frost and each other. Under every loose stone are mites, springtails, beetles grazing lichen. A burrowing, furred swimmer — half mole, half otter, no bigger than a forearm — paddles through the melt-ditches between houses with only its nose above water, and its tunnels collapse and refill daily.

It is a dense ecology with almost no green in it. The animals eat each other, eat algae that blooms in the melt, eat mineral film off stone, eat the abundant waste of the birds above and the fish below. The bowl is a closed, crowded chain, perched high, bright, and cold.

The town fills the bowl to its edges, because it has to.

There are perhaps seventy dwellings, and that is a town. They are not scattered. They are built in the eroded flats and along the weeping channels, shoulder to shoulder, wall to wall, roof overlapping roof, lanes no wider than two people can squeeze past, alleys that are really drainage ditches with planks over them. Houses are low, square, massively layered: a stone footing sunk into slush, a frame of scarce wood and bone lashed with sinew, walls of sod and woven skin and flat slate, roofs weighted with stones and ice slabs. Older houses have newer lean-tos built against them, and newer still have store-pits dug into their floors and boat-like sleds stored on their roofs. There is a central market hollow where the melt collects, ringed by drying racks, shared ice-storehouses, and a communal hearth-house that is the only building with a chimney tall enough to clear the roofs. Retaining walls, culverts, little bridges of woven branches, steps cut into rock — an infrastructure, organized and urban in its habits, laid out to direct water away from doors and toward the fish pools.

All of it is made with primitive means. No wheels — the ground would swallow them. No metal to speak of except a few prized, small knives traded up from below, kept wrapped in fat. Everything else is chipped stone, bone needles, stone adzes, gut cord, chewing and tying and patient handwork. Ice blocks for building are sawn with stone-toothed saws and dragged on sleds. Lamps burn fat and the dung of the burrowers, because wood is too scarce to waste. It is town-making done entirely by hand, in cold that stiffens fingers, and therefore done constantly.

Because the ground moves.

This is the turbulence. The base is settled, but the surface churns. Frost heave lifts a house a finger's width on a night, tilts it, drops it. A new ice lens grows under a lane and shoves the retaining wall out. By morning a channel that served one row of houses has been blocked by ice and floods another. The market ice thickens overnight and cracks in the sun, creating new pools and destroying old paths. Houses are bailed, re-levveled, re-tied, their doors re-hung, their internal walls moved to shore up a side that sank in slush. A dwelling that was a winter home becomes a fish store in spring because the slab of ice it sits on has literally drifted two paces toward the center. Creation and destruction happen at walking pace, every day: ice builds the floor in the night, melt unbuilds it by noon. The inhabitants live in the middle of that churn with an anthill, bazaar-like energy — carrying, mending, shouting in low voices, trading fish for fat, for a stone blade, for a length of cord — always rebuilding the place they live in.

That is why it is so strange that it is quiet.

For all that motion, the bowl makes almost no sound. The cold itself seems to swallow it. Breath freezes before a shout can form; people speak low, in short phrases, their words puffing white. Snow and slush soak up footsteps. The rim walls block the high wind and turn it into a thin, occasional sigh. You hear, instead, the small sounds: the drip-drip from the weeping rocks, a faint tick-tick as ice expands after dark, the soft squelch of the lanes, the muffled murmur of the market that never rises, the click of a bone needle, the subdued thump of a fish hitting the underside of a pool lid. The birds above arrive and leave without song, only a rush of air. The turbulence is visual and manual, not acoustic — a frantic, silent churning under a bright, cold sky.

And there are other odd rules that emerge once you stay a while. Ice here forms clear as window glass in the intense light, so walking the town you walk over its own history: last year's lanes, a drowned storehouse, tools dropped a decade ago, all preserved, doubled and distorted beneath your feet. Water in the narrowest frost-cracks does not run down, as it should, but climbs a few inches up a stone face before freezing, as if pulled by cold. Rocks weep even in full sun, and the weep is colder than the air. Nothing rots. Things that fall in the slush do not decay; they freeze-mummify and are found, months later, intact and faintly glowing in refracted light. The people do not bury their dead deep; they lay them in shallow ice niches in the rim wall, where the weeping water seals them in transparent stone, and they remain visible, smiling faintly through the ice, part of the architecture.

By evening — though the light stays hard until it drops abruptly — the bowl settles into itself again from a distance: a white and grey hamlet, packed tight in its polished hollow, steam and condensation rising off every roof, birds coming home in a silent white rain, fish turning under the surface like slow silver weather. It looks established, in-balance, long-existed. Up close it is wet, crowded, bright, freezing, and never for a minute still.
