**The Seyra Basin**

It sits higher than almost anything around it, and yet it does not feel cold. That is the first wrongness, and it sets the terms for everything else.

From the passes at five thousand metres, the air is thin enough that you have to climb in short, deliberate breaths. Your lips crack. Your shadow is hard-edged and dark. And under that thin air, the basin steams. It is equatorial highland — the sun passes almost overhead, a perfectly ordinary sun, giving perfectly ordinary, white-balanced light, but the stone is too hot to sit on barefoot at noon and the ground is damp under your boots at the same hour. The basin holds its own weather like a lid. Clouds do not mainly come from outside; they are born inside, made from geothermal warmth rising through the floor and from the ceaseless exhalation of the forest itself, and they hang, dissolve, and are born again in the same valleys.

It is not one valley. It is a region, a cluster of linked basins spread over perhaps forty kilometres, seen all at once from any ridge: a western bog of black water and sedge, a central lake chain, an eastern wall of mossed cliffs and hanging meadows, passes that drop away to lower country you can see for a day's walk but which is already clouded over. The horizon is layered — foreground spongy turf, middle slopes in green, background crest-lines worn soft.

The mountains here are old and settled. They have the shape of things that have been left alone for a very long time. Even the harshest cliffs are shouldered and rounded, draped in talus that has turned to soil, undercut by waterfalls that have polished their lanes smooth as wood. Red-brown loam lies deep in the hollows. Black basalt shines wet even when it has not rained, because the rock weeps. Every cliff face has its seep-lines, its darkened streaks, its beads of water that gather, run a hand's breadth, and vanish into moss. Travertine terraces, ivory-white and ribbed, step down from the hot springs where the water arrives mineral-sweet and sulfurous and warm enough to bathe in. Obsidian shards lie in the streams, glassy and cold to the touch in one place, sun-warmed in the next. Nothing looks fresh. Nothing looks new. The whole place has reached an equilibrium and sits in it, patiently.

Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it. The ground is a sponge. In the bogs you sink to the ankle and the water comes up around the print, clear and iron-tasting, full of blue-green algae mats that shift underfoot like velvet. Lakes are perched, shallow, and ringed not with sand but with floating turf. Small streams split and rejoin a hundred times across a meadow, making braids, then disappear into root mats, then emerge again on the other side, cooler. Rain, when it comes, rarely arrives as a storm. It arrives as a slow vertical mist that thickens until the canopy drips for an hour after the sky has cleared, and individual drops hang in the air long enough to be seen turning in the light. At dawn every leaf, every stone, every blade of tussock grass is beaded. Condensation is a form of weather here, daily and reliable. By mid-morning the forest is exhaling visibly, white breath rising in columns from the warm pools and the wet earth.

And it is lush, densely, layeredly lush, in a way that altitude should not allow.

There is no bare rock that is not also a garden. The cliffs are vertical forests: moss, then ferns, then orchids and bromeliads rooted in cracks, then vines hanging straight down for metres, thick as your wrist, swaying slowly. On the flats the tussock grass grows waist-high in hummocks so close they touch, humming when the wind finds them, each hummock a small ecosystem of its own. Tree ferns open like umbrellas over a darker understory of broad-leaved shrubs. Bromeliads the size of urns hold a mouthful of water in their centre, and in that water live frogs, beetles, and mosquito larvae in complete miniature worlds. Orchids cling in drifts. The ground between trees is not soil so much as a mat — moss and liverwort and low creeping herbs interwoven so tightly that a fallen leaf takes years to reach the earth.

This density extends upward and downward. Lianas bridge one tree to another, not merely for support but as highways. Epiphytes bridge the lianas. Birds move through all three layers without ever quite landing on the ground. At the water's edge, reeds and giant sedges make a third wall. Every square metre has at least three presences touching or nearly touching. It is high-saturation living, green on green on green, made vivid by the wet: everything gilded, everything reflecting.

The fauna answers the abundance. At midday the basin is loud in a way that is not a single sound but a palimpsest of sounds arriving from different heights. High, thin, metallic, from the insects — a cicada-like chorus pitched so high you feel it between your ears, and swarms of small flies and beetles whose wing-cases are iridescent as oil on water. Mid-level, from the meadows and ponds — warblers and other tussock birds singing in short, unhurried phrases, dragonflies ticking over the lakes on wings that catch the sun, frogs clacking and popping from the bogs in bass notes so regular they become a kind of clock. From the forest, deeper and harder to place — the grunt of a large, shy mammal moving through the understory, the flap of a raptor high on a thermal that rises from the hot stone. And underneath all of it, threading through the other sounds, a low hum.

You feel that hum before you locate it. It lives in the molars. It rises from the ground when you sit on the warm basalt, and from the bog water when you put your hand in. Rocks tick as they heat and cool through the day. Steam sighs from cracks. Waterfalls — there is a new one every few hundred metres, a white thread falling thirty metres to a mossy ledge, then another — make a constant, distant roar that never quite stops, so that silence, when you notice it, is only an interval between choruses.

The animals themselves are strangely at home in the heat and the thin air. A small, dark squirrel-like creature with enormous lungs and a prehensile tail moves through the canopy carrying its young on its back. Frogs with mottled green skin carry strings of eggs along their flanks. The lakes hold small silver fish, endemic, blind in the bright water for half the day, and a beetle that walks upside-down on the underside of floating leaves, its feet still dry. Pollination is done less by bees than by a fierce, abundant guild of flies, which at this altitude and warmth never seem to thin out. Lizards bask on the travertine where the stone is hottest. Nothing here is sparse. Even predation is layered: snakes in the warm rocks, hawks above the passes, and in the deep bogs, things you hear but do not see surfacing.

You can walk for a day and see nothing built. Then, in the afternoon, you will find something.

A low ring of stones in the bog, fused together as if they had been melted and cooled in place, too regular to be natural, no mortar, no tool-marks you can name, about knee-high, with a shallow channel cut through its centre. Moss grows on it in a perfect, though unfamiliar, geometry. A smooth pillar in the forest, warmer than the tree beside it, its top worn concave, holding rainwater that never seems to evaporate. A line of ankle-high markers across a meadow, marching in a straight course for two hundred metres, now bent, lichen-covered, and half-sunk — but the grass is greener and the soil richer along that line than anywhere else. A dome half-buried in the travertine, ceramic-like and seamless, breathing slowly with the condensation cycle, in and out, in and out.

These are the scattered remains, and they are not ruins in any dramatic sense. There is no city, no road, no wall. There are perhaps a dozen such places in the whole basin, perhaps more that have been wholly taken back. They are small and faint and partial, the occasional artifact that the forest has not yet decided to finish digesting.

But they are not inert. That is the second wrongness, and the deeper one.

The plateau itself is laced with a substrate you cannot see, made of mixed minerals, crystal lattices, and the fungal network that runs through everything. It works — if that is the word — by principles current physics describes only in fragments: massive parallelism in chemistry, information carried in water and in the slow movement of ions through wet stone, self-repair at the nanoscale, cooling and filtering and buffering performed not by a device but by the arrangement of the whole. The nodes you find are endpoints, places where the system is focussed, where water is sieved finest, where a slope is held at exactly the angle that prevents a landslide, where heat is drawn off the hot springs and spread into the soil so the roots stay warm but not cooked. Nothing blinks. Nothing projects. It happens at the pace of soil formation.

That pace is the ruling law of the place. Seyra changes, but it changes as a mountain changes. A tree that obliterates a clearing takes a thousand years to do it and another thousand to rot into loam. A lake shore moves a handspan in a human lifetime. A waterfall cliff loses a pebble per decade. The system of nodes is decaying, but so slowly that decay looks like maintenance. Rain here does not carve a new gorge in a season; it perfects an old one. Even the weather has two speeds: the quick, local cycle of mist forming and dissolving in an hour, and the slow, great cycle of water entering the rock, travelling through it for years, and emerging in a different spring, tasting different.

The strange things happen at the edges of that slowness, if you watch long enough. On certain evenings, water runs *up* a cliff face of black obsidian, in thin branching lines, against gravity, following the mineral veins. Some orchids open only facing away from the nearest other orchid, as if pollination required a kind of privacy the substrate enforces. Pebble beds in the streams sort themselves over the years — by colour, by size — in bands too neat for unaided current. The frogs pause, all at once, when the low hum changes key, and resume when it settles. Birds sing the same phrase for years, their young learning it note for note. At dusk, the moss on the warm stones gives off faint plumes of heat that are visible as shimmer, rising straight up and not dispersing, a small weather of their own.

None of it is violent. None of it is loud in the way of a machine. It is loud the way a forest is loud: constantly, abundantly, alive. And it is strange the way a very old, very well-tended thing is strange — not alien, but no longer quite classifiable as wilderness or artifact. It is a third state: a world that was engineered once, by minds or processes we no longer have a vocabulary for, and has since been left to run so slowly and so completely that the engineering has become indistinguishable from the ecology.

At evening the basin does what it does every day. The sun, still ordinary, drops toward the western ridge and the light flattens and warms. The mist thickens inside the valleys, not falling, rising. The insect chorus changes pitch. The frogs take over the bass. The hot springs steam more heavily as the air cools a fraction — still hot, always hot, the stone holding the day's heat under your back. The weeping rocks weep a little more. Somewhere below a node you passed, water moves through a channel cut a thousand years before you arrived, filtered, cooled, remembered, and emerges in a bog on the other side of the plateau, tasting of iron and green.

It does not sleep. It settles.
