From a distance it does not look like a finished thing so much as a settled one.

The plateau is high enough that the air is thin and cool against the skin, and the light never quite commits to being day. It is a flat, diffuse grey-white, coming down through a permanent skim of cloud, and by late afternoon it is already the color of early evening. Electric lights are on all the time — not bright, just present, a low amber or white glow in ten thousand windows and under the decks of the gantries, as if the place has decided that dimness is a condition to be lived with rather than corrected.

What you see first, from the ridge road, is not a city so much as a topography. The natural slope has been taken and re-taken until there is no natural slope left. In its place: terraces. Step after step of poured concrete, cast stone, steel decking, corrugated iron, and glass, each level retained by a wall, each wall carrying pipes, wires, walkways, planters bolted to its face. The forms have weathered into their own state. Rust has bloomed and then stopped blooming. Concrete has cracked in the same familiar branching patterns for decades. Moss has colonized the north sides of everything and stabilized there. It is not new, and it is not ruining. It is established — long-existed, in balance, as if builder and decay have come to an agreement and are renewing it every few years in small, local ways.

That is the first of its paradoxes: it is completely made, and it is also constantly coming apart and being remade, and neither process is winning.

Up close, nothing touches natural ground. Even the cliffs at the far edges, where the plateau drops away, are not rock but successive pours of slag-concrete and blockwork, built up to hold the terraces in. The ground under your feet is deck, or pavement, or grating over a culvert, or the roof of the level below. When you need soil, it has been brought in bags and bins and tree-pits and long troughs perched on ledges. And on that made ground, vegetation is unreasonably lush.

It is not wild in any romantic sense. It is dense, close, low, and green everywhere a handprint could fit. Ferns push from expansion joints. Vines have been trained — and then have escaped the training — over conveyor housings and stair rails and the sides of water tanks. In the gutters, where runoff gathers, watercress and algae make bright slicks. On the middle terraces, between workshops, are the food terraces: long, low greenhouses with condensation running down the inside of their glass in permanent rain, and open beds of leafy crops, beans twining up cable, small stunted trees in concrete tubs whose roots have learned to follow the seams of the deck below them. Moss thickens on every damp iron surface until the iron looks soft. The plants are not decoration. They are part of the machine. They cool the walls, they slow the water, they hold the dust, they tell you when a drain is blocked because they grow thicker there first. A terrace that goes brown is a terrace that is failing. A terrace that goes too green is also failing, but slower.

Water is never far away, but it is never spectacular. The word for it here is wet. The air itself carries it. Warm air from inside — from kitchens, greenhouses, workshops, bodies, engines idling on lower power — meets the cool outer shells of the buildings and gives up its moisture. So the walls weep. Not in jets, but in a perpetual thin film that gathers on cold concrete and cast stone and runs down in dark streaks, beading at mortar joints, dripping from every pipe elbow, every windowsill, every lip of decking onto the deck below. After a rain, which is frequent and unremarkable, the whole place ticks and pings as it drains. Channels run constantly along the edges of each terrace, shallow and fast, under grating where you can hear them if you crouch: a clean, close sound of water moving over concrete just beneath your boots. Puddles do not stand long. They are busy. The water table, such as it is, is a managed skin just below the decks, recirculating up through filters and algae beds and down again. Nothing is allowed to waste it, and nothing is allowed to let it accumulate.

Despite all this water and all this building, it is mostly quiet.

That is the second paradox, and the one visitors tend to notice first once they are inside it. For a place of this density and industry, with this much motion, the sound floor is low. The engines that are everywhere — hydraulic lifts, small freight rails, overhead cranes, belt conveyors, the generators that power the terraces — are old, slow, and well-lagged. They move on pneumatics and electricity rather than roar. They sigh, they clunk softly, they whirr under insulation, they throw a steam vent in a brief white exhale and then close again. The packed walls absorb what is left. The wet vegetation absorbs more. People, who are everywhere, speak at a working murmur, as people do when they have been in the same damp, dim air for years and shouting would be an effort lost to the terraces.

What you hear, in order, is: water dripping, in rhythms that vary by level; the soft rolling of cart wheels on wet decking; a distant, muffled thud of a press somewhere three levels down; the high faint buzz of transformers; occasionally an eel-thin squeal of a rail cart taking a curve; and never, or almost never, a bird. The quiet is not empty. It is full of small, close sounds, and because the place is so packed, those sounds are immediate. A cough two decks over carries. A kettle in a kitchen behind a thin wall carries. The whole thing hums at a level just below conversation, like something thinking.

And yet it will not sit still. If the sound is hushed, the motion is turbulent — anthill turbulence, bazaar turbulence. At any given minute, some thousands of things are in transit: carts of produce moving up a ramp, a crane slinging a beam from one roof to another, a belt carrying sorted scrap in a steady dribbling stream, workers and residents flowing between shifts in a packed, unhurried stream along the narrow walkways, shutters opening and closing, tanks being tapped and drained and refilled. Whole sections are in rotation. A workshop will be gutted and refitted into a drying room while its old machines are carried out in pieces and reused two terraces down. A retaining wall that has been in place for forty years will be taken down block by block in a morning because the culvert behind it has shifted, and by evening a new wall — same blocks, re-laid, some replaced — is already being planted with ferns. It is creation and destruction on a small, constant scale, a churn that has become the stable state. From above, you can see it: light flickering in windows as rooms change use, steam pulsing, carts threading like blood cells. The form stays. The contents do not.

Everything is used, because there is no room for anything not to be. Density here is not crowded in the sense of a single street. It is saturated in three dimensions. A wall is not a wall; it is a wall with a pipe, a planter, a sign, a vent, and a nest-box that is empty. A roof is not a roof; it is a roof with tanks, a greenhouse, a laundry line, a solar hatch, and a walkway to the roof next door. Between one terrace and the next there is no gap: the underside of the upper deck is the ceiling of the lower workshop, lined with ducts and hanging lamps and storage. Between one building and the next there is no alley; there is a shared service spine, damp and green at the bottom where the ferns grow thick in the drip. If a space stood empty for more than a few days, someone would have put something in it. That is not a law written down, but it is the rule the place lives by, as firmly as drainage.

The industry is mundane and scaled to the terraces. No great chimney plumes — the air is too cool and too wet for them to draw well — but a forest of small stacks and vents, each releasing a thin, quickly dispersing breath. The factories make things for the place itself and for the valley below the plateau: building components, bound plant fiber, processed food, glass, tools, the constant supply of replacement parts for the place. Power comes from the high edge, where the wind is broken by the towers and the water is collected and slowed, and is distributed down through thick bundles of cable that sag, deliberately, between anchor points, dripping condensation. Light, heat, water, and material all move in loops. Waste heat from a lower engine warms a greenhouse above it. The outflow from a greenhouse feeds the algae beds. The algae beds feed a fish tank in a basement that no animal larger than a forearm ever sees.

Fauna, in the nonhuman sense, is rare. There are no dogs, no livestock out in the open, no flocks. The few animals that persist have learned to be small and discreet. Moths around the lamps at night in summer, silver and battered by the damp. Drain eels in the culverts, moving like slow punctuation in the flow. Pigeons — a few, stubborn pairs — in the eaves of the highest terraces. Rats in the deep service spines, heard more than seen. A cat or two per district, kept indoors mostly, formal owners of a particular windowsill. That is nearly the whole list. The place is too cool, too wet, too made, and too constantly disturbed for anything larger to find a niche. People fill that niche instead; human presence is the dominant animal life, and it is everywhere, in layers.

The rules are all ordinary, which is why they hold. Everything must drain. Everything must be reused. Nothing natural may be exposed for long, because the damp will take it back and the terraces will slump. Plants may grow where they like so long as they do not block a drain or a walkway, and if they do, they are cut back and composted into the bins. Noise must be kept low — not by ordinance, but because the walls are thin and the air carries and everyone prefers it that way. Structures must be inspected after heavy condensation or rain, not because something might collapse dramatically, but because a missed drip becomes a softened joint becomes a softened foundation over years.

By night, which arrives as a slight dimming rather than a darkening, the place reveals its true extent. The lamps come up everywhere, a thousand small points spread across the stepped slope, reflected in the wet decks and the running channels and the clouded greenhouse glass. From the ridge you can see for a long way — district to district, terrace to terrace — and the whole valley of it fades into mist at the lower edges, as if it were continuing past what you can see. It probably is. The plateau was built long ago and has been full for longer, and it has settled into that fullness in the way a natural thing might settle into a hillside.

Nothing about it is strange, except in the aggregate. No mystery, no apparition, no unexplained gleam. Just cool air that is always a little too wet, dim light that is always a little too low, green that will grow on anything if you give it water, and the endless, quiet churning of a built world that has learned to keep itself alive by never quite finishing any part of itself.
