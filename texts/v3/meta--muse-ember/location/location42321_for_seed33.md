The first thing is the height, and the cold that comes with it.

You are not on a mountain so much as on top of one. The air is thin and cool all the time, cool in a dry way that has nothing to do with ice — there is no frost here, no damp, no condensation. Exposed stone stays cool through the whole slow day. The light is dim, from a small, pale sun that never climbs high. It makes the sky dark overhead even when the horizon is lit a dull grey-copper, and it makes long, faint shadows that fall in more than one direction. Details in hollows go dark early. Distances look sharp, because there is nothing wet in the air to haze them, but they do not look true.

There is no water anywhere. Not in the air, not in the ground, not in the hollows. The channels that cut the plateau are dry gravel beds, shallow and rounded, carrying only wind-blown dust. The pale crusts on flat ground look at first like salt, but they are dry mineral efflorescence, powdery silica and feldspar dust packed hard. Grains tick against your boots. Nothing soaks. Nothing melts. Nothing rains.

There is no vegetation, either. Nothing green, nothing leafed, nothing rooted. What spreads in black and rust-red dendrites across the stone is not alive — fractal mineral growth, a few millimetres a year, branching like frost patterns but dry to the touch. It creeps at night and stops in the light. Where it is thickest the rock is soft and gritty underfoot, as if the surface is slowly turning itself into soil without ever becoming soil. No animal eats it for nourishment in any plant sense. It is just there, a pattern the stone makes to cover itself.

The plateau is vast in the open sense. It extends past sight in every direction, multi-featured and regional: basins a kilometre across, fields of basalt spires jointed like organ pipes, terraced rises, dry pans, and — gradually, as you walk — straight lines that should not be there.

Erosion here is established. That is the word for it. Everything has been worn long enough to have settled. There are no fresh cliffs, no sharp new faces, no raw cuts. Spires are rounded at the base, towers are softened at the corners, channels are shallow dished lines in the dust. The whole place has reached its own state, weathered to a single palette of warm grey, dark basalt, and pale dust, and it stays in that balance. It is not being torn down quickly. It is resting in a form it has had for a very long time.

Except it is also, underneath that stillness, active. The quiet is deceptive.

Wind comes in slow, regular cycles, not gusts so much as currents that think about where they are going. Sheets of fine dust move across the plateau in ribbons, in the dry channels, against the lee sides of walls, constantly re-laying themselves in thin dunes that no sound accompanies. At dusk — a long, dimming without a true sunset — cooling stone ticks, one tick, then another, far apart. A rock settles somewhere with a low groan you might never hear again. Your own steps are muffled by dust; you feel them more than you hear them. Most of the time it is silence, of a kind that makes the rare sounds seem placed, not made.

Then the lines resolve.

A terrace is too level. A rise is aligned with the next rise. A basalt fissure runs straight for half a kilometre and meets another at a right angle. Mesas turn out to be blocks. Spires turn out to be towers. The dust on the ground is packed along avenues that run true north and true east for as far as you can see, even when they should follow the fall of the land. Doorways open in tower walls, tall and narrow, dark inside. Arches span empty streets. Plazas are broad, empty pans of paved stone. Walls are layered — an outer wall, a ruined inner wall, a later wall built in the gap, all in the same weathered stone, all softened to the same grey.

This is a city. Settlement-scale, then town-scale, then larger than any town should be at this height, extending past the immediate into districts you see as low silhouettes in the dim light. And it is built without a single device. No metal. No wire. No wheel-ruts. No lamps, no engines, no glass — only stone laid on stone, dust rammed between courses, weight holding weight. If there were tools once, they are gone. If there were builders, they left no bones, no marks, no other technology. Nothing hums. Nothing lights up.

And it is empty in a way that is part of the plan, or part of the forgetting. Density is almost nothing per volume. Between one cluster of towers and the next there may be kilometres of bare plateau, of empty avenues, of courtyards with nothing in them but dust and mineral dendrites. Inside the built areas themselves, buildings are hollow shells, plazas are hollow, streets are hollow. You can stand in the middle of the city and see almost no city — just vast space between presences, a few isolated masses against the sky. It spreads like a city that was laid out for a population that never came, or that came and left so completely that even the idea of crowd has eroded away.

The fauna are rare, in keeping with everything else.

You may walk for a day and see none. Then, far down an avenue, a grazer. Tall, grey, slow, with a long neck and legs jointed in a way that makes you count them twice. It moves without sound, scraping its mouth along the mineral crusts and the dust on the stone, taking in grit and surface growth the way other animals take in grass — except there is no grass, and what it takes would poison anything that needed water to digest it. It carries its body low between the towers, coming out at the dim parts of the day, travelling the avenues as if the city were its migration route. When it stops, it stops completely. It looks, from far away, like another piece of architecture that has decided, briefly, to be an animal.

Smaller ones live under slabs and in doorways, stone-coloured, slow, making that ticking sound when they shift. And something hunts the grazers. You never see it close. You see a place on a tower wall that was shadow and then is not, and you see a grazer a hundred metres further along the avenue than it was, moving faster than grazers move. The predator-prey pace is the same everywhere here: slow, spaced, silent. Nothing chases loudly. Nothing as a rule moves at all, except that everything, over time, does.

Because the rules are worn.

Reality here is eroded past stone. You notice it first in distance. A tower looks near — its courses, its doorway, clear in the thin air. You walk toward it for an hour and it is no nearer. You walk for another hour and it has not grown. Then you turn a corner in the avenue and it is suddenly beside you, and the avenue you thought you were on is a different one, with the same arch, the same cracked paving, the same dark doorway, but facing the other way.

Recurrence is everywhere. The same plaza appears at the end of three different streets. The same view of spires repeats with one spire missing. A block you pass in the morning is ahead of you again in the afternoon, slumped a fraction more downhill, as if the whole city is creeping, very slowly, toward the edge of the plateau and then, at the edge, beginning again. Shadows fall upward off the tower walls toward that low sun, and a second, fainter shadow falls downward, as if light is coming from both sides of the world. The horizon does not close. At the far rim it bends, slightly, upward, and for a moment you can see the underside of a sky that is not above you.

Sound arrives out of order here, when it arrives at all. A stone ticks before the temperature has changed. A distant groan of settling rock echoes in your chest before it reaches your ears, if it reaches them at all. Mostly there is just the visual fact of movement — dust currents, a tower slumping a millimetre, a grazer's neck moving — with no corresponding sound, as if the air is too thin and too dry to carry the world to itself.

And yet it all continues. That is the strangest part, and the most established. Nothing is new, nothing is finished. The dust rivers run in their dry beds every day. The mineral dendrites spread and are scraped and spread again. The grazers migrate along the avenues between the empty districts. Towers settle. Cracks widen at a pace you could watch if you stayed still long enough, which you cannot, because staying still makes the city seem to shift around you. The dim sun moves. The cool holds. The silence holds, broken only by ticks.

You are on a high, cold, dry plateau with no water and no plants, where an enormous, device-less city has been weathered until rock and building are the same material, until erosion has become the architecture. It is open and empty and urban at once, rare with life, constant with slow motion, quiet with movement. And because everything here has been eroded so long and so thoroughly, it is no longer just stone that is wearing thin.

The edges of things are wearing thin.
