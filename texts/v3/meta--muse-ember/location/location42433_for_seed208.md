It is not a marsh, though it is wet everywhere.

You come onto it first at foot level. The ground is stone, low and open, and the water is not in a river or a lake but in the stone itself. A thin film lies over everything, broken only where your boot presses it aside. Where you step, water wells up in the print and closes slowly behind you. The stone is cold through the sole, cold enough to ache after an hour. The air is cool too, and it does not clear. It hangs in a permanent, low mist that gathers on your eyebrows, on the backs of your hands, on the upper faces of rocks, beads and runs and gathers again. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a flat grey without shadows, as if it were always just before dawn. The sky is a lid. The horizon is very far, and there is nothing to stop the eye or the wind except the land's own shallow swelling.

This is an old place in a way that is no longer dramatic. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing is new. The plain is a worn dome, hardly rolling, polished by water over timescales that have taken all the edges off. Boulders the size of huts have been rounded down to humps, half-sunk, pitted and smoothed until their origin is unreadable. Smaller stones are like river pebbles scaled up, glossy and blind. Even the faint structures people have made — a crescent of three slabs, knee-high, for a windbreak; a cairn marking a channel; a shallow runnel carved into silt to lead water from one pan to another — have already softened at the corners and begun to sink in. There is no soil to speak of. In the hollows there is a grey silt, gritty and itself waterlogged, and beyond that, stone again. The landscape has settled into its final form. Rain falls and drips and does not, in a human lifetime, change the shape of anything.

There is no green. No grass underfoot, no reed at the water's edge, no leaf, no twig, no root. The first time you notice it is by the silence under your feet — no rustle, no crackle, only the slap and suck of wet stone. What grows here is lower and stranger than plants and does not need light the way plants do. In damp patches, black and ochre crusts spread over the stone in irregular sheets. Pale rings, white-grey and faintly luminous, expand and contract over the silt. In the pans and seeps, translucent threads and slime-films drift and cling. Fungi, in the broad sense, and simpler films still — things that eat dissolved stone and the cold air's moisture, not sunlight. Every so often a flat pulses, inflates with invisible gas, and releases a soft puff of spores in a pale cloud that the mist takes away. Over years, the whole mat migrates, a fraction of an inch at a time, downslope. A black flat from a decade ago is grey now. Nothing roots. Nothing ever will; the stone is too cold and too wet, the light too dim, the water too constant for seeds even if seeds arrived.

And for all that — for all the emptiness between things — it is not empty.

The water is always moving, though slowly. It emerges along invisible lines in the stone, in weeping boulder-fields where each rock has a darker wet stain running from its crown and gives off water in a steady bead, a thread, a drip. It spreads into sheet-pans, mirrors so shallow your reflection is only a ripple. It condenses, it percolates, it rises and falls with the mist's slow tides. A bank of fog will crawl across the plain like a tide coming in, the dripping will thicken, the pans will swell a finger's width, and hours later it will recede and the films will tighten. Between one pan and the next there may be a quarter-mile of bare, slick, wind-polished stone where nothing moves at all. But where the water sits, life is crowded.

Over the pans hang clouds of midges, so dense they darken the grey air in a shivering sheet, rising and falling together. In the water, filter-feeders wave feathery gills, translucent as the film itself, straining the silt. On the stone at the water's edge, slick-grazers — flat, limpet-like, pale grey, blind — rasp their way across the fungal crusts, leaving polished trails behind them. They are eaten in turn by tap-predators, six-legged and long-feelered, that patrol the pans by touch and by listening, tapping the stone with a hard forelimb and waiting for the answer. Silt-worms, white and eyeless, knot and unknot themselves in the wet grit, turning it over. Everything is colourless or grey or black, because there is no plant pigment to borrow. In the dim light they do not need to be seen. They need to be heard.

Because the sound is deafening.

It is not one sound. It is layer upon layer until your own voice is lost in it and you feel it through your teeth when you stand on the wet stone. The wind, with nothing to rustle, hisses straight and unbroken across the open miles. Every weeping rock drips onto a hollow worn into the stone below, and the hollow is a drum. Thousands of drums, out of time, ticking and plinking and their collective hiss is like rain on a tin roof laid over the whole horizon. The pans are stippled without pause by condensation falling from the mist, a fine patter. Under that, the grazers rasp, the midges hum, the predators tap, the worms suck and release in the silt. When the mist-tide comes in, the dripping doubles, the drums thicken, and the roar rises until you must shout to be heard a pace away, and then give up shouting. It is how the animals hunt, how they find each other, how they stun prey in the shallow water: not with eyes, but with amplified vibration through wet stone. The place resounds because the stone is wet, smooth, and hollowed everywhere. Some of the cupped hollows sing a clear note when a drip finds them — a pure bell-tone that the predators favour and will wait beside for an hour, listening for prey to come to the ringing.

People come, but sparsely, and they do not stay long. The built things are scattered so far apart that you may walk a day between them: a windbreak crescent, a grinding slab smoothed concave by use, a cache of hammerstones and flakes of harder stone, water-worn even in the hand, left beside a pan. Their tech is hand-deep — stone pounders, scoops carved from bone, channels dug in silt with a curved flake to keep a pan from stagnating or to lead water to a shelter. They harvest the fungal mats and the grazers and the filter-feeders by scraping and pounding, using the same stones for tool and for table. They build nothing that roofs itself; there is no wood, no fibre, nothing to burn or bind except what the animals provide. Their marks are faint because the plain takes them back so quickly. A runnel fills with silt, a slab sinks a finger's width in a season, a flake is polished by the film of water that never leaves it.

The rules here are closed and old. Water lifts minerals out of the stone it has been wearing for eons. The films and fungi break those minerals down and hold them. The grazers eat the films, the filterers strain the water, the predators eat the grazers, the worms eat the dead of all of them and return it to the silt. The mist takes the water up again and sets it down on the cold stone. Nothing green ever enters the cycle, and nothing needs to. It is an ecology after plants, or before them — an end-state that is still, furiously, active: dripping, crawling, hunting, sporing, tapping, migrating in slow waves across the stone.

And there is a strangeness to it, moderate and constant, that makes you distrust your eyes. The pans reflect the grey sky, but on still moments — there are few still moments — some reflect nothing at all, a flat blackness where the animals have gathered below the surface in a solid sheet. The fungal rings brighten, faintly, when the sound is loudest, as if the vibration feeds them. The water in the films sometimes runs, for a stretch, fractionally uphill, following not slope but the resonance of the stone. You learn not to look for a source. The water comes up out of the ground everywhere, the sound comes up out of the ground everywhere, the life comes up out of the ground everywhere, and between those weeping, tapping, glistening presences there is only the long, cold, polished emptiness stretching to a horizon that never quite arrives.

You leave as you came, wet to the bone, ears ringing, boots full of pan-water that will drain away into the stone within the hour. Behind you the mist does not lift. The drums go on ticking over the worn, final, shining plain, and the grey light holds, and the world, having lost its plants and its sharpness and its soil, goes on dripping and listening to itself.
