At first, it is only water.

Not a sea with a shore somewhere. Not a lake you can cross. Water in every direction, cool enough that your teeth ache if you stay still, pressing on all sides with a steady, submarine weight. You can drift for a long time and see nothing change except the slow green-grey shift of light, the occasional flicker of something far off, and the feeling that the current has turned again.

That is the first truth of the place: it is vast, and it is mostly empty.

The scale is not that of a town, or a harbor, or even a country. It is the scale of weather. Currents move across it the way winds move across a continent, in broad, slow fronts that take days to pass. A warm plume from a factory cluster can rise five hundred meters and bend sideways for kilometers, making its own local summer of bubbles and plankton, and you can feel the temperature change on your skin before you ever see what made it. Distances are not measured in minutes. A node of activity — a cluster of towers, a yard, a junction — may be ten, twenty, forty kilometers from the next, and between them there is nothing but volume. Fish pass in schools like sudden weather, and then they are gone. Plankton drift like snow that doesn't fall. For hours you can have the whole world to yourself, if you don't count the water.

And then, in the middle of that emptiness, you find something made, and you realize the emptiness was designed.

The ground here is not ground. The lowest elevation, the floor that everything else stands on, was cut, leveled, and poured. It is a made plain, extending in flat-terraced planes and sharp, geometric embankments. Where a natural coast would have a slope of sand or rock softened by time, here there are retaining walls, graded spoil terraces, ballast fields of recycled ceramic and concrete chunks, all of it recently placed and still visibly new. The edges have not been worn. Corners are crisp. Paint is still bright. Welds are unscarred by barnacles except for a thin, first crust at the waterline. Silt has not yet had time to settle into the corners. This is a young place in the way a building on the day it opens is young — structurally finished, but not yet lived into by erosion. Nothing has crumbled into pleasing shapes yet.

Everything is built. There is no natural substrate to turn to. If you scoop up what looks like sediment, it is manufactured sediment, dumped and compacted by machines. If you look for a rock, it is a cast block. The hills are slag heaps. The valleys are excavations. The reef, such as it is, is a structure. Even the islands — and there are islands in the sense of dry volumes, pressurized air pockets inside steel hulls and concrete shells — are built. Air itself feels like an imported material here, kept in rooms and tubes and transparent transfer locks, smelling faintly of oil, ozone, and rubber seals.

Superimposed on this made floor is a second world of levels, because the plan was under the surface from the start. You live below the water, obviously, but also below the original surface of the earth. The main inhabited deck is in a great cut, a trench-city sunk into the drowned land, its streets running straight for kilometers in a grid that the current has learned to follow. Pipelines run like roads between districts. Power cables arc from mast to mast in catenaries that sway in the surge. Transit tubes pulse with small manned submersibles, tugs hauling containers, maintenance crews in bright suits crawling along handrails. It is industrial in the old, physical sense: cranes that swing, elevators that clank, pumps that thump, diesel engines turning generators, foundries making heat that blooms through the water, conveyor belts feeding hoppers, men and women welding at midnight under work-lights because the shift doesn't stop. Nothing here is fully automatic and nothing is primitive. It is scaled production, electrified, mechanized, human-scaled and planetary-scaled at once.

The light is normal, which is perhaps the strangest normal thing about it. It is not the black of the deep sea, and it is not the blue of a shallow lagoon. Up above, where the water is thinner, daylight still comes through as a wavering, green-blue noon, enough to read by, enough to make shadows fall upward in the thermocline. Down here, on the main deck, most light is made. Long rows of lamps on masts and on the undersides of platforms create pools that look like streets at night on a surface world, but they never turn off. They are white and steady, in the low twenties of degrees cool, practical light. It falls on new concrete, on steel, on the backs of fish. In the planted arrays — because there are planters, concrete troughs bolted to the sides of structures, holding imported soil — that light is enough for a thin photosynthesis, a sparse film of algae and seagrass and experimental kelp that grows in neat, husbanded rows and is cropped before it can thicken. Vegetation is present, but it is sparse, auxiliary, tended. It does not run wild. It cannot root in the sterile made ground, and what does try to grow is immediately grazed.

And yet, for all that sparseness, life is abundant. Not dense life — you have already learned how empty the volume is — but abundant in aggregate, over the whole vastness. The structures are reefs by accident and by design. Eels coil in the warm outflow pipes. Crabs walk the sharp edges of the terraces in armies, overturning new growth. Jellyfish gather in enormous, slow bells around the discharge plumes where the water is a few degrees warmer and nutrient-rich, pulsing in unison like traffic lights. Schools of mackerel and herring turn inside the grid streets, flashing silver between lamp masts, hunted by larger fish that use the avenues as hunting lanes the way a wolf uses a forest track. Filter feeders hang from the undersides of platforms, opening and closing. Once in a while a whale or a great shark passes at a distance, too large for the grid, a silhouette that makes the masts look small, and the whole neighborhood of small life goes quiet for a minute and then resumes.

The sound is constant, and it is loud. The deep sea on a natural world is often imagined as silent. This is not that. There is a low hum that you feel in your molars, the collective drone of generators and pumps and air recyclers. Water moving through gratings makes a rushing, tearing sound. Distant metal groans as the current loads the structures; somewhere, always, a sonar pings, a hull rings, a valve shuts with a clang that travels for kilometers in the cool water. Fish click, shrimp snap, submersibles whine as they pass overhead, their propellers stirring the light into shivers. At night — and night here is only a dimming of the lamps to a lower setting — the sounds layer differently but do not stop. It is a working soundscape, a place that is always in motion. The dynamics never settle: tides still pull, even under water, though they pull against walls; currents reverse around the pillars; traffic flows; shifts change; plankton blooms are grazed and regrow downstream; a school arrives, a school leaves; a factory ramps up and the plume thickens and the jellyfish follow it. It is active in the way a surface city at rush hour is active, and in the way an ocean is active, both at once.

The rules are ordinary, which is why the locals find it ordinary. Pressure is a fact, not a mystery. Currents stronger than you are will carry you away if you are careless. Do not cross a thermal plume without permission — it is hot, it is full of effluent, and it is full of animals that will sting you. Structures hum when the loading changes. Biofouling is beginning but has not yet choked the works; in a few years, perhaps a decade, the sharpness will soften, the paint will go, the algae will thicken in the lamps, and the place will begin to look older than it is. For now it retains that disconcerting newness: a planetary-scale construction, still fresh, still echoing with the tools that made it, not yet forgiven by time.

That is what it is like to live in a built ocean. Not a weird place. The weirdness, to the people who work here, is in the idea that there was ever a natural seabed at all. Children grow up thinking concrete is a kind of rock. They learn to swim the way surface children learn to ride a bicycle, not for sport but for commuting between buildings. They measure distance in current-hours, they know which lamp-avenues the predators use in winter, they can hear a pump fail by the change in the hum.

You arrive expecting wilderness and find industry. You arrive expecting crowding and find emptiness. You arrive expecting darkness and find a well-lit, cool, loud, moving world under water, made entirely by hand, stretching far enough in every direction to make its own weather, sharp-edged and young, and populated, sparsely and abundantly at once, by things that have decided, quite naturally, to live in a city that happens to be at the bottom of the sea.
