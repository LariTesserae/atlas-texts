You come up to it from below the weather.

There is a gap in the rim — not a pass worn sharp by ice, but a broad, lumpy saddle where the mountain has simply given up on being a mountain. Everything up here is old in a way that is no longer dramatic. The ridge around you is not craggy; it slumps, humps, and smooths itself off like clay under a thumb. Stones have no edges to cut you. They are grey and bone-pale, pitted and softened, as if they have been in a river for a thousand years and then lifted out and left to dry. This is the oldest surface a place can have: it is not collapsing, it is finished.

Inside the rim is a dish. You can walk its whole length in twenty minutes, perhaps less if the fog is not in, and hold it all in your head at once — that is part of its strangeness. It is small, bounded, complete. And yet because it is almost empty, you can lose yourself in it anyway.

The light here is ordinary daylight. High, clear, thin, blue when the clouds let it. Not a perpetual twilight, not a blind glare. It falls flat and honest on damp stone, and the cold comes with it. Cold that is in your lungs and in the soles of your feet. Cold that keeps frost in the shaded hollows at noon, that makes your breath a brief white thing, that makes the stones feel colder than the air.

And then, at your feet, it is not empty at all.

A lush, low world covers the dish. Not trees — there are only a few, twisted and far apart, clinging in the lee of a wall — but a dense, living carpet knee-high and lower: moss in thick velvet lawns, bright green where it is wet and almost black where it is in shadow, fern fronds uncurling from cracks, cushion plants like tight green pillows, alpine herbs with waxy leaves and small stubborn flowers. It has grown straight over the street lines. The farther you look across the empty air, the closer the ground is to packed and humming with life. Density here is vertical. At ground level, crowded. At head level, nothing.

Because it *is* a town, after a fashion. Once you learn to see it, you cannot unsee it, and the size of the emptiness becomes a kind of architecture.

There is a central plaza, a wide oval of moss and worn flat stone, empty except for a shallow basin in the middle that holds dew. From it, broad swales radiate outward — streets that have subsided into soft troughs, lined now with the low, slumped shells of houses. They are not far apart, but they are far. Measure twenty, thirty paces between one foundation and the next, and nothing in between but lawn and fog. Rings of terraces step up toward the rim, each terrace a broken retaining wall of uncut stone, no mortar, no tool marks left — erosion took those first. The walls lean. The houses lean, a degree every century, mid-fall and held up by moss growing in their joints. Their roofs are gone to turf and moss, indistinguishable from the ground except that they are hollow underneath.

The doorways are still there. Stone lintels, rounded on every corner, opening into dark interiors that are damp long after any rain. There is no wood left, no metal, no glass, no nail, no wire, no hinge. No hum, no equipment, no light of its own. Whoever laid this out built only with stone, and stone is all that has stayed. Technology, if there ever was any, has been returned to soil. What is left is organized space.

The water does not run. That is the first rule you learn, and the second is that it is everywhere.

There is no stream. There are no falls, no lake. Rain comes, when it comes, in brief, hammering squalls that drum on the stone slabs and vanish — the moss drinks it, the stone drinks it, the thin soil drinks it. What stays is damp. Condensation beads on the cold undersides of walls in the morning and grows, impossibly, until the whole face weeps and the water runs not down but *in*, into the porous stone, so the interiors are wettest when the outside is only misty. Fog pools in the plaza and in the street-swales like water finding its level, hangs there, lifts, and then the moss lawns begin to breathe it out again in slow white exhales at midday.

Puddles appear in hollows and crack with a skin of ice overnight. They do not spread. They are pockets, intermittent, brief, smelling of wet rock and peat. You step, and your boots come away dark.

The cold drives the motion. This place is not still, despite its age. It is active at the surface the way a sleeper breathes. Frost heaves small pebbles up in the night and lets them drop with a clatter at thaw. Moss swells and contracts. Trees creak. The late sun warms the stone just enough — a fraction of a degree — and the whole dish tick-tock-tinks as it expands, as if it were cooling off after a fire. Clouds climb out of the bowl below the rim and drown the rim itself, then tear. A weather cycle can pass in an hour: frost, mist, sun, squall, mist again.

And it is loud. That is the surprise. You expect altitude and emptiness to be silent. Instead the empty stone bowl is an instrument, and the damp cold keeps it playing.

Wind spills over the rounded rim and funnels down the streets. Each hollow house is a resonator, each doorway tuned to a different note, so a steady wind becomes a low moaning chord that shifts as you walk, one wall humming bass, the next sighing higher. Drips fall from lintels into stone basins and echo — late, three times, because the bowl throws the sound back. One drip, then a pause, then two more, as if something answered from under the moss. At noon the ice in the puddles pops. In a squall, rain on the flat plaza stones is sudden percussion, hundreds of drops striking in unison, loud enough to make you flinch and then stop as abruptly as it began. Sometimes a pebble, loosened for a hundred years, finally rolls, then rattles, then cascades in a small avalanche of clatter down a terrace wall. Far below the rim, a cliff you cannot see sheds rock with a sound like a distant door slamming.

Life, other than the green carpet, is rare to the point of rumor.

You may go a whole day and see no animal at all. No birdsong — there are too few birds to make a chorus. No flies rise when you step in the moss. Once, perhaps, a solitary raven crosses high and does not land. Once, a small, blunt, pika-like thing may sit up on a warm stone, watch you with complete indifference, and vanish into a crack. Under the moss there is movement — you hear faint scrabbling, feel the ground give — a blind, subterranean fauna that lives in the damp cold and almost never surfaces. When a call does come, from the lone wren in one of the far trees, it is startlingly loud and harsh in the empty air, and then the bowl gives it back to you.

That is the strangeness that settles over time: nothing here is sharp, nothing here flows, nothing here is crowded in the air, and yet nothing is quiet or dead. The lushness is not tropical. It is cold-lush, saturated, patient. It grows in straight lines along the old walls because the walls hold damp. It paints the houses in green. It has learned to build, in the absence of builders, a town of its own — terraces of turf, avenues of fern — over the town of stone.

You could live here, if you did not mind the cold, and the loneliness between houses, and hearing the place talk to itself all day. You would learn where the fog pools first, which doorway moans when the wind is west, where the dew-basin fills enough to drink from if you are careful. You would learn that this is an end-state. Erosion has done its work. Growth has done its work. They now balance, moss for stone, drip for freeze, and the whole small dish sits high above the clouds, damp, ancient, and awake — a city flattened to its final form, and replanted.
