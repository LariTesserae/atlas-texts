**Istrenkor**

It is high before you know it is high. The air is thin and it hurts in the back of the throat, and it is cold in a way that does not come from wind — a cold that lives in the stone and comes up through the soles of your feet. Above you there is no weather, only a low, close ceiling of condensing mist that has been there longer than any season you can measure. The sun has not risen. It will not for a long, patient stretch of the cycle, and the light that makes this place possible does not come from the sky at all.

At first it seems empty.

The ground is new. Not old in the way mountains are old. The plateau is still coming up, ridge by ridge, in a slow tectonic swell that takes centuries to lift a hand's breadth. Nothing has had time to be worn smooth. There are no rounded pebbles, no soil, no soft humus underfoot. The rock is black basalt and glassy obsidian, fractured in clean, recent planes that can cut you, and it is wet all the time.

Wet is the wrong word for a puddle. The rock weeps. In fresh cracks, water beads and gathers and runs in a thin, black sheet, even where there is no rain to feed it. Moist air is lifted from the warmer lowlands below and shredded against this cold height, and it lets go of itself here, constantly, in a drizzle too fine to call rain. It condenses on every surface and stays. Your boots leave a dark print that fills again in seconds. Water does not freeze on the surface, though the air is well below freezing, because the plateau itself is warm underneath. Geothermal heat bleeds up through the new stone, and the water that percolates through it is mineral-laden and faintly acrid, salted enough to stay liquid a few degrees past sense. It pools in basins that are still deepening. It glints, black and undisturbed for days at a time, reflecting nothing but the faint glows below it.

You search, without deciding to, for anything green. There is none. No grass in the microscopic fissures, no moss on the north sides, no shrub clinging to a ledge. The planet of this place has skipped plants entirely, or has outlived them, and the ground is mineral from horizon to horizon — stone, glass, and the pale crusts where water has evaporated and left its burden behind. On the wettest stones there is a slick, almost imperceptible film, not a plant, not quite a fungus either, but something simpler and older: microbial felt, grey and translucent, that grows on inorganic wetness the way damp grows on a wall. It is the only ground cover, and you will learn to read it, because everything that lives here is eating it, or eating something that ate it.

Then the city begins, and it does not begin so much as continue.

Istrenkor is urban at a regional scale. From any high point you can see it going on — districts, terraces, bridges, towers — until the mist and the dark take them away, and then, far off on another ridge, more glows. It is dense in layers. There are streets at foot level, paved in slabs of matte ceramic that never seem to wet, no matter how much water beads on the stone beside them. Above those streets, a second and third storey of platforms and filaments are strung between the towers, and below them, in cuttings and vaulted tunnels, another city lit from underneath. The structures are not built in the human sense. They are precipitated, lattice by lattice, out of the same mineral-rich water that weeps from the rocks. The water carries its building matter in solution and leaves it as it evaporates, cools, or loses pressure. A tower is a slow crystallization. A bridge is a tension of filaments that thickened themselves over a decade in response to a load it anticipated before the load arrived.

This is technology that is not so much advanced as post. It does not run on combustion or current that you could meter. It works the temperature difference between the warm interior of the earth and the cold air above it, piezoelectric stone that flexes with uplift, phase-change membranes that inhale mist at night and exhale it where it is needed. Materials decide their own density. A wall that was soft yesterday is firm today. A doorway warms a fraction before you reach it, not because it sees you, but because it reads the heat gradient you bring with you and makes room. Nothing here decays quickly, because erosion has barely begun. Edges are absurdly sharp, even on things that look ancient. A broken span does not crumble; it re-grows, inch by imperceptible inch, the way the plateau itself grows. The whole built environment is in that same embryonic state as the rock: emerging, not finished, and perhaps never to be finished on any timescale you could use.

You understand, slowly, why it is so loud.

There is the drone first, before you can place it — a low, resonant hum that comes up through the stone into your teeth, from the deep lattice under the city where heat engines turn over in aeons. Then water: not a river's rush, but a thousand drips, amplified by hollow towers, ringing like bells struck and left to sound inside stone. The new fractures in the plateau open with a groan you feel in your chest, once an hour, once a day, and water immediately begins to thread them. And over all of it, the fauna.

They are overwhelming, and that is the real surprise of the place. In the dark, in the cold, with no plants, you expect scarcity. Instead you are never alone on a surface for more than a few seconds.

They are blind, almost all of them. Eyes would be a waste in permanent night and a liability in mist. They navigate by heat, by vibration, by the electrical fields the city makes, by smell — wet stone, ozone, ammonia, faint sulfur. The ground grazers come first to your attention because you must step around them: low, slate-grey, amphibian-smooth animals the length of a forearm, moving in a slow, deliberate shuffle, hundreds of them, shoulder to shoulder, endlessly traversing a plaza in a tide that takes an hour to pass. Their skin is slick and beaded; they leave a faint mineral sheen where they go, and the biofilm on the stone grows thicker in their wake. They eat that film, and the dead of their own kind, and the mist itself, which they filter with feathery gills on their necks.

Above them, and so dense that at times the air itself seems to move, are the drifters. Large midges in a cold climate, wings beating slow enough that you can watch them, bodies translucent and faintly lit from within. They hang in thermal columns over the city's heat vents, by the thousands, and you breathe them in by accident, and they settle on your face in a fine, cold snow and take off again. On the walls and in the lattice, hairy clingers work their way upward in long processions, filtering condensing water and harvesting microbes from the ceramic. In the black pools and slow channels, something paler and longer swims just under the surface, stirring the reflection so that the lights below seem to move when nothing above them does.

Every one of them glows, a little. Not bright — there is no bright here — but in a dim, blue-white bioluminescence that is not decoration. It is language, and warning, and simply excess energy made visible. When a ground tide crosses a street, the street itself lights up in a moving pattern like a tide of stars. When the drifters rise over a vent, the whole column shimmers. You learn to see by these moving constellations, by the amber warmth that leaks from tower joints, by the faint sparkle in obsidian where the stone is still stressed and new.

The ecology, if you can call it that, is closed and slow and runs not on sunlight but on chemistry and heat. The microbial film feeds the grazers; the grazers feed the drifters and the clingers; the dead fall into the water; the water carries their minerals to the built lattice, where they are built again into tower and bridge; the tower and bridge exude heat and moisture, growing more film. It is a loop with no outside. The city is not separate from the fauna. The fauna live in its pores, clean its membranes, carry its spores in their gut, and their bodies are, in part, made of its offcuts. The technology is not separate either: it was grown to work with this loop, or it has learned, over the slow centuries, to be indistinguishable from it.

Everything moves at the pace of geology. A pool fills by a centimeter a year. A ridge rises imperceptibly while you stand on it. A tower seals a wound in its own skin over the course of a winter you will never see end. The animals do not hurry because there is nowhere to hurry to; food is everywhere, and so is everything that eats them. If you try to move at your own speed, you will stumble, you will be run over by a tide of grey backs, you will get in the way of a bridge that is deciding, very slowly, to be somewhere else. You learn to wait. Waiting is how you get dry — you do not — and how you get warm — you do not — and how you learn to hear the place properly: the drone, the dripping, the chittering that can rise at times to a deafening, close chorus, thousands of small throats working at a sound you start to feel as a pressure behind your ears, and then, just as suddenly, falling back to the bells of water in the stone.

By the second day, if you can call it a day, the strangeness has settled into something almost ordinary, which is perhaps the most unsettling part. The buildings breathe condensation and do not get wet. The black pools have lights in them that are not reflections. The streets re-pave themselves under the feet of the animals. The rock under the city is still being born, and the city on top of it is still being born, and the animals that cover both are living their entire, overwhelming lives in a present tense that stretches so long it looks like stillness.

You cannot get a view of Istrenkor. The mist closes at fifty meters, the dark closes at a hundred, and the city closes everywhere else. You can only be in it: cold, wet to the bone in a way that is no longer unpleasant, ears full of sound, sight reduced to glows and silhouettes and the patient, layered presence of life on every surface you touch. That is the rule of the world here, and it holds everything together — the weeping stone, the unplanted ground, the precipitated towers, the blind and shining multitudes. Nothing is finished, nothing is dry, nothing is the only thing of its kind, and nothing, it seems, intends to hurry for you.
