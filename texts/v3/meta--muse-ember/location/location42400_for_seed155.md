The light here is completely ordinary, and that is the first wrong thing.

It comes in the morning the way it should, white-gold and flat, casting short, ordinary shadows across a place that is not ordinary at all. The sky above is a pale, high blue, thin with altitude. The air is cold — not bitter, not alpine in the dramatic sense, but persistently cool, so that your breath fogs even at midday and everything metal sweats condensation in the shade. You are very, very high, on top of a mountain that has long since given up being a mountain.

This is not a peak. Erosion has finished its work here. What was once rough and vertical has been worn down over an age so long that the word *ancient* loses its meaning; the plateau is near-flattened, a final form, settled. There are no cliffs to speak of, no sharp edges, no new stone. Boulders, where they can be found under the roots, have been rounded to almost nothing. The soil is deep to the bedrock. It has always looked like this, the people who tend the districts say. Before the city, before the factories, the plateau was already a flat green sea stretching to a horizon that is always, always visible.

Because the vegetation is total.

Not forest. Not jungle. A mat. A living substrate that covers everything, without break, from the far edge of the region to the far edge of the next valley, miles in every direction. From a tower you can see other settlements — a smudge of dark stalks, a line of chimney-plants exhaling vapor — but between them there is only mat, a cool, damp carpet of dwarf shrub, lichen, stalk-grass, and moss pressed so tight you cannot find a square of bare earth without tearing roots. It breathes. It swells a little with the morning fog and contracts in the afternoon sun. At night it ticks and sighs as it grows, a sound that carries for miles across the flat because there is nothing to stop it.

You do not walk on ground here. You walk on plant.

The city is grown, not built, and that is why it can be both urban and entirely vegetal. It is settlement-scale, organized into wards, rows, rings and districts with numbers painted in sap on the sides of stalks, but every wall is a hardened stalk-wall, every roof a leaf that regrows when cut, every road an avenue of two facing rows of living posts with a pressed-moss lane between them. Houses are hollowed stems that were grafted into shape in moulds and then left to finish growing on their own — some leaning, some still slowly arching toward the light, merging at the corners with the neighboring house until a whole terrace becomes one organism with many doors. Warehouses are vast, low, flat-topped mats of woven root. 

And the industry is grown too. This is a working place, mechanized, scaled, loud with engines. The factories are not stone and steel in the old sense; they are foundries hollowed from giant chimney-plants, smelters that make glass from silica weeds, mills that grind moss-peat into brick while steam engines huff and sigh in a rhythm you feel in your molars. Belts run between plant towers, carrying seed-pods to be planted as the next quarter. Electric cables are root-cords, humming faintly where they pass through damp stalks. Fermentation vats bubble in the cool air, warm to the touch, smelling of yeast and wet iron and crushed lichen. Nothing is sterile. Everything is tended, because a machine left untended will root, and a plant left untended will, given time, harden into a tool.

Water, in the usual sense, does not exist here. There are no rivers. There is no lake. The damp parameter of this world is condensation. Every night the cold is enough to wring moisture from the thin air, and it beads on leaf-metal panels, on the cold sides of boilers, on the undersides of broad leaves in the mat, and runs, slowly, not downhill in any useful way, but into cisterns — hollow stems, old engine reservoirs, the cupped hearts of giant leaves where a shallow lens of water sits for weeks, evaporating and reforming. Rain is a rumor, an event that perhaps happens once a year at a predictable hour, a warm condensation that falls for twenty minutes and never quite reaches the mat, soaking the upper leaves and then stopping. Harvest crews go out at dawn with hoses and buckets to collect the night's dew from the condensation fields. Clothes are always a little damp. Your skin is always a little damp. Nothing flows away.

That is one of the rules: water does not flow. It gathers, it hangs, it is drunk by the mat where it stands.

The density of presences is sparse, despite the total cover. You can walk for six hours across the mat and see no building, no person, no animal, only the endless green-grey sward and the wind. The wards are scattered, isolated nodes in a vast emptiness of plant. A factory district here, a cistern ring a mile distant, a cluster of stalk-houses folded into a hollow that is no more than a shallow depression in the plateau. The silence between them is not silent, though. The plateau is loud.

Wind roars across the flat and through the hollow plant architecture, making the whole region into a resonating instrument. Pipe-trees sing. Stalk-houses hum. A distant engine will set the mat itself to a low tremor, and that tremor will travel, unbroken, for miles. Belts slap, pistons thud in slower time, steam sighs through sap-lines, and at night the growth comes — a cracking, creaking, settling as the mat expands in the cold. Even the emptiness makes a noise. It is the sound of a large, old, living surface under a high sky.

Fauna are rare to the point of legend. There is nowhere for an animal to hide in a flat mat that is watched by everything, and almost nothing for an animal to eat that is not already being eaten by the city. Occasionally you will see the high birds — or the things taken for birds, seed-pods with membranous wings that ride the wind between wards and burst when they land. In the warm nooks of the smelters live small, cold-blooded beetles that feed on rust and yeast. That is the extent of it. The ecology is plant, machine, and the few humans who tend both. Predator and prey are replaced by feeder and fed: the mat feeds the factory, the factory feeds the mat with heat and ash and vibration.

Which brings the other wrong thing, the breakdown.

Because everything is grown, nothing stays finished. A house that has fruited will drop pods that root in the lane and become, in a season, another house, smaller, facing the wrong way. A dead factory has a habit in this place of splitting open and seeding its own replacement — rows of young chimney-plants pushing up through the old floor, already aligned in the old grid, already beginning to harden into sheds. The avenues do this too. An unused avenue grows longer, week by week, the mat stretching its formal lines out into the emptiness until it ends, abruptly, in a field of wild mat that has forgotten geometry. A used avenue shrinks. The distance between the Fourth Ward and the Cistern Ring is not fixed. It depends on how many feet and wheels have pressed it lately.

Age runs backward here, in patches. Near the oldest root-heart of the plateau, where erosion has long since made soil of everything, plants grow slow and massive and grey, as if primordial. At the edges of new districts, plants grow fast and bright and artificial, as if they were just planted — because they were. You can walk from a district that looks a thousand years old to one that looks a month old without crossing a river or a road, only a change in the color of the mat.

And sound, here, sometimes arrives before its cause. You will hear the slap of a belt on the far side of the mat a second before the engine's piston catches the light. A tower will echo a tool that has not yet been struck. The workers do not remark on it. They have learned to work by the second sound, the true one.

It is all active, in a slow, multiple way. The daily cycle is a fog cycle: clear, ordinary light until late afternoon, then condensation thick as milk rolling in from the edges of the plateau, shrinking the world to a few steps, then clearing again at night when the stars are sharp and the growth-ticking is loudest. The seasonal cycle is a leaf-turn and a spore-drift, green dust moving like slow snow across the mat, settling on roofs and re-rooting overnight. The industrial cycle is unchanged: shifts, firings, harvests of glass-leaves. All of them run at once, not in harmony, but in layer — biological pace over industrial pace over geological pace, the last of which has already ended, leaving this flat, final, thriving surface.

At dusk the light is still normal. The sun sets in the normal place. The shadows lengthen normally across the mat to the horizon, where another ward's chimney-plants are still exhaling faint vapor into the cold. Below, in the cisterns, the day's condensation hangs in beads. Somewhere a boiler sighs. Somewhere a stalk splits with a soft pop as it grows an inch thicker.

This is the oldest stable state of a place that never stopped growing. A high, cool, damp plateau, covered entirely in plant, dotted sparsely with grown towns and grown factories, loud with wind and engines, empty of animals, ordinary in light, and quietly impossible in the way it reproduces, stretches, remembers, and turns its own dead into its own future. It does not end at a wall. It fades, mat to mat, into more mat, to the edge of sight, and then continues.

It has already become what it will be. It is still becoming it.
