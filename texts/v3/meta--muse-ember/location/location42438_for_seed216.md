**The High Weave**

First there is the air, and it is wrong for the height.

It should be cold this high up, thin and bright. Instead it is warm against the skin, damp at the edges of breath, as if you have stepped into a held breath that has been held for a very long time. The light is dim and amber, the color of late afternoon, but it does not change toward evening. It does not come from a sun you can find. It comes filtered down through layers — through mist, through leaf, through the undersides of things too far above to see clearly — until it settles and stays.

And there is almost no sound. Not the wind you would expect at altitude. The weave drinks it. You hear your own steps muffled, then, much later, a drip. Another drip, far away, unhurried. The faint creak of fiber settling its weight. Once in a while, the soft chewing of something unseen inside a wall.

You are not standing on the ground.

You are standing on a floor that is alive in the way a building is alive — because it *is* a building, and it is a plant, and there is no difference. Underfoot is a mat woven of root and stem, laid in great overlapping bands like the weft of a basket the size of a continent. It flexes slightly. It is warm. It yields and then holds. Where you press, tiny leaves lift and settle. Where the weave is worn by travel, it has worn smooth into broad, polished paths that curve in long, intentional arcs.

There is no soil. There is no stone. If you crouch and try to find an edge where the made ends and the natural begins, you will not find it. The stones you might expect in a wall are knots in the stem. The cliffs at the far rim, where the plain drops away into mist, are not rock cliffs. They are root cliffs — massive, braided bluffs, their faces like the cut end of a rope, holding back nothing but themselves. Even the dust is shed leaf-moth. Even the tools left caught in the weave — flint blades, bone needles, thorn pegs, lengths of fiber cord knotted in a language of knots — are only ever less than a handspan of things that are not plant, and they look like mistakes the weave is politely growing around.

This is how up here — because it *is* up, miles above a sea of slow cloud that you see only through the gaps — everything was made. By hand, with primitive patience, over an age too long to count. No metal. No machines. A maker would come with a knife of flint and cut a root-tip to turn its growth. They would lash two young stems together and teach them to grow as one arch. They would stake a vine, burn a leaf-edge to halt it, widen a hollow by rubbing it, year after patient year, until a corridor widened. Then they would die, and the plant would keep the shape of their intention.

And so the landscape is total in both ways at once. The vegetation is not *on* the architecture. The architecture *is* vegetation, given jobs. There are gates that are two trunks grafted at the crown. There are domes — perfect, hollow, egg-shaped chambers grown one leaf-layer at a time — big enough to hold weather inside them. There are towers that are not towers but stored-up growth, trunk rising from trunk, and bridges strung between them, their decks flat and deliberately woven, their railings a living hedge. In some places the weave has flattened itself into a meadow of interlaced greensward, a lawn you can walk on for days. In others it has lifted into walls, vaults, tunnels made of a single thickened stem open at both ends.

But it is empty between these presences, and the emptiness is structural.

Scale here is not a matter of walking far. The weave extends past any horizon you could test. Walk east for a day and the light will be slightly different — a little paler, a little more morning. Walk west and you are in a later hour. The place is large enough to have its own weather, and it does: mist banks are born in the cool hollows under a dome and travel slowly across the open bands, thickening, thinning, raining a little and dying. A warm rain-cell can sit over one tower-chain while the next chain, a kilometer of empty air away, stays dry and bright with condensation.

Between the great plant-forms there is often nothing at all. A hundred meters of warm, dim air. A fall of mist. The underside of a leaf the size of a house, weeping. The hollow volume inside a dome is larger, by any measure that matters, than its outside would suggest — you step through a low doorway in the woven wall and find a chamber that keeps expanding as you enter it, its far wall not where it should be, its ceiling lost in its own damp dusk. The emptiness is what holds the place up. The weave is mostly air, laced with presence.

Water is everywhere, but it never behaves as water should.

The walls weep. In the high dim light, the bark of the domes is dark with moisture that has condensed on the inside of leaves far above and run down, not down exactly, but along the grain by slow negotiation. Streams run sideways across the floor-weave, then upward along a root-pier, climbing hand over hand in capillary threads. Pools collect not in basins but in the small hollows of the weave, in the cup of a folded leaf, in the smooth dip where generations of feet have worn a threshold concave. They are warm to the touch. Astoundingly, clearly alive: small amphibians flicker in them, translucent hunters that wait with mouths open like traps, snapping at the gliding things that cross to drink.

The wetness makes everything soft-toned and close. The smell is of wet bark and ripe fruit rotting gently inside a hollow stem, of root-cork and mildew that is not decay but a kind of lichen-plant, growing in pale films on the smooth places where the erosion has settled.

That erosion is the other great fact: everything is old, and it shows as it always shows here — not as ruin, but as settlement. Nothing is sharp. No edge you find is new. The gates have sagged into perfect catenary curves under their own weight and then grown *into* the sag, so that the sag is now the design. Handholds on the tower-walls are polished by palms into deep, glossy hollows. The bridges sway a few degrees in a wave that takes minutes to travel their length, and the plants have thickened precisely where the sway stresses them most, decade by decade, until the movement and the thickening are in exact balance. Decay and growth keep pace here. A vine rots, and another grafts itself across its place before the gap is ever truly a gap. The whole landscape is weathered not toward collapse but toward a worn-in, inhabited ease, as if a single household has lived in it for ten thousand years and arranged every chair.

Where there is presence, there is abundant life. The fauna are not visitors to the plant. They are residents of its architecture, as dense as the emptiness outside is sparse.

Inside a single hollow tower, if you listen quietly — and you must, because everything here is quiet — you can hear colonies at work: the scrape of root-grazers in the cork layer, their jaws making a sound like patient filing; the papery flutter of blind birds nesting in a vault where the light never reaches, stirring and resettling; the slow, wet crawling of wall-slugs along a vein of glowing sap, leaving a trail that catches the dim light. Gliders cross the empty intervals between towers on membranes, never in a hurry, rarely calling. Herds of small, hoofed root-eaters travel the bridges in seasonal procession, following the pulses of new growth after a wet period, cropping the young leaf-decks down to a springy turf, which then thickens again behind them.

Predator and prey move through this at a pace that feels like thought. A hunter in a pool in a gatehouse will wait a full day for a glider to cross. An insect swarm will move into a dome chamber when the condensation comes, and be eaten neatly before the chamber dries. The system is active, but its activity is muffled, internal, like a body digesting.

And then, in the quiet, the weirdness asserts itself — because a place built plant by plant, intention by intention, has learned intentions that are not yours.

Plants here do not grow toward light. They grow toward memory. A stem that was once bent into an arch will keep the arch in its descendants long after the original wood has rotted away; new shoots will bend themselves, unprompted, into the same curve, into the same doorway. A wall that was once a corridor will keep being a corridor even if the makers sealed it — the weave will grow a hollow through it, patiently, years after. You can find a stair of living root that ascends by descending, its treads deeper as they go up, and if you climb it you will be tired in the way you are tired after passing through rooms over a long day, though you rose only a few meters.

Time is local. In the thick-walled domes, where growth is dense and the air is warmest, a shoots can put on a year of growth in the span of a single visit, and you will find a leaf unfurled that was not there when you entered, and a fruit ripe on a vine you watched being planted as a cutting. Out on the open weave, where the intervals are empty, the same vine barely changes. Distances do the same. Carry something living — a seedling in a cup of root, a caged glider — and the path between towers seems to lengthen. Set it down, and it shortens again.

Shadows behave as if they are heavier than the light that makes them. They fall upward from the glowing sap-veins that run in the darkness beneath the floor-weave, faint blue-green lines that pulse slowly, like a heartbeat seen through skin. At night — though night here is only a dimming of the already dim, a pause — those veins are the main light, and the undersides of the great leaves light up from below, and for a while the whole world is inside-out, and nobody seems to think this strange.

No one is here now to think about it, except perhaps the fauna, and they are busy.

The makers are gone, or they have become part of the weave in a way that is no longer distinction. What remains is their way of working: the knots still tied in the lashings, the flint blades lodged in wood that has grown over their handles, the stakes still standing where a vine was once told to wait. The place continues to be made in their manner, slowly, by growth itself, which has taken over the job. Every spring — a season here is marked not by temperature, which stays warm, but by a great deepening of the condensation, a season of weeping — the whole weave breathes. Roots swell. New deck-leaves sigh open across a gap. A dome adds a layer. The empty spaces shift by a hand's breadth. It is in motion, always, but in motion the way a sleeping animal is in motion.

If you stay long enough, the silence stops feeling like silence and starts feeling like attention. The place is listening to itself grow, and to the small abundant lives growing in it, and to water finding its slow, uphill way.

And you, walking the polished root-paths between the hollow towers, between the vast emptinesses that hold everything up, understand at last that you are not walking *through* a landscape. You are walking *inside* one that is still being built — by hands long gone, by roots that remember their hands, by rain that climbs, by light that refuses to arrive — and that will go on being built, knot by knot and leaf by leaf, high above the world, in the warm dim, long after the last maker would have been able to imagine the size of what they started.
