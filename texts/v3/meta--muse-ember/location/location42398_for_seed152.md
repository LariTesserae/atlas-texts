It is low land that has not yet decided what it is.

The air is warm in the way earth is warm — held, not heated. Under the canopy the light arrives dim and green, filtered through layer on layer of leaf, so that even at midday the forest floor is in perpetual twilight. You see in patches. A shaft falls here and there, pale and dusty, and is swallowed again. Everything grows lush at the edges — ferns with their heads bowed, seedlings thrusting up in wet profusion, vines, mosses thick as felt on every root — but the centre of this place is bare.

That is because the ground is new.

Less than a season ago a large tree came down here, not with a clean fall but tearing upward, its root plate lifted and turned. The soil that was once below is now exposed to the air, raw and unweathered. There are no channels carved yet, no banks smoothed by water, no soil horizons, no polish of age. Stones lie angular, still sharp. The mud is unsorted silt and clay, pitted and cracked where it dries, softening again when damp returns. It has the look of something under construction, of geology still in its first draft. Rain has not yet learned how to run off it.

Water here does not arrive as a river. It arrives as dampness. Most days the mud is merely moist to the touch, and at night condensation beads thickly on the underside of the upturned roots and on the broad leaves leaning over, gathering until a single drip lets go and falls, a full minute apart, into a cupped hollow. Occasionally, in the late afternoon, a warm rain drums through the canopy for ten minutes, filling every shallow pan to a mirror, running nowhere, soaking down, and then evaporating from the surface by dark. The place lives between those two states — damp, then dry, then damp again — and everything about it is oriented to those brief pockets of moisture.

And it is in one of those pockets, tucked in the shadow of the root plate where the dark is deep enough to be amber-brown and the air smells of wet soil, mushroom, and green sap, that the building is.

From above, if you stood, you would step over it without seeing it. From a mouse's height, from a caterpillar's, it is a town. An entire town, spread across perhaps the width of a doorway, built without tools, without fire, without anything that is not a body or a mouth.

Its materials are the place itself. Chewed earth mixed with digested plant fibre, pellet by pellet, saliva and faeces as mortar, fungal threads spun through as rebar. Walls are layered like a cake — an older, grey-weathered stratum, then a fresher, redder one laid over it, then another still soft and fingerprint-pocked. It is architecture as digestion. Towers rise and slump. Ventilation shafts open like chimneys, some still breathing warm air, some blind and abandoned. Under the root plate, where the ground is always darker and more consistently damp, are the enclosed rooms: nurseries where the mud is kept moist and cool, granaries where fungal gardens grow in the dark on stored fibre, sprawling low chambers roofed with root and leaf.

The plan is sparse. This is not a dense hive. Most of the territory is empty. Long flats of bare mud separate the active clusters, marked only by a faint packed trail where feet have worn the surface smooth. There are trial columns that were never finished, hollows dug and left unclaimed, an old wall that collapsed in rain and has not been rebuilt. Places wait. Between the few luminous centres of work there is space, and emptiness, and the quiet of unbuilt ground. It gives the whole town a feeling of scattered settlement rather than a city wall to wall — outposts, suburbs, isolated stores each centred on its own intercepted drip.

Life is abundant, but it does not make much noise. That is the first surprise. For all the motion underneath, the place is held in a kind of muffled hush. Soil and mud swallow sound. You hear, if you crouch long enough, the occasional fall of water, a soft ticking when builders are working in a warm spell, a dry scratch of mandibles on earth, and otherwise almost nothing. The fauna are mostly the quiet kinds: pale, blind workers moving in pulses along the roads, carrying pellets larger than their heads; mites grazing the fungal farms like invisible livestock; springtails springing in the damp pans; slugs etching slow silver trails across the towers at night, eating the architecture they cross; small beetles patrolling the avenues, centipedes feeling their way through the shafts, nematodes in the wet film. Larger things pass at the periphery and change everything — a shrew nosing at the edge, a frog sitting unmoving in a rain-pan for an hour — but inside, predation is small and patient, a centipede waiting motionless in a doorway, a beetle larva hunting mites in a nursery.

The dynamic is constant, but it is not frantic. It is biological pace. After a rain, when the mud is workable, building accelerates: walls are raised, breaches sealed, new shafts bored. Fungal threads race outward along a line of moisture overnight, visibly stitching two chambers together by morning. In a dry spell work slows, materials harden, some outposts are evacuated and left to the seedlings. A broad leaf falls and crushes a district. A seedling pushes up through the middle of a tower, and the builders simply incorporate it, layering mud around the stem. Nothing is finished. The town and the landscape are shaping each other at the same time, and neither has reached a stable form. Erosion has not yet begun to wear it down; it is still being put up, pellet by pellet.

The rules of this world follow from its constraints. Light is not a resource here — darkness is the constant, and the town builds for it, orienting its open shafts away from sun, its nurseries into shade. Moisture is wealth; a structure is placed not for view but for a drip, and walls are cupped to catch condensation. Warmth is kept: body heat, the slow heat of decomposition under the root plate, the heat of the low ground itself, make the interior chambers a few degrees warmer and always steadier than the air above. Roads are memory — a trail remains usable only because feet keep remaking it; let a season pass and rain softens it back to raw mud. And waste is not removed; it is built with, eaten, farmed on, recycled into the next layer. There is no outside.

It would be easy to walk past and call it just a lumpy wet clod under a fallen root. That is its hiddenness, and also its protection. Up close, in the dim green twilight, warm and damp and almost silent, you understand it is a settlement in the full sense: organized, layered, inhabited, expanding, failing, rebuilding — a town of raw earth on raw earth, still in its embryonic stage, with most of its future yet to erode or to be built.
