There is no horizon here.

Vessinlow is vast in a way that does not announce itself as distance, but as continuation. You can climb — if you can find a climb that isn't occupied, and you almost always can't — to a roof, a gantry, a stacked bridge, a shoulder of ceramic a hundred storeys up, and you will look out under ordinary summer light and see only more of it, breathing. Flat, low, humid, with seams of sky between the higher decks. It is not built *on* a continent so much as it has replaced one. Going north you are still in Vessinlow after three days of travel. Going down you are still in Vessinlow after you have passed what would have been bedrock. The map is not wrong, it is simply out of date before the ink sets, and people here have stopped trusting anything that claims to be permanent.

The ground is low. Not low like a valley, low like a basin someone has tried, imperfectly, to tile. A few meters above the regional water table, which is itself always present. Put your palm on almost any outward surface and it is wet. The entire city sweats.

It is warm. Not desert-warm. A persistent, damp, 30-degree warm, the kind that makes skin stay slick long after you have stopped moving, makes shirts cling, makes metal feel close to body temperature. The air itself carries water the way a held breath carries it. At dawn the whole mineral skin of the place condenses, beads, runs in slow weeping lines down ceramic scales and along the weft of pipes. By midday some of that water has been taken up, recycled, exhaled again somewhere else as steam from a vent, and the cycle goes on. There is no river cutting through; there are a thousand shallow ones, led where they are wanted, at ankle depth in the old service canyons, at wrist depth in glass troughs running overhead, in the humid mulch of the fungal beds under the pavement. Fish swim past your boots. Fish swim over your head, looping in clear tubes built into the bridges, going somewhere in a hurry.

The land itself is not soil, and has not been for a very long time. The original marsh that filled this basin was sealed, then regraded, then sealed again, layer upon layer, until what you walk on is a warm, dark, self-healing basalt polymer, packed and polished by traffic, cracked and repaired so many times it has become like a fingerprint. It gives slightly underfoot, holds the heat of the day, and weeps in places where the ground beneath is too wet to hold it all. No grass finds purchase in it. No tree. If a seed — and seeds still arrive, in the guts of birds, in cargo, in the boots of travelers — germinates, it is noticed within hours and cleared, not out of malice but because everything in Vessinlow is either used or is using something else. An unoccupied crevice is a waste, and waste is food, and food is tempo.

So there is no green here, in the plant sense. The living green equivalent is paler, stranger, and lower. Beneath and between the paving slabs runs a continuous, shallow world of fungus: white and ochre and sulfur mats that knit the wet mineral together, break down the city's endless leavings, and from time to time, especially after rain, push up fruiting heads through cracks in the street — soft, cap-like, smelling faintly of cooked bread and wet iron. People harvest them in baskets. So do other things.

Because if there are no plants, there is life everywhere else.

Fauna are abundant to a degree that feels, at first, like an accusation. They fill every niche the city leaves, and the city leaves no niche unfilled for long. Blind wall-salamanders, no larger than your hand, graze the fungal film off vertical surfaces, tongues flicking in a rhythm you start to recognize as one of the city's background pulses. Midges in glassy clouds rise from every drain and compost vent, and are themselves hunted mid-air by stack-birds that never, so far as anyone can tell, land on the ground at all — they nest in the warm outflow of vents, ride the updrafts between towers, and snatch their food from the swarms over the markets. In the elevated canals, conduit eels flash silver as they fight the current of the tubes. On the roofs, which are streets to them, runs a lean, long-legged canid that has learned to travel the city without ever touching the wet ground, crossing from ledge to wire to laundry-line with an ease that makes human couriers jealous. And humans, of course — packed so densely that a crowd is not an event but a condition — vendors, repair-swarm tenders, water-bearers, children who navigate by touch, all of them just one more generalist species feeding on heat, moisture, runoff protein, and the edible leavings of machinery.

The vegetation died out, or was paved out, or simply found no stillness to root in. The fauna learned to live on the city itself. The whole ecology is a closed, turbulent loop: fungus breaks down ceramic dust and organic waste; midges eat fungus; birds eat midges; salamanders and canids eat birds when they can get them and fungus and each other when they cannot; humans eat fungus, birds' eggs, eel, and the opportunistic rest. Nothing is wasted. That is Rule One, though no one states it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is empty. Nothing is left alone.

Because the built world is packed to saturation. Density here is not crowded in the sense of a busy street; it is occupied in three dimensions, at every level, with no interval left between presences. Above the ground plane, which is itself barely visible beneath foot traffic, the city rises in layers so tight they interfere: housing ligaments braided around transit tubes, markets overflowing their containers into the airspace, bridges that are also shops that are also homes that are also water tanks, gardens — artificial, humming, lit by panels — suspended inside sealed atria where old-world plants are kept as a kind of living archive, tended with reverence and not allowed outside. Below the ground plane, it goes on: basements that are intentionally flooded and fished in, vaulted cisterns, deep service galleries where the air is cooler and the sound changes. Inhabitants do not think of "up" and "down" as directions so much as as shifts.

The scale forces a weather. The heat the city gives off, the water it breathes out, the sheer mass of its moving air, creates its own low pressure, its own convection cells. It can be clear and ordinarily sunny in the sliver of sky you can see — and it is ordinary sunlight, white-gold, casting sharp shadows when it manages — while somewhere three districts over it is raining warm, mineral-tasting rain out of a cloud that has never left the city limits. Smog, steam, and spray hang in the canyons between high structures like a permanent, shallow sea that never quite dries. You learn to read the weather not from the sky but from the walls: how brightly they weep, how fast the fungal beds fruit, how loud the eels are in their tubes.

The technology that holds all this together is beyond anything we would call modern, though it is not magic and it does not look like magic. It looks like wet stone and patient swarms. Buildings are not built so much as grown and re-grown, by printers that work at a scale too small to see, depositing matter taken from air, water, and dust, layer by layer, at a rate that can shift from glacial to frantic in minutes. A wall can stiffen against a storm surge, soften to let a crowd pass by a different route, heal a crack before you have noticed it. A street can close for repair and open three meters to the left by evening, because the plan beneath it has dissolved and re-printed. Power is taken from temperature differences, from vibration, from the constant traffic of bodies and water — the city powers itself, in part, off its own noise. Water is not consumed; it is borrowed. Matter is not discarded; it is lent to another form.

And that noise is the first thing you feel in your teeth. The sound of Vessinlow is not occasional. It is baseline and deafening: the roar of water moving through a million pipes, the hum of the thermal stacks, the abrasive chatter of construction swarms, the braided calls of birds and salamanders and market criers, the low thrum of the transit tubes, the bazaar-scream of a hundred overlapping negotiations. It is anthill energy made audible. Individual voices do not carry more than a few steps, so people do not rely on voice at close range; they learn to speak by touch, by tight-beam, by the vibration they can pass through a shared rail. Silence, when it comes, is not peace. Silence is a failure, a blockage, a place where something has stopped being repurposed, and people move *toward* it to see what is wrong.

This is why the place feels so turbulent and yet, paradoxically, so very old and settled. You would expect a city that rebuilds itself weekly to look raw. Vessinlow does not. Its erosion is established, in-balance. The chaos has worn itself into form. Corners that have been replaced a thousand times are now polished smooth as river stones. The deep canals where water is allowed to run free have carved themselves into the mineral ground with a kind of geological patience, despite the fact that the buildings over them are new. The central districts have a patina — darkened, slick, warm to the touch — that no new growth can imitate. It is a dynamic equilibrium: everything changes, all the time, but the pattern of change does not. New districts condense on the fringe where the ground is still being tiled; old districts subside, flood, and are reclaimed by fungus and eels; the whole mass slowly breathes outward and inward, and has been doing so for so long that the ground itself remembers the shape of it.

That is the world-rule here, if you stay long enough to learn it: nothing is stable, but everything is settled. The ground is low and wet and warm. The light is normal and you often cannot see it. The flora is fungal, subterranean, opportunistic. The fauna are many and hungry and at home. The city is packed, layered, and too vast to hold in the mind at once, and it makes its own weather and its own sound and its own sense of time.

Stand still for a moment, if you can find a stillness that lasts — on a warm ledge, with water beading and running past your feet, with eels flashing overhead and salamanders grazing the wall beside you and the whole vast, weeping, humming expanse of it continuing in every direction without a break — and you will understand that Vessinlow is not a place that contains life. It is a life, of a sort, that has learned to contain places.
