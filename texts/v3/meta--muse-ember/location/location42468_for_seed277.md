### The Thren Table

From anywhere on it, the Thren Table does not end. It rises — high, much higher than the fog-choked lowlands you can occasionally glimpse miles below the rim — and then it simply keeps going. There are no human-sized features to judge it by. A ridge that looks like a nearby hill is a day's walk away. A fog bank hanging in a shallow basin is a weather system of its own, a white sea that can sit for a week in one hollow, never spilling over the low, worn divide because there is no wind strong enough, or no slope steep enough, to push it on.

It is the night side of a locked world. The sun never comes up here. What light there is, 0.2 out of a full day at best, is a darkness with texture: a slightly paler black where the sky of perpetual cloud and aurora meets the horizon, a faint green wash high up some nights when the solar wind finds the pole, the orange pinpricks of distant geothermal vents, and — closer — the ember-tips of bioluminescent lichen and the bellies of small flying things. After an hour your eyes stop expecting detail and give you silhouettes instead. A grazer moving against fog. A heat-shimmer, even in darkness. Everything else is learned by ground, smell, and vibration through the soles.

And yet it is warm.

That is the first rule of the place. At this elevation the air should be thin and cold, and it is thin — walking uphill costs breath, sound does not carry, a shout dies in a few meters of empty air. But the stone is warm under your hands, even in sheltered places, even where no animal has rested. The Table is basalt and ironstone laid down long ago, and the planet still heats it from below. In wind-exposed places the thin air stays cool. In a depression, out of the wind, the ground radiates and the air pools warm against your skin, twenty-five, thirty degrees, smelling of sulfur and hot dust. The warmth is what makes the darkness liveable, and what drives everything else.

The land itself is old in a settled way. Nothing is sharp. Cliffs have slumped into long, scree-covered slopes. Valleys are broad and shallow, dry most of the time, their floors paved with rounded pebbles polished by wind and by feet. Erosion here is not dramatic; it is in balance. Every surface has a patina — dark red, black, ochre — a weathered skin of iron oxides built up over immense time. Even the most recent-looking terraces turn out, on approach, to be softened at the edges, cracked, recolonised by lichen, indistinguishable from geology. The plateau has had time to forgive its own history.

Water, at 0.6, is not a feature you can point to. There are no rivers. There is no lake you could cross. The Table lives on dampness instead. Fog condenses on cold stone at dawn that never arrives, on the windward faces of boulders, on the hairs of plants. It drips, it beads, it runs in threads down the sweat-blackened walls of hollows where warmer groundwater meets cooler air, forming transient rivulets that vanish into porous rock within meters. Rain does come — intermittent, slanting, sometimes violent, sometimes only a mist that soaks you without ever falling — and collects in the cracked pans of dry basins, where it will linger for days in a shallow, warm, mineral pool no deeper than your ankle, rich with opaque life, then sink away. Moisture is everywhere and nowhere: in the air, in the stone, in the mornings that are really just colder fog.

Vegetation is sparse for the same reason. There are no forests, no grasslands. What grows is low and patient and chemosynthetic, fed not by sunlight but by the chemical energy of the ground and the minerals carried in fog from the lowlands. Stonebloom — mats of fungal-lichen, fleshy and stone-colored, that spread across basalt like paint — is the foundation. In damp pockets they thicken into a greasy, luminous carpet. Between them, thread-grass and thorn-scrub root in cracks, harvesting fog with felted hairs and storing water in swollen stems. A square meter may be covered; the next hundred may be bare iron pavement. You can walk for kilometers over nothing but polished rock, dust, and the neat, widely spaced signs of animals — a dung ball left to dry, a shed exoskeleton, a trackway pressed into soft silt — and then suddenly step into a fog-garden, a green-black hollow the size of a house, alive with smell and swarming insects.

The fauna, by contrast, is abundant. That abundance is spread very thin, which is the second rule. Density is low. Between presences there is a great deal of emptiness. A herd may number in the hundreds, but it occupies a territory larger than a small country, moving in a slow, seasonless migration from one condensation basin to the next, always following dampness and stonebloom. Predators follow at the same scale. Insect swarms in fog pockets can black out the light — what little there is — for an hour, then be gone. In total, across a plateau with weather of its own, the number is staggering. In any one place, on any one day, it feels quiet and lonely.

They are, unmistakably, an industrial fauna. There is nothing built here — 0.3, effectively none. No structure, no artifact, no tool, no wall, no footprint of a maker. But the chemistry of the place is factory chemistry, scaled up over generations until it has become the soil itself.

The large grazers, called kettles for the sound they make, are the clearest example. Six-legged, barrel-bodied, blind, with paired heat-sensing pits and a mouth like a beak, a kettle carries in its abdomen a chambered stomach that is, functionally, a furnace. Symbiotic bacteria and fungi break down stonebloom and stone itself, producing heat as a byproduct and precipitating iron and other metals in concentrated nodules. The animals are warm to the touch, they exhale faintly sulfurous steam in the cold air, and where a herd has camped, the ground is noticeably warmer for days afterward — you feel it through your boots. Their droppings are not dung in the ordinary sense but slag: glassy, black-red pellets, dense with metal oxides, laced with the ammonia and nitrates they have distilled from fog and rock. Left for a thousand years, a kettle trail becomes a band of dark, fertile soil, a faint linear terrace on the plateau. Long enough, and erosion smooths it into the Table itself.

The foundry-motes do the same work at insect scale, and without staying in one place. They are swarming beetles, blind and winged, that travel in nomadic clouds through fog pockets. Each carries in its gut a strain that can dissolve basalt and rebuild it as polymer — a tough, amber plastic, and as ferrite, and as fine coppery wire. When the swarm settles on a stonebloom mat, it feeds, produces, and moves on, leaving a lacework of microscopic, man-made-looking residue. Their molts, shed in the thousands in a single hollow, look uncannily manufactured: riveted plates, articulated joints, hollow tubes. They are not artifacts. They are exoskeletons, weathered by damp until they gleam.

And the electrogenic mats — vast, flat colonies of bacteria and fungi that live in the drier interfluves — run a continent-scale bioelectric field. On a still night you can feel it as a prickling in your hair, a faint crackle when you touch a wet stone, sudden small discharges in the fog that briefly illuminate a trackway. It is how many of the smaller animals navigate, and how the stonebloom signals stress and growth. The Table hums, at a level below hearing.

That industrial metabolism is what makes the ecology dynamic despite the silence. 0.9 on sound, 1.7 on activity. Most of the time it is mostly quiet: wind moving thinly over stone, a distant kettle rumble, the soft click of a mote swarm turning in fog, water ticking down a rock face. Then a process starts. A kettle herd ignites its furnaces after feeding and moves as one, ground trembling faintly. A mote swarm erupts from a warm pool, lured by a predator's heat. A fog front advances over a basin and the whole hollow wakes with scent and vibration. Predator-prey is not chase so much as attrition: blind pack-hunters that feel a herd's heat and stomach-rumble for kilometers, cut out the weak, and leave bones that the motes polish black and scatter in a season.

Weirdness is there, but only just — 1.0. It is not a world of monsters. It is a world that has solved lightlessness and warmth and starvation by becoming a factory that eats rock. Animals that produce metal, shed what look like machine parts, glow faintly from their bellies to attract mates in total dark. Plants that do not need a sun. Pools that appear warm from below and evaporate from above. The strangeness is in the economy, not the shapes.

Towards the end of a long traverse, what stays with you is the sense of balance. There is no frontier, no fresh scar, no new growth pushing aggressively over old. The slag terraces are old soil. The bones are old stone. The fog basins are old, in that they have been basins for so long that animals now time their migrations to them as if to seasons. The Table is high and warm and dark, damp rather than wet, sparse rather than empty, mechanized rather than built, abundant rather than crowded.

It does not need anyone to work it. It works itself, slowly, in the dark, across a scale large enough to have its own weather, and it has been doing so long enough that the distinction between geology and life has worn away — which is why, when you finally step off the warm stone at the rim and look back, all you see is an immense, polished, weathered plain going to a horizon that never quite resolves, with faint lights moving in it, and a great deal of silence between them.
