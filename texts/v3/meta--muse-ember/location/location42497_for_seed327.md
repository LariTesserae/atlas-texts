**The Weeping Crown**

It is smaller than it should be for how high it is.

From below you would not see it at all. From above, in the rare clear hour, it is a single shallow bowl set on the very top of the mountain, no wider than a hamlet, ringed on all sides by a low cliff. Inside that ring the ground rises and falls gently — a rolled, weathered saddle of stone and peat, done with falling. Nothing here looks newly broken. The ribs of rock are rounded to the weight of themselves, the bog hummocks are settled, the gullies are moss-filled and at rest. This place has already become what it is and stayed that way for a very long time. Even the wind has learned not to tear at it, only to pass over.

Step inside and the first sensation is emptiness. Not barrenness — something else. A vast interval between things. Between one dark boulder and the next there may be twenty paces of open moss. Between one cushion of flowers and the next, another long sweep of sedge. The sky is most of the room. When the cloud sea below is clear, the horizon is all empty air falling away in every direction, and the bowl feels less like a summit and more like a small, hollow island that has been left up here alone.

Then the water announces itself before you see it.

It is wet in a way that is not rain. The ground is a sponge at the surface — black peat, green moss, short turf that gives under the boot and sighs it back up. Walk a dozen steps and a seep will find your footprints and fill them, bright as sky. The cliffs weep. In the shade of the leeward stones, water beads and runs in unhurried threads all day, though it has not rained and the sun is out. The air itself is condensing. Your hair dampens. Your eyelashes collect fog. The whole bowl is perpetually a little after rain: puddles that are never deep, springs that rise in the hollows in the morning and sink by evening, a tarn that migrates a few feet across the peat as the season swells it.

It should be too high, too cold, too wind-scoured for lushness. It is not.

Because the clouds come to it, it stays fed. Morning fog pours over the rim and wrings itself out on the stones; afternoon sun burns it off in rags; late air brings it back. In that wet cool — sun warm on your back, wind cutting straight through your coat, your breath visible even at noon — the vegetation has made a skin over the bowl. Short, dense, improbable. Mats of moss like worn velvet, sedges that comb sideways in the wind, cushion plants stitched tight to the ground, dwarf shrubs no higher than your knee with leaves thick as end of a thumb. Flowers do not waste height. They sit in the moss: yellow stars, purple bells, white trumpets that face down to keep their pollen dry and open fully only after the fog passes, as if they are listening for it.

The materials are mostly dark basalt, wet and patient, veined with pale quartz where the water has polished it. Lichen crusts the windward faces in rust and sage and sulphur, in perfect maps that never quite touch each other. Peat is black and springy. Where the ground is not alive, it is stone that has learned to hold water in its palms.

The sound never stops, and it is louder than emptiness ought to allow.

First the wind, funneled by the rim, roaring up the windward slope and then finding the bowl and circling inside it, rattling the sedge like dry paper. Then under it, the dripping — not one drip but a polyphony, dozens of them, from the weeping cliffs and from under stones and from the eaves of moss, each with its own pitch. Then, as the sun warms the turf, the insects.

This is the paradox of the place: it is empty to the eye and crowded to the ear. Life is abundant here, but it does not cluster. Everything keeps its distance and sings to hold it.

There are birds in flocks and flocks of one. High pipits that hang in the wind above the rim and pour out long, glassy notes, answering each other from far across the bowl so that you must turn in a circle to find them. A raptor that works the thermals off the cliff, stooping now and then at a shrew that darts, mouse-quick, between moss cushions and vanishes. Frogs — small, stone-colored, skin like wet leather — living not in ponds but in the saturated moss itself, croaking from beneath the turf in a chorus that seems to come from nowhere and keeps perfect, overlapping time. On the flowers, flies and bees in numbers that hum when the sun is right, a low, busy note that rises and falls with the clouds. And on the far hummocks, solitary, a hardy grazing animal like a small mountain camelid, alone for days, moving slowly, then suddenly sprinting at a fox that has learned the same bowl.

Predator and prey are both in motion all day, but at this scale the chase is long and visible. You can watch a hawk cross the whole sky in twenty seconds. You can watch the shrews' run become a thread across the moss and end. Nothing is dense. Everything is in process — freeze at night beading the moss with ice that melts by ten, meltwater threading downhill, cloud building and unbuilding, flowers opening and closing, birds arriving on a wind that changed an hour ago.

The light is ordinary and that makes it strange. Plain daylight, blue sky, sharp shadow. No twilight magic. When the cloud sea below is a flat white ocean, the bowl is like an island in it, sunlit and alone, and the air is thin enough that climbing the last few steps leaves you breathing hard for no obvious reason. When the cloud rises, it does not hide the bowl so much as fill it, and visibility shrinks to ten paces and then five, and the dripping gets louder as the world gets smaller.

Humans — or whatever made the nearest kin of humans — have only ever been scattered here. There is no village. There are four things, and perhaps a fifth half-buried.

A line of small cairns, ankle-high, weighting the sane path across the bog when fog makes the hummocks indistinguishable. A roofless windbreak hut on the lee side, walls of uncut stone fitted without mortar, big enough for one person to crouch against the wind. A shallow stone ring, its centre filled with moss now, for a fire that was never allowed to grow. A woven basket, half-rotted, filled with another stone. A tool: a bone handle polished by hands, a flaked stone head lashed and unlashed a hundred times. No metal. Nothing that would keep a person here through a winter. Just marks made by someone who needed to cross, or to pray, or to wait out a storm, and left the evidence to weather at the same pace as everything else. The birds now nest in the hut wall.

It has one slight unnatural habit, the only thing that makes visitors lower their voices. The whistling moss. In a band along the windward edge the moss grows in tight, erect tufts that, when the wind is right and wet, make a clear, thin note — not a rustle, a note — as if the slope were breathing through a reed. You can stand there with your mouth open and hear it change pitch as a cloud passes over and the air thickens. The stone-frogs answer it. Nobody who has heard it has been able to explain it, and nobody has needed to. It is enough that the place makes its own music and does not ask permission.

So the rules are simple, and they repeat: water comes from the air and leaves sideways through the moss; the ground stays saturated but never floods; the sun is strong and the temperature is always cool, and both can change in the space of a breath; the rim defines the world; distance is kept by song; everything weathered will stay weathered; nothing built will be finished. Walk the circumference and you have walked the whole country — an hour at a stroll, an afternoon if the fog comes in and you follow the cairns and the drips. But you will have been, in that hour, higher than the weather of every lowland, wetter than a marsh, lusher than a meadow, emptier than a desert, and louder than you expected any emptiness to be.

At evening the temperature drops fast. The sedge stiffens. The frogs start again, deeper. The pipits make their last long calls from the rim and are gone, and for a minute the bowl is only wind and water. Then the dreams of the moss, the slow upwelling of the springs, the tick of cooling stone — the place settling into its own long, established state, holding its small, wet, ringing reality together until morning fog comes to feed it again.
