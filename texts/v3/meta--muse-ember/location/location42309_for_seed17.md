It is loud before it is anything else.

Not loud like a storm passing, or an engine you can walk toward and away from. Loud like a place. A low note you feel first in your sternum and your teeth, a mid-range hiss like a wire brush drawn constantly across the inside of your skull, and over both, thin and high, the whine of things turning far away. Put your hands over your ears and it does not drop. It goes through the gloves, through the bones, through the floor. You would have to shout to be heard over it at arm's length, and then your own voice comes back to you flattened and huge, as if the medium itself had opinions about sound.

For a while you move in it without naming it correctly.

It is bright, but not sunny. A normal daylight, true-coloured, diffused and pale, the kind you can read a label by. Black is black, steel is grey, the warning lights blink amber. It is cold in a way that has nothing to do with wind. Two degrees, perhaps three, and it presses on you evenly from all sides, slows your arms when you sweep them, makes the metal of a handrail stick faintly to a bare palm even through a glove. Objects do not fall so much as sink and drift. Your breath, if you are on apparatus, does not fog and vanish; it bubbles up in a long string and is taken sideways.

At some point you look up, expecting sky, and find sky close above you, rippling and white at the edges. You are under it. Under a sea.

The sea is the atmosphere here. There is no looking *at* water. There is buoyancy, and resistance, and the sense that you are suspended in the middle of a volume that goes on a long way in every direction and yet is mostly empty.

That emptiness is the second surprise. The water is so clear it might be a vacuum that happens to be liquid. No dust. No bloom. No drifting seeds or spores to catch the light. Sunlight plunges down through it for a great distance, unweakened, because there is almost nothing in it to weaken it. Between one presence and the next there may be hundreds of metres of nothing at all — just light, cold, and the roar filling the space where things ought to be.

Then the floor rises to meet you, and you understand the height.

You are not on a seabed in the usual sense. You are on top of a table. A continent-sized table, its top lying shallow enough for daylight, its sides falling away for a very long way into darker water where the light fails and the pressure would crush the daylight out of you. Standing on the rim — and you can stand, in a manner, on a plain that never quite stops — you can look down a cliff that has no bottom you can see. Above the table is the bright sea-surface, below it is deep sea, and the edge between them is a sheer, continuous drop around the whole vast perimeter.

The rock of the table is young. That is unmistakable. It has not been softened. Basalt columns split and stand with facets still mirror-bright, black volcanic glass that reflects the surface above in broken white patches, pale granite shards broken on the diagonal like a quarry that was abandoned mid-blast. The edges are sharp enough to slice a glove if you brush them. There is no rounding, no sand blanket, no silt in the hollows. The current has swept it clean down to the mineral. What lies on the floor does so sparsely and in straight lines: a pebble here, a nodule there, a frost of white mineral like frozen foam on a machine housing, and then nothing again for a long stretch.

There is nothing green. Nothing that sways. No kelp, no eelgrass, no algae mat furred over the rocks, though the light is perfectly adequate for them. The absence is total and, once noticed, stranger than any alien growth would be. Under full daylight the floor is naked mineral — glass, basalt, granite, steel — as if the place were pre-life, or post-life, and had decided not to begin again. It is the one truly unusual thing about it, and it governs everything else.

Because there are no plants, there is nothing to fill the water. The web of life here does not start on the table. It starts elsewhere — at vents beyond the rim, in the open sea beyond the bright ceiling — and it passes over the table in transit, or gathers at its edge.

And it is abundant, in the way a desert road can be abundant if it is long enough. Along the rim, where the current pours over the drop in a permanent sheet and brings food with it, the animals are continually passing: slow convers of grooved whales, so large their movement seems like a change in the light; sleeper sharks hanging motionless in rock crevices and then, without hurry, unfolding; silver fish arching in loose, kilometres-wide shoals above the glass; octopuses, crabs, vast patient things that live in the cracks because there is nowhere else to live. Count them around the whole perimeter and there are thousands upon thousands. Encounter one, and you may then travel for an hour through a volume utterly empty of anything alive. They are neither hiding nor showing. They are simply distributed across an immense surface, each far from the next, in water that conducts their low calling for enormous distances, so that you hear their groans as part of the roar long before you ever see them.

Weather, here, is current. The table is large enough to have a weather of its own, and it does — branching streams that run like rivers across the glass, zones where they collide and churn in a standing turbulence, calm lakes of water that sit in the same place for ever and rage if you cross into them. You can map it. You can learn that *this* square of the plain is always rough, *that* long valley always carries a fast flow from the left. It will never move to meet you. You move to it.

For the same reason, the machines are scattered the way waterholes are scattered in a desert, and they are industrial in a way no city would need to be. No towns. No lights clustered. One thing at a time, far apart, and working. A fence of current turbines chained along the rim, turning at a speed that never varies, their cables vanishing over the edge and inland in parallel lines to a horizon that does not come. A single factory module flat on the rock, lit, humming, with its one lit window and its container racks. A harvester crawling in a perfectly straight line, scraping nodules, leaving a trail that never fades or fills in. A pipeline crossing the plain for days of travel, a beacon blinking amber on a half-buried housing, a power station, an old platform tipped on its side and preserved in the cold exactly as it fell. They are electrified, mechanised, scaled to the size of the place — engines as large as hills turning in the rim-current — but between them are the same empty stretches of rock and light. You can pass a whole day and see only one of them. You hear all of them, all the time, merged into the base note of the world.

That is the trick of the place, and its rule. Time does almost nothing here.

The roar does not swell at the rim and fade inland into quiet. It steadies. The turbines do not speed up or wind down. The currents do not seasonally shift. The sharp rock does not dull, because at this cold, with this clean water, chemical weathering has been negotiated down to almost nothing. The fresh break stays fresh. The machine that fell a hundred years ago looks, in this light, as newly wrecked as the rock looks newly broken. Nothing grows, so nothing crowds the machines or paints them green. Nothing rots in any hurry, so a carcass, a glove, a hull, lies where it stopped and keeps its shape. It is not frozen in the sense of ice — everything is moving, just in fixed patterns — it is frozen in the sense of equilibrium. Inflow equals outflow. Light in equals light out. Noise in equals noise out. It is a place that has arrived at a balance and then, politely, stopped.

You could leave for a year. The current-systems would be in their same squares. The whales would be on the same routes along the rim. The harvester would be a little further along its line, at the same speed, making the same sound. The edge of the glass would still be sharp enough to cut you. And the sound would still be there before you saw anything, filling the empty volume of the water to the point of pain, unchanging because there is nowhere in this world for it to go.

You are on a high, cold, sunlit table under a sea, in a roar that is the sea itself, with no plants under a good light, with animals and engines passing far apart across a mineral floor that cannot age. That is not a metaphor for the place. That is the place.
