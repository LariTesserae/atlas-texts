You come down into it sideways, under a lip of fresh earth, and then you are inside a room with no roof.

That is the first true thing about it. It is not a landscape in the way a marsh or a plain is a landscape. It is contained. About thirty-five paces long and twenty wide, a low tray scooped out of a low hot flat, dropped below the surrounding ground by a couple of meters, so that from the outside you would see only a damp rise and a line of reeds. From the inside, you cannot see out at all. The sky is a rectangle of perfectly normal light above you. Everything else is floor, wall, water, steam, and noise.

The walls tell you how young it is. They have not had time to soften. One side is a near-vertical cut, banded black clay and pale mineral earth, still sharp at the corners, still shedding shards that lie on the floor like broken crockery. Underfoot the ground is not worn smooth by water, as old places are. It is a mosaic of just-broken plates: cracked mud that has curled up hard, black silt that sucks at your boots, drifts of white silica crust that looks like frost and crunches like thin ice, and ochre stains where hot water has run and left its rust. The edges of the pool channels are knife-edged. The concrete pad in the middle is cleanly split, its aggregate still bright. Nothing here has been rounded yet. Erosion has started, but it has only begun to bite.

Water is everywhere, but it is not deep. The water table is just under the skin of the place. Anywhere you dig, it wells. Thin sheets cover the low floor, two or three centimeters deep, following the tilt of the tray to a central pan where it is ankle-deep. Channels a knee wide cut between them, clear and still. The water is hot — too hot, in the center, to stand barefoot, and in the steaming cracks at the wall it must be near boiling. The air above it is hotter still, or at least saturated, so the heat does not sink and leave. It hangs in the pocket. Sweat pours and does not evaporate for a long moment, then beads and runs again. You are wet before you have walked ten steps, not from rain, but from the place itself breathing out.

On a normal day the light here is normal — late morning sun, ordinary brightness, ordinary color. But the steam lifts in slow, vertical columns from the cracks, unbent by any wind because there is no wind inside the walls, and it diffuses the light into a warm white haze near the ground. Droplets condense on the underside of the pipe, on the cool north face of the rock, on your forearms and your eyelashes, even while the sun is overhead. In one corner, where the steam plume is thickest, it turns to a fine, warm drizzle that falls straight down and never quite reaches the ground. It is rain that belongs to the room.

The sound is the opposite of the stillness. It is loud, and it is constant, and for that reason you stop noticing it as events and start feeling it as a condition. There is a low diesel throb from the pump skid that you feel in the soles of your feet as much as hear, a patient, laboring rotation, perhaps sixty beats to the minute, shaking the shallow water into a faint trembling. Over it, a high-pressure hiss from a fracture in the far wall, like a kettle that will never be taken off the heat. Under both, a subterranean gurgle, as if the earth itself is swallowing and letting go, a slow bubble and release every twenty seconds or so, traveling up through the floor. And everywhere, drip. Water falls from the pipe onto a steel tray and makes the same clear ping, over and over, precise as a metronome, echoing faintly because the tray of the basin acts like a bowl. You would have to shout to be heard across it, and even then the pocket would answer you.

Between the sounds there is a great deal of emptiness. Density here is sparse. The floor is mostly bare mineral — wet clay, white crust, black mud — with long spaces between presences. That emptiness is part of the temperature and the wet. Nothing crowds in. The objects that are here are scattered far apart, as if dropped.

At the northwest end, on its cracked concrete pad, is the pump: a rust-streaked industrial skid, diesel tank crusted at the cap, a belt drive turning slowly, a lever wired in the on position. A thick galvanized pipe arcs from it across the whole pocket to the far wall, sagging slightly in the middle, jointed, still warm to the touch. It drips at two places. At the southeast end, a single power pole leans at an angle, its transformer humming with a thin electrical buzz, a cable half-buried in the mud trailing away under the spoil heap and disappearing. A toolbox lies open and empty beside the pad. A length of drill pipe sticks upright out of the pool, capped, weeping steam around its base. These are not a settlement. They are occasional artifacts, a single small attempt at industry, left to run or left to rust where it stands. The machinery is not scaled to the place. It is too big, too loud, too purposeful, and the place has simply continued around it.

Vegetation is present only at the margins, where the ground cools by a few degrees. Seven or eight clumps in all, spaced far apart: stunted saltgrass at the edge of the spoil heap, a low samphire rooting in the black mud, sedges where the inflow pipe leaks and makes a cooler trickle. In the shallow water at the very edge, where the temperature falls below forty, there is a thin green-black film, algae or slime, in patches no larger than your hand. The rest of the floor is bare. The plants do not thrive; they creep. A few centimeters a year is fast for them.

There are no animals. Not one. In a wet low place like this, on a normal day, you would expect birds over the water, insects in the steam, frogs in the shallows, tracks in the mud. Here there is nothing. No birdsong — the machinery and the hiss have taken that register — and no birds circling the plume. No mosquitoes, despite the water and the heat. No tracks, no droppings, no disturbance in the dust except your own. The center of the pan is too hot and too mineral, and the vibration in the ground never stops, and the air is too thick with sulfur and diesel to breathe easily. Life has kept to the cool edge and even there it is only plants, and only sparsely.

The weirdness of the place is not dramatic. It is the kind you notice after you have stood still for a while. The surface of the central pool does not ripple. Where the pipe pours in, the water should churn; instead a translucent mineral skin forms instantly and heals, so the pool reflects the sky like sheet glass even as you hear the rushing below it. The pool level does not fall. Evaporation is constant, steam is constant, yet the water stays at exactly the same mark against the white crust, because the ground feeds it from underneath at exactly the rate it is lost. The drips fall at exactly the same interval, the gurgle comes at exactly the same interval, the diesel turns at exactly the same slow rate. It is a loop closed on itself.

And that is its rule, and its slowness. Nothing here hurries. The water cycles: up as steam, down as warm drizzle, into the ground, back up through the cracks. The mineral crust thickens by a fraction of a millimeter in a season. The pipe sinks, very slowly, into the soft mud. The plant clumps expand at the periphery and die back at the center. The pump will not outlast the place; the place will outlast the pump by a very long margin. Over a day, if you stayed, you would see only the light move across the normal sky and the steam keep rising. Over a year, you would see the crust creep a little further over the black mud, the shards at the wall shed a little more, the concrete heal its crack with white salt.

You leave the way you came, ducking under the lip of fresh earth, back into the ordinary low marsh air, which feels strangely cool and dry by comparison. Behind you the throb and the hiss do not fade gradually. They are cut off by the wall. That is the pocket's last trick: it is loud inside and silent outside, hot inside and merely warm outside, wet inside and water near the surface outside — the same world, but held, for a small contained space, in a different state.
