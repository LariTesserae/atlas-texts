You come down into it more than you climb. There is no mountain, no gorge, no long approach. The surrounding land is low and flat, a plain of pale, dry ground, and this is simply a place where the plain dips — perhaps five or six metres below the level outside, no more than that. You step over a rounded lip of stone and the world narrows at once.

From the middle you can see all of it. The hollow is small, twenty or thirty paces across, and completely bounded. Limestone walls rise on every side, not steeply, not freshly broken — settled, scalloped, and softened by a very long time of doing nothing in particular. Outside the rim there is plain; inside there is this one room of air and stone, open only to a small, dim disc of sky far above. That is the whole place, and every boundary of it is visible from anywhere inside.

It is dark down here, but not black. The walls lean far enough inward that direct sun never reaches the floor. What light there is comes down diffused and grey-green from that small opening, thinned and reflected off stone. Colours go muted quickly. The pale rock becomes a uniform grey-tan, the dust a matte grey, the few green things a dull, dusty green. There are no hard shadows because there is no hard light to make them. Shapes separate slowly as your eyes adjust: wall, floor, stone, plant, empty space between them.

And it is warm. Not hot, not the white heat of open desert sun. A steady, indoor warmth, like the air in a closed room in summer, or the warmth that stays in stone after the day has gone. The warmth does not change as you move. It is the same at your ankles as at the top of your head, the same against the wall as in the middle of the floor. The air is still and thick with it. It does not circulate, it does not rise in a current, it does not cool at any point. It has simply pooled here, below the rim, and stayed.

The floor is flat and fine. Not sand, exactly — a dry mineral silt, calcareous dust, the product of the walls wearing down grain by grain and settling. It is packed in places, loose in others. It holds no damp clumps. Walk across it and it gives a soft, almost soundless crunch, then settles again behind you. Scattered through it, far apart, are stones: flint nodules and broken limestone, half-sunk, their upper surfaces rounded and weathered. There are no fresh edges anywhere. No slab has fallen recently. No crack is newly opened. The dry runnels in the walls — old water courses, though no water has run in them for longer than anything here can remember — are smoothed and shallow. Everything has reached its own state and is in balance with it. The erosion is not active. It is finished, established, and has been so for a very long time.

There is no water. No pool, no damp, no drip, no seep, no wet stone, no mud. The dust stays dust. The cracks stay dry. Nothing here smells of water or rot, because nothing here can rot in the ordinary wet way. The smell is of warm stone and dry earth, faintly green where a plant is, and mostly of nothing at all. The plants themselves live on vapour and whatever moisture the rock holds deep in its fissures, far below the still air. They do not drink in any visible way.

They are sparse, and they are few. That is the first thing you notice once your eyes adjust — how much of the place is simply bare. In the whole hollow there might be half a dozen things that could be called vegetation, each separated from the next by metres of empty dust.

Against the base of one wall, where a little soil has collected in a crack, is a small shrub with thick, leathery leaves, grey on the underside. It has not flowered. It has probably not flowered for a long time, if ever, here. Near the opposite wall, in another fissure, a cushion of moss lies grey and crisp, and another, smaller. A tuft of dry grass, straw-coloured, has rooted in a pocket of silt and stands still. A low, woody plant with small waxy leaves spreads its roots wide in the dust, as if searching. On the shaded faces of the stones, lichen makes pale crusts — green-grey, ochre, almost white — ordinary rock lichens, growing at a pace too slow to notice.

None of it is spreading. None of it is growing in any way you could measure. The shrubs are not lush, not dying either. They are dry, dormant-seeming, persistent. They have the look of things that found a marginal equilibrium: enough dim light, enough warmth, enough mineral moisture in the stone to keep their tissues from quite giving up, and not enough of anything to allow more. There is no leaf-fall, because no leaf is green and growing to fall. There is a little dead material on the floor — a dropped leaf, a dry twig — but it lies where it fell, long since, and has not been turned over or rotted into the dust. There are no flowers, no berries, no fallen fruit, because there is nothing here to pollinate them and nothing here to eat them.

That is the other absence, and it is complete. There is no animal life. No insects in the air — the air is empty of them, uncrossed by a fly or a moth. No lizards on the warm stones, though the stones are warm. No birds ever come down into the shadow, and no bird calls from the rim. No tracks in the dust, no webs in the cracks, no bones, no droppings, no burrow holes, no disturbed earth. The plants have the place entirely to themselves, and even they are barely using it.

So there is silence, and it is not the kind of silence that is broken by wind. There is no wind down here. The rim turns it aside, and what little air there is does not move. The shrubs do not rustle. The dust does not shift. Nothing drips, nothing scurries, nothing calls. Your own breathing seems loud in it, and then even that feels absorbed by the soft walls and the fine floor. It is a silence with a texture, made of thick warm air and distance between things. When a small stone settles somewhere on the wall — a rare, quiet tick of cooling mineral — it is noticeable precisely because nothing else happens.

Nothing has been built here. There is no wall, no shelter, no cairn, no path worn into the dust, no cleared place, no hearth-ring, no cut mark, no piece of wood carried in and left, no metal, no cloth, no made artifact of any kind. The place is untouched in the sense that no hand has arranged anything in it. The softest stone here would flake at a blow and give an edge, of the sort an early hand might have struck for a tool, pre-metal and simple, and the dust is full of stones the right size to lift. But no one has lifted them into a shape. No one has stacked them, no one has struck them. The most primitive possibility of making is present in the material and has never been taken up.

That, and everything else, is what makes the place feel unchanging rather than merely quiet. This is not a moment between changes. The light does not alter with an hour, because the sun never falls directly here. The warmth does not rise and fall, because the air does not circulate and the stone does not cool. The plants do not grow through a season, because there is no season reaching down into this shadow. The dust does not wash away, because there is no water to wash it. The walls do not crumble, because there is no frost and no fresh fracture to propagate. It is a small, low, warm, dry hollow that has settled into complete equilibrium — ordinary limestone, ordinary dry-country plants, ordinary physics, nothing strange or glowing or floating, nothing anomalous at all except how thoroughly it has stopped.

You could stay an hour here, or a day, and be unable to say what time it was by anything in the place. The dim light would be the same. The warmth on your skin would be the same. The shrubs would not have moved. The silence would not have thinned. The boundaries would still be exactly where they were, close around you, all of them visible, all of them old.

And then you would climb the ten steps back over the rounded lip, into the flat light of the plain outside, and the hollow would close behind you, empty, dark, warm, and unchanged — as it was, and as it will remain.
