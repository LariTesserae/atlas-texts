It is smaller than you expect. You can see across it from any point — a shallow cupped place, perhaps two hundred paces from one lip to the other — and that should make it simple. It isn't.

You cross into it without a gate. The rim is low and rounded, nothing more than a continuous swell of stone and packed earth, worn by an erosion so complete that nothing has an edge anymore. Boulders are smoothed like river stones, though they sit where they fell. The path is smoothed. The walls of the rise are smoothed. Even a broken fence-post you find later has gone soft at the break, its grain raised and grey, as if it has been held under water for a very long time. This is a place that has not been made, so much as settled. It has come to its final form and stayed there.

Inside, the land drops only a little — a man's height, perhaps less — but it drops enough. It is a sink. The air changes in that drop. It grows warmer and heavier and brighter all at once. The sun is high and white-gold, unfiltered except by a permanent humidity. Light does not so much fall as settle, pooling on wet surfaces. There are no hard shadows at noon. Everything glistens.

Your feet are damp within three steps.

There is no lake here, and yet water is everywhere. It is never deep enough to swim in and never absent long enough for the ground to dry. The soil is a black-brown sponge full to the surface; your weight makes it shine. Shallow sheets move and vanish in a slow tide of evaporation and condensation, so a pool you stepped around in the morning is gone by midday and another, tea-colored and ankle-deep, is open by evening where there was only mud. The rocks weep. In the shade of the great stones, water beads on the undersides and falls in slow, separate drops, far apart. The leaves weep too — large, glossy leaves that drip in bright weather, as if the air itself were condensing on them. The whole basin breathes water in and out.

Vegetation is lush, but it is not crowded. That is the first strangeness. Lushness here lives in patches, with a great deal of pale, bare silt between them. Between one tree and the next there may be twenty paces of open ground, like a garden arranged by someone who loved emptiness. There are eight trees, perhaps nine, spaced so widely they seem to be keeping a distance from one another. Each is old beyond reckoning, low-branched and leaning, its trunk furred with moss and film-ferns, its roots spreading wide and shallow in the sponge-soil. Under each canopy the understory is sudden and exuberant — carpets of fern, broad-leafed things that fold and unfold, hanging mosses like wet hair. Then, abruptly, it stops. A ring of plain mud. Another patch of green. The lushness is local, intense, and surrounded by quiet space.

The stillness is physical. Sound does not travel here the way it should. The wet air and the soft ground swallow it. There is no birdsong. There are no insects that whine. The only regular sounds are your own squelch, the infrequent drip from a leaf, and the faint tick of something settling in the mud far away. Even the water, when it moves, makes almost no noise, as if it is moving through felt.

At first the emptiness seems real. Then you look closer, and it isn't.

The mud between the green patches ripples. Not with wind — there is hardly any wind — but with life. When you kneel, you see that the surface is a living skin. Millions of tiny grazers, pale and translucent, no longer than your thumbnail, cover the silt in a slow, collective drift. They are blind, soft, six-legged paddlers that eat the algae film and the fallen leaf-mulch, moving in loose waves across the basin over the course of hours. A handful of mud lifted in your palm squirms as one body and then settles. The shallow pools are full of their cousins, swimming up in motes; pick up a leaf from the water and it is furred with them. In the sunbeams the air is not empty either, but full of a warm, slow snow of even smaller fliers — sun-motes, winged specks that catch the bright light and never seem to land, hanging in the still air in such numbers that the light looks solid.

That is the overwhelming thing here. The large presences are sparse — those eight trees, a few big stones, the rare long shape in the central pool — but the small presences are without count, in the air, in the water, in the soil, on every wet surface. You are walking on fauna. The ground is fauna, held together by it.

And it is active, in that slow, thorough way. In the main pool, where the water is deepest — a forearm's depth — something long and blind rises with the patience of a reed. It is not a plant. It glides, barely disturbing the surface, and the paddlers at the edge thin out where it passes and slowly close again behind it. Predator and prey here do not rush. The whole basin is in motion, but at a pace that feels like watching time-lapse in reverse: colonies migrating from one patch of algae to another, leaves falling constantly from the low trees and dissolving into black mulch within days, pools expanding and contracting, condensation forming at midday and lifting as mist in the evening. Nothing is still for long, and nothing changes its shape.

There are things built here. Very few, and very scattered, as if someone left them and the place took them.

A low ring of stones, half-sunk, in the northern silt — a hearth, a pen, a place to sit — now just a crescent of grey boulders softened at the corners. A metal frame, maybe a swing, maybe a shelter, its legs fused into the trunk of one tree and its crossbar rusted to a copper-green curve that sags in the middle. A small, flat disc of pale material near the center, perfectly level, where the water is clearer than anywhere else and always a little warmer underfoot. The disc is always dry in the rain, though everything else weeps. Water that rolls onto it beads and runs off to the sides again, as if it were lightly repelled, a millimeter above the surface.

You begin to notice other impossibilities that are too small to point to. Your footprints in the silt fill with clear water and then smooth out by the time you look back. A twig you snap is reattached by morning, not healed — deliberately placed, as if the ground keeps tidying itself back to a state. The temperature never falls. No matter how long you stay, air that should cool toward evening stays warm and bright-soft, and the mist that forms is warm on your skin. The water chemistry, if you could test it, would be perfect and constant, though the basin is fed by nothing but sky and seepage. It is being maintained, continuously and invisibly, by a process that is no longer distinguishable from the place itself. The soil, the fauna, the weeping stones — they are the engineering. Whoever or whatever made this made it so well, and so long ago, that it has eroded into geology. The most advanced technology here does not look like technology at all. It looks like the oldest, most settled natural thing you have ever seen.

That is the rule of the place, as far as you can learn it: nothing is sharp, nothing is dry for long, nothing is loud, nothing fully decays, and nothing is allowed to grow out of its allotted patch. It is in a final, ancient equilibrium that is still alive — an end state that keeps breathing, an ecosystem held in stasis by its own inhabitants. The trees drop leaves; the leaves become paddlers; the paddlers become the long blind hunters; the hunters die and become mulch; the mulch becomes trees. And at every step the basin corrects itself back to the same soft curves.

You could walk the whole of it in five minutes. You will stay longer, because the longer you stay the more the emptiness fills with slow motion — the drifting skin of grazers, the hanging motes in the light, the pools quietly changing their edges. It is warm on your shoulders, damp on your ankles, bright without glare, and so quiet that your own breathing seems intrusive. The sparse trees watch from their distances. The scattered remains watch in their softened way. And under everything, the overwhelming, silent fauna goes on with its work, keeping this small, low, ancient hollow exactly as it is.
