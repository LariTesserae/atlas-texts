The way in is not a door.

It is a pipe, waist-deep in warm water, bored straight through the mountain. You duck under riveted iron, hand sliding along a rail grown slick and green, and the water pushes you forward — not fast, but with intention. It has a direction. In here, everything does.

The pipe opens.

Sound arrives first because there is nowhere for it to go. A low thump, once every seven seconds, from somewhere below. Water sheeting over steel somewhere to your left, pipes singing in a high, vibrating chord, drips falling from a great height and striking in perfect, echoing time. Then light — ordinary, midday, sun-yellow light, the kind you would expect on a hill in summer — slanting down through a cracked dome far above you. And air, close and saturated, warm as breath, smelling of mineral water, hot oil, wet concrete, and leaves.

You are in a circle. That is the first true thing about it, and the last. The horizon is a wall, perhaps sixty paces around, and you can take in the whole of it at once. This is a pocket. A single, contained room that has become a world.

It was built, completely. There is nothing natural under any of it. The floor — thirty metres below where you float — is tile and concrete. The walls are boiler-plate and poured concrete and glazed ceramic, white and green, in an industrial spiral that winds from bottom to dome. The dome itself is iron framing and glass, most of it gone, blown or rotted out. The central thing, the thing the room was built around, is a turbine pit and a colossal boiler, a blackened island of machinery rising out of the water and ending in a thicket of pipes that vanish into the ceiling. It does not look new, or even worked-on. It looks settled.

That is its age. The erosion here is not ruin; it is finish. Rivets have been polished by current into pebbles. The concrete at the waterline has dissolved back to its aggregate, a band of exposed, rounded stones. Steel plate has thinned in scallops, like the inside of a shell. Paint survives only in places water and air cannot reach, as ghosts in corners. Everything made has been worn for so long by warm water that made things read as geology. It has the patience of a riverbed.

Except that the riverbed is still running.

The lower three-quarters of the bell are submerged. You do not stand here so much as you are buoyed, debouched into a capped sea the colour of green glass. Beneath you, visible for a few metres in the clear, warm water, are submerged catwalks, fallen gauges, a drowned stair. Then it darkens, though not to blackness — light is normal here, sun through the dome — and the fish move in slow, scattered shoals. Above the water is an air-pocket under the dome, a warm, breathing layer maybe twelve metres deep at its tallest, where the spiral catwalk climbs the wall in broken rings. Half the rings are gone. The ones that remain are gardens.

That is the second surprise, and it keeps asserting itself: the lushness. Nothing is supposed to grow in this. And yet plants are everywhere water and iron meet, which is everywhere. Ferns uncurl from expansion joints. Mosses and algae film every tile in velvets of green and black. Vines have come down through the broken dome, true vines, and their roots hang quivering toward the waterline, some reaching it, some still forever descending. On the catwalk planter-troughs — built by the builders, long since filled with manufactured grit — are small trees with mangrove habits, roots gripping rusted iron instead of soil, trunks leaning to the light. Orchids cling to warm pipes. Duckweed patches the surface in still corners. And from the underside of the dome, in the hottest air, an inverted forest grows downward, pale-leaved, thirsty, roots bare in the steam.

They are not planted. They were brought — in seeds, in mud on boots, in the inflow — and they stayed because the iron feeds them. Corrosion is their soil. Where metal fails, they root. Where they root, metal fails faster. The room gardens itself by eating itself, slowly.

The fauna is abundant in the same scattered way. That is the trick of density here: there is a great deal of life, and a great deal of empty space between it. A school of carp-like fish crosses the centre, fifty strong, then nothing but open water for a long while. A turtle surfaces off your left shoulder, exhales, and is gone. Snails graze the tiles in wide, sparse constellations. Frogs sit on pressure gauges like green knobs, throats puffing. Eels slide in and out of the pipework with electrical ease. Above, in the warm pocket, swallows and small mountain birds nest in the dome framing and dash out through the broken glass and back — out into a wind you can hear whistling around the tower, and into air so thin and high that clouds sometimes pass *below* the windows.

For that is where it sits: at the crown of the mountain. The tower is the highest built thing for miles. The pipe you entered climbs from far below. From the catwalk, through a missing pane, you can see the world drop away in every direction, blue haze and cloud-sea, and the sun is close and sharp. Yet inside, the glass and the steam and the waste-heat of the slow machinery keep it warm — warm water, warm air, a greenhouse at the edge of the sky. Life up here lives inside a bubble of lowland summer.

At night, or when the wind shifts, the bubble rains. Condensation gathers on the cold dome at sunset and falls in a fine, warm shower for an hour, ticking on the water surface, feeding the ferns. In the morning, the inflow surges. Fresh water enters through the upper vent in a white rush, cold enough to make steam bloom where it meets the warm tank, and the whole sea turns over in a slow rotation — in at the top, down the wall, across the turbine floor, up through the boiler pit, out the lower pipe. Debris rides this circuit for days. Fish follow it. A leaf that fell from the dome may make three full circuits before it settles in a grate and becomes a plant.

The machines have not stopped. They cannot, or will not. The turbine turns at a pace that is almost biological now, a heartbeat. When it labours, the lights in the upper rings flicker — a few old electric lamps, buzzing, moth-battered — and the eels gather to the vibration. Pipes knock and settle, musical. A gantry crane, rusted solid halfway across the room, drips in a steady percussion. The whole place is loud precisely because it is a sealed drum. Every process announces itself: the splash of a turtle, the far-off boom of the pit, birds screaming outside the dome, rain on glass, your own paddle.

No one maintains this. No one needs to. That is the rule that holds all the others together.

Everything is made, so everything must be reused. Water is the terrain, and it is also the weather, the road, the soil. Plants grow on corrosion and shade the water and feed the fish that feed the birds that feed the plants. The mountain provides wind and pressure and the inflow, but gives no ground, no seedbed, no natural substrate at all. If the turbine stops, the rotation stops, the water stagnates, and the oldest, most settled things here will, in time, become smooth enough to be called stone.

You can swim — if you are built for swimming — from one island of machinery to the next. There are only three: the boiler, the crane, and a fallen section of chimney, long since become a reef, furred in algae, with turtle shells tucked into its hollow like offerings. Between them is long, green, echoing emptiness, light falling in shafts and breaking.

It is a hidden place, in the end, because all its entrances are under water and all its windows are at the top of the world. You found it by following current. It will go on without you, in its warm, loud, rusted, leafy circuit, an entire landscape folded into a single room, turning slowly on a peak in the sky.
