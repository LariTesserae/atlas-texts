It is not night here. There is no day for it to be opposite to.

The dark is a material thing first. You feel it on your skin before your eyes learn anything. The air is low and warm and pressed down, as if the whole basin were a shallow bowl catching its own breath. It carries heat from below and holds it. Sweat starts at once and does not dry. The damp sits in your hair, beads on the back of your hands, slicks the inside of your elbows. You cannot tell, after a while, whether that wetness is yours, or the air's, or the leaves'.

Because that is the first water here: not rivers. There is almost nowhere for water to run. It rises from warm ground in invisible steam, it gathers on every cool surface and sweats off again. The stone sweats. The great leaves sweat. Mosses are always beaded. Once in a while, when a gust works through the canopy or a vast swarm passes overhead and shakes the branches, all that stored condensation lets go at once and there is a brief, startling rain — not from the sky, but from everything above you, a drumming patter that lasts a minute and leaves the air thick and then stops. In the hollows between the plates of earth, water collects in pockets. They are warm, soupy, knee-deep at most, covered in a filmed skin of tiny floating plants that glow faintly at the edges. Frogs sit in them up to their eyes. Nothing flows out. The pockets evaporate and refill in the same place, over and over, like the land is breathing moisture in and out.

And the land itself is young. Not smoothed. Not worn down into hills. The basin floor and its rim are made of fresh plates, tilted and cracked where they cooled and settled — black basalt, glassy shards, slabs of sulfur-yellow crust, mud that has dried into sharp polygons and then softened again. Every ridge is a broken tooth sawing up out of the green. Every gully is a fresh crack with unweathered edges that will cut boot leather and then be overgrown in a week. You walk on ground that is still deciding its shape: spongy in one step, where water and roots have made a mat, hard and ringing glass the next, steaming faintly where a seam of heat comes close to the surface. Sometimes, without preamble, a jet of vapor sighs out of a crack and a whole declivity fills with white mist for a moment, smelling of hot stone and rot, and all the insects hanging in the air scatter like ash.

The vegetation is lush in a way that has nothing to do with sunlight. There is none to be had. What light there is, the place makes for itself.

Look down. That is where the sky of this place is. Low light comes up from the ground in slow patches — from fungus shelves stacked on rotting trunks, from the veins of broad fern-fronds, from the bellies of grub-like things half-buried in leaf litter, from algae films on the pocket-waters. The leaves are not green so much as translucent: waxy, paler than skin, with branching veins that pulse a dim amber or bruised blue when the air warms. In darkness your eyes learn to read by that glow, and by it the green becomes a different kind of green — deep, wet, layered. Three, four canopies of it, one above the other, never quite touching the ground, never quite ceasing. Vines as thick as your wrist climb the sharp rocks and then climb each other. Ground herbs unfurl leaves the size of shields and hold them up to catch the condensation. Ferns do not grow in cathedrals; they grow everywhere, in cracks, on the backs of stones, on the bark of other ferns. Even the new cracks, still warm, have a fringe of pale root-hairs and tiny shoots feeling their way out.

Everything overlaps. That is the density. It is not empty space that plants happen to fill; it is presence on presence. A leaf is not just a leaf — it is a raft for mites, a perch for a frog, a drip-line for other plants below, a shelter for something chewing its underside. The litter underfoot is not litter for long. Fallen fronds are eaten from below while still falling, tunnelled, raised up again as mushroom caps, eaten again. Life is so fast here that decay and growth happen on the same stem at the same time: a flower opening at the tip, fruit ripening below it, leaves yellowing and dropping at the base, all within arm's reach on one vine. In warm air, in fresh mineral soil that no rain has yet washed thin, things grow visibly if you watch long enough. A shoot will extend, just a little, while you look at it. A bud will swell and burst with a sound like a bead of resin popping. Time-lapse, without the lapse.

The fauna are overwhelming in scale of number first, and then in scale of everything else. The ground moves. Not in one direction, but in pulses. Worm-carpet under the leaf mat. Ants in columns that braid and unbraid around stones, carrying fragments larger than themselves, following scent-currents that shift with the steam. Beetles clicking against each other in the dark. Spiders have webbed the glades shut with sheets so fine and so numerous that mist snags on them in the morning-and-there-is-no-morning. They hang between trees like damp cloth, and the cloth is alive with small white things.

The sound is the next thing, and it does not leave. At first it seems like many sounds — drips on leaves, a hiss from a vent, wings, a frog's machinery of clicks, something large and wet crashing through undergrowth far away. Then you realize the sounds themselves are layered, and the layers are alive. Insects stridulate in bands that start and stop and start again a half-beat later from a different patch of dark. Frogs call from the pocket-waters in a chorus that rises until your teeth ache. Birds — or bird-shaped things, or things that do what birds do — scream and answer themselves from the canopy, the answer delayed by the sharp ridges and arriving as a second, distorted voice. When a swarm lifts off a rotting trunk, the whine is a single chord that fills your chest. When the steam vents sigh, the whole basin hisses. When the rock cools and ticks and cracks, it sounds like knuckles. You raise your voice to test it and it is taken by the noise almost before it forms. It is not a loud place in the way a waterfall is loud. It is loud the way a crowd is loud, each voice distinct and the total inescapable.

And over it all, moving through it, are the bigger presences. You rarely see them whole. A shadow with too much weight for the branch it crosses. A long pale limb uncurling from the canopy, feeling for the warm pocket-water. A herd — no, a flock, though they walk — passing through the glade in a body so continuous that the far side of the glade is blocked for minutes, hooves and feet and tails stirring the leaf litter into brief weather. Their eyes shine where your light catches them, amber and green, and then they are gone and the sound of them rolls away off the ridges.

The turbulence is not just weather. There is no normal state to be turbulent against. The ground heaves a little every so often, shrugging a stone off a root, opening a crack that steams and fills with green within days. Swarms pass like fronts, darkening the glow from below as they go, shaking condensation loose in sheets. A bloom opens on a thousand plants at once because the warm air changed, and for a day the whole basin smells sweet and fetid, and the flies come thick enough to blot the light they themselves make. A tree falls — not cut, not even dead in the ordinary way, but rotted through so fast at the base that it simply gives up one afternoon — and before it hits the ground, things are already living on the part that will land. New ridges rise a finger's width in some seasons; elsewhere a sharp spire, too thin to hold moisture, stands bare and black and hissing in its own heat while plants crowd its base, waiting.

There are no built things. Nothing straight. No path holds. No wall, no roof, no artifact that was meant to keep. The only architecture is woven and tunnelled and alive — the termite mounds that rise like smoking chimneys from the plates and are themselves full of other lives, the nests stitched from spidersilk and leaf-fluff that dissolve back into the canopy within a week of being abandoned, the burrow-mouths in the soft mud. And yet there are signs of a kind of making. Under a stand of broad-leaved trees, a scatter of smooth stones, heel-sized, cracked shells all around them, piles of husks in a meticulous, repeated pattern on a flat slab. Something here has learned to use a tool and has not learned, or has not needed, to build anything to keep the tool in. The stones lie where they were left, wet, already greening at the edges. The makers themselves are only glimpsed: a hand-like shape, small and quick, a face turned toward the glow.

That is the rule, as far as one can be made: light is not given, it is made, laboriously, by living things, and so the world is dark above and faintly luminous below, and everything shelters and feeds in that low glow. Water is not given either; it is borrowed from the air and the warm earth and returned, and so there are no great flows, only pockets and drips and brief rains from the leaves. Soil is newborn and rich and unstable, and so growth is riotous and rootless at once, always colonizing, always collapsing. Nothing here persists in the form you found it. Death is not a later chapter; it is folded into the growth, and the clearing a body makes is filled before the body has stopped steaming.

Stand in one place a long time — if you can stand without touching something alive doing something to you — and the basin resolves into its larger scale. It is not a cave, not a grove. It stretches past hearing in every direction, ridge after sharp ridge, each enclosing a similar low bowl, each bowl a little different in which swarm is passing, which vent is sighing, which plants are in bloom. Far off, where the rim is lowest, a faint amber haze hangs in the air, not dawn, not dusk, just the sum of a million small lights below a ceiling of absolute dark. The warm air moves from bowl to bowl carrying spores and midges and the smell of crushed green things, and the sounds braid together over the ridges into one continuous, deafening, breathing noise, so that when you finally turn to go — because the ground is shifting under you, because a vine has reached your shoulder, because the swarm you were standing in has decided to move — you realize you have not seen the place at all.

You have been inside it, and it has been all around you, many presences to the volume, near-touching, in layers, hotter and darker and wetter and louder than you expected, and it will have changed its shape in the minutes you were there.
