It is below, and it is bright.

That is the first wrongness of it. You descend — down a slant of warm stone, through a tear in the green overhead, fifty, eighty metres below the level of the country around you — and expect shade, coolness, a cave smell. Instead the light meets you stronger. It is white, high, and almost still. It does not slant. It presses down through a round of open sky far above the rim and through the leaves that have half-closed that sky, and it bleaches everything it touches to a yellow-green glare. Your shadow, when you make one, is a small hard black patch directly under your feet. It does not creep. By the time you have been there long enough to notice, it is still there, in the same place.

The air is hot in a close, greenhouse way. Not the dry heat of a desert, but the heat of a mouth shut for a long time. It sits against your skin. It sits in your throat. On the raised parts of the ground you sweat at once and the sweat dries white on your arms, and leaves there are papery and pale. Down in the hollows, where the light is filtered and the air does not stir, the same air is damp. You feel the two weathers a few steps apart, with no wind to argue between them. There is no wind. That is part of the bargain.

The place is large. Once you are down, you realize it is not a cave, not a room. It is a valley that has sunk. From a low ridge in the middle — a spine of raw mineral that runs across the basin, not yet rounded, not yet softened by any long weathering — you can see across the plant mass to the opposite wall, a couple of kilometres away, and along the basin to blurred green distances where one terrace falls to another. The walls are near. The walls are high. Stone above you on every side, sharp-angled and newly split. Facets big as doors, pale where they have just been exposed, with no patina, no soil, no moss thick enough to soften them, no rounded pebbles collected at their feet. It looks like the earth was broken open recently and no time has been allowed to tidy the break. Fracture lines are clean. Dust is absent. Nothing has eroded because nothing, yet, has had the chance.

And yet the floor is alive entirely. Or rather, the floor *is* plant.

You do not walk on ground as such. Underfoot is a spongy, interlocked mat — roots, rhizomes, fallen leaves pressed flat but not rotted, stems woven through stems. It gives and springs back. It is warm from the light above. In places it is tacky with sap. In places it is fibrous as rope. Bare mineral shows only where the walls rise above the green line, and even there, thin filaments, algae, first root-hairs are already tracing the cracks. There is no clearing, no path, no bare patch large enough to stand clear of a leaf. The vegetation is the substrate, the architecture, and the weather made solid.

It is dense in layers. At ankle height, a carpet of small fleshy shoots, each with two round cotyledons still attached like tiny shields. At knee height, a tangle of stems, near-touching, all leaning toward the light they cannot reach differently. At head height, broader leaves arch and meet, making tunnels and vaults and small rooms of green. Above them, other leaves, larger, translucent against the white light, make a second roof, and a third. You move through it by pushing, gently, and it closes behind you without a sound. From the ridge, this density resolves into something else: a single, undulating sea of plant that fills the sunken valley from wall to wall, its surface broken only by pools and by the raw mineral spine and by a few stone terraces where the green is thinner and hotter.

Everything here is young. That is its other wrongness. A tree should not be this size and this unfinished. The largest trunks rise like pillars, smooth and green, but their crowns are still folded buds, unopened, fist-tight. Ferns unroll to the length of your arm and stop mid-curl. Vines have climbed a wall and stopped before they have decided how to flower. Leaves are all at the stage just before they become leaves — creased, half-furled, dew-wet. This is a forest at the moment of germination, enlarged to valley scale. It has the smell of crushed green and warm sap and split rock, sweet and mineral at once. It does not age past that moment. It does not fall. Leaf litter underfoot is fresh, colour-bright, and never becomes leaf mold. There is no soil because there has been no time for soil, and no decay because decay would be a kind of time.

Water is present the way a held breath is present. It is damp, not wet. There are no streams. Nothing flows downhill to carve a channel — the stone is too new, the plant too complete. Instead, in the deep hollows between roots, in cupped leaves, in the low terraces, water gathers in black, still pockets. They are warm. They mirror the white light and the green roof, perfectly. Now and then, from a cooler leaf high above, a single drop condenses and falls, and falls again from the same leaf, and again, with a small ticking sound that you can set your listening by. Occasionally the heat will lift a fine mist off a pool and let it settle again on nearby leaves, beading them. That is all the rain there is. It never rains from the sky. The moisture is made here, in place, and stays here, in place.

Sounds are mostly the absence of them. The quiet is a material, like the heat. No birds. No insects in chorus. No wind in the canopy, because the canopy does not move. When you step, the root mat gives a muffled *thump* that is immediately swallowed. A stem you brush will creak once, the same creak, and then hold. The drip. Your own breathing, too loud. That is a near-complete inventory. If you stay still long enough, your ears begin to make sounds to fill it.

Life, other than plant, is rare to the point of rumor. You will not see it unless you look for a very long time, and in a very small way. Pale mites moving almost invisibly across a leaf, beetles with shells like polished seeds that walk one leaf and stop, forever on that leaf. And once — perhaps on the far terrace, perhaps you imagine it — a larger animal, slow beyond slowness, low-bodied and mottled green-brown, making its way along a root-bridge at a pace that makes it indistinguishable from the growth it crosses. It does not startle. It does not hurry. It belongs to the same equilibrium as everything else. The plants feed it, or it prunes them; the distinction is too fine here and too slow to matter.

There are signs of building, scattered, faint, biological only. No metal. No glass. No tool of any kind, no device, nothing that was made by craft in the way you understand craft. But there are low domes woven from living stalks, tied with vine, on the drier rises — five, six of them across the whole basin, some intact, one collapsed into a ring of stems that has not decayed. There is a cairn of balanced stones on the mineral spine, three high, unexplained. There is a platform of bound reeds over the deepest pool. There are faint parallel grooves on a smooth wall-face where something once dragged, or rooted, or was dragged by roots. They are marks, not messages. Whoever or whatever made these things left no other trace, and has not returned, or has become so continuous with the place that return is indistinguishable from staying.

This is the rule of the world here, and it is what holds all the contradictions together: it does not change.

Not slowly. Not seasonally. Not at all. The light does not travel, so the shadows do not travel. The pools do not ripple unless you touch them, and when you leave, they are glass again. The buds do not open. The fallen leaves do not fall. The stone does not erode. The water does not move from where it condenses to where it drips. It is an equilibrium perfected at the instant of its birth — a valley newly sunk, newly green, hot and bright and damp and dense, and then held. You could mark a leaf in your mind, walk away for what would be days elsewhere, and come back to find that leaf in exactly the same fold, catching exactly the same white light, with the same drop suspended at its tip, about to fall and never quite having fallen anywhere else.

It feels, standing there, less like a place in nature and more like a place under construction that has been left open to the sky — scaffolding of root and stem and raw stone, waiting for the builders to return and finish it. But the waiting is not of the kind that ends. The heat will be there when you return. The damp pockets will be there, warm and black. The green sea will stretch to the walls in the same unaged youth, layer upon layer, below the surface and under the light, quiet, dense, and complete.
