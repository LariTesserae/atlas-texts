### Tessik, on the Drowned Summit

From the ridge you expect a summit — cold rock, lichen, wind that finds every seam — and for a moment you get it. The climb is high enough that your breath comes short and the air tastes thin and clean. Below the rim, clouds are still trapped in the valleys, pale and motionless. Above, the sky is ordinary and bright, the sun white-gold enough to warm your shoulders even though the air stays cool, even at noon.

Then the rock falls away into a bowl, and the bowl is full of water.

Tessik is not a lake town on a shore. There is no shore. The whole basin, perhaps eight hundred paces across, is submerged to a meter, two meters, in places five. The far rim is visible from anywhere in it — a ring of weathered cliffs and scree, dark stone smoothed by centuries of rain and slow seepage, now meeting the water with no beach at all. Water simply presses against rock, and rock holds it like a cupped hand. This is the entire world of the place: one high, cool, bounded sea, sitting in the sky.

And the sea is not empty water over earth. The bottom is not earth. The bottom is vegetation, layered and total.

Under the clear surface — clear enough in the normal midday light to see down to the amber-green depths — lies a living mat, six feet thick in places, of interwoven roots, rhizomes, peat-moss, sedge and reed rootstock, old stems bound into new stems. It floats and it is grounded at once, a spongy substrate that yields under a pole, bubbles faintly when pressed, and holds the water within itself as much as under itself. In the hummocks it rises a hand's breadth above the surface, dark and wet and trembling with frogs. Between the hummocks the water is open, sunlit, full of submerged pondweed waving like slow hair.

Everything that lives here lives on, in, or through that mat. Reed and rush in dense, hissing beds. Water lilies in broad, quiet meadows where the channels widen. Algae forests below, swaying patiently in the cold. Alder-like trees whose trunks rise straight from the water, roots locked into the mat, canopies interlacing above houses. And plants are not just the ground and the wild — they are the architecture. Willow is woven living into walls and left to root. Roofs are thatch that is allowed to green and moss in the damp air, sometimes to take seed and flower. Piers are not driven into soil; they are lashed to root pillars that the town has grown, year by year, around wooden posts, until post and root become one thing.

The town itself is scattered across this. That is its odd urban logic: settlement-scale, elaborate, layered — and sparse. Dozens, hundreds of structures, but with great empty water between them, whole meadows of lily and open channel where nothing stands except birds. You can cross Tessik in an hour by dugout, and never pass a boat.

It is built with hand tools only. No metal worth naming, only a few hammered bits, bone hooks, flint-edged blades, clay pots darkened by smoke. Boats are single logs hollowed by fire and adze, poled rather than paddled, their poles feeling for the old causeway that runs beneath the water from rim to rim — a broad track of flat stones, worn smooth and green with algae, now more a memory under the boats than a road. Houses are on piles. The lowest room is always flooded, quiet and lit from above, where fish drift through the doorway and old furniture stands under water for a generation. Life is lived upstairs, in the dry rooms above the lily line.

The layers show its age. The oldest work is stone: foundations and the causeway, sunk into the mat until only their smoothed tops show, grey and settled. Above them, timber — pale where it is new-cut, silvered where it has weathered a hundred wet years, leaning a little and quite stable in its lean. Above that, the woven houses, reed and fiber and living branch, patched and repatched. An established place does not look new anywhere. Every roof sags under its thatch and its birds. Every pier is stained dark to the waterline. Nothing is collapsing, either. Tessik has long since found its own form and is content to keep it.

Between the house clusters it is still a town: storehouses on raft platforms, fish pens woven from split reed where the current — a slow, barely perceptible drift from the snowmelt seep on the north rim to the south — passes through, floating gardens anchored by root, small stone hummocks where the only fires are allowed to burn. Those hearth-hummocks are rock piled and raised, kept deliberately above water, and on them pots simmer and smoke rises thin and straight in the cool air. Everything else is damp. Nothing is allowed to dry out here. The weather itself is patient: rain comes as a wall crossing the rim out of the cloud below, curtains the basin for an afternoon, and leaves. The water level does not surge. It breathes, perhaps a finger's width in a season.

For all that slowness, Tessik is loud.

Sound carries over water and the mat magnifies it. Frogs are on every leaf, in their hundreds, a deep, companionable chorus that starts at dusk and does not quite stop. Reed beds hiss when the wind comes off the ridge. Fish jump — suddenly, with a slap — among the houses. Herons call from rooftops, startled wings beating wet air. Otters slide down piles and bark. The mat itself drips and trickles, water finding its way through root hairs with a sound like distant rain inside the ground. Wooden boats knock gently against piers, over and over, all night. Nets splash. Children shout across channels, and their voices arrive whole, unsoftened, because there is nothing here to absorb them.

The fauna is abundant in step with the plants. Silver fish move in shoals through the streets of the town, flashing under the houses and through the flooded ground floors. Eels live in the drowned stonework. Crayfish walk the causeway at night. Dragonflies stitch the air above the lilies. Ducks and grebes nest in the raft gardens. Birds have claimed the town roofs entirely — swallows in the eaves, owls in the hollow trees that serve as pillars, a heronry in a half-dead alder that is also somebody's wall. It is an unusual ecology only in its completeness: plant substrate below, plant architecture above, and animal life threaded through both so densely that the quietest hour still hums.

Tessik endures because it moves so slowly. A root pillar takes a childhood to grow thick enough to bear a house. A house, once anchored, sinks by inches in a decade and rises by inches when the mat swells in a wet year. A flooded room becomes a fish nursery, then, when the mat thickens, a planter. A channel choked with weed is not dredged so much as allowed to become a meadow, and a new water-street is poled open beside it. Erosion here is not dramatic. Stone is polished. Timber is softened. Peat slowly forms where old reeds fall and are not eaten. Change is measured in generations, and the town has the confidence — the weathered, settled confidence — of a place that expects to be exactly as it is, only greener, for a very long time.

At evening the light goes ordinary gold and then flat. The water turns dark green-brown and reflects the rim. The fires on their stone islands come up. Frogs take over. Upstairs in the dry rooms, lamps of rendered fat are lit, and below them, in the submerged rooms, fish drift past the windows of light, and the whole town — stone, timber, fiber, root, water — sits quiet and loud at once, high in the cold, drowned, and utterly at home in its own skin.
