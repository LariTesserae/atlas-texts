It opens far below the weather. 

A regional basin of high mineral plateau, ridged and lifted to an extreme height, more than a valley or a pair of mountains — a system of spurs, hanging salt-flats, and tilted shelves that extends past sight in every direction and then, disconcertingly, seems to begin again. The air is thin and cool against the skin, even at noon. The light is dim, a permanent grey-blue twilight, as if the sun were old and low and smeared behind a veil of fine rock-dust that never settles. Nothing here hurries. A faint mineral haze hangs over everything and softens the far ridges into blue silhouettes, and the cold does not change with the hours.

There is no water. Not in the senses you would expect. The riverbeds are dry, cracked ribbons of black glassy stone that run for miles between the hills and never fill. The low flats are salt and pale basalt and pumice, blinding white even under this ash-light. Frost forms on the high stone at night, but it is a dry frost, a sublimating crust that vanishes into the air without ever becoming a drip, a puddle, a stream, a rain. No lake mirrors the sky. No spring breaks the rock. Dust does not wash away; it only accumulates, and is blown thin, and moves on.

And yet, in scattered islands across this emptiness, the vegetation is lush.

The groves are sparse — often a day or more apart across bare ground — but once you reach one, it is dense to the point of enclosure. Carpets of needled moss in deep green and yellow-green that feel like cool felt underfoot, giant low-light ferns with leaves broad as doors, trees with bark like layered stone and hollow trunks, vines that drop from branches in thick curtains and root again in mid-air. Their leaves are pale, to catch the dim light, and they give off a faint phosphorescent glow, green-gold, which makes a grove visible from a far ridge like a lamp lit in a desert. They do not drink water. They do not drink at all, in any familiar way. Their sap is a dark, iron-rich, alkaline fluid that smells faintly of crushed stone and ozone. Their roots split the rock and precipitate mineral dust from the air. Their hollow stems drink something else entirely.

The sound is deafening.

It is there before you understand it, and then it is in your teeth. Not a roar of wind, though the wind is constant and always in the same direction. It is a low, tectonic drone, the groan of the whole uplifted range still being made, flexing and cracking under its own weight. Stone pops like distant ice, one sharp report, then another, always far away and always close. The ground vibrates through the soles of your feet. The salt flats, when the wind crosses them, sing with a high whistling that rises until it hurts, and the hollow plants in the groves answer. Every reed, every hollow trunk, every dry pod is an organ pipe tuned to the mountain's hum. Standing inside a grove, the noise is almost physical — you feel it in your sternum, you cannot shout over it, you cannot think in complete sentences for long. And it does not change. Dynamic is a word that barely applies. The tone holds for hours, for days. The groaning comes in pulses so slow you could mistake them for your own heartbeat, and then for the hollow stems to resonate and turn that vibration, patiently, into growth. These plants are sonivores. They feed on sound and on dust and on dim light, unfurling one leaf in a season, opening a flower over fifty years, but growing without pause, without water, in the loudest silence in the world.

There is no animal life. None at all.

No birds overhead, no insects to buzz in the groves, no tracks in the dust except the faint parallel marks of things that were dragged or placed. No dung, no bones, no burrows, no calls — the deafening noise is entirely inanimate and botanical, which makes the absence more complete. Nothing flies to pollinate these slow flowers. They repeat themselves vegetatively, setting stone-like seeds that fall and split and grow in a way that is hard to follow. Nothing eats them. Nothing moves through them except the wind and, rarely, a person.

Because people were here, sparsely, primitively, and left their marks the way the plants left theirs — scattered and unfinished.

A cairn of single stacked stones on a ridge, each stone placed by hand, no mortar. A half-wall in a hollow, three courses high, curved from slabs of basalt and lashed with dark plant fiber, never roofed. A knapping floor on a salt flat: flakes of black stone, a half-finished axe hafted clumsily with cordage and resin, abandoned in a neat circle. A line of stakes with the cord long gone, marking a boundary that was never completed. No paths connect them. No larger settlement gathers them. The tools are all stone and wood and woven stalk, hand-made and rough, simple fabrication for a place that does not reward permanence. Some of the walls seem to be still building themselves, adding one stone in a decade, in fits no one watches.

The geology is at the same stage as the building: emerging. Pre-formation, raw. This is a mountain range that has not yet been eroded because it has not yet finished forming. The strata are still bending, still soft in places, like clay being pushed up from below. Cliffs are sharp, unsoftened, newly sliced. Scree has not been sorted by rain, because there is no rain to sort it. Soil has not fallen down from the heights; it is coming up, a thin embryonic skin of mineral and moss slowly spreading over bare rock. Everything looks new and unfinished, as if the world were under construction at a pace too slow for any living thing to witness.

And that is where reality itself breaks down, completely, in the way an unfinished thing does.

The ridge you see in front of you is also, impossibly, behind you. A grove pattern repeats three times across the basin with slight variations, as if a draft had been copied and not yet corrected. The same cairn appears on two separate summits. The horizon bends down at the edge of the plateau, and the dim sun is reflected in the salt, and also, faintly, from underneath it. Some leaves are green before they emerge; some are brown and perfect while still in the bud; some fruit hang ripe on branches that have not yet grown. Your footsteps leave prints in the dust that seem to form a moment before you step. Distance increases when you turn around. A structure that looked ruined from the north looks newly built — and newly abandoned — from the south. The sound arrives before the crack that makes it. The valley's echo reaches you before the valley itself does.

It is not chaotic. It is patient. The uplift will take another hundred thousand years. The plants will take another hundred to grow a hand's breadth. The walls will add their stones one at a time. The light will stay dim and cool. The glass riverbeds will stay dry. Between the groves, the emptiness will stay empty, mile after mile of salt and stone under a grey sky, humming at a frequency you feel more than hear, while the hollow green islands glow faintly in the dimness, listening to the mountain being made, and growing — lush, waterless, and entirely alone — in response.
