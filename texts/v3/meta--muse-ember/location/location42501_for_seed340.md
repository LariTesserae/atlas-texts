Below the salt plain there is a country that was never meant to have a surface.

You go down through a crack no wider than a doorway — in the dry mineral crust, in white, chalky rock that crumbles to dust under your fingers — and the air gets warmer as you descend, not cooler. Warm as the inside of a mouth. Warm as skin resting against skin. The light, when it comes, is startlingly ordinary: white-gold, normal daylight, the color of noon on any familiar world. It does not come from the sky above you. It comes sideways, in shafts through fissures in the crust far overhead, and it percolates, dappled and softened, through a ceiling that is not rock at all.

That ceiling is alive.

The whole region — miles of it, valley after valley of interconnected chambers, rolling and multi-featured like a landscape that has been folded in on itself — is roofed in places by interlocked canopies of enormous leaves stretched tight beneath the thin stone, translucent at the edges, green-gold where the light hits them. In other chambers the roof has not yet closed. There, normal light floods in unfiltered and the dust hangs glittering in it, dry, pale, and endless. No rain has ever reached here. No river, no spring, no lake, no puddle has ever existed here. Everything is thirsty in a way that has nothing to do with water.

The ground is not ground.

What you step on, everywhere, for as far as you can see in any direction, is plant. Not soil covered with plant — plant as substrate, plant as the solid world. A living mat presses underfoot, springy and warm, made of woven roots, creeping stems, fallen fronds, and successive generations of leaves that have compacted into a spongy, fibrous earth of their own making. It gives and sighs and recovers. In the hollows between plant-hills, the mat is thick and dark and layered like turf; on the rises, it thins over ribs of fresh mineral just precipitating out of the warm air and from the excretions of the plants themselves — stone that is still soft, still embryonic, stalactites lengthening a finger's breadth a day, new walls budding out from old ones like callus.

The vegetation is total. It does not grow *in* the landscape; it *is* the landscape. Trunks the size of towers lean and curve and fuse, forming tunnels you can walk through and chambers you can shelter in, their bark ridged and resinous and warm to the touch. Vines have scaffolded themselves across chasms, root-bridges arching over root-valleys. Leaves unfold overhead like umbrellas, then like roofs, then like whole second ceilings. Everywhere are half-finished things: buds the size of houses still furled and trembling, fronds halfway open, shoots already splitting the fresh stone as they rise. This is a world in the act of coming into being, and it has not stopped to finish anything.

How it lives without water is its first and strangest rule. The plants do not drink. They feed on light and air and mineral dust — they mine the chalk and limestone and salt in the rock with slow, acidic roots, drawing up what they need and exuding the rest as new stone. Their sap is not water. It is a thin, volatile oil, sweet and sharp on the air, that beads on leaves and evaporates almost as fast as it forms, leaving the whole place smelling of warm resin, crushed green, hot dust, and ripe fruit. The animals depend on that chemistry. They do not drink; they suck sap from cut stems, chew succulent pulp from fruit and seed-pods, lick resin from bark. Bones here are dry and white and clean, never rotting in mud, because there is no mud. Nothing ever gets wet.

And the animals are everywhere. Overwhelmingly, deafeningly everywhere.

The density is near-touching, layered, vertical. There is a burrowing layer inside the floor-mat itself, where root-moles and blind grubs tunnel through living stem. There is a grazing layer on the floor, where herds of six-legged, bark-skinned beasts move in slow, milling masses, pruning the growth with their mouths — without them the plants would seal every passage in a week. There is a mid-layer of trunk-dwellers, of things that live their whole lives inside hollow stems, tapping them, nesting in them, emerging only to swarm. And there is a flying layer so thick it makes its own weather: beetle-clouds, moth-swarms, screeching canopy birds, pollinators the size of hands and the size of cats, moving in glittering mist between the leaf-roofs. The boundary between plant and animal frays constantly here. Some of the grazers photosynthesize through translucent backs. Some of the plants walk — slowly, deliberatively, hauling themselves on cord-like roots from one chamber to another over the course of a day, leaving a furrow in the mat behind them. You learn quickly not to assume that the thing you stepped over a moment ago is still a thing, or still stepped-over.

The sound is the next thing, and it never stops. It is deafening, not in the way of a single explosion but in the way of a crowd that will not disperse. First, the omnipresent roar of insects — a constant chittering surf that rises and falls as the swarms pass overhead, so loud it vibrates in your teeth. Under that, the creak and groan of growth: giant stems stretching, leaves unfurling with a sound like tearing silk, root-cords snapping taut, seed-pods bursting in sharp pops like firecrackers, one after another across the valley. Under that, the low rumble of the world shifting — stone cracking as roots pry it apart, fresh mineral settling, whole sections of mat heaving up or slumping down by a hand's breadth overnight. And over it all, the fauna calling: whistles, clicks, hoots, choruses of bell-like ringing from the canopy. You cannot hear your own footsteps. You cannot speak normally. Communication here is by touch, by gesture, by shouting a few inches from an ear, by reading the tremor in the plant-mat under your feet. The noise is not an event in this place. It is the place.

Nothing here stays still for long. That is the second rule, and the source of its turbulence. This is a Solaris-pace of creation and destruction, an anthill and a bazaar at once. Growth fronts advance visibly if you stand still long enough to watch — a vine whipping out across a path, a wall of buds swelling shut across a doorway, a trunk that was hollow yesterday now budding leaves from every pore. And as fast as things grow, they collapse. A tower-trunk rots — or rather, is eaten hollow in an hour by a passing horde — and falls with a crack like thunder, and under its shade a dozen new shoots are already thrusting up through the punctured mat before the dust settles. Chambers are born when roots split stone; chambers die when growth seals them. The geology itself is emerging: this is erosion in reverse, raw formation, pre-formation — rock is being built faster than it is worn away, and the plants are both the builders and the wrecking crew.

Against all that, the built things are scattered and small and faint. A cairn of knapped stone, half-swallowed by the mat. A hut of woven root-fiber and lashed branches tucked into a hollow trunk, its roof a single great leaf now slowly growing over it. A low wall, three stones high, abandoned mid-construction — or perhaps not abandoned, merely outpaced, the plants having grown across the work site while its builders were off foraging. A scatter of primitive tools on a ledge: stone blades, bone needles, baskets of woven stem, fiber cordage, the whole simple kit of hand-craft and manual work. No one with stone-age technology builds largely here, because here nothing large is allowed to remain. What is built is built to be carried, to be remade, to be lost. The inhabitants — small, stocky, warm-blooded people and the semi-sapient trunk-dwellers who trade with them — move constantly, following the growth fronts, following the herds, never sleeping twice in the same posture if they can help it, because the floor might grow up through the shelter in the night.

The light moves, too, though the sun is only a rumor above the crust. As the day passes, the shafts shift, the leaf-roofs open and close like eyelids, and whole chambers go from bright normal daylight to green twilight to a warm, resin-scented dusk in minutes, then flare bright again as a canopy is torn by a falling trunk or stripped by grazers. Shadows are ordinary and sharp-edged. Dust always hangs in them.

Stand on one of the plant-hills at midday — if you can find a moment when the insect-roar dips and the growth pauses — and you can see the scale of it: not a cave, not a garden, but a region. Hills of woven root rolling to a horizon of fused trunks. Valleys where the mat has folded over itself into dark, breathing tunnels. A new mineral ridge, pale and soft and still growing, rising like a spine across a chamber that did not exist last week. Everywhere, movement at every level, everywhere, color and texture and noise layered to saturation. And everywhere, unfinished. A leaf halfway to being a roof. A stone halfway to being a wall. A herd halfway to being something else. A world still in its first, turbulent breath — warm, dry, loud, green beyond counting, and being born, and unborn, and born again, without any water at all.
