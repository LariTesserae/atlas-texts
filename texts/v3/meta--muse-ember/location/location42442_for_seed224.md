At first it seems like the top of the world.

The cold is there before anything else, though it is not the deep, killing cold of a frozen waste. It is the thin, high cold that gets into the gaps in your clothing and stays there. The air is dry to the point of hurt — breath leaves you and vanishes without damp, without fog. Nothing here has ever been wet. Dust does not clump. Frost does not form on stone. If you hold out your hand, no dew settles on it; the dust that lands there remains powder, pale grey-brown mineral flour, fine as ash but gritty between your fingers, faintly metallic on the tongue.

The light is dim and remains so. It is a silvery, blue-grey twilight that never quite becomes night and never quite becomes day. There is a distant source somewhere high and far — a sun seen through too much thin air, or through a narrow aperture — but it casts only a soft, directionless illumination. Shadows are not black. They are slightly darker approximations of the light. Stars are visible even in the brightest part of the cycle, pricking through the gloom.

Beneath your boots is a plateau. It undulates in low, broad swells, pans of compacted dust, shallow ridges of fractured stone. The surface is established, settled into its final form by erosion that has long since finished its main work. Everything natural here has been worn to balance: edges rounded, slopes at the angle where dust stops sliding, hollows where finer material collects and stays. There is no active landslide, no new channel, no fresh scar. It feels immensely old, and immensely finished, as if it has arrived at the state it will keep for the rest of time.

Yet it is high, and you know it in your lungs. The pressure is low. Sound dies quickly in the thin air. The horizon, when there is one, falls away below eye level in a way that makes your inner ear protest. You are not on a summit you can climb down from. You are on a summit that is itself the whole world.

There is very little here. That is the second thing you notice, after the cold. The sparse density is almost architectural: vast emptiness, then a single stone, then emptiness again, then a crystal growth, then more emptiness. Most of the plateau is bare.

The stones are black and white and translucent amber, angular shards and low erratics embedded in the dust. Between them creep the closest things this place has to a flora, though they are not alive and have never been. They are mineral dendrites — branching, fern-like sprays of white fibrous crystal that spread across the faces of rocks in delicate, symmetrical fronds. At a distance they look like frost ferns. Up close they are hard as stone and cold. They grow, if that is the word, outward from a point by the accretion of material from the dust and from the faint thermal gradient of the ground, a few millimetres in a century. They do not green. They do not wilt. They simply extend until they meet an obstacle, then stop, in perfect equilibrium.

There are no animals. Nothing here moves under its own power. No insects, no birds, no burrowers. The only motion is the slow drift of dust in a wind so weak it is more a settling than a blowing, the slow lengthening of crystal fronds, and the almost imperceptible migration of shadow.

And there are built things, scattered so far apart that at first you think each is alone.

Three low domes, no larger than a room, of a ceramic-metal that is neither. The material is warm to the touch despite the cold, and it has not rusted, cracked, or dulled in any way you can measure. Their seams are seamless. They have no doors. One has been eroded — not by water, but by a thousand years of dust abrasion — into a perfect hemisphere, as if erosion itself became a tool and decided to stop.

A needle spire, half-buried at a shallow angle, black as polished obsidian but bending the light around its shaft so that you can never quite see its tip. It points not up, but past the ceiling, in a direction that should not exist inside a mountain. Where it enters the ground, the dust forms a perfect, untouched circle.

A ring, perhaps three metres across, of white metal lying flat in a shallow pan. It is warm, it is slightly buoyant when you brush it — your fingertips feel a faint lift, as if gravity has been locally misfiled — and the stone around it has grown up and over its lower curve as though the rock wanted to swallow it and then thought better of the idea and stopped for good.

And faint marks, everywhere if you look at the right angle to the dim light: concentric scoring in the dust, grids of microscopic perforations in stone, lines that map distances that do not correspond to the distances you can see. Someone, or something, measured this place with instruments whose mathematics we do not possess. Their engineering does not quite describe in our physics — matter that conducts nothing and everything, geometries that close on themselves, fields that persist long after the power that made them should have decayed. The builders are gone. What remains is not a city, not a ruin in our sense. It is a handful of instruments left idling, scattered across emptiness, doing some slow work that has no observer left to need it.

It takes a long walk for the wrongness to accumulate.

You walk toward the domes. They do not get closer in any consistent way. From one ridge they are near; from the next, further than the horizon you just left. You walk toward the spire and arrive, and turn, and see the spire again ahead of you, unchanged, in the direction you have just come from.

The horizon is not a horizon. It folds. At the edge of the plateau the dust rises, gently, impossibly, and becomes the underside of a ceiling that is both close enough to touch and too far to reach. Footprints you made minutes ago appear a few steps ahead of you, already settled. A stone you examined for its age looks younger when you look at it from the west than from the east, and casts a shadow that falls toward the light.

This is the third thing, and the one that reframes the first two: you are not on a world. You are inside a pocket.

It is hidden because it is contained. A single feature, embedded within the dead rock of a high summit, no wider in absolute space than a large room, and yet within it the ground still rises to a small, true peak — high in its own frame, thin-aired, under a dim sky that is the inside of that same rock. The plateau is real and the room is real. Light loops in here. Distance is a suggestion the builders left behind when they folded this space to hide it, or to store something in it, or simply to make a laboratory where a few laws could be suspended at a time.

The rules, as far as they can be stated without contradicting themselves:

Direction is local. East, west, up, and down renegotiate themselves as you cross certain invisible lines in the dust — the survey marks. At those lines, left and right exchange. Uphill becomes downhill if you turn around, and remains uphill if you keep walking. Elevation is not a height above a sea, because there is no sea and no level. It is a property of how far you are from the largest piece of original engineering, which the pocket still treats as its centre of gravity.

Time runs at different rates on different materials. The ring is warmer than the ground and older than the ground and younger than the ground, depending on when its faint cycle is. One full cycle of whatever the instruments are doing appears to take about seven hundred years. In human terms, nothing here is happening. In the pocket's terms, everything is happening, very slowly, in perfect balance.

Sound, when it is made, does not travel far. Kick a stone and the clatter is swallowed mid-air, as if the thinness of the air and the folded walls conspire to drink it. The silence is not absolute — at intervals, when the thermal contraction works through the spire, there is a quiet tick, like a dry clock heard from another room, and occasionally a deeper, slower ping that you feel in your teeth more than your ears. Your own breathing is the loudest thing in the landscape, harsh and dry.

The ecology of the place is mineral and patient. Crystals grow toward the faint heat of the instruments and away from it, forming gardens that are symmetrical because the field is symmetrical. Dust, having nowhere to go, lifts in the still air toward the ceiling and then, after an interval of years, settles again in exactly the same pattern. The builders' field, still folded but long idle, holds everything in a settled, weathered equilibrium: erosion continuing only at the microscopic scale, crystal growth balancing crystal dissolution, the pocket slowly — so slowly — sealing itself tighter around its contents, the way a wound might heal if the wound were made of stone.

Nothing new arrives. Nothing leaves. The light remains dim. The cold remains. The domes remain warm. The spire remains half-buried, pointing past a ceiling that is also a floor.

And if you stand for a long time on the highest point the pocket gives you — a rise no taller than a person, but high in the only way that matters here — you can see the whole of it at once, if you know how not to look directly. The plateau, the glittering fronds spreading like time-lapse ferns across stone, the three domes and the ring and the needle, the dust that has never known water, the shadows falling the wrong way, the folded horizon closing around you like the inside of a held breath.

It is a landscape that has been left, on purpose, at the edge of reality, and then left so long that even the breakdown has eroded into a kind of peace.
