At first you do not see it.

You feel it against your face — a heat that has no sun in it. Dense, dry, and still, as if the air itself were baked and left to settle. There is no glare to adjust to, no horizon to find. Only a very faint orange breathing from somewhere low and far, and the smell: resin, charcoal, hot dust, and something sweet underneath, like sap boiled down too long.

You are high up. That is the first true thing about the place. The ground falls away behind you in long, eroded steps for miles before it flattens, and the air is thin in a way that makes the heat feel sharper, more mineral. This is an ancient plateau, perhaps the oldest piece of solid world that has not yet given up. It was flattened long before anything was built here by wind, by heat, by the slow cracking and crumbling of stone in dayless warmth. Its edges are rounded. Its mesas are stumps. Everything here has been worn down to its final, settled form — and then built upon, worn down again, and built upon again.

There is no water. Not here, not on the whole shelf as far as anyone can tell. No rivers cut the plateau. No lakes collect in the hollows. No rain darkens the dust. What moisture exists is locked inside things, or moves in secret: sap under pressure in living tissue, condensation beading for an hour inside a sealed pipe, a trace of brine in salt-flats between the roots. The chemistry of the place is not aqueous. It runs on oil, latex, resin, and ash. Hot sap circulates where water would. At night — and it is always night, in the practical sense, the light score of this world kept near zero by cloud, by angle, by a permanent dusk under a thick sky — the resin thickens. By dayless day it thins and runs.

Because the world is dark, the plants have learned another way to eat.

Vegetation is not a covering here. It is the substrate. It is all the living mass there is, and it has been persuaded, for longer than any record kept in it can name, to become building.

You step, and the ground gives not like soil but like a mat. Under a crust of baked clay and white mineral salt and black slag-brick, the earth is woven. Roots the thickness of your forearm run in straight, deliberate lines, grafted to one another, pruned to right angles. They are not wild. In the depths, older root-lattices have petrified. They ring like wood when struck, hard and resonant, and they hold up weight. Newer roots thread through them, pale and hot to the touch, seeking the warmth of buried engines.

Above you, the architecture does the same thing. There are no trees in the ancient sense, but there are towers that grew as trees and were finished as towers. Their trunks were trained, hollowed, and braced with iron hoops riveted directly into the living bark. Their canopies were cut back into flat, overlapping roofs of stiffened leaf and bast-fiber, lacquered with resin until they shed ash. Their branches are scaffolds. Their hollows are workshops. Houses are espaliered thickets, walls of woven wattles grown to a meter thick and then baked from within by waste heat until they are half charcoal, half timber. Even the roads are not laid so much as grown into place: a causeway of compressed fiber and earth, constantly patched by root growth finding the cracks and binding them shut.

Everything is made. There is no natural substrate left to stand on if you dig. A spade — if there were hands to hold one — would go through ash, then brick, then petri-fied wood, then a layer of old woven mat, then ash again. The plateau’s geology is sedimentary in the human sense: centuries of occupation compressed into strata. Factories are built directly into the root-mat. Silos are swollen seed-pods, their walls living, their doors cut and hinged with metal. Conveyors are not on legs; they are carried on trained vine-trusses that flex and sigh.

And it is industrial. Not cleanly, not newly. The industry is old and hot and woody. Heavy engines live in the flanks of the towers, their cylinders wrapped in insulation of bark and asbestos-like fiber. Belts of treated hide turn pulleys the size of rooms. Furnaces, low and continuous, vent not smoke so much as a heat-shimmer full of resin vapor and fine white ash. The light of the place is almost entirely theirs: the deep amber seams where a furnace door has not quite sealed, the cold white arc of an electric starter, the phosphorescent skin of certain heat-fed fungi that grow only on the inner walls of ducts — the one concession to wildness, and even they are cultivated as living insulation. In darkness this hot, light is never ambient. It is emitted, rationed, and it casts hard shadows that are always occupied.

It is packed. That may be the most oppressive fact after the heat. There is no empty lot, no fallow field, no unclaimed gap between presences. Every square meter is either grown, built, paved, or buried under something that was. Even the vertical spaces are saturated. Terraces climb the old plateau steps in proliferating rings, each one dense with low workshops pruned into the slope, each workshop roofed by the next terrace’s root-mat. Aerial walkways of bamboo and cable cross at three and four levels, so that at noon — if noon could be named here — the ground would be in permanent shadow from structures and from the structures’ own living extensions. The air itself feels crowded, tasting of dust that has been lifted and resettled a thousand times.

There are no animals. Not a bird in the black sky. Not an insect in the warm stillness. Not a beast among the roots. The fauna count is effectively zero, and the silence that results is not empty. It is a working silence. Once and again you hear the tick of cooling metal, the slow groan of a root-truss taking weight, the pneumatic sigh of a plant-factory venting resin-steam, a distant, muffled clank as a hook on a vine-crane drops a load of bricks. The turbulence of the place is not acoustic; it is temporal. It is quiet this minute, and violent over a season.

Because everything is made from plants, and plants want to grow, and the engines want to burn, the whole region lives in a state of hyperactive, low-grade creation and destruction. A heat pulse from deep below — these worlds breathe from underneath, geothermal and unstable — will cause a district of root-walls to swell, split, and exude hot sap that hardens into amber panes. A scaffold-tower, overgrown at its base by a root that was meant to be a support beam, will lean for years, then shear and fall into the street it was shading, and within a week new shoots will be binding its wreckage into a new foundation. Factories explode not often with fire, but with pressure: resin boiled too thick in a hollow stem until the stem bursts, coating a whole intersection in a glossy, sticky amber that then fossilizes in the heat. Ash devils — dust whirlwinds without water, without wind proper — tear along the terraces, lifting salt and charcoal and seed, dropping them again in drifts that immediately begin to root.

This is the loop the place has settled into. It is ancient, and it is finished, and it will never stop. Growth produces fuel. Fuel produces heat. Heat kills growth. The dead growth bakes into permanent structure. The structure is overgrown. The overgrowth is burned for the next cycle of building. The ash fertilizes — not with water, but with minerals — the next generation of straight-line roots. Erosion here is not primarily the wearing away of stone by water, but the wearing away of everything by heat, by ash-wind, by thermal expansion, by the simple fact that in a hot, dry, dark place, organic matter does not rot. It chars. It petrifies. It becomes geology. The oldest layers of the city are indistinguishable from the oldest layers of the plateau, because they are made of the same stuff, laid down in the same patient order.

Seen from the highest terrace, then, as your eyes — or whatever instruments the automated tenders use — take in the open scale of it, the landscape resolves into something stranger than a city or a forest. It is a regional shelf, miles across, segmented into a dozen interlocked districts, each a different age of the same cycle. In the north, where the plateau is highest and most eroded, the root-mats are thin and the towers are skeletal petrified trunks, museums of early building, their engines long cold but their woodwork still holding the skyline like ribs. In the central basin, the density is at its maximum; the factories breathe in compound, drive-belts hung between grown arches, their roofs a continuous canopy of lacquered leaf that rains ash instead of water. In the south, the terraces step down toward a salt plain where the plantings are poorer, more mineral, cactus-like forms grafted into slag walls, their flowers reduced to vents. All of it is in darkness. All of it is hot. All of it is gently, constantly remaking itself in silence.

The rules of this world, if you stay long enough to infer them, are simple and absolute:

Nothing grows here unless it is built first — planted in a prepared bed of ash and brick, grafted, guided by wire and machine.

Nothing built here survives unless it is grown over — left to be bound, reinforced, and eventually replaced by living tissue.

And nothing is wasted, because there is no water to carry anything away. Waste becomes ground. Ground becomes root. Root becomes wall. Wall becomes ash. Ash becomes ground again.

It is a place at the end of life, in the sense that there is nothing outside it left to colonize and no animal to miss it. It is a place at the beginning, in the sense that its plants are still, in their heat-fed darkness, aggressively alive. Between those two facts, it hums — quietly, densely, turbulently — a made landscape of plant and engine, built a thousand times over on the same ancient, flattened, high ground, and destined to be built a thousand times more.
