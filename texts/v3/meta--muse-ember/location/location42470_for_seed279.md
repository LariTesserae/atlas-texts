You hear it before you see it, and the hearing doesn't stop.

Not a wind alone. Not rain alone. A roar, low and near, the way surf sounds too close to be surf — a hollow booming underneath a constant ticking and dripping, thousands of notes at once, so dense they stop being individual. It gets louder when you stop walking. It gets louder still inside the clusters, and drops in pitch, as if you have stepped into a throat.

Then the land shows itself through it.

It is low. Not a valley, not a mountain foot, but a broad, lifted flat that has only just finished being made. You can see the horizon in every direction, grey and unbroken, and nothing between you and it rises more than a man's shoulder, often less. The rise is not rolling. It is faulted. Straight fracture planes cut the ground in long, angular seams, lifting one slab a hand's breadth above the next. Where a slab has broken, the edges are still fresh — glassy black basalt, sharp enough to cut skin, its vesicles open and shining. There is no soil rounded down by rivers, no slope softened by time. This place is young in its bones. It has not yet learned to be smooth.

Nothing green grows here. Not a blade, not a moss film, not a lichen crust. For as far as you can look, the ground is mineral only: wet stone, silt pressed to stone-hardness, fine grit that fountains up in tiny fans where water seeps out. The pools are clear and colourless, and they stay clear. Something in the water — perhaps the sheer, constant upwelling of dissolved rock — keeps it sterile and bright. Even the faint biofilm you might expect in a cool marsh never gets a foothold. The world is pre-life, or post-life, and has settled for stone.

And the stone is crowded.

At first you take the dark shapes for scattered boulders. Then you realize they are everywhere, overlapping, near-touching, layered in low ridges that run for hundreds of paces and then break and resume, as if a single forest has been truncated and laid down to root in the ground. They are columns and broken columns, hollow tubes of basalt from knee-height to above your head, most of them leaning a degree or two in no particular direction, their tops snapped flat or jagged. In places they stand so thick that you must turn sideways to pass, shoulder to shoulder, a tight, upright rubble. In places there is a brief clearing with only a few, standing alone in a shallow pool. The scale keeps opening past the clearing — basin after basin of the same dense mineral growth, extending to the dim horizon without a far shore.

They weep.

That is the water here. It is not a river, and it is rarely a rain, though the air is saturated enough that your breath and your coat collect droplets and everything you touch is wet. Water is near the surface everywhere, and it finds the stone. It beads on the vertical faces of the columns in perfect, unmoving pearls, gathers along fracture lines until a bead is too heavy and falls — not in a rain, but pillar by pillar, tube by tube, a slow, patient chime. Inside the hollow columns it stands at different levels, dark and still. On top of some flat-topped columns there is a tiny perched pool, an inch deep, round as a borrowed sky, that has no visible inlet. It simply appears there, sweating from the stone's own cold, and stays, and very slowly, over a season, over a year, creeps higher by a hair.

The ground itself is a shallow sheet of the same water, rarely more than a finger deep, running never fast enough to be a current. When you step, it parts without a splash and closes behind you. The water does not muddy. It smells of wet rock and cold air.

The light is dim and sourceless. There is no sun disc, no shadow to read by. A low ceiling of condensed cloud hangs motionless — mist that is not drifting so much as resident — scattering what little light there is into a flat, pearl-grey wash. Colours are muted to their minerals: black, iron rust, bone white where a column has split and exposed its pale interior, the grey of the water. Your own breath clouds briefly. The stone under your hands is cool, and stays cool, even where it has been out of the water longest.

The sound is the columns. Wind, when it comes, finds the tubes and is amplified and refined by them. The dripping water strikes inside hollows and rings. Each pillar has its own tone, governed by how far its internal water has risen, and as that water rises — a millimetre in a month, a centimetre in a decade — the whole field is slowly tuning itself, downwards. You could stand here for a day and hear the same pattern repeat without variation, and return in a year and find the pattern a fraction lower, a fraction fuller. Nothing hurries. A pool that drops loses its level so gradually you would swear it is constant. A fractured slab settles for centuries before it cracks again. The place changes, but on a timescale that makes a human stay feel like a single held breath.

There are marks that are not quite natural.

Scattered very thinly among the columns — one here, then nothing for a long stretch, then one again — are small built things. A ring of upright slabs, waist high, incomplete, its interior clean of silt. A low wall of stacked stone that runs twenty steps in a straight line and stops, for no reason you can see. A single squared block set in the middle of a pool, a little taller than the others, its top pocked with shallow, repeated marks. They are made of the same stone as the land, without mortar, without tool-gleam, without iron, without any artifact left beside them. No path leads to them. No wire, no light, no plastic, no engine-sound anywhere in this wet roar. They look less like ruins than like someone began something and, slowly, lost interest — or like a species with no tools thought once, loosely, in stone, and did not think it again.

Life, other than stone and water, is rare to the point of rumor.

Once, in a deeper pool between two columns, you see a movement that is not water. A pale, translucent ribbon uncurls from the shadowed mouth of a hollow tube, no thicker than a finger, blind and eyeless, and hangs in the water, filtering. It stays perhaps a minute. It withdraws. In another pool, a few paces on, there are faint, overlapping rings expanding with no drip to cause them, as if something large and soft has turned over just beneath the surface and decided not to come up. That is all. Most of the hollow pillars are empty. Most of the pools hold nothing but their own reflection of the grey ceiling. The fauna, if it can be called that, lives inside the stone's water, and only rarely shows itself at the mouth.

And there is the strangeness that accumulates the longer you stay still enough to notice it. Some beads of water do not fall. They climb, imperceptibly, a fraction up a fracture face against gravity, then slip and fall with a tick that is louder than it should be. Some pools, when a drip strikes them, do not ripple at once; the ring expands a full second later, as if the water is remembering. On some columns, the pool on top is perfectly still while the air around it is full of falling drips, and your own shadow — such as it is in this dim light — does not fall across it at all. It seems the water is not following a single rule of down. It is following the stone, leading, condensing, seeping, dripping, rising in air as mist and falling again as theft from cold surfaces, a closed, slow loop with no beginning you can point to.

That loop is the country. Low, young, wet, and crowded with hollow stone that sings because it is wet. No plants to soften it, no animals to animate it in any number you could count on your hands, no machines to interrupt the sound. Only a dense mineral forest standing in its own shallow sea under a dim sky, dripping and booming, changing so slowly that the only proof of time is how much deeper the note has gone since you first heard it.
