It is not a range, or a valley, or a forest. It is one room at the top of the world.

The peak called by lowland maps Shaelir has been pushed up so recently that the mountain has not finished becoming a mountain. Its uppermost needle is fresh fracture — black obsidian and splintered pale limestone, quartz veins still bright and unweathered, edges sharp enough to open skin like a broken dish. There is no scree slope worn round by centuries, no soil, no stained cliffs. Frost has cracked the rock, but it has not softened it. Scree here is still cubes. Faces still show the pale, clean interior where they split.

You would not find the Hollow from below. Just below the true summit, on the shadowed side, a slit no wider than a shoulder drops into a fractured dome. The dome is maybe forty paces across, cathedral-high in the middle, narrowing to crevices and pockets all around. You can touch every wall without moving from the center. That is the whole of it: a bounded, high, stone room, cool even in summer, the air thin when you come in from the wind.

Below you, far below, is a sea of cloud. The wind outside roars over the needle. Inside, the air is still and damp and almost lightless. What little light comes through the slit falls in one grey shaft and dies on the floor. The rest is darkness — but not empty darkness.

You hear it first, long before you understand it. A loud, continuous shimmer of sound, too constant to be wind. Clicks, chimes, scratches, low drumming against stone, wings beating in a dense cloud you cannot see. It is not a call and answer. It is a weather. Every surface is making it. In the depths, where the light never reaches, your own breathing seems rude and too loud, and the sound does not stop for you.

Then, as your eyes learn the dark, the dark begins to resolve into presences.

The ceiling is not stone. Or rather, stone is hardly visible under what hangs from it — thousands, perhaps tens of thousands, of furred clingers, bat-shaped but wingless, dangling by long, jointed fore-limbs from every fracture and ledge. They hang in layered curtains, shoulder to shoulder, body to body, damp fur matted into felt. Each one is the length of a forearm, blind, its face a small pale mask of closed skin where eyes would be. They do not fly. They lick. With long grey tongues they lap at the condensation beading on the rock above them, and the constant vibration of their wings — tiny, useless wings folded flat against their backs — keeps the air trembling and shakes droplets loose to fall.

That falling is the only rain this place knows.

Water here is never a river, never a pool you could sit beside. It is intermittent moisture, a film on stone, beads that gather and run in threads, a dampness in the air that condenses on your sleeves and on their fur. In the hollows of the floor it collects in palm-sized pockets, clear and cold, and vanishes into fur and mouths almost as quickly. Once in a long season a storm finds the slit and throws snow and blown rain straight down the shaft, and the whole hanging curtain flinches and goes silent for a heart-beat, and then resumes.

The floor is not stone either, at first glance. It looks like pale, breathing gravel: a near-touching carpet of slow crawlers, each as big as a forearm, segmented, blind, the color of wet bone. They move a hand's breadth in an hour, perhaps less. They orient by tapping. Every one of them carries one thing.

That is the level of making here. There is nothing built. No nest, no path, no fire-mark, no woven structure of any kind. The crawlers — and the clingers, too — use the pockets the fracture gave them, and nothing else. But a crawler grips in its front pair of stubby limbs a single angular hammerstone, fresh-broken, heavier than its head. Over a lifetime, it knocks. It knocks at the guano crust is thick, at the fungal mat, at the stone where moisture hides in a pore. The sound of that knocking is part of the shimmer, a slower beat underneath the chimes. They are learning, in the most primitive way, to open the world where it will not open itself. The stone is inherited in the sense that those who drop it die in place and another finds it, but nothing is passed on, nothing is saved.

Between floor and ceiling, the air itself is layered. The smallest inhabitants — chime-midges, no bigger than a thumbnail — fill the middle volume so densely that in the shaft of light they are visible as moving dust, and in the dark they are a solid, humming body. They do not bite. They gather spores and lick the same condensation, and they are eaten, in endless patience, by the clingers above and by the crawlers below when they fall.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought, which is how you know the animals rule here. Where the light shaft strikes, there are a few strands of pale moss clinging in the driplines, and three or four stunted ferns with leaves like bleached paper, never quite opening. Lower down, the real green is replaced by fungi — thin, white, leathery mats that grow on the guano and waste and on the bodies of the dead, and are eaten again. It is a closed cycle, almost hermetically so. Nothing leaves. Nothing arrives except the occasional insect blown up from the cloud-sea, and the rain, and the slow, slow rising of the mountain itself, which is why the rock stays sharp. The world grows upward faster than it can weather.

Everything is slow except the sound. A clinger is born on the ceiling, licks, chimes, and dies there, its young taking its place on the same ledge, over a life that may last a hundred years. A crawler may not move a body length in that time. A stalactite grows the width of a hair in a decade. If you stay for days — and the cold will make your fingers numb enough that you won't want to — you will see no change at all, except the chorus rising and falling like a tide, very gradually, over hours. The loudness is constant, but it breathes.

The rules emerge from that. Sight has been traded, entirely, for hearing and touch and taste. Eyes are not closed; they simply never formed, and the skin of the face is smooth and unbroken. Sound, here, has work to do: it is how they find the driplines, how they find each other in a crowd where no stone is visible, how they know where the hammerstone has struck through. The chime-midges' wingbeat is so steady that condensation forms preferentially where it is loudest — the animal's noise literally calling water out of the cool air to the sound of it. Moist fur, moist stone, moist breath: nothing in the Hollow is ever fully dry, and nothing is ever wet enough to rot in the ordinary way. The fungi and the crawlers take the dead before decay can begin.

It is strange, even to itself, this assemblage: a fauna so overwhelming that the landscape is mostly animal, packed near-touching in layers — ceiling, air, wall-crust, floor — holding up a world of almost no plants, no structures, no light, at an altitude where the air is too thin for anything large to live and too cold for anything quick. The crawlers' tapping, the clingers' licking, the midges' chime: a primitive craft of stone-on-stone, performed by blind creatures who never see the peak their room crowns, never see the cloud-sea below, never leave the forty paces that are their world.

Leave, and the wind takes your ears again, and the needle's fresh faces glitter in the sun for a moment at the slit — and then, behind you, the shimmer continues, unheard, patient, eating its own condensation, rising a fraction of a hair above itself, year after year, in the dark.
