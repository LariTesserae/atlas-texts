At first there is only the weight of it.

Warm, and pressing from all sides at once, so that you cannot tell which way is down except by the slow ache in your ears. You breathe, and the breath comes back damp and sweet, as if through a cloth that has been left in a bath. Everything is wet, not in the way of being rained on, but in the way of a condition. Water is not in lakes here, or rivers, or the sea beyond a shore. It is the medium the world is held in. It lies in films over stone, it fills the hollows of leaves, it gathers inside the stalks of plants until they are translucent with it, it hangs in the air as an fine, invisible weather so thick that your skin never dries. To be here is to be submerged, whether you are standing, swimming, or walking upright through what seems, for a moment, like air.

And this is the top of things.

That is the strangeness you keep returning to. The ground underfoot rises and falls in long, slow swells, too gentle to be hills, too broad to be anything but a plateau. It goes on, and on, and on, until the mist takes it. This is high ground, thousands of meters above any lowland that water might have come from, a summit that has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The air above the canopy, when you can glimpse it through layers of vapor, is pale and thin and very far away. But the heat comes up from below, from the stone and the peat and the water itself, which holds the warmth of the sun long after the light has been taken out of it. Your own body heat blooms back at you. The plateau is warm like the inside of a closed hand.

The stone is the oldest thing here.

It has been eroded past all change. Every edge has a radius. Every groove, every ancient riverbed cut into the plateau before water learned to stay, has been softened until you could press your thumb into the lip of it and feel no beginning or end to the wearing. The rock is grey-green and dense and tender, like soapstone, like slate that has spent a million years being soothed. It does not crumble. It does not sharpen. In places it is polished by nothing but pressure and time, so smooth your hand slides over it and finds, faintly, shapes that have almost vanished — a spiral worn to a rumor, a waterline made by water that has not been at that height for an age. New sediment settles into the old hollows as fast as the old sediment dissolves. Nothing sharpens. Nothing breaks. The plateau has reached its final form and stayed there, primordial and settled, older than the water that now covers it, and the water, in turn, looks older than the stone.

You move through it more by touch than sight.

The light never really arrives. It is dim at all hours, a perpetual green-gold twilight filtered through water and mist and a canopy that is not a canopy in the usual sense. Above you, where the water surface should be, there is a ceiling of drifting gardens — mats of entangled rhizomes and floating leaves, peat-like islands lashed together by roots, turning almost imperceptibly in slow currents. Their undersides are dark. Their tops catch what light there is and pass it down in patches, so that bright places here are not shallow and dark places are not deep. You might step down into a pool of perfect clarity that is in fact many meters deep, the bottom lit by a bloom of algae, and then step up onto a ridge that lies in complete shadow under a single fallen frond.

For the vegetation does not grow in trees. It grows in layers, touching, overlapping, near-touching, a density that fills every cubic meter of air and water with presence. The first layer is at your feet: moss like wet felt over the stone, soft sponge-peat that sighs under your weight and releases a slow bubble into the water, tiny star-shaped rosettes, hair-fine grasses that comb themselves flat in the current. The second layer is at your waist and chest: broad ribbed leaves, fronds as long as your arm, stalks hollow and filled with water that sloshes faintly when you brush past. The third layer is overhead, an interlacing of uparching stems and hanging roots, so close that to walk you must part it, and it closes behind you without a sound. Everywhere the hanging tips drip. Everywhere the water is full of what the plants shed — spores, pollen, fragments of leaf, seeds no bigger than dust — so that the water itself seems alive with suspended green, thousands of presences in the space of a hand, drifting, settling, beginning again.

It is lush in the way of a place that has not been interrupted. Nothing here is wasted. A leaf falls and is already half-decayed where it lands, already food for the layer beneath it, already sweet with fermentation. A root pushes into soft stone and finds not resistance but a groove made for it eons ago, and follows it, and thickens, and becomes a pillar. Flowers open in pulses, whole patches at once, for reasons of pressure or warmth or something internal to the plateau, and their color is muted — amber, white, a bruised violet — because the light cannot afford anything brighter. There is no soil in the usual sense, only peat and felted root and a black, warm mud that holds the imprint of a foot for a long time and then forgets it, slowly.

The silence is not empty. It is pressured.

Water takes sound and bends it. Your own pulse is the loudest thing, a slow knocking that the plateau seems to answer, minutes later, from under the peat. Now and then a bubble leaves the mud and rises — or, sometimes, does not rise. A drop forms on the underside of a leaf and either falls, as expected, or detaches and climbs a little way into the air before remembering gravity and falling after all. There is a faint ticking from the plants as they take in water, a creak when a stalk, swollen with gas, shifts its weight. Once in a long while you hear something that might have been a call, but it is so muffled by layers of water and vegetation that it could equally be the groan of a root under strain, or a current moving through a hollow stem far away. The world is doing an enormous amount of work and making almost no noise to do it with.

For it *is* working. It is active in every direction. Currents comb the algae on the rocks straight, then, hours later, comb them straight the other way. The water level — if there is a level — rises and falls by a hair over the course of a day, enough for lily-like leaves to lift and settle, enough for wet stone to show a darker band and then lose it. Over the plateau, vast as it is, weather makes its own slow circuit: a cloud shadow passes even under the water, a heavy rain dimples the surface far above your head and for a moment the light fractures into moving nets. Warmth pulses up from the peat in invisible plumes. Gases are made and remade. The floating gardens turn, very slowly, like the hands of a clock that no one is watching. Growth, decay, deposition, dissolution — predator and prey are not the terms here, for there are almost no predators, and almost no prey that move. Instead the plants feed on each other, parasitise each other, shade each other out and then open a gap by dying in perfect unison, so that light falls through all the layers at once and a whole undergrowth ignites into leaf. It is an ecosystem in constant, silent motion, but the motion is the motion of a thought, not of an animal.

There are animals, but they are rare enough that to see one feels like a trick of the light.

In the course of a long walk — and you do walk, for days, guided only by the changing pressure in your ears that tells you the plateau is swelling up or down — you may see, once, a shape suspended in a clear pocket of water below you, long and unhurried, with the frilled, translucent fins of something that has never needed to hurry. Or you may see eyes, two small dark beads opening in a cluster of moss and closing again. Most animal life here, if it can be called that, has surrendered its separateness and become plant-like: slow, sessile, photosynthetic at the edges, filtering the green soup of the water through a fan that opens and closes for hours at a time. The great energy of this place is not in hunting. It is in making and unmaking leaves.

And there are built things, scattered so sparsely you begin to doubt them.

Once, perhaps in a day, you come to a place where the density thins. Seven slabs stand upright in a circle, half-buried in peat, smoothed by water exactly as all other stone is smoothed, but placed — undeniably placed — in a ring. There is a lintel stone lying across two of them, its underside worn by a passage of water, or of bodies, that has long since stopped. A little further on, a platform of woven roots, too regular, too flat, to be only a mat. A doorway-shaped hollow in a boulder, water running through it. These things are not made of anything other than stone and root. There is no metal, no glass, no tool-mark that is not also a natural groove, no sign of the hand that might have set them. They are faint marks, partial remains, artifacts that have been in the process of becoming indistinguishable from the plateau for so long that the distinction is now philosophical. Nothing here was made by anything that needed tools. Everything is made out of itself.

Reality, at this altitude of water and age, does not hold its shape firmly.

You learn its rules only by losing them. Water flows, on certain mornings, uphill along the old erosion grooves, thin streams running in reverse, carrying spores with them to places they could never have reached by falling. Roots grow, not only down or out, but toward sounds that are not there, toward the echo of their own pressure. You walk in a straight line through the dense layering for what feels like hours, and the vegetation gives way to the same clearing you left, except that the slabs in the circle are a little more buried, or a little less, and the light has gone amber where it was green. You cannot be sure you have looped. The plateau is so vast, and so self-similar, that sameness itself folds space. The horizon is always a misted waterline, the ceiling is always a drifting garden, so there is nothing to triangulate except the slow swell of the ground under your feet, and even that can be lying, for the stone remembers being a mountain and sometimes the water remembers being air.

Nothing new is young here. That is perhaps the central law. A fresh frond unfurls already softened at the edge, as if it has been thinking about falling for a thousand years. A rock freshly exposed by a shifting root is already polished, already green with algae, already ancient. The erosion finished its work in the deep past, and what has replaced it is not change but the endless, quiet repetition of the final state — as if the world tried every other possibility, wore itself out, and now simply continues.

By evening — though evening here is only a dimming of the dim, a thickening of the gold — you are neither dry nor drowned, neither on the surface nor beneath it. The mist above the water has closed in to within arm's reach. The leaves brush you with a touch that is almost, but not quite, a hand. Somewhere below, a bubble rises, or does not. Somewhere far off, a current turns the floating gardens another degree. The plateau breathes in and out, warm and wet and immense, waiting for nothing, having arrived at everything it will ever be, and going on, in silence, doing it all again.
