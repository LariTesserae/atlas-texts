You come up through the cloud and the mountain stops.

There is no long ridge, no traverse of snow or rock before it. One moment you are in wet white, hearing water somewhere below you, and the next the slope breaks — sharply, almost vertically — onto a flat shelf no wider than a hamlet. It is small enough that from the middle you can see the edge in every direction. The whole place is a single room of air perched at extreme altitude, perhaps four or five hundred metres across, rimmed by cliffs that fall away into cloud. It feels abruptly made, and it has not had time to soften.

The rock is young. It is black basalt and fresh grey tephra, unweathered, with edges that would still cut a boot. There are no rounded valleys, no old scree slopes smoothed by ten thousand rains. Every crack is new and angular. The cliff faces are faceted like a broken dish, and rain — and there is always rain in some form — runs off them in new channels that have not yet deepened. If a gully exists, it exists for the first time this season. The ground underfoot is warm. Not sun-warm. Deep warm, from below. You can feel it through the soles in the morning, in the seeps where thin sheets of water cross the stone. This is a summit that has recently punched up, perhaps still rising by imperceptible increments, and it has brought some of its interior heat with it.

That warmth changes everything about the altitude. At the equator, under a diffused but ordinary daylight, the thin air does not bite as you expect. You sweat in a fleece. Your ears pop coming up through the cloud sea, your breath is short on the slightest climb, water boils at a temperature that would leave it tepid below, but the air itself stays mild and saturated. Light is normal, flat and saturated, no high-altitude glare. The cloud below filters it. Colors are wet and deep: green, rust, black stone, copper.

Water is not a feature here. It is a condition.

It does not gather. There is no lake, no pool that lasts. The shelf tilts outward in every direction at once, so water, once it arrives, leaves. It arrives constantly — as fine rain that is really condensing air, as mist driven over the rim, as sheets that weep from the rock faces even on days when the sky is clear. The basalt sweats. Moss mats, thick as mattresses, hold it and release it slowly. Rivulets start at your feet and grow as they cross the flat, becoming ankle-deep sheets over smooth stone, then find a crack and plunge. On the rim there are dozens of small waterfalls hanging over the edge, each with its own thin pattern, roaring faintly into the cloud below where you never hear them land.

Everything is film-wet. The inside of a shed drips. Condensation runs down the galvanized iron in vertical beads and does not dry. Your jacket never dries. Puddles form in the leaf litter and in concrete that still bears sharp formwork marks, but they are always draining toward an edge. The rules are simple: water comes from the air, touches a surface, and runs off the world.

And life, for that reason, is absurdly lush.

The vegetation has colonized the new rock in the few slow seasons since it emerged, and it has done so with a density that crowds out everything else. Cloud forest, but without any altitude of its own to temper it — broad-leaved shrubs, tree ferns taller than a man, bamboo clumps, moss that climbs the rocks and then climbs the structures, orchids and bromeliads fastened not to trees but to pipes, cables, the sides of tanks, the slats of a conveyor. Where there is soil at all it is thin, black, and held together by roots. Where there is no soil, plants live in the wet film itself. Vines have crossed a path and sealed it. Ferns grow out of rusted gutters and from the joints of a concrete wall. The green is layered, near-touching, high-saturation — three or four kinds of leaf over every patch of stone.

The industrial presence is therefore not separate from the green. It is tangled in it, and dense where it exists at all.

There are only a handful of built things on the whole shelf, scattered across the wet flat like artifacts: a pump house with its door rusted shut, a weather mast thrumming in the wind, a row of cylindrical tanks, two low sheds with corrugated roofs that sag under moss, a concrete plinth for a generator, a hut where someone must sleep. But on the one true terrace — the only place flat and sheltered enough — they are packed shoulder to shoulder. Pipes run overhead on brackets, then dive into the ground, then surface again. Cables sag between poles and disappear into fern. A belt conveyor, driven by an electric motor, crawls through a shed and out the other side, carrying crushed green matter very slowly. There are vats, presses, a small turbine set into a culvert where a rivulet is tightened to a channel, humming transformers, a fence that ticks with electricity. It is a small processing plant, stranded at the top to work what grows here and to catch the water that falls here, and it operates at the pace of the altitude: laborious, thin-breathed, patient.

It is loud, and it has always been loud, but not frantic.

The sound is first the water — a constant roar and percussion of sheets over basalt, more like weather than a stream. Over it, the generator drones in a low, electrified hum that you feel in your teeth. Metal ticks as it warms and cools. The conveyor gives a slow, grinding clank, then a long pause, then another. Dripping is everywhere, out of rhythm, from eaves, from rock, from pipes. Wind over the rim adds a steady rush that makes conversation a shouted thing even between two people standing close. And yet all the motion is slow. The belt inches. The turbine turns but does not hurry. The mist arrives from below in the afternoon and erases the edge, then recedes again. A worker moves like someone carrying water uphill in a dream. Nothing rushes because nothing can; the air is thin, the supply of cut vegetation arrives in small loads, the mountain is still deciding its shape.

Fauna is rare to the point of being a rumor.

You will not hear birdsong. You will not see mammals, tracks, or disturbed leaf litter. If you lift a wet stone, you will find stone. The plants and the machines are the inhabitants. Once, perhaps twice in an hour, a single cloud hummingbird will dart through the terrace, impossibly bright, and be gone over the edge. Once a high hawk circles far below the rim, in the cloud sea, and does not come up. Insects are present only as a faint presence in the air when the sun breaks through. The absence makes the lushness feel more deliberate, as if the green and the industry have arranged themselves between them.

There are small unusual things, the kind that accumulate when a place is new. Lichen forms on the tanks in branching patterns that look like wiring diagrams. Mushrooms grow in the oil that has leaked onto concrete and never washed away. The condensation on the inside of the shed glass runs in slow, branching rivers for hours after the mist has gone. The water that weeps from a fresh cliff face is warm to the touch. Nothing supernatural — just a young, wet, high place that has not yet settled into familiar habits.

By evening the cloud returns. It does not fall; it rises from below and fills the bowl to the rim, and the shelf becomes again an island with wet stone, dripping iron, dense green pressing against every surface, the slow clank of the press, and water leaving in all directions over sharp, new edges. You can walk the whole boundary in twenty minutes. You will still be wet, still warm, still out of breath, and the place will still be making itself.
