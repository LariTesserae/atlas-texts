It is not a place you reach. It is a place that is *inside* another place.

High up — two, three thousand metres up on a bare volcanic plateau where the wind is thin and dry and the daylight is hard — the mountain is still warm a long way below the surface. In the cooling rock there is a pocket, a gas-vesicle trapped as the lava stiffened, no larger than a small room. From the outside it does not exist. There is no mouth, no cave entrance, no crack a hand could fit through. The only traffic in or out is air, and animals no bigger than the crack between two stones.

Inside, the pocket is not empty.

### Room, terrain, heat

The chamber is roughly almond-shaped, perhaps fifteen metres long, eight across, and low enough that in places you would have to stoop. The ceiling is not a ceiling so much as the underside of a jumble of basalt slabs locked together, sharp and fresh, interlocking like broken crockery. The floor is the same material, tilted very slightly toward the hottest seam. The walls are near-vertical, bowed, raw.

Nothing here has been smoothed. Erosion, in the usual sense, has barely begun. The crystal faces are unweathered. Edges still cut. Dark glassy shards lie where they fell when a slab first split, and nothing has yet washed them away, because there is no water to wash anything. No rain gets in. No drip, no seep, no puddle, no mud. The only dampness in the whole system is locked inside minerals, released in microscopically small amounts when rock is chewed and heated. The air is hot — well over fifty degrees at floor level, hotter still in the fissure at the eastern end where the mountain's residual heat leaks in — and it is dry in the way an oven is dry. Dust hangs instead of humidity: iron-grey, faintly yellow with powdered sulfur, magnetic in patches, coating teeth and shell and lung.

The light is almost none. A little comes from that fissure — a dull, cherry-red glow in the deepest cracks, no brighter than embers under ash — and a little from the animals themselves, a faint, phosphorescent skin-glow along the seams of their shells and, more useful, the red eyeshine that moves across every surface. Beyond two metres, the pocket is only suggested by sound and by heat on the skin.

And it is hot in a way that makes stillness impossible to maintain for long. Different parts of the room hold different temperatures quite stably: the seam is the furnace, the floor is warm, the ceiling a few degrees cooler. The animals arrange themselves by that gradient and, being patient, stay arranged.

### The noise of a factory with no factory

You would hear it long before you could see anything sensible. The pocket is loud, persistently, mechanically loud. Not chorusing, not wind, not water — all of which are absent — but the noise of work.

A low grinding, stone on stone, hundreds of sources at once. A rhythmic clink-clink-clink, like rivets or loose bolts in a tray. Bellows wheezing as segmented abdomens expand and contract. A high, occasional squeal as a polished shell slips against another. A soft, constant shushing, as if a very large brush were being dragged slowly across a very large floor.

It holds at roughly the volume of a working mill, and it does not really stop. It eases and thickens in slow waves — one zone of the chamber grinding hard while another rests — but over the course of an hour, or a day, the impression is of unbroken industry. There are no built things in here to make this noise. No engines, no tools, no conveyors of human making. The machinery is alive.

### Density

The most disorienting thing about the pocket is that there is nowhere to put yourself that is not already occupied.

Every available surface — floor, wall, slab, pillar, ceiling, and the backs of other animals — is covered, shingle-like, edge to edge. The density is close to total. An animal cannot fall without landing on another animal. It cannot turn without nudging its neighbour. When a layer on the floor shifts, the layer above it shifts a moment later, like a crowd on a slow train.

At a glance the chamber looks like textured stone. Look longer and the texture breathes. Look longer still and a patch of “rock” on the far wall crawls a centimetre, then stills, then crawls again. The wall is mostly beetles. The floor is mostly beetles, and the beetles standing on the beetles on the floor.

There is no empty ground to excavate, no untouched corner, no place where the landscape is just mineral. The mineral is the animal’s workplace, its food, and its substrate, all at once.

### The inhabitants

The dominant residents are the bellows beetles: thumb-sized, steel-grey to black, low and oval, with heavily ribbed abdomens that visibly expand like little forge bellows. Their mandibles are serrated and iron-hard. They do not eat plants — there are none to eat — and they do not drink. They stand on the rock and scrape it, grain by grain, ingest the powder, and pass it through a gut that runs hotter than the air around them. What is useful — metals, sulfides, bound compounds — is absorbed. What is not is excreted as dense, dark slag pellets, which immediately become the grit that other beetles grind in turn.

They work in lanes and in shifts. A beetle will scrape for a long time in one spot, anchored among its neighbours, head down, abdomen pumping. Then it will stop, back up a few millimetres, and a different beetle will take its place, as if the job were being handed over. The effect over the whole floor is something like a slow loom, or a small factory floor that runs twenty-four hours without anyone clocking in.

Around and among them move the clinker-striders: thinner, longer-legged,  almost stick-like, with shells that sound like struck tin when they touch. They do not grind much. They patrol, clattering, constantly repositioning the pellets and the dust, nudging dead shells, testing the temperature of a new crack with their antennae, keeping the upper layers from stagnating. Their movement is what makes the whole packed surface seem to shimmer.

And beneath both, and over both, are the smallest and most numerous: mites no bigger than pinheads, fast by the standards of the place, living in the seams between shells, cleaning — if that is the word — the films of mineral oil and metabolic waste from the larger animals’ joints. They are the only part of the ecology that could be mistaken for something delicate.

There are predators, of a slow, patient kind. A broad, many-legged grub works the cooler end of the chamber, half-buried in pellet-drift, taking mostly mites and the occasional weakened beetle. Its presence is registered mainly by the way the striders give it a wide, clattering berth. No one here is fast. No one here needs to be.

This is not a bizarre ecology. It is a familiar one, translated: grazers eating their substrate, scavengers cleaning up, a low predator taking the sick and the slow, everything bound by heat and dryness and the rock itself. The weirdness is only in how completely the translation has been made. These are industrial animals in an inorganic world, and industry is not something they use. It is something they are.

### Rules of this world

There is no water cycle. The cycle is mineral: rock to animal to pellet to dust to rock again, warmed all the way through by the mountain’s stored heat. Nothing rots in the wet sense. Dead shells dry, split, and are ground down and eaten in their turn. Nothing grows up from the ground. The only “growth” is the slow addition of animal mass, and the slow subtraction of the chamber wall, which the beetles are, millimetre by millimetre, turning into themselves.

Because there is no built shelter, the animals make shelter by crowding. Because there is no wind, air moves only as the bellows bodies move it, pushing heated air from the seam toward the cooler ceiling and back again through the interstices of the crowd. Because there is no light, almost no one has eyes that are good for much except detecting the red glow and the shine of a neighbour’s shell. Because everything depends on the mountain’s heat, everything waits for the mountain’s rhythms.

And those rhythms are slow. That is the second disorientation, after the density. For all the noise, almost nothing has changed since the pocket formed. The crack at the eastern end is opening by fractions of a millimetre a decade. The pellet ridges in the central trench are building at the rate of a fingernail’s growth. A beetle’s lifetime of scraping will remove perhaps a gram of stone. The chamber itself is still emerging — still cooling, still settling, still deciding its final shape. It is a landscape in its first draft, raw and un-eroded, and it will remain an early draft for a very long time.

If you stayed — if you could stay, dry and hot and deafened, packed in among them — you would learn its small weather: the seam brightening a fraction as outside pressure falls with a distant storm on the plateau, the grinding thickening in the warmest hour, the eyeshine shifting as a wave of striders crossed the ceiling. But the arrangement of the room would be recognisably the same tomorrow, and next year.

Overhead, the plateau goes on being cold and bright and empty. Under it, in a room no map will ever show, something warm and dark and crowded works steadily at turning stone into life, and life back into stone, loudly, slowly, and without ever having built a thing.
