It is not a mountain, though it is high. From below it reads only as a tableland — a vast, flat-shouldered lifting in the world, its rim worn soft as an old coin. There are no peaks, no fresh cliffs. Everything that could be eroded has been eroded. The stone is dense, iron-stained, cream and rust and dove-grey, polished by sun on the swells and pitted on the hollows into a honeycomb of shallow cups and tunnels. No edge is sharp. No scree lies fresh. The whole place has the patience of something that has finished changing and has settled, for a very long time, into the form it will keep.

You understand the height only when you are on it. The air is thin in the lungs and warm on the skin at once — a warm, high light, clear and ordinary, not the glare of a desert nor the dimness of a forest, but a bright, dust-free noon with short honest shadows. The ground holds that warmth. Long after the sun has moved, the stone under your hand is warm, especially in the pits, where heat gathers and stays. Below you, far off, the lower country is a blue haze you can see for miles in every direction. The plateau extends past sight, interrupted by shallow basins, low domes, and long, dry swales that are more like wrinkles than valleys. It is open in scale, regional, many-featured — but the features differ from each other by degrees, not by kind. A step here, a pan there, a field of more closely pitted stone. Distances look near because the light is so clean, and feel immense because there is nothing tall to measure them against.

Rain is a rumor here. It comes, occasionally, as warm drops that strike stone and disappear almost at once, swallowed by the pores. There are no rivers. No streams run, no mud, no wet earth. The damp lives elsewhere: inside. At night the warm rock radiates its heat away and the air that has been in contact with it cools just enough to sweat. By dawn the stone is faintly tacky. Condensation collects in the deep cups, in the cracks, on the undersides of overhangs the size of a hand. It forms small, perfect pockets — a thumbnail of water in a stone basin, held there for weeks, reflecting the sky. A low mist forms too, but not over a valley. It forms at ankle height, in the hollows, and disperses when the sun touches it. Your boots get damp not from puddles but from pressing into stone that is damp an inch below its dry skin. After the rare rain, the smell is mineral and warm-baked: wet stone, a little iron, a little living dust.

Because there is almost no soil — erosion took it long ago and left only stone and stone-dust — true vegetation is sparse and has the character of things that survive by being unnoticed. A lichen rim around a water cup. A rosette of grey-green succulent leaves tucked into a seam, so low it looks like part of the rock's pattern. A clump of wiry, thread-thin stems that flowers once in several years and then resumes doing almost nothing. No grass, no trees, no green dominance. Most of the ground, at first glance, is bare.

That is the misdirection. The ground is not bare. It is covered.

Kneel, and the texture resolves slowly, the way the place does everything. The bare stone is a living crust, many presences to a handspan, near-touching, layered in places three or four deep. Flattened grazers the size of a coin move across the surface, fringed at the edges, scraping the biofilm from the stone with toothless mouths. They advance perhaps a body-length in a day. Where they have passed, the stone is cleaner, slightly paler. In the pits, other things live head-down in the pores, only their domed backs showing, overlapping like shingles. Translucent things hang beneath ledges, their slow heartbeats visible through amber shells. Blind, thread-like things move inside the rock itself, in channels that run for meters under the surface, emerging only as a wet gleam. All of them are silent. They have no lungs to voice with, no wings to beat the air. They breathe through skin and shell, they taste the air chemically, they signal by touch and by slow trails of scent left on stone. 

The fauna is overwhelming not in noise or speed, but in saturation. There is no sterile patch for long. A cleared place will be recolonized not by a rush but by an accretion, neighbor by neighbor. The large ones — barrel-bodied, armored crawlers no higher than a knee, moving a few meters in a week, their skin damp to the touch — are only the visible tip. Beneath and between them are millions of the small, and beneath them more. The plateau at dawn, when the pores exhale, seems to breathe: a vapor rises from thousands of pits at once, faint as breath on glass, and in the evening it draws back in. What looks like stone from a distance is, up close, a body crowded with its own inhabitants.

This is also how the erosion continues. It is no longer weather alone. The living things open pores, widen them with their bodies, deposit mineral as they feed, seal them again with their shells, and break the stone down from inside grain by patient grain. The plants depend on these trails — their roots follow the old paths of grazers, where dust has collected. The fauna depend on the condensation the stone holds. The water pockets depend on the fauna not drinking them dry, and they do not; they absorb, they sip, they mostly live by the dampness in the air of the pores. Nothing is wasted for long. When one dies in place, its shell becomes a shelter, then a mineral, then part of the path.

The slowness is the rule. A season is a long pause. Things grow, but by accretion, not by leap. A grazer will feed, seal itself, wait through a dry spell that lasts longer than you can imagine, feed again. Births happen where the parents stand; the young live on top of the old, or just beside them, and the layer grows thicker rather than wider. There are no migrations, no chases, no sudden emergences after rain. Even the scattered structures fit this tempo.

Now and then, in a field of stone, you come on a mark that is not quite natural and not quite made. A low arc of larger pebbles, half-buried. A disc of cleared crust, smoother than the ground around it, perhaps a hundred years in the making. A small cone of pellets, a cairn of old shells, a single ring of stacked slabs no higher than your ankle, collapsed on one side. Nothing cut cleanly, nothing with a tool's edge, no smoke, no track, no device. They are the faint artifacts of the place itself — accumulations worked by claws and bodies over durations that turn a midden into a monument, and then, by erosion, back into stone again. They are sparse. You can walk for a long time and see none, and then one, and then none again.

The light stays normal through it all. The colors stay muted — ochre, ash, translucent amber, the dull green of the few plants — because everything has agreed, over an immense time, to match the stone that holds it. The silence is not empty. It is a thick silence. Your own footsteps sound too loud on the crust, too abrupt. The stone ticks faintly as it cools in the evening. Somewhere, very rarely, a shell clicks shut. Otherwise, the most densely inhabited ground you have ever stood on makes almost no sound at all.

It feels, standing there, warm and high and damp in the pores under your feet, that you have come upon a community at the end of its long experiment. Nothing invades it, because nothing is empty. Nothing hurries, because nothing needs to. The plateau has been ancient rock, then ancient waterless rock, then rock colonized so thoroughly that the rock and the life are no longer separate things to name. They touch each other everywhere, and so the whole place holds together — not in balance, exactly, but in a settled, primordial stillness that will go on, grain by grain, for longer than any of its inhabitants can measure.
