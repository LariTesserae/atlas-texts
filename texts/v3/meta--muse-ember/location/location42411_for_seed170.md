**The High Foundry**

The first thing is not stone, but heat coming through your soles. You stand on a pavement of black basalt and baked red shale, broken into plates by old cracks, each plate iron-hard and faintly warm to the eye long before you touch it. Between the plates is a fine ochre dust that does not blow so much as creep — wind pushes it in low sheets, a hand high, and lets it settle again exactly as it was. Everything is dry in a way that has nothing to do with thirst. No dew, no damp in the hollows, no wet smell after the brief coldest part of night. Moisture exists only locked away: in the crystal lattices of white salts that bloom in the cracks, in gypsum that crunches underfoot, in the vapour the land itself exhales at rare fissures and immediately loses.

Above you is ordinary daylight. A plain white-yellow sun, a pale blue that bleaches quickly to haze, shadows falling straight and hard and true to colour. That is the strangest mercy here, because almost nothing else behaves plainly. The air is thin — you are high, on a plateau that lifts a full mountain-range above any lowland that might have had weather — and thin air lets the sun through undiluted and then lets the stone give it all back. Rock in shade is still hot enough to flinch from. Rock in sun is hot enough to tick, expand, and tick again, a slow percussion as the whole surface breathes in and out with temperature.

Nothing grows. Not grass, not scrub, not a green thing of any kind. The pavement is occasionally spotted with a dull amber-black film, no thicker than paint, that spreads across stone and salt alike without root or leaf. It is not a plant. It eats light and iron and will not be hurried. Where it runs, the stone is faintly magnetic to a compass and the dust gathers into a fine, clinkery grit, as if something has already smelted it once and left the slag to weather. There is no food web built on leaves. There is a food web built on heat.

The land itself is old in its bones. This is not a young, raw country still being carved. Every slope has found the angle it can live with and stayed there. Gullies are choked with their own debris. Cliffs are not sharp; they have been softened, stepped, and armoured with a lag of gravels too heavy for the wind to move. Mesas stand in tiers, their caprocks polished on the windward side, their flanks aproned with talus that has not shifted in centuries. If you watched for a day — and you could, for almost nothing visibly changes in a day — you would see only dust creep, a salt crystal lengthen by a fraction, and heat-shimmer rise off the black plates in wavering sheets. The great processes run on a patience measured in decades. A rock falls, once. A salt pan cracks, and the crack spends a year closing.

And because it is high and open, it goes on further than you can take in. Plateau after plateau lifts to the horizon in the same reds and blacks, each one a little paler with distance, each valley a dry basin of the same baked mineral. You can see for a hundred kilometres, flatly, clearly, in ordinary light — and the distance lies. A ridge that should be a day's walk does not get nearer at a walker's rate. A sound from it arrives, full and clear, before you have convinced yourself it is moving. The horizon does not so much recede as repeat itself, stratum for stratum, like the same page printed over and over with slight variations.

At any given moment it looks empty. That is honest. You could cross a valley and see no creature, no track, no green, no sign of habitation at all. There are no structures. No road, no wall, no shard of glass, no bone that stays a bone. The dust takes footprints and films them over, slowly, in its own time. It is a wilderness untouched by making.

It is also, underneath all that, loud and industrial and alive to the point of overwhelming.

You hear it before you understand it. A low roar that is not wind — wind rides on top of it, separate. It is the sound of something vast working. A bellows sigh that takes a minute to complete. A grinding, far off, like a mill the size of a mountain turning over once and settling. Crackle, as from high voltage, popping in the dry air even when the sky is clear. Metallic ringing from deep in the stone, felt in the teeth through the soles of your feet. Near certain places the roar rises until your own voice seems small and ridiculous, and still the place does not seem to move. The sound is coming from production, not from movement.

That is the fauna. It does not present as animals in the usual sense, and it does not crowd the view. It is spread out, solitary, continental. The great mesas on the horizon — the ones you have already filed away as geology — some of them breathe. Once in a season, once in a decade, a range lifts its back a hand's breadth and exhales heat and iron-smelling vapour, and the dust on its flank slides in slow, silent avalanches that take hours to finish. Those are the Furnace-bodies. They feed on ironstone and basalt, on the heat of the sun stored in the plateau and the deeper heat rising through it, and they process it like a foundry processes ore: taken in at one end as stone, worked in internal chambers to temperatures you would expect from a kiln, expelled at the other as clinker, fine magnetic grit, and the amber-black film that creeps over the pavement. They are not built. They are not machines. But their metabolism is industrial at a scale no factory could match, and the whole plateau is their floor.

Around and between them is the rest of the overwhelming part, the part you cannot count because it is not countable. The air, which looks empty, carries a constant drift of heat-motes — particles, spores, fungal-simpler propagules — that do the small work of the foundry, carrying charge, seeding the stone-film, stitching the dust into new pavement. It is in your lungs as a taste of hot copper and ozone. It ticks against your skin like static. It is why the emptiness feels full, why the silence, such as it is, roars. Density in the eye, emptiness; density in the ear and the lung, abundance beyond any meadow or reef. The creatures are spaced kilometres apart. Their fields overlap anyway.

This is where the world stops keeping its categories straight, and it does so quietly, without tearing. A hill is a creature that has been still so long that erosion has dressed it as a hill. A creature is a geological process that has learned to breathe. Cause and effect lag behind each other here by minutes: you hear the intake of breath long after the flank has already risen; you feel the heat of a metabolic pulse on your face after the glow deep in a fissure has already gone out. Shadows are honest from the sun, but every stone also lays a second, fainter shadow that belongs to its stored heat, and that shadow moves on its own slow schedule, lingering where the stone no longer is. Walk a straight line and your compass will wander, not wildly, but with intention, as if north has been pulled a few degrees toward the nearest Furnace-body. Walk it again and the salt pan is a little larger, not because it grew in the interval, but because the interval is not a reliable quantity near the vents.

Nothing here rushes. The violence, if you want to call it that, is all in the chemistry and the noise. A living vent can spit a bead of molten sulphur-metal that hisses and sets in seconds — the only fast thing in the country — and then that bead will sit, cooling, for years, being reabsorbed by the film that eats stone. Storms, when they come, are not rain. They are dry, electrical, and slow: the sky darkens to a bruised yellow, the crackling doubles, the dust lifts in a single sheet that blots out the next plateau and then, after a long grey afternoon, settles back in the same order it rose. No water falls. The air simply smells more strongly of the foundry while it happens.

Stand here long enough and the paradoxes stop feeling like paradoxes. A hot place at altitude. A desert with no water cycle at all, whose creatures carry their fluid in molten compounds sealed in their own bodies. A mineral world with no plants, whose life eats rock and light and excretes industry. An empty plain whose every cubic metre of air is busy. A place with no artefact, no building, nothing made — that nonetheless works, day and night, like a plant at full production, because the production *is* the life. The land has been eroded and settled and weathered into its own final form, and its final form turns out to be not a form at all, but a process: slow, loud, hot, and so vast and so patient that to look at it is to be, incomprehensibly, inside something that is breathing.
