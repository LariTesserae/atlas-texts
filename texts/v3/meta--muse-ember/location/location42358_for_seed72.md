The Steamhollow is a town grown inside a wound in the earth that has not yet healed.

You descend into it by lift, ears popping on the way down into pressure and heat that increase with every meter. There is no sky to arrive under. There is a door at the bottom of the shaft, and beyond that door a room — a very large, very low room — of wet stone, cut through by a town.

It is only about two hundred and forty meters from the east wall to the west wall. You can stand in the middle, on the slight mound where the power house sits, and see the whole of it at once: the far wall sweating in the dim light, the ceiling a dark, irregular slab fifteen to twenty meters up, patched with props and cables and the occasional hanging stalactite too young and thin to be pretty. This is not an ancient cavern, polished by time. It is embryonic geology. The rock is still raw, still soft in places, still being made. Fresh cuts are everywhere — a new borehole in the east wall venting a thin hiss of steam, a road cut that exposes banded strata like pages, a rubble pile where the ceiling slumped last year and is still settling, inch by inch. A leak under a pipe has started a new stalagmite on the floor of the laundry, white and pointed, growing fast enough that the workers step around it. Erosion here is not wearing things away so much as it is *beginning*. Sediment comes in thin dunes across the streets and is patiently washed away again. Mineral crusts white-out the steel.

The town exists because of the heat. Temperature is the first rule, and it never relents. The air in the inhabited bowl sits at 38 to 42 degrees, damp and still. Near the vent in the southeast it is hotter, close to 50, and the rock there glows faintly dark red under the dust. The hot brine wells up from below, 45-degree water that fills the lower canals and steams where it meets the cooler walls. Nothing dries. Concrete is always dark with moisture at the foot. Boots squeak and splash. People live lightly here — shorts, thin shirts, sandals on sticky feet — and work in shifts that follow the lamps, not the sun. The sun is a rumor from the surface lift. Heat, by contrast, is free and constant and inescapable, and it dictates everything: where houses can be built, how far a factory can sit from a vent before the workers faint, which streets are used only at night when the pumps are throttled.

Because the world is below the surface, light is rationed and dim. Amber sodium lamps hang eight meters up on poles, their light pooling in warm, orange circles that barely reach the ceiling. Between the pools is wide, soft dark. In empty districts the lamps are switched off entirely to save power, and the dark reclaims them within minutes. There is no dawn, only a change of shift and a flicker when the turbine load shifts. The light is enough to work by, to read by, to keep moths off the food. It is not enough to see the whole town at once, which is good, because the town is sparse.

Density is low despite being urban. This is a settlement of maybe seven hundred people, but they are scattered across the bowl with much emptiness between them. Factories, houses, and storage sheds sit apart, separated by wet, vacant lots where a building was demolished or collapsed and where the floor is now a shallow, warm sheet of water or a mat of algae. Streets are laid out on a grid, named and signed — Low Canal, Turbine Row, The Drying Sheds — but they feel half-finished. Layered construction tells the story: the original stone footings of the drilling camp, then cinderblock work sheds from the first industrial phase, then corrugated lean-tos and foam-insulated additions bolted on later, all of them beaded and streaked with condensation. Scaffolding stands for months. A half-poured foundation waits for concrete to set in air that is too hot and wet for it to ever set properly. Everything is infrastructure — pumps, pipes, conveyors, a narrow electric cart track that loops the bowl, the turbine hall that hums day and night — but it is infrastructure that is always being repaired, always leaking, always crusting over with white mineral scale where the brine brushes it.

Water is moderate, not a flood, but it is inescapable. The rock weeps. In quiet corners you can hear it first: a steady interval of drips from the ceiling, each one landing in a pool or on a tin roof with a clean *tick*. On the west wall, water sheets down in thin, continuous films. Condensation rains, very fine and warm, over the hottest pool in the center-east, a permanent little weather system that never clears because the pool never cools. The main canal runs slow and brown-green along the south side, taking runoff from every street. Lower houses have their thresholds raised on stone steps. At ankle depth in the low lots, the water is warm and slick with biofilm. It smells of wet limestone, of sulfur at the vent, of hot oil and bleach from the laundry, of ammonia from the accumulated guano.

Vegetation is sparse, almost an afterthought, and what there is tells you how little here wants to grow. There are no trees. There are tufts of moss and liverwort where drip water falls regularly and a lamp happens to stay on long enough. There are thin green slicks of algae in the canals under the lights, and pale, stunted ferns in planters outside the clinic that the children water with condensate. The hydroponic room behind the market produces lettuce and tomatoes that are white-green and watery, grown under electric tubes that compete with the street lamps for power. Most food is grown differently — mushrooms in the warm, dark rooms under the turbine hall, fed on waste heat and sawdust, and fish.

The fish are part of why the fauna overwhelms everything else. Weirdness here is low — nothing magical, nothing impossible. This is a normal cave ecology, just on a hot, wet, industrial scale, and it has run unchecked in the dark between the lamps. The animals outnumber the people by thousands to one.

In the canals the water looks milky with fish: translucent, blind, blind-eyed cave fish and shrimp, so dense they move as a single slow current, brushing against your waders. The hatchery that was built to farm them for protein has long since leaked its stock into every connected pool. On the walls and under floor tiles, isopods and pale cave crickets move in rippling mats — if you lift a drain cover, you will see hundreds of white backs shifting. Troglobite moths erupt around any lamp left on, a blizzard of dusty wings that the workers wave away and the bats eat. Up on the ceiling, the bats are a living blanket, perhaps ten thousand of them, their guano feeding the fungus gardens and their chittering rising and falling with the lamps. In the shallow pools at the edge of town, blind salamanders hang motionless in the warm water, gills feathery, waiting for drip-borne insects to fall.

They are not monsters. They are ordinary troglobites, eyeless and pale and patient. But there are so many of them that the sound is constant — not just the industrial drone of pumps and the turbine, loud and unvarying, but the animal layer underneath: the clicking of crickets, the rustle of moths, the soft splash of fish, the squeak of bats when a lamp flickers. The town is loud in the way a forest is loud, with life, not with events.

And it is slow. Dynamic change here is measured in crust and in generations. The canal water moves, but slowly. The stalagmite under the leak will take years to be noticed. The east wall borehole that hissed last month will become a shelf, then a ledge, then a new street corner. The whole bowl is still forming, and the town migrates with it, abandoning a flooded lot here, building a new shed there by the warmth, always in small, patient increments. If you stay a week, nothing seems to change except the lamp oil and the fish levels. If you stay a year, the white mineral lines have climbed higher on the pipes, the salamander pools are a little wider, one house has been quietly lifted two steps.

That is the rule that holds it all together: the dark belongs to the animals, the lit pools belong to people in shifts, the heat belongs to no one but governs everyone, and nothing — not stone, not steel, not the town itself — is ever finished or ever dry.
