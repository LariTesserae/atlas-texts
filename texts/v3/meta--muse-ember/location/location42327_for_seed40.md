High up, the light arrives before anything else does.

It is bright in a way that has no warmth to it. An overexposed, white-blue light that pours over everything and bounces, and bounces again, off pale metal and ceramic and resin. Even in the deepest recesses between structures there is light — not sunlight exactly, but the memory of it, held and re-emitted by the surfaces themselves. The air is thin and cool. Your breath shows for half a second even standing in full glare. The metal under your hand is cold despite the glare, cold the way a thing at altitude stays cold. The horizon is wide and open and stays that way, one basin after another of this thing extending past sight, interrupted only by higher stacks of the same.

From a distance it reads as a mountain range. A plateau, a ridge, a valley cut between high shoulders — high country, sharp-featured, young. Get closer and the mountain resolves, and then un-resolves, into construction.

There is no bedrock to be found. No natural substrate anywhere you can reach. The ground is flooring. The cliff face at the edge of the world is strata: hulls, containers, furnace shells, cooling fins, retaining walls, platform decks, all locked together in bands. Everywhere the edges are new. Nothing has been worn soft. Paint is unchalked, corners are 90 degrees, broken panels lie where they fell with the bright inside of the break still bright. Erosion here is not a slow smoothing; it is a recent emergence. This landform was put up, recently, at height, and the air and the occasional damp have not yet had time to teach it to crumble. The scree at the bottom of a sheer face is not stone. It is shards of panel and snapped conduit and curled sheet, glittering and sharp.

And it is dense to the point of touching. You do not walk on a plain. You walk in a lattice that has been layered until it is almost solid, and then layered again. Terraces overhang terraces. A walkway passes close enough to a factory wall that you could read the stenciled number on it — and that walkway is itself the roof of another walkway, and the wall is the side of a tower that rises through the next three levels above you. Streets are not so much streets as gaps in the saturation, narrow unbuilt seams where light falls straight down and where damp collects. In plan it would be a valley: a central basin, ridges of cranes and antennae and vent stacks rising around it, secondary basins branching off. In experience it is near-touching at every scale, a high-saturation of presences per volume. You are always beside something, above something, below something that is also above something else.

It is damp, but never wet. That is the rule of the water here. There are no rivers. There is occasional rain, intermittent and local — a pocket of sky between towers will sweat, condense on the cold underside of a platform, and let go a brief, sparse fall that lasts a few meters before it is reabsorbed or evaporates on a sun-warmed panel. Mostly water lives as film and bead. It beads on the north sides of pipes. It condenses overnight — a long overnight, in geologic slow time — in the enclosed hollows where warm air from deep in the lattice meets cold upper metal, and it stands there in the morning in shallow, breathing pools no wider than a plate, trapped in the dish of a discarded machine housing. In the shadowed gutters it makes a dark line. It never runs. It persists, and then, over hours, it disappears without going anywhere.

Vegetation has not been invited. It has come anyway, sparsely. Mats of moss the color of pale hair in the corners where the damp line holds. A thin, wiry grass in the sweepings — and the soil is sweepings, shredded insulation and metal dust and plastic fiber compacted into a grey mat that holds moisture for a while. Lichen in white and rust-orange crusts on sun-baked ceramic. A vine that is mostly a root, feeling its way along a cable tray for twenty meters to find another damp seam. It does not make a forest. It makes a sparse, opportunistic suggestion of green against all that manufactured surface, and it grows at the pace of everything else here: patiently, across long durations, apparently not moving at all until you have been gone for years and return to find the seam greener.

Fauna is not sparse. Fauna is abundant, because the built world is habitat, and habitat here is everywhere, layered just as thickly as the structures.

Swallows and pigeon-like birds nest in the mouths of dormant engines by the thousand, wheeling in the gaps between towers in slow, bright flocks that cast no real shadow because the light is coming from everywhere. Moths and small flies hang in the permanent glare around lamps that never turn off, drifting like slow snow, thick enough that you walk through them and they part and close behind you. Beetles work the warm vents. Lizards, grey and excellent, bask on panels that have held the sun for days — days here are long — and then dart into ductwork. In the plate-sized pools, silvery fish turn in circles, improbably, in water that looks too shallow to hold them; they have learned the damp, they have learned the slow. Deeper in the lattice, where the air is warmer and never fully bright, there are blind, soft things in the pipes, and things with many legs in the insulation, and the whole dense stack is threaded with their sounds if you listen close enough.

Which brings the sound. For all this density, for all this industry, it is mostly quiet.

Industry is here, unmistakably, at industrial scale. Factories the size of basins. Conveyors wide enough to drive on. Cranes that straddle an entire district. Furnaces, smelting halls, power halls, the scaled production of a civilization that built its own landscape and then kept building on it. But it does not roar. The air at this altitude carries poorly, and the machines themselves have slowed themselves into near silence. A crane lifts, but at a meter an hour; you can watch it for an afternoon and see that it has moved. A conveyor inches. A furnace exhales heat on a cycle measured in years, warming its district for a season of slow years, then cooling for another. There is a tick, somewhere, every few seconds. A drip, somewhere else, every few seconds, out of phase with the first. A faint electrical hum that you feel more in your teeth than hear. The birds, when they call, sound muffled, as if heard through a thin wall. No traffic, no shouting, no grinding. Everything mechanized, everything slow, everything patient. The place makes its sounds at a distance and the thin air edits them down to a whisper on arrival.

And then the weirdness, which is not an event but a condition, seeping.

Reality here is overbuilt. It has been built on itself so densely, and so high, and in such bright, cold light, that it has begun to thin and double in places. Not dramatically. Slowly, as everything else does.

A tower you passed a moment ago is there again, behind you, when you turn — same tower, but the rust is ten years further on, or the paint is fresh. A bridge crosses a gap and, halfway across, the gap is a different gap, on a different level, and you finish the crossing on the other side of the basin from where you started, though the bridge has not moved. Stairs that go down a level arrive, damp and mossy, at a level that is also up, with the condensation beading in shapes that look like the tread of the stair you just left. Sometimes the light has no single direction: you cast a faint shadow to the north and another, weaker, to the south, and the strong light in a dark interior is coming not from a door but from the wall itself, bright as noon, cold as winter.

Distance stutters. Two stacks that should be far are near when you are not looking at them. A sound arrives after its echo — you hear the drip, and then, a beat later, the faint click of the machine that caused it. Objects retain afterimages physically: look away from a bright panel and the panel is still there, a pale shape in the air where it was, for a breath longer than it should be, and you can almost touch it.

It is not hostile. It is breakdown in the old sense: a wearing-through of the single version of things. The place was made so completely, with no natural substrate to anchor it, that it has begun to make alternatives of itself in the margins, in the damp pockets, in the long bright days. A day — a bright period, a slow cycle — lasts so long that things have time to exist twice. A season is a mechanical exhalation. A new level grows, a decade at a time, on top of a level that is already rusting in a way that suggests it will be new again.

So you stand, cool air in your lungs, bright light on your face, on a floor that is a roof that is a wall that is a cliff, surrounded on all sides and above and below by made things near-touching, listening to the quiet drip and tick, watching birds wheel slow in the glare and fish turn in a pool that should not be deep enough, moss creeping in a gutter, a crane suspended mid-lift since morning, and you understand that this is not a place built on a landscape. This is a landscape that has been built — recently, sharply, densely, industrially — until it broke, gently, and kept going.

It will keep going. Slowly.
