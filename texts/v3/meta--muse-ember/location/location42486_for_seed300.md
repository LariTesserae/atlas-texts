It is not a valley so much as a dent.

You stand on its rim — a low, damp crest of wet slate and packed earth, barely higher than a man — and you can see the far side. That is the first surprise. It is small. A long field across, perhaps less. A hamlet of ground. And yet from that rim it does not look small at all. It looks crowded, and layered, and too full for its boundary.

Everything drains inward here. That is the law of the place. The ground falls away in short, scoured steps and then levels into a floor that never quite gets dry. The air in the bowl is cooler than the air on the rim, and wetter, and it catches in the throat like a held breath. You descend and the sound of the outside — wind in anything taller than grass — simply stops, as if you have gone under water without breaking a surface.

The floor is not earth, not quite. It is earth that has been taught by industry and then left to the water. Wet concrete has slumped into soft terraces. Rectangular pans, shallow as saucers and wide as rooms, are stepped down in all directions, overlapping, shoulder to shoulder. Between them run channels, cut and overcut, lined with stone that has been polished by ten thousand passages of brown water. Pipes cross low overhead, so low you must duck, and again at ankle height, and again buried under mud that keeps pushing them up. Cables hang in sagging festoons between posts and fall to the ground and rise again. Nothing here has room to be alone. The density is not of trees or houses. It is of works — of uses laid upon uses until the hollow feels saturated, like a sponge that has taken all it can and still receives.

And it is wet in a way that is more than rain. This is a place after rain that never ends. Not flooded — there is no lake, no broad marsh water to wade through — but water is near the surface everywhere, and at the surface, and on every vertical thing. It beads on iron even in still air. It sweats from the weeping rock at the edges, where conglomerate cliffs, grey and rust-banded, drip in slow, irregular rhythm onto ledges below. It seeps sideways out of bankings. Put a boot down and the print fills before you lift it. The mud is cold through the sole. Everything has a sheen, and under the sheen, another sheen.

The vegetation has largely given up. This is scoured, mineral ground, too wet and too washed to root well. There are no woods, no hedges. Only films of moss the color of verdigris on the north sides of concrete, black-green algae in the still pans, a sparse fringe of sedge where a channel has breached and slowed, and here and there a stunted birch, knees in black slurry, all its leaves small and too far apart. Lichen maps the oldest stone. Where a bank has freshly slumped, it is bare and raw for a day, then it too acquires a skin. Life pushes in as a stain before it pushes in as a plant.

The built things are scattered among the pans like things dropped. A pump-house with one intact pane of glass, its door fused shut by rust and swollen wood. The roof of a shed collapsed inward and now serves as a shallow pond. A row of intake houses, roofless, their interiors full of standing water that reflects the flat sky. Marker posts, a gantry, a length of narrow-gauge rail sunk to its sleepers. None of it makes a town. It makes a debris field that has been arranged, over a very long time, into something that almost coheres.

Beneath and between all of it, the industry still runs. That is the strangeness that holds the place together.

It is not a factory in any one building. It is a system spread across the whole dent: hydraulic rams in pits, Archimedes screws half-buried in channels, their blades turning with a slow, grinding patience; sluice gates that lift and settle; pumps in relay, one after another around the bowl, sucking brown slurry from a higher pan to a lower, then on again. Electric motors sit in the wet like animals drinking, their casings beaded, their cables leading nowhere you can trace. The machines do not roar. They thump, softly, like a heart heard through a wall. They sigh through valves. In the cool, damp air their breath condenses and for a moment you see it, white against the grey, and then it is gone, absorbed.

Light here is ordinary daylight, and it is enough. It is a flat, overcast noon that levels shadows without warming anything. The sky above the rim is pale and uncommitted. Clouds have been caught in the bowl and travel slowly from pipe to pipe, thickening, thinning, never clearing entirely. Mist does not rise so much as settle, and resettle, and in the middle of the day it will burn off the centre first, leaving the edges still blind.

For all the machinery, it is quiet. That is the second surprise. A turbulent place should be loud. This one is not, because the turbulence is not in the air. It is in the ground, in the water, in the mud that moves like something very slow and very heavy.

Stay an hour — stay long enough for the cold to get into your hands — and the place rearranges itself.

A pan on the eastern step, which had been placid, begins to rise. Not by rain. By a pump two terraces up, which you had not noticed running, delivering its slurry in a series of soft surges. The water spreads to the edges, finds a low place in its wall, and breaches. It does not burst. It flows, thick and brown, in a tongue that widens as it goes, taking a new channel down through the pans below, filling margins, covering a path that was open ten minutes ago. Downstream, a dam of deposited silt that had held for who knows how long dissolves from underneath and subsides with a muted sigh, and the water that was behind it redistributes in a brief, silent cascade.

On the far bank, a slope that has stood since before you arrived creeps. You see it happen only because you are watching: a crack opens like a mouth, a slab of wet earth the size of a door tips, very slowly, and slides into the channel, throwing up little ripples. It settles. In an hour it will have a damp moss film. In a season, lichen.

The screws turn. A gate lifts by its own pressure and drops. A pipe that had been dry begins to weep from a joint, then to run, then stops, as if water has found and then unfound a route underground. The deep pan at the very centre — dark, almost black, holding the sky in a perfect, unrippled disc when the pumps pause — receives the final washings and gives nothing back but a surface that moves, faintly, in concentric rings with no visible cause. Something in its depths has shifted. You do not see it. You see the rings.

This is the loop of the place: every movement is small, viscous, and quickly settled, and nothing ever reaches an end. Water is lifted and dropped, lifted and dropped. Slurry travels downhill and is pumped uphill again to travel downhill. Condensation forms on cold iron in the morning and rains, in tiny drops, from the undersides of pipes in the afternoon, back into the pans it came from. The basin is almost closed. What little leaves, leaves as dampness on the wind that climbs the rim. Most of it stays.

And that is why it looks old. Erosion here is not a ruin. It is established. The rust is not fresh red; it is black-orange, layered, a thousand cycles deep, and the sharpest edge of a bracket has been softened by wet air. The concrete terraces have been rounded by water running over the same lines for so long that the water seems to have memorized them. New slumps, when they happen, do not look violent. They look inevitable, and they look as if they have happened before in exactly this way. This turbulence is not a storm passing through. It is the settled state. It has been churning in this small, low, cool hollow for long enough that the churn itself has become the form. Everything is in balance because everything is moving.

Fauna, where there is any, moves like the machinery. Rare and pale. A frog in a pipe joint, glistening, that vanishes when you look directly at it. Dragonflies over the warmer pan, where the motors bleed a faint heat into the water, their wings catching the flat light. Once, a heron stood in the central pan for a long time, grey as the mist, and did not catch anything, and left the way it had come, without a sound. In the deep channels, eels or something eel-like move in the brown flow, never surfacing. There are no birdsong choruses, no insects in the air, no mammals except for the prints — yours, filling with water, and an older, narrower set that goes from pan to pan and stops at a pump-house and does not come out.

You learn to read the place by its surfaces. A wet handprint on iron means a pump has recently been touched by something — pressure, a shift in the water table. A bright streak of algae on stone means that channel has run clear in the last day. A clean, white line in the mud means a gate has just lifted. The smell tells the rest: cold stone, wet iron, a faint sourness of works oil, the mineral tang of the weeping rock. It is the smell of a place that is always being washed and never quite clean.

By the time you climb back to the rim, the bowl below has changed its pattern of bright and dark, its routes and levels, without once raising its voice. The pans are still stepped. The pipes still cross and recross. The central disc is still holding the sky. But the water is in different places, the channels have taken different shortcuts, a new deposit bars one corner, a new runnel opens another. It looks, impossibly, both exactly as it did when you arrived and not at all.

That is the place. A small, saturated dent in low ground, dense with tired industry, sparse with green, cool in the middle of the day, quiet enough to hear water ticking from stone to iron to pan, and forever — not violently, but without pause — coming undone and remaking itself in the same familiar, weathered shapes.
