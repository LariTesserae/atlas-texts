First there is the light, and the cold that does not come from it.

It is ordinary daylight, clear and even, the white-yellow of late morning with no glare and no dimming. It falls flat across a high country and stays at that same angle. The clouds, few and thin, do not drift. You can stand for an hour and nothing in the sky will have committed itself to moving.

Below that light the land is young.

It rises — not to snow peaks, but to a broad, lifted plateau, high enough that your ears feel the thinness and your breath shows and fades in the cool air even while the sun is on your neck. The rock is fresh. Not weathered into slopes, but split. Great fracture planes run straight for hundreds of paces. Cliffs are clean and vertical, their edges still sharp enough to cut a boot sole. Scree slopes are impossibly steep, consisting of angular shards, split boulders, splinters that have not yet rounded. There is almost no soil except where something living has trapped it. Everywhere the stone looks newly broken, newly placed, as if the mountain was assembled yesterday and has not yet had time to soften.

And it will not soften.

That is the first rule you learn, though no one tells it to you. The moss does not spread over the doorstep. The thatch does not gray. A bead of water forms under a ledge, swells to the exact size a bead can hold, and hangs there. It does not fall. A berry ripens on a shrub and stays ripe, glossy and tight, neither dropping nor rotting nor shrivelling. The grass does not bend, because there is no wind to bend it. The insects do not cross the path. You come back to a place you left, and it is precisely, uncannily, as it was — not preserved in a glass case, but in a perfect, living equilibrium. Everything consumes exactly what it makes. Everything grows exactly as much as it dies. Nothing accumulates. Nothing wears away.

Water, then, is not a river. There is no river. The dampness is in pockets, in the way this high, cold stone holds moisture against itself.

Walk a little and you find the pools: no larger than your hand, tucked in hollows between tilted slabs, perfectly still, reflecting the sky. There are frogs in them, motionless, their eyes above the surface. Elsewhere the rock sweats. Under every overhang, in every shaded crack, condensation gathers in fine beads on the undersides of stones, on the waxy leaves of plants, on the cold north faces of blocks. It does not run. It does not drip, though it looks as if it should. It simply stays damp, keeping everything it touches lush and cool to the touch. After a while your sleeves are wet from brushing through it, your skin smells of wet stone and moss, and the air itself feels moist without ever quite becoming rain.

For the vegetation *is* lush, in a way that should not be possible at this height and temperature. Not forest — there are no tall trees, no canopy to shade you. Instead a low, dense, impossibly green mat covers every sheltered place: cushions of moss thick as a mattress, ferns unfurled to the same size forever, grasses so thick your feet sink, shrubs of dark green with thick waxy leaves that hold the dew in bright beads, each leaf the same size it was an hour ago. Between the sharp stones, the green pours like water filling a crack. It is everywhere you look once you start looking, a saturated carpet interrupted by bare pale rock, as if the plateau has been upholstered.

And the ground in that green is, quietly, full.

You do not hear it first. That is the second surprise. It is mostly quiet — a silence deeper than emptiness. A single bird call, clear and spaced, the tick of a stone cooling, the very occasional tap of a hammer far away. Otherwise nothing. No buzzing, though moths hang in the air in such numbers they make a dust. No chirring from the grass, though the grass is thick with beetles, shiny and slow. The fauna are abundant, but they are abundant in the way of things that do not need to announce themselves. Ptarmigan-like ground birds, mottled to look exactly like the stones they stand on, do not flush until you are on top of them. Small mammals peer from burrows in the moss. Snails inch — you can see a trail of silver behind one, and the trail will still be silver tomorrow. Frogs wait in the hand-pools. Sheep and goats in stone pens stand in their thick wool, chewing, unhurried, their bells silent. Swallows and swifts nest in the eaves of houses, wings folded, occasionally making a tiny adjustment, the same adjustment, back and forth.

They are part of the equilibrium too. The young stay young. The old do not age. A marmot sitting in the sun will be sitting in the sun, in the same posture, when you look again.

It is the houses that tell you this is not wilderness.

You find them first as a cut step in the rock where no step should be. Then a straightened edge. Then a low wall, laid without mortar, of blocks squared by hand. The wall is crisp; the chisel marks are still pale against the weathered face, as if they were struck this morning. Tools lie where they were dropped — flint hammers, stone adzes, bone needles — and they are still sharp. No metal to speak of. No glass. No wheel-ruts deep enough to hold water. Everything is made of stone, turf, wood, woven fiber, and fired clay, made by hand and made Euclidean.

And there is a great deal of it.

This is a town grown to city size, but spread so thin across the plateau benches that it feels empty. The scale is regional, open: you can stand on a ridge and see, three valleys away, another cluster of the same pale roofs, another walled field, another empty plaza, all separated by leagues of moor and green mat and sharp rock. The built places occupy only the flatter places, and even there, density is low. Houses are spaced far apart. Streets are absurdly wide, paved with flat slabs, laid in a grid that follows the fracture lines of the stone, and they are almost empty. Plazas are larger than marketplaces need to be. Granaries, kilns, workshops with charred interiors, cisterns cut deep into the rock, long low aqueduct channels that carry no water but only the idea of it, condensation walls of woven fiber strung between posts to catch the damp air and drip — very slowly, and then not at all — into stone troughs.

Layer upon layer is visible because nothing decays to hide it. A house is built into the gable of an older house, whose wall is built into a still older retaining wall. The layers do not crumble into each other; they remain readable, fresh, like a cliff section cut through a building. Roofs of turf stay green-gold. Roofs of slate stay sharp. Doors are small, thick, made of planks pinned with wooden pegs. Windows are slits.

A few people live here, moving sparsely between the many structures. You see one here, one there, in woven wool, carrying a basket, hammering at a lintel that is half-carved and will, you understand, remain half-carved. Their work is organized — the town is planned, with quarters for craft, for storage, for animals, with fences and gates and wells — but it is primitive work, manual craft, and it is not progressing toward completion, or decay. Smoke rises from a kiln in a thin, straight column that does not disperse. It goes up and stops, like a bead of water.

At the edges of the town the building stops, and the moor resumes, and the green mat closes over the last stone paving as if the town were a temporary arrangement. Empty fields lie inside low walls, lush and untouched, their crops at a perfect, ripe, unchanging stage. Empty pens. Empty workshops. An entire street of houses with thatch still golden on the ridges, chimneys cold, birds nesting undisturbed in the eaves. The infrastructure of a city; the population of a scattering.

That sparseness, that quiet, that is what makes the abundance bearable. The air is cool and clean and smells of damp stone and moss and faint woodsmoke and animal fur. The silence is not an absence of life; it is life holding its breath so as not to disturb the balance. You hear your own steps on the slab street, too loud. Each step is clear and does not echo long. The stone, being fresh, rings a little.

Toward evening — though the light does not change its quality — you climb to the highest bench, where the rock is most fractured, and look back. The pools are still there, glittering. The towns are still there, in their valleys. The moss is still green. The sheep are still there. The bead of water under the ledge by your foot is still there, swollen to its perfect size, waiting to fall and not falling.

It is a place that has just been made, and has been made just so, and will go on being just so. Not ancient, because nothing is worn. Not new, because everything is finished and inhabited and full. It holds all its contradictions — high and damp, lush and cold, urban and empty, full of life and almost soundless — in the stillness of a world that has found its equilibrium and intends, forever, to stay there.
