From the rim the world is small enough to hold in one look, and empty enough that you wonder if you have made a mistake.

It is a bowl, not much more than an hour's walk across, set high in dry stone. The rim is broken and weathered to a smooth, settled edge, as if it has been there for a very long time and has forgotten to be dramatic about it. Wind has polished the cliffs into terraces and shallow basins where dust collects. Inside, the floor drops away in a single pale dish. You can see the whole of it at once: nothing rising to interrupt the horizon except the rim itself, closing you in on all sides. There is no way out that you can see, and no water to mark a way through.

The air at this height should be cold and thin. It is thin — your ears pop on the climb, your breath comes short — but it is warm. The stone under your boots holds heat through the day. The light, even at midday, never quite arrives. A high, permanent veil hangs over the bowl, amber-brown and smudged, like smoke held up by something far away. It does not move so much as breathe. Under it, shadows are soft and everywhere, and colours lose their edges. Greens go dark, almost black. Reds go dusky. It is perpetual late afternoon, with a warmth that does not lift.

And there is no water. Not a river, not a puddle, not a damp hollow. Rain does not fall here; the cloud is not for rain. The ground is cracked into large, tilted polygons of pale mineral, salt-crusted in the seams, pumice-light and hot to the touch. It crinkles underfoot, then snaps. In the cracks it is a shade darker, a little cooler, but still dry as bone. The huts down below have no cisterns, no channels, no jars for storing. The very idea of stored water seems foreign to the place, like a word in a language no one here speaks.

Which makes the groves impossible at first.

You hear them before you see them. The bowl is loud — constantly, unreasonably loud. Wind runs across the open floor with a roar that never settles, scraping dust, hissing through stone. Over that, another layer: the crackle and pop of the ground as it warms and cools, small tremors ticking through the slabs, pebbles dancing and resettling. And from somewhere far across the emptiness, a clattering, rustling, whistling that rises and falls like a chorus trying to tune itself.

You walk toward it. For a long while there is nothing but that pale, cracked plain, sparse to the point of loneliness. Then, suddenly, green.

Not scattered shoots. A grove, dense and lush and towering, standing alone in the emptiness like a small, self-contained forest that has decided to travel. Inside it the dim light goes darker still under a canopy of broad, overlapping leaves — fan-shaped, waxy, dark purple-green, some of them grey underneath. Vines as thick as your arm hang and interlace. The trunks are not wood as you know it but bundles of hollow, fibrous stems bound together, creaking against each other. The air inside is cooler, still warm, and smells sweet and sharp and mineral, like hot stone and crushed leaves and something faintly sulfurous.

The lushness is real here, extravagant even. Leaves pile on leaves. Flowers open and close in minutes, releasing yellow clouds that drift and make you sneeze. Young shoots push up through the mineral crust in tight, furled fists and unroll in an afternoon to the size of a shield. But between this grove and the next — and you can see the next, a dark clump perhaps a quarter mile away across bare stone — there is nothing. Four, five, six groves in the whole bowl, each one isolated, each one dense within itself and utterly alone outside it. The emptiness between them is part of the place. It gives the wind room. It gives the animals room to run.

How does it live? There is no root-water to find. You kneel and look. The roots do not go deep; they spread shallow and sideways as a mat of breathing filaments that mine the cracks, drinking salts and powders from the rock. The leaves do the rest. They are sponges, finely porous, constantly shivering in the wind. The plain is not as empty as it looks: in a low, invisible way, the ground exhales. From hairline cracks in the slabs comes a warm, faint vapour — not steam, not smoke, something in between, carrying hydrogen and carbon and the sharp smell of the stone itself. The groves have grown only where that breath rises. The leaves catch it, condense it into a clear, slightly oily fluid that is not water, and drink it straight from the air. At night, when the vapour thickens, the leaves drip. Animals come to lick the drip. You learn, by watching them, to press your mouth to a cool leaf in the morning and taste something clean and metallic and sweet. It is the only drink here, and it never pools.

Life here gave up water long ago and learned to live inside a different fluid, carried in bodies, in sap, in the hollow stems of the trees. It works precisely because the place is high and warm and sealed: the vapour is trapped under the veil, recirculating up from the vents and down again into leaves, never leaving the bowl.

The animals have learned the same lesson, and they are everywhere in numbers that do not match the emptiness. They come in waves.

First the air. The sky, dim as it is, blacks out for a moment when a swarm lifts from a grove — thousands of glass-winged things, no bigger than moths, rising in a column that hums so loudly the wind seems to pause to listen. They scatter to the next grove, to the next, pollinating, stripping, seeding as they go.

Then the runners. On the plain between groves, herds move. Six-legged, long-necked grazers with sail-like ears that flap to cool them, their hides mottled to look like cracked stone when they stand still. They do not eat the grove in a steady way; they arrive as a frenzy, strip a whole side of a tree in an hour, leave it shivering and bare, and run on. Within days, from the basal shoots, the tree pushes out a new crown, denser than before. Creation and destruction here are not seasons apart. They are hours apart, overlapping.

And the strange ones, the ones that make the place feel wrong in a way that takes time to name. Burrowers that look like lumps of leaf and only reveal legs when they move. Clingers that hang under leaves and are indistinguishable from unopened buds until they blink. A kind of slow, plant-like animal that sits on a trunk for a whole day photosynthesizing through its back, then stands, walks on root-feet to another tree, and sits again. The groves themselves seem to walk, very slowly: growing hard toward a fresh vent, dying back where a vent has gone cold and silent, the whole clump migrating across the plain over weeks, leaving a trail of withered husks and fresh seedlings behind it like footprints.

The sound never stops, because the place never stops. The hollow stems of the breath-trees are tuned by the wind; sometimes the whole grove sings, a low, fluting chord that vibrates in your teeth. Sometimes gas builds up inside a swollen trunk and it pops with a sound like a distant gun, releasing a burst of seeds that rain down steaming. The ground tremors in small, irritated jerks — no earthquake, just the floor shifting as pockets of vapour find new cracks — and each time, somewhere, a pebble field ticks and settles, and a new crack breathes, and a seedling that was waiting in the dust wakes up.

Under all of this churn, the stones themselves are old and settled. No fresh scars. The terraces where dust has gathered in the wind's lee, the smooth bowls worn in the rim, the polished channels where gas has escaped for centuries — they have the weathered, in-balance look of a place that has found its own state and means to keep it, however turbulent the living layer above them may be.

There are signs that others have lived inside that balance, faintly. Scattered, never clustered. A circular windbreak of stacked slabs, no roof left, just a knee-high wall open to the sky. A shallow kiln of fused ash and pumice. A single obsidian blade, beautifully made, left on a flat stone. A woven mat of dry fibre, stiff and grey. Paths, not roads, just places where feet have worn a slightly smoother line between groves, stepping around the cracks. No metal, no pottery that holds liquid, no wheels. Simple tools for simple work: to shelter from the wind, to cut fibre, to sit in the grove's shade and wait for the leaves to drip. They are not ruins so much as occasional artifacts, as if someone comes here now and then, does a little, and lets the plain take it back.

Stand in the middle of the bowl at dusk — though dusk here is indistinguishable from noon — and listen. Wind roaring over empty stone. A grove a hundred paces away clattering and singing. A herd calling to each other across the emptiness, weird, echoing cries. Swarms humming overhead. The ground ticking like cooling metal. A trunk popping somewhere. It is loud, and it is crowded, and it is sparse, all at once. The groves flare and die and flare again. The animals boil and vanish. The vapour rises. The leaves drink.

It should not work — lush life with no water, high and warm and dim, in a small sealed dish of dry salt — and yet the whole bowl hums with it, stubbornly, wastefully, beautifully in balance. Everything here expends itself quickly and grows back quickly, and the stone underneath, patient and established, holds the pattern together while the green world rushes across it like weather.
