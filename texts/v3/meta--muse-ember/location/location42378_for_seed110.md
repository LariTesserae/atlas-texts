It is high, but you would not know it from inside.

Outside, the mountain is all wind and thin cold air and a long climb over raw, sharp stone that has not yet been worn smooth. There is a slit, no wider than a shoulder, in the ridge — and then the world closes behind you and shrinks.

This is not a landscape you walk across. It is a pocket. A room, barely. You can crouch, and with your arms stretched you touch something in every direction: warm wall, wet wall, slick ceiling low enough to brush your hair. No larger than a small storage pit, no taller than you are tall when kneeling. Embedded, hidden within the high rock, as if the mountain swallowed a breath and forgot to let it go.

And there is no light at all.

Not dim light. Not a gloom you could adjust your eyes to. Zero. Your eyes open and do nothing. So the place begins as pressure and touch and heat.

The air is the first material. It is warm — warmer than body-warm, a steady sauna warmth that does not come from the sun — and saturated. It presses in, velvet-thick, humid to the point that breathing feels like drinking. Water is everywhere, but it is not a river and it is not rain. The rock weeps. The air condenses. Every surface sweats.

Small beads gather on the walls and run, very slowly, in crooked threads that never quite reach the floor because more beads join them, and the thread thickens, and the thread thins again. Droplets cling to the ceiling in crowded rows, some hanging for what feels like eternity, trembling but not falling, others falling at a fixed, metronomic interval into a shallow pool no bigger than a dinner plate. The pool's surface is glass-still and filmed over with a pale mineral skin that cracks and seals in the same instant when a drop lands. The level does not rise. It does not fall. Evaporation lifts exactly as much as condensation puts back. The water is warm and tastes, on the lips, of stone and salt and a faint sulfur-metal tang.

This is a wet place in the sense of a sponge that can hold no more. It drips where you stand. It condenses on your own skin faster than it can evaporate, so you run with your own sweat mixed with the mountain's.

The rock itself has not finished becoming rock.

It is not eroded. Erosion would imply age, rounding, patience. This is before that. The walls are raw: spongy in places, like wet ash or unfired clay left to soak, sharp in others, bristling with small crystal teeth that have only just pushed through the surface. Blisters bulge from the stone and do not pop. Stubby stalactites, only a knuckle's length, crowd together like unfinished teeth. Mud that is half liquid, half solid bubbles without breaking — a tremor of air rises, swells, and settles again, leaving the same dimple.

It is geology under construction, and it will never be completed. That is its rule. For every grain of mineral that precipitates out of the warm seep and thickens the crust a fraction, another grain on the edge dissolves back into the water. Building and unbuilding at the same rate. A formation that is perpetually embryonic. No gradients, no progress, no decay. If you left and returned in a year you would find it precisely, oppressively, the same. If you stayed a year, your own footprints in the soft mud would fill and level and reform as if never made, and yet never quite disappear.

Because the space is so small, everything is dense.

The walls are layered upon layer, near-touching. Overhangs, pockets, crevices within crevices, folds of mineral skin overlapping like scales. You cannot put your hand anywhere without displacing something — a slick lip, a sharp spur, a hollow that sighs. The air itself feels crowded, not empty, as if the moisture has a weight of its own and the heat is a presence leaning on your shoulders. High saturation, but not of color — of touch, of sound, of smell.

Sounds are sparse, and therefore every sound is particular. Mostly quiet. Then a drip. A faint tick as a cooling crystal contracts. A soft hiss as steam finds a new crack and then thinks better of it. The wet, muffled beat of your own heart, which is louder here than it should be because there is nothing else to compete with it. The pool does not lap. The trickle does not wander. The same few notes repeat, forever, in the same order.

Life, where there is any, is sparse and strange to define.

No plant could photosynthesize in this darkness, and there is almost no soil to root in. In the places where seepage is most constant — along one seam where water moves slowest, in a shallow shelf above the pool — there are patches of living felt. Pale, hair-thin filaments, rootlets like fine white threads, fungal pads no larger than your palm, soft and cool under the fingertips. They grow in tufts, then stop. They do not spread. They feed, apparently, on the minerals themselves, on the warm dissolved stone. They are the only green-thing this place has ever made, and they are not green, because nothing here needs to be.

Animal life is rarer still. You will not meet it by accident. Once in a long while — if long while means anything here — there is a disturbance in the pool: a slow, blind, eel-pale thing, no longer than your forearm, that slides through the warm shallow water and wedges itself into a crack in the wall and stays there, breathing so slowly you could miss it entirely. It has no eyes that matter. It has no hurry. Or, on the soft mud, a cluster of minute worm-tracks, fresh and then unchanging, leading from the pool to nowhere. That is the whole census. One resident. Occasional visitors that leave a trace and never return.

And yet there are human marks. Scattered, primitive, and puzzlingly fresh.

Not buildings, really. A low, half-finished ring of palm-sized stones by the pool, as if someone meant to make a place to sit and never sat. A shallow bowl pecked out of the soft rock with a hammerstone, its edge still sharp, meant to catch the drip. A few flaked shards — simple, hand-worked stone, no metal, edge chipped in a single deliberate line — half-sunk into the mud where the rock is soft enough to receive them. Grooves scratched with a fingernail or a point, three parallel lines, then stopped. They look like someone's first attempts at making a tool, or making a mark, left here during a single visit and never elaborated. Primitive tech in a place that never taught anyone anything. They have not rusted, not been worn, not been covered by sediment. They are as they were laid down, in a place where nothing becomes old.

That is the strangeness of it, held perfectly in equilibrium: a high, warm, wet, dark pocket; raw stone still trying to be born; water that never runs out and never accumulates; a sparse felt of life and a single blind animal and a few abandoned stone tools, all crowded together, all near-touching, never changing.

You could stay crouched here and the warmth would never climb or fall. The droplets on the ceiling would never finish falling. The embryonic crystals would never become columns. The pool would never overflow. The hiss, the drip, the tick would repeat until the distinction between a moment and eternity blurred, not because time has stopped, but because the place has learned to balance itself so exactly that nothing has room to happen.

It holds its breath at the instant of formation, high up in the mountain, and does not exhale.
