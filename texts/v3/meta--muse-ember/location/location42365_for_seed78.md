First there is the sound, because there is almost no light to see by.

A low roar that is not quite wind and not quite earth. It rises and falls across a space too large to measure, and in the rises you can hear separate things break apart: a crack like a rifle-shot far away, a long creak as if a great hull were flexing, a thin piping in the wind, the muffled drumming of many feet/beating too far off to count. Then a pause. In the pause the emptiness is complete.

Darkness here is not night. It is the condition of the place. The sun, when it comes, comes low and brief and grey, a smear on a horizon that never quite arrives, and is swallowed by fog before it can warm anything. Above is a sky of deep slate, always close. Below is the ground. And the ground is not ground.

It is plant.

Walk and you do not walk on soil. You walk on a felt, a cold spongy mat of interwoven roots, rhizomes, compressed leaf-bases and half-dead stems, inches to a foot thick, everywhere, without break, to the edge of sight and beyond. It gives under the boot and then holds, wet, resilient. If you kneel and tear at it — and people do, with stone and antler and hands — it does not reveal dirt. It reveals more plant: paler, tighter, still alive, white tips pushing. The whole substrate of this high world is living tissue, a single shallow ocean of root pressed flat against stone.

That is why it can be total and empty at once.

Above that mat, there is almost nothing. The air is vast and hollow. For hours you can walk and the highest thing beside you is ankle-high: sword-leaves, cushions of waxy rosette, creeping shoots that keep their heads down against the cold. Then, far off — a hundred paces, three hundred, a day's walk — a presence rises. A pillar.

They are not trees in any familiar sense. A pillar plant is a single hollow column, wider than a house at the base, tapering, ridged, its bark furred with frozen condensation and old leaf scars. It rises twenty, forty, sixty meters into the dark air, straight and blind, and ends in a crown of stiff fronds that do not so much photosynthesize as wait. Between pillars is only mat and fog and cold. You learn distance here not in miles but in pillars: the next one is too far to reach before your breath freezes in your scarf.

It is a high place. The air is thin and bites. Ears pop on the climb up through the cloud deck, and then you are above the lower weather, on a plateau that was lifted recently — geologically speaking, yesterday. The rock proves it. You never find a rounded boulder, a worn hollow, a soft slope. Everywhere the stone that breaks through the plant mat is fresh, angular, black-grey, split along new fractures that are still opening. Frost gets in at night and wedges them a fraction wider. By morning there are new shards sharp enough to cut glove leather, new cracks humming with cold. The cliff at the plateau edge is a fresh tear in the world, dropping straight into cloud that rarely lifts. Erosion has barely had time to begin its work. The plateau is still sharp, still young, still settling into itself.

And cold holds everything in place. At 0.5 on any honest scale, this is a place below freezing for most of its year, damp cold that does not melt. Water does not come as rivers. There are no streams chattering over stone, no lakes. Water comes as dampness itself.

Fog rolls in from the void beyond the edge, thick and slow, and simply stops on things. It condenses on the waxy leaves of the mat — each leaf beaded, each bead hanging until it joins another and drips, a tiny, intermittent rain that is not rain. It condenses inside the hollow pillars, where it collects in dark pockets and only occasionally overflows, trickling down the inside of the trunk in a secret, cold thread you can hear if you put your ear to the bark. In the mat, moisture is held like water in a sponge, never pooling, never running. Once in a while, high above, a weather cell forms over the plateau itself — the place is large enough to make its own — and sleet falls for an hour, needling the fronds, vanishing instantly on contact with living tissue that is just below freezing. Then the sun — that grey smear — may touch the beads and for a moment every leaf is lit from below, pale green, almost translucent.

Color has learned restraint here. No bright green. The mat is olive, grey-green, straw-pale where it dies. The pillars are ash and black. The rock is blacker. Animal fur and feather are off-white, smoke-grey, soot. In darkness, color is a waste of energy, and the place spends almost none.

Yet it is loud, and it is moving. Always moving.

The dynamic is not in the pillars, which grow in pulses so slow you would miss them if you watched, but in the mat, and in what lives in and on it. Because the mat is alive over its whole vast extent, it is not still. In the cold it creeps. Frost heave lifts stones and drops them again a hand's breadth away. Root felt tightens and loosens with damp. If you lie flat on a clear — clear is a relative term — morning, you can feel a faint tremor through the plant layer, a slow circulation, sap and air moving in the rhizomes beneath you like a tide in a shallow sea.

And the fauna is abundant, in the way of a place that hides its numbers underground and in the air.

You do not see them at first because density is an illusion. The volume is empty; the life is in the substrate. Peel back a square of mat — careful, it resists like hide — and beneath is a city. Blind, pale grubs the length of your thumb, blind beetles with no eyes but long antennae, worms that pulse and burrow, things with too many legs that fold themselves away from the light. They are the true farmers of this world, chewing the old plant mass, making it new again. They click and rasp and sigh in the dark warm — relatively warm — layer just under the surface, where the plant’s own respiration keeps it a few degrees above the air.

Above, the surface fauna travels in waves. Herds come from beyond the fog, not many in a line, but a line that does not end: low, shaggy grazers with heavy shoulders and horns that rake the mat as they feed, walking a path worn into the root felt a foot deep and kept clear by generations. Their approach is the drumming you heard first, a low thunder transmitted through the plant ground before it reaches your ears. Behind them, smaller runners. Above them, when the light is at its grey peak, birds — not singing so much as shrieking, a flock of ten thousand that fills the hollow air for a minute and then is gone, leaving the emptiness louder by contrast. Predators follow the herds, not in packs but singly, patient, pale, moving as ripples in the mat if you watch the grass-like shoots bend in sequence.

Everything eats everything else, in its turn, in slow cold motion. The grubs eat the mat. The runners eat the grubs. The birds eat the runners. The pillar plants, when they finally die — and they do, rarely, spectacularly, with a crack that echoes for miles as the frozen trunk splits and topples in one piece — feed the mat for decades, because nothing rots quickly in this cold. Decay is arrested. The dead lie where they fall, preserving shape, until they are incorporated, foot by foot, into the living ground. The past is not buried. It is woven in.

That is a rule of this world: cold preserves, dampness captures, light is rationed. Life arranges itself around condensation points, around cracks where the young rock still breathes, around the slow drip inside a pillar. Movement is expensive because heat is expensive, so everything moves in pulses — a season of growth, a season of migration, a night of frost-splitting so loud you wake with your ears ringing, a day of fog so thick you lose the pillar you were walking toward.

The marks of the built are as sparse as the pillars.

You will walk for days and find nothing made. Then, pressed against the base of a pillar where the wind is less, a ring of stones, half-sunk into the mat, blackened inside by a fire long cold. A shelter of woven plant fiber stretched over a frame of bone, collapsed under ice, its entrance still tied shut with sinew. A single tool left frozen in the mat: a hand-axe of chipped stone, hafted with antler, or a needle of bone for mending nets. A cairn no higher than your knee, built by someone who needed to mark a condensation pocket that only drips in certain weather. Scratch-marks on bark at shoulder height, where claws or hands have tested the pillar for water inside.

The people — if they can be called people, and they are few — are primitive in the most literal sense: no metal, no wheel, no fire that lasts, only stone, bone, antler, fiber, and knowledge. They dig for edible root-tips with picks. They weave the mat itself into blankets, because it insulates. They listen to the pillars in wind to predict weather cells. They follow the herds, and the herds follow the fog. Their settlements are not villages. They are single huts, single hearths, re-used for a generation and then abandoned to the mat, which slowly swallows them without rotting them: the stone ring remains, the fiber skeleton remains, the place is marked long after the maker is gone.

It is unusual, but only just. Nothing here violates physics. Plants do not walk. Animals do not glow — though in the darkest hours, pressing your palms into the mat, you can feel a faint warmth, a living heat, and swear the feeling is light of another kind. The strangeness is in the proportions: a continent of plant, an inch thick, holding up a sky of empty air; pillars like towers with no purpose but to stand and collect fog; a fauna that lives mostly below the floor it walks on; a young, sharp, high plateau that is already covered, already alive, before it has had time to erode.

Stand in the middle of it at dusk — that brief moment when the grey smear is at its widest and the fog is thinning — and scale defeats you. The mat goes to the horizon and the horizon goes on. The pillar you came from is already a dark shape too far to return to before dark. Somewhere a herd is drumming toward you through the plant ground, somewhere a pillar creaks as ice splits its fibers, somewhere condensation is beading and dripping, leaf by leaf, across a surface too large to count. The air is cold and damp and thin and it smells of wet stone and faint resin and distant animals. Above, no stars make it through. Below, the whole world is breathing, very slowly, under your feet.

And it will not stay as you leave it. That is the final rule. The mat has grown a hand's breadth while you stood here. A crack has opened. A flock has crossed the sky unseen. The weather that formed over the plateau has already died at its edge. You do not visit this place. You are briefly, loudly, allowed to cross it, while it continues — vast, dark, cold, damp, plant-alive, and empty — in its own motion.
