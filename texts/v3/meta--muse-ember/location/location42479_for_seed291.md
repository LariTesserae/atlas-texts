It is flat, for as far as you can see, and the light is ordinary.

That is the first surprise. Not twilight, not aurora, not the white glare of snow. A plain, grey-green daylight, the kind of noon light you get in a cold country when the sun is up but never high, and steam keeps softening its edges. You can tell true colors here — rust orange, wet concrete, soot black, moss green so saturated it almost looks painted — because nothing is coloring the air except the air itself.

You are in a low place. A basin, really, sunk between shallow rises that are not hills so much as unfinished heaps. From any of them — from a slag heap still being added to, from the roof of a casting hall — the whole town-v valley spreads out and does not end where you expect a town to end. It feathers. Stacks, then more stacks a mile on. Scaffolds, then fields of spoil, then more scaffolds. Scale is open and regional, not a single dense core. It goes on past the immediate, past the noise they don't make, into a damp horizon that is never quite clear.

The ground under your boots is not finished. That is the second surprise, and it explains almost everything else.

This is raw geology, still coming into being. There are no old riverbanks worn smooth, no weathered cliffs. There are fresh cuts of cinder and clay, layers of brick and ash and iron filings still sharp, concrete slabs that were poured last week and are already heaved up a hand's breadth by frost. The land is not being eroded away. It is being built up, unevenly, by machines and by dumping and by frost and by what the town discards. A street that was level in the morning will be cambered by evening, not by flood but by lifting. New soil forms out of spoil. When a wall collapses — and walls do, regularly — it does not become a ruin. Within days it is gone, ground down by its own inhabitants into fill for the next wall, which rises a foot to the left of the last.

Everything is packed. That is the rule you learn as you walk in. There is no empty lot. There is no gap between presences. A building leans on the next, and a third is built across both their roofs at the second story, and a walkway bridges at the fourth, and a pipe runs diagonally through all three for good measure. Density is saturated: walls are covered in signs, in pipes, in nests, in vines, in laundry, in felt curtains hung to keep the cold from the doorways. The ground floor is rarely a street. It is a tangle of conveyors, cellars, warm tunnels, mossy drains, market stalls. People live above the works and beside them and, in summer, in them. Even the air is packed — with midges, with spores, with starlings, with the fine dust of grinding, with breath.

Urban, yes. Industrial, emphatically. This is a town organized around production at scale, electrified and mechanized and layered. Steam engines sigh — they do not roar — under shed roofs. Electric trams run on a loop that is part road, part elevated track, part temporary trestle that can be taken up and relaid. Cranes turn slowly all day, all night, like patient animals. Foundries, loom-shops, sorting-sheds, condenser farms: districts defined not by wealth but by function, and each district is rearranging itself. That is the turbulence.

It is never still, and it is almost always quiet.

The dynamic here is hyperactive in the anthill sense: streams of workers in identical felt coats moving between machines, children running messages across rooftops, carts rerouting around a newly heaved slab, a bazaar of spare parts and warm stones and moss tiles that opens in a doorway and is somewhere else by afternoon, steam venting in plumes that turn to fog and then to nothing. Solaris-like, in small: a quarter will be dismantled on Monday by its own crews, its iron taken down to be recast, its space filled by a garden of ferns growing out of a furnace, and by next month a new shed will be rising there, different, taller, already sprouting moss on its north side.

And yet the sound is low. Not absent — you hear the drip of condensation, the soft clink of tools, the hum of generators through your soles, a distant bell muffled by layers of hanging cloth. Cold air traps sound. Moss absorbs it. Felt, wool, stacked goods, packed bodies, all the thickness of the town, swallow it. Heavy machinery moves slowly. People have learned to work without shouting in a place where shouting would carry too far and accomplish too little. The quiet is not peace. It is pressure. It is full.

The cold is the other constant. Temperature here is low in the ambient, in the big air, in the damp that never leaves your cuffs. Your breath shows. Metal sticks to skin. Frost forms in the shaded corners of doorways even when the sun is out. And water — water is the scarcest thing in this lush place.

There is almost none you can drink from the ground. No river has carved this basin. No lake sits in it. Rain does not fall from the sky in any ordinary way. Water exists here as damp: as condensation.

When cold air meets a warm pipe, it beads. When warm air from a vent hits a cold fin, it rains — locally, indoors, upward on the underside of a condenser, then dripping down in a thin, needful trickle into a tank. Dew forms thick on leaves in the morning, on the insides of windows, on the backs of machines, and is wiped up by hand or by specialized brushes on slow rails. Pockets of water collect where a footprint in warm gravel holds a little melt, where a cooling tank sweats, where boiler eels stir in the dark. Occasionally the whole sky fogs — not from weather, but from a hundred stacks venting at once on a cold morning — and for an hour everything glistens and the ferns drink.

That is why the vegetation is lush, in spite of the cold, in spite of the little water. The plants have learned, or have been bred, to live off condensation and heat and industrial waste. Moss is thick as carpet over every north-facing stone, over roofs, over the backs of sleeping machines. Ferns grow out of cracks in furnaces, their fronds translucent where the heat hits them. Vines spiral up pipes in perfect, unnatural geometry, insulating them against the frost. Fungal shelves bloom under every warm conduit. Lichen paints the stacks in rust and sulphur yellow. Algae fills the condensation tanks with green. Nothing is cut back. The town does not own the plants; the plants occupy the town, as fully as any factory does. They are part of the infrastructure. A roof that is mossy is a roof that does not freeze and crack. A wall that is ferned is a wall that holds its heat.

And the fauna is abundant, because the town is a forest of warm, wet, protected microclimates in a cold desert of air. Boiler cats sleep on generator housings, fat and unbothered. Pigeons nest inside the bellies of cooling towers. Blind fish live in the condensation tanks, rising when the drip comes. Dragonflies patrol the steam. Rats gnaw through felt and make prettier nests. Lizards bask on sunlit iron. Worms work the new soil as fast as it forms. Bats hang in the attic layers where the tram wires hum. Small, numerous, not charismatic in the wilderness sense — abundant in the sense of being everywhere, under every plate, in every pocket of damp the damp provides.

The weirdness is not in any one of these things. It is in how they keep swapping places.

Machines give birth to plants. Plants short-circuit machines, and the machines are redesigned to welcome the short. Buildings grow new floors when a vine takes root on the roof and a family moves up to tend it, because a tended roof is a warm roof. Condensation sometimes runs uphill for a few minutes when a condenser cycles, and the children bet on it. Animals hibernate in warm pipes in the depths of winter and wake in the manufactured summer of a vented shed. Workers seasonally migrate not away from the town but deeper into it, from the cold sorting yards to the warm loom quarters, following heat the way birds follow a river that is not there.

That is the world-rule, if you want one: nothing here erodes into the past. Everything is emerging, and everything is being taken apart and remade while it is still in use. Water forms where heat and cold meet and is harvested and used and lost to air again. Land forms where waste accumulates and is heaved and dug and re-formed. The town forms where the land and the plants and the machines and the animals have agreed, temporarily, to occupy the same packed space, and then they disagree, and they rearrange.

You walk out, if you walk out, the way you came — down a lane that may not be a lane tomorrow, under a bridge that may be a garden by next week, past a fern that is growing out of a motor and a motor that is humming under a fern. The light is still normal. Your breath is still showing. The drip, drip of condensation is still falling, somewhere inside a wall, never quite making a puddle. The town is still building itself, quietly, turbulently, lushly, in the cold, in the damp, in no hurry at all to be finished.
