The first thing is the light.

It is not sun on a landscape. It comes from above, fierce and white-gold, and it comes back up from below, bounced, softened, and bounced again. The horizon is not a line so much as a brightening, a white-gold haze into which everything distant dissolves and then, after a long while, reappears. Shadows are short and thin, and at noon almost obedient. The air is warm — not hot, not oven-hot, but warm as a sunlit wall at the end of a summer day, and so dry that your mouth knows at once there is nothing in it to drink, nothing to rain, nothing to smell of wet earth. It smells of hot ceramic, faint ozone, copper far away, and dust that is not dust.

And silence. Your own breathing is a small, embarrassing weather. Your footsteps make a dull, swallowed thud and are gone.

You expect, at first, a desert. A vast, low, bright desert under a white sky. Then you look down.

The ground is not ground.

It is a made skin, stretched as far as you can see. Great tesserae of sintered material, bone-white and pearl and pale ochre, fitted together without a visible seam. Where a seam shows, it is a hairline of dull gold where an old repair has healed. The colors shift in vast bands across the plain, kilometers wide, not geological strata but manufacturing runs, age bands, generations of the same material laid and relaid. Underfoot it is warm, faintly yielding, as if you were walking on the shallow roof of something hollow. When you crouch, the surface gives a fraction, like skin over a drum, and under your palm you can feel a very slow pulse, not a heartbeat but a thermal one.

It is dense, though it looks empty. The longer you look, the more presences there are, near-touching, layered, high-saturated in a way that has nothing to do with noise. Small nodes like polished pebbles are set in ordered drifts. Lines of light run just below the surface, amber and white, branching and joining. Fin-fields rise a hand's breadth here and there — low radiator fins, turned up to the sky like a sparse, metallic grass — and vanish again. Far off, a glider crosses at a height that should make a sound. It makes none. It is a white, wingless capsule sliding on an invisible rail, silent as paper. Above it, other lanes. Above those, others. The airspace is stratified with traffic that never rises out of the bright haze because, down here, high is expensive and unnecessary.

The mapmakers call it the Brightflat, for want of a truer name. A flat, because it is low —  nothing here rises more than a few meters, and even that is not a hill. A bowl a kilometer across, where dust sits deeper, is a valley. A braided ridge of piled, broken, re-laid plates, polished by wind, is a mountain range. A long, low arc of vaulted structures, a heat vault, a terraced cut where the layers show, are the only relief. It is continent-sized and planet-sized at once. You can walk for days and the sky does not change its mind; you can fly for hours and the horizon does not close. Yet the weather is its own.

There are no water clouds. The sky is washed pale blue-white, hazed with mineral aerosol. Weather here is made of heat, light, and charge. In the distance you see cells: vast, slow-moving patches where the shimmer thickens, where dust devils rise in silent dances — columns of the ground's own particles lifted and turned, sometimes a hundred meters tall, sometimes spreading until they are a small climate a hundred kilometers wide that travels across the plain over weeks. Inside such a cell the air goes static and white, visibility fails, the ground's stored warmth leaks out in sheets. There is no rain to end it. It ends when the dust settles, again, on itself. Heat columns shiver and pour upward in clear towers. Where the light catches them, the lanes of gliders bend around them like water around stones, though there is no water.

Below the skin it is crowded in a way the surface only hints at.

Cut through a terrace and you see strata, all of them made: a conduit layer humming with slow light, a transit layer where small, blind carriers glide in sealed tubes, a habitat layer of stacked, vaulted chambers no larger than your hand on the scale of this world, a deeper layer where heat-veins glow dull red and carry the day's absorbed warmth down to a sink that never fills. The density is not urban. There are no streets shouting. It is the density of a coral, or a circuit, many presences per volume because almost everything that lives here is small, solid-state, and content to be near-touching. Machines are the population. The rest are guests.

And it is active — actively, multiply, impossibly quietly active, at many different speeds at once.

The fastest things are light itself, flickering through the harvesting fins, charging and discharging in microsecond negotiations no eye can follow. The gliders move at a stately, continuous pace, mile after mile, never stopping, never hurrying. A maintenance swarm — a glittering, insect-like cloud of noiseless drones — crosses the plain at knee height, pausing over a mend-line, knitting it closed with a brief flare of conducted light. A dust storm migrates at walking speed, redistributing the shredded remains of old machines into new sand. And slowest of all, the plain itself breathes.

Once a day, with the sun, the whole skin expands a hand's breadth. Seams open microscopically, close again at night. Continents of plate drift on ridges of molten salt and liquid metal a few meters down, a centimeter a year, relieving the stress of eons of thermal expansion. Valleys of dust deepen. Mends heal. The world is in motion like an animal asleep, and almost none of that motion makes a sound, because sound is waste. Vibration is loss. The few things that do sound — the tick of cooling ceramic, the hollow knock under a loose plate, the faint electrical sigh before a static discharge — are noticed precisely because the rule is silence.

The materials are beyond any modern workshop, and they have had an ancient time to become inevitable. The skin remembers its shape under grims of heat and returns to it. An inscription cut into a plate a thousand generations ago is worn now to a broad, shallow groove, polished by wind-blown grit until the letters are only a pattern in how light and heat move across it — which is to say, the memory is still there, stored in conductivity, not in ink. A dropped tool does not stay dropped. It is, slowly, unmade and remade into the ground. Nothing rots here because nothing is wet. Nothing dissolves. Everything abraded falls as fine, bright dust, and the dust is the ground in waiting. If you scoop it, it is warm, impossibly fine, and it gleams faintly in your palm because some of it is still storing the noon.

This explains the erosion, which is also the age. This is a primordial thing that has settled into its final form: not because it was built once and left, but because it has been remade into the same form so continuously that old and new are indistinguishable. There are no ruins. Ruin would imply abandonment, and abandonment is not a state this place permits. There are no frontiers, no new growth eating into wilderness, because there is no wilderness. The sharpest edge in the world has been sanded by its own dust for so long that all edges are gentle. The oldest polished arch and the newest repair shine with the same soft, sun-bleached luster. It looks end-of-life and beginning-of-life at once, a thing that has outlived its makers, its purpose, its language, and kept going out of material habit.

Life, where it persists, is sparse, and it persists at the edges of the made.

In the deeper dust of the bowls, where a plate has lifted a fraction and a coolant bleed has left a mineral seep, grow the stitchmats. They are not plants as you know them. Low, dark, leathery mats spread like drying puddles, amber-green and black, no flowers, no fruit, only a fine stitchwork of filaments rooting not into soil but into manufactured topsoil — ground-up ancestors of the plain itself. They need no water because there is none to need. They drink light directly, and take their chemistry from dust and from trace gases in the air, and they open and close with the brilliance of the day as other plants open to rain. They are sparse because the plain is bright and hot and hungry, and only in the softest dust, in the shade of a fin-field, in the lee of a heat vault, can they hold. Where they grow, the air is very slightly sweeter, and the ground's light-conduction is altered, as if the mat were a dark, living lens.

Fauna is rarer still.

You may walk a whole season and see none. Then, a kilometer off, a shape moves against the bright haze and resolves: a grazer, pale as the ground, barrel-bodied, six long legs folded to keep its belly off the warm skin, moving with a slowness that seems designed to match the plain's own drift. It is solitary. Food is sparse, water is internal — it carries its life's moisture sealed inside itself in crystalline stores, painstakingly recycled, never excreted except as mineral pellets that become dust — and it listens, and navigates, largely through its feet, feeling the minute tremors of the plate-breathing and the distant passage of carriers below. It is not preyed upon in any way you can see. The predators here are mostly machines: harvester swarms that descend on a patch of light like gnats and graze it clean, repair swarms that follow them and knit the grazed fins smooth, cleaner drones that hunt both, a silent ecology of makers eating makers in endless, balanced cycles. The grazer is an anomaly, a sealed, wandering visitor from an older, wetter logic, tolerating the Brightflat because the Brightflat tolerates almost anything, provided it does not make a noise or waste matter.

The rules follow from that.

First: light is the only input that matters. Everything else is rearrangement. Second: heat must go somewhere, and down and up are the only directions. The deep sink takes it, the sky takes it, and the plain's warm skin is simply the thermal compromise between the two. Third: nothing is lost. A body that dies here is unmade, molecule by molecule, into dust; a machine that fails is veld into the ground; a storm is only the wind moving the world from one place to another. Fourth: silence is not an accident but an engineering ethic, kept so long it has become a climate. You learn to hear with your feet, to see movement in the shiver of light on the plates, to sense a glider by the brief, moving shadow it throws like a fish under ice.

At dusk — for there is a dusk, though the light is so bright that dusk is a long, gradual dimming rather than a fall — the stored day comes back up. Patches of the plain glow faint amber from within, maps of where the sun was harvested at noon, slowly cooling. The gliders' lanes turn to strings of soft white beads. The stitchmats close. The grazer, if you are lucky enough to still see it, settles its legs and becomes a pale mound indistinguishable from the ground that made it, ancient and new at once, waiting out the bright cold of the night without a sound.

And then you walk on. The horizon does not come closer. The light does not diminish. Under your feet, the warm, made, breathing skin goes on for thousands of kilometers in every direction, dense with silent presences, alive with slow and fast processes, polished by an age no record can hold, built and rebuilt until building and erosion are the same thing. It is a place that has finished becoming itself, and therefore never finishes moving.
