**The Weeping Works**

It would fit inside a small town. From the low ridge on its north side you can see the whole of it at once — a shallow bowl perhaps three hundred paces across, ringed by a crumbling stone wall and a line of wire that was once a fence. It looks, at first, like a garden that has forgotten its gardeners.

You can walk around it in an afternoon. You cannot walk through it in an afternoon. That is the first contradiction the place teaches you.

The air changes as you come down into the bowl. The elevation is barely lower than the fields outside, but everything in here is lower in another sense: light ends sooner, sound ends sooner, the horizon ends sooner. A warm, wet weight settles over you, not rain, not quite mist, but the atmosphere itself condensing. Your skin is damp before you have touched anything. The ground does not look like ground. It is a skin of living matter, spongy and interwoven, roots and rhizomes and decaying leaves pressed so tightly together that soil is only visible where a foot has pried it open — and even then the wound weeps a little water and begins to close.

There is no empty space here. The density is not that of a jungle you can cut a path through; it is saturated, layered to the point of suffocation. Canopy, then a second canopy just beneath it, then a hanging thicket of vines, then standing stems, then a ground layer so packed with ferns and grasses and moss that the lower two feet are a separate, darker country. Epiphytes coat every trunk. Fruits hang in the understory where flowers should be. Water stands in the cup of every leaf, in the joints of every stem, in shallow marsh-channels no wider than your hand that meander between the roots without ever quite deciding to flow.

The water is always near the surface. Step anywhere and the ground yields, then rises back around your ankle with a soft squelch. Stones weep. Not metaphorically: mineral springs seep from the low wall, beading and running in slow, bright lines, staining the bricks a rusty orange. The core of the bowl is a bog that has never cleared, carpeted in duckweed and algae and sheets of a flat, pressed-leaf plant that grows in overlapping plates, like tin laid over water. Everything is post-rain here, permanently. Condensation runs off the undersides of leaves in a continuous, irregular percussion. It drips from the canopy in a sound that is close, personal, and never stops.

It takes a while to understand the scattered built things, because the plants have not left them as ruins. They have settled them.

A boiler, half-sunk, its dome a hill under boiler-moss and copper vine. A chimney whose brick has softened over a long, established time to the texture of river stone, now a hollow trunk where a creeper has grown up through the flue and out the top in a plume of green. A shed roof, inverted, holds rainwater like a great leaf and feeds it into a pipe-grass that runs down its rib. The old conveyor belt has become a bridge, woven over and under with roots until it is stronger as plant than as rubber. An engine block lies on its side in the marsh-channel, its cylinders filled with reeds. It is not broken, exactly. It has been eroded into balance. Iron has rusted, softened, been eaten. The plants take the rust. The leaf-tips near the engine blush russet and orange where the iron feeds them.

Tech did not leave here. It was absorbed and slowed. Somewhere under the root-mat a generator still turns, but at a pace so gradual you would swear it is not turning at all — a flywheel that completes a revolution in a minute, in five minutes, producing a deep groan, a clunk, a shudder that travels up through the wet ground and out through the soles of your feet. Electric wire runs through the vines, not as a fence anymore but as a nerve. On humid afternoons you feel it before you hear it: a faint prickle in the air, and the coppery roots spark almost imperceptibly where they cross a bolt, a tendon of liana. The plants have learned the shapes of industry and they repeat them without meaning to. New leaves unfurl already folded, creased in right angles. Stems grow in straight runs, then clip themselves*. Vines root in perfect rows along the old concrete seams, following the lines where men once laid foundations, as if the foundations are still instructing them. Fruits ripen and fall and ping, quietly lit, like rivets dropping into a drum.

The sound would be, if there were animals, an understatement to call it loud. There almost are no animals. That absence is what makes the noise noticeable. No birds. No insects in any number. On the second day you might see one frog in a leaf-puddle, very still, or the ripple where a single salamander moves in the marsh-channel. The fauna is rare enough that its presence feels like an event, and its absence feels deliberate. The place does not need it. Plant does the work of animal here: it pollinates by wind and by its own rubbing, it moves by seed and by growth, it defends itself by sheer, impassable thickness.

So the loudness is something else. It is water clattering on sheet-leaves. It is fruit falling a foot, then a bone-white liana snapping back. It is the hiss of steam escaping through a hollow stem where a pipe cracked long ago and now lives. It is the long, slow creak of the whole mat breathing, expanding in the warm afternoon and contracting, minutely, at night. It is constant dripping into metal — drip, drip, drip into the boiler, into the inverted roofs, into the engine cylinders — so that the bowl is filled with a polyrhythm of small metallic notes, never in time with each other, never stopping. It is surprisingly industrial, this green quiet. Like listening to a factory from inside its lungs.

Light is normal, daylight is daylight, but daylight rarely arrives unmediated. It comes through in mottled, green-gold patches that never stay still because the leaves are always settling, shedding, replacing themselves. In the open center, where the marsh-plates catch the sun, the reflections double and dazzle. Every surface is wet, every surface reflects. You are lit from below by the water as often as from above by the sky. On overcast days the whole bowl glows faintly, a sourceless phosphorescence from trapped light and wet photosynthesis, warm on your face like a greenhouse lamp that has been on for a hundred years.

And that, perhaps, is the timescale. Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks freshly destroyed. This is not a collapse. This is a settlement. Erosion has had time to work the brick soft, to round the edges of the concrete, to rust the steel down to soil, to grow moss into the mortar until the wall is part wall, part garden. The growth itself, though it must be constant, is slow in its effects. The form is finished. The place has reached its packed, self-consistent state and now changes only internally: a leaf dies and is instantly replaced in the same square inch, a root thickens by a hair, the generator turns one more slow revolution, the marsh rises by a finger's breadth after a week of condensation and sinks again. It will be like this for perhaps a century more before anything shifts in a way a human could mark on a map.

If you stay still long enough, your stillness is incorporated. A vine, finding your boot immobile, puts out a tendril and tests the lace. A film of algae begins on the skin of your sleeve. The dripping from above finds your shoulders and runs, warm, down your back. You realize the plants are not overtaking the machines, or invading them. They are using them. The pipes are vessels, the wires are nerves, the flat sheets are leaves, the flywheel is a heart beating slowly enough that you have to believe in it to see it move.

The Works does not want anything from you. It is too complete. It is a small, low, warm, wet world that holds all its water and all its green and all its slow iron inside a boundary you could see across in a throw, and yet the interior is so saturated that crossing it is a journey of hours of pushing, ducking, climbing, listening. It weeps, it hums, it drips, it grows in rows. It has been in balance a long time, and it intends, patiently, to stay that way.
