It is high, and it is small.

That is the first contradiction the place gives you, and it stays. You come up through thinning air — not thin in the way of cold peaks, but thinned and warm, sun on the back of your neck even though the air is wet enough to bead on your skin — and you arrive not on a summit ridge but inside something. A shallow, rock-rimmed hollow, perhaps a hundred paces across, bounded all the way round by leaning crags and a fall of cloud. From the highest stone inside it you can take in the whole of it at once, and yet it is old enough that its forms have settled. Nothing here looks new, or sharp, or in the middle of being made. Everything looks as if it has decided what it is and agreed to stay that way, patiently, for a very long time.

The ground is mostly stone. Dark, pitted, grey-black rock, layered where it breaks and soft where it doesn't, worn into broad shallow hollows and low domes. Edges that must once have been angular have been worried smooth — not by rivers, there are no rivers here, but by the same small amount of water falling in the same places for centuries. Along the inside faces of the rim, drip-lines have polished themselves into grooves only as wide as a finger, curving down to a lip and then letting go. In those grooves the rock is darker, and sometimes rust-orange with algae, sometimes slick with a film that looks like oil and smells faintly of iron and wet earth. Between the hollows the stone is warm. The sun finds it even under cloud, because the light here is ordinary daylight — white, true-coloured, moving at the usual pace — just broken and softened by mist that is never quite gone.

Water is not an event here. It is a condition. The air is condensing all the time, a little at a time, so that your hair dampens without rain, and leaves shed without being rained on. Every surface weeps. You can put your palm flat on a rock that has been in sun and feel it cool and wet underneath, as if the stone itself were sweating under its own warmth. After a while you understand that it is: in the day the rock exhales its held moisture, in the night it drinks it back, very slowly. Pools are small and shallow and numerous out of proportion to the size of the place — in stone basins no bigger than a bowl, in the cups of rosette plants, in the spoon of a fallen leaf that has kept its shape for a season. They are not connected. They do not flow. A footprint fills immediately and stays, reflecting the sky, and then, after a long time, sinks away into the ground again. The soil, where there is soil, is thin, black, spongy, saturated a few centimetres down and springy underfoot, as if the high ground were sitting on a hidden reservoir that sighs up through it.

Because of that, the smell is permanent post-rain: wet stone, fern crushed underfoot, humus, something green and faintly sweet. It does not intensify and fade. It just is.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not dense. That is the second contradiction, and it governs everything. You walk a long way between plants. Ten, twenty paces of warm bare rock, moss in patches, damp grit, and then a single presence: a tree-fern unfurling one frond at a time, each frond long as your arm, the trunk furred and ringed; a broadleaf clump whose leaves are each a hand across, thick, translucent at the veins where the light comes through; a vine that has climbed not a tree but a leaning slab and pinned itself there with aerial roots like guy-ropes; a rosette low to the ground holding a perfect, still pool in its centre that may have been there, refilling, for years. Then nothing again for a while but stone and moss and the sound of dripping.

Each plant seems to have been there a very long time and to be in no hurry to be anywhere else. Growth is by accretion: moss an inch thick over a fallen trunk that is now only a moss-covered ridge; lichen in slow, perfect discs; a fern whose new frond this year is still tight as a shepherd's crook, and will not open for another slow season. The trees lean inward, all of them, slightly, toward the centre of the hollow, as if listening to something. Their leaves drip in private rhythms.

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. It is the accumulation of small misfits. Some leaves, when the light hits them right, are clear enough that you can see the water moving inside them, a slow circulation, upward against the stem. Some stones are hollow underneath in natural chambers, and when a drip finds entrance to one it booms, a low, wooden, drum-like note that rolls across the whole hollow and shakes the dangling fronds. At first you think the sound must be coming from animals. It isn't, mostly. It is water hitting stone basins and hollow stems and the taut skin of a big leaf, amplified by the concave rim. The sound of the place is loud, percussive, irregular — drip, drip, a sudden hollow *bong* from the high ledge, wind combing through a stand of dead fern stems tuned like pipes — and then, between those notes, a silence so complete you can hear your own swallow. The loudness is not chaos. It is the stone answering, every time it is touched.

Fauna are rare in its proper sense: not absent, but spaced so far apart in time that you will likely leave having seen almost none. A pale, eyeless amphibian perhaps, in one of the warm pools, that does not so much swim as reposition itself once an hour. A beetle, shiny black, on a moss hummock, moving with great deliberation. Signs that are fresher than anything you expect: a trail compressed in the damp soil that must be days old and still holds its shape; a hair, or something like a hair, caught on a fern; a footprint beside a basin that you cannot match to anything you have seen. Once — very rarely — a call starts somewhere beyond the rim and comes into the hollow and is enormously amplified by it, as if the caller were just overhead, and then ceases, and does not resume for a very long time. The animals do not live here in numbers. They pass through the high wet air, or they live here one to a hollow, for a lifetime.

There is nothing built. No path, no cairn, no cut mark, no ash, no fibre, no flake that is certainly a tool and not a natural split. The stone would flake easily if you tried; the wood is ample; the place is, in that sense, exactly at the stage before making begins, or after making has been utterly weathered away beyond recognition — but the erosion is so settled, so established, that you begin to think the second is wrong. This hollow has been in its present state longer than any maker would have had a reason to stay. The only disturbance you can read is speculative: a moss ridge where something large once fell or lay, the polished groove where hands might once have gripped to climb, ambiguous at best. Your own footprints are the newest feature, and within minutes even they are softening, filling, recovering.

Time here does not move in events. The dynamic is slow down to the molecular: a basin filling a fraction of a millimetre a year from a single drip; a tree that fell before your grandparents' time now indistinguishable from the ground except by its rippling line of moss; temperature rising through the morning and falling through the evening with less than a few degrees of difference, weak gradients everywhere, water creeping rather than running, roots creeping rather than branching in haste. It has the feel not of a young landscape being carved, but of an old one that has finished carving itself and now maintains, drip by drip, its own shape.

And so you stay a while, because there is a while to stay in. The whole world of this place is visible from the stone you are standing on — rim, hollows, scattered giants of plants, pools catching light — and yet you could spend that whole while discovering only that you have not seen it all, because the things that happen here happen too slowly to be seen, and the things that are here are too far apart to be taken in together. The mist thickens at the edges and thins in the middle. A drip finds the hollow stone and booms, and a frond shivers, and somewhere an unseen animal moves once and is still again. The rock under your hand is warm and wet at the same time, giving up water and holding it, in balance, as it has done and will do.

It ends where the rim ends, abruptly, in a drop into cloud that is both lower and warmer than the air you came through. But inside, enclosed room of a world, high and damp and lush and empty, it goes on without you, at its own pace, weeping, listening inward, making its small loud music from water and stone.
