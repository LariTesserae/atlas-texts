**The Weeping Height**

It begins with the light, and the wet in the air.

You have come up high — high enough that your breath comes shorter and the cool does not lift even under full sun. The light is bright and white and close. There is thinness to it. It does not warm so much as it bleaches. Below your feet and out to the horizon everything is answering it: wet stone, shallow water, pale lime, clay tile. The pans of water are mirrors squared off by the sky, so even in shadow the ground gives back light, bluish and doubled. Mist does not drift so much as hang. It condenses on your hair, on your sleeves, on the back of your neck, and keeps hanging there because the air is saturated and cold and almost still.

Except for the water, it is quiet.

Not empty in the sense of silence — there is a constant, soft multiplex of drips. A tick from a joint. A sheet of water whispering over a lip. A low, slow splash where a channel drops one hand's breadth to another. It never builds. Nothing rushes. The sound is patient and it comes from everywhere and nowhere in particular, because water is everywhere.

There is no natural ground here. Not a patch of it. You look for a place to step that was not made and you do not find one.

This is a high, open plateau built entirely of itself, and it is packed past any allowance for space. Basin touches basin, wall leans directly on channel, stair rises directly out of a pool, channel crosses over a passage on a little stone bridge, passage runs under a basin. A slab's edge is another slab's wall. A drain is a step. A step is a drain. Terraces descend in sharp levels for as far as you can see through the mist, and on the far side they rise again in the same hard geometry — not hills, but courses of work. From a distance it could read for a town, or a valley of reservoirs. Up close it reads for one continuous, interlocked surface, saturated. There is nowhere unoccupied, nowhere left over. Even the flat places are tiled and grooved and edged. Your shoulder brushes a wall when you turn. Your foot lands on something cut, laid, jointed, or fired.

The materials are few and they are all mineral. Basalt and granite blocks hammered to size, slate slabs, unfired and fired clay, lime mortar, copper staples hammering two stones together. No soil anywhere between them. Where earth might have been, there is a manufactured fill: crushed stone, clay puddled and beaten flat, tile made in low stone kilns whose mouths are cold now. The work is primitive and you can read the hands in it. Chisel stripes still show on fresh faces. Hammerstones rest in niches where they were set down. Clay pipes are joined with lime and slightly out of true, and the joints weep. Copper pins are driven, not cast. There are no engines, no wheels in use, no machines with moving parts except the simple lifts and stone levers that raise a sluice and let it fall again by its own weight.

Everything is young. That is the second strangeness. The edges have not been softened. Corners are knife-sharp. The channels are crisp V's and U's, not yet rounded by flow. Mortar is still pale and clean, almost white where it has not yet darkened with damp. A face recently split shows its fresh interior, pale and unweathered, and will cut your palm if you are careless. Dirt does not accumulate in cracks because there is barely time for dirt to stay, and water is always taking it away. Geologically and humanly, this place has only just emerged — a newly raised, newly built height — and it has not yet had time to become shabby.

And yet water is already its element. It is wet in the way of a marsh-edge without any marsh. Water lies near the surface under every slab and finds the joints. It weeps. It seeps upward through the lime in dark, spreading damp that grows slowly and never dries. In the covered sections it does something unusual: the cold stone sweats the cloud as it passes through, so it rains *inside* the vaulted channels, a fine, slow rain falling from the ceiling onto the water below, in bright weather, while you stand dry in the open. Condensation beads on the undersides of overhangs and falls in slow, separate drops, each one catching the light before it goes. Every basin is full to a measured lip. Every walkway has a shallow trickle running beside it, ankle-deep in places, sheeting thin over stone, always moving but never moving fast. Mud there is none. Your soles are wet but not dirty.

That wetness, and the brightness, and the constant washing over polished, soil-less stone, is why nothing green lives here. There are no plants — no grass between slabs, no vines in cracks, no trees to break the lines of the terraces. There is nothing for a seed to root in, nothing to hold it against the water, nothing to shade it from the glare, and nowhere for it to be left alone. The smell is not earthy. It is cold stone and wet clay and lime, clean to the point of sterility.

No animals either. No birds cross the brightness. No insects rise at your ear. No footprint crosses a wet slab except the faint drag of a tool. Life as you know it has been designed out, not by cruelty but by function. The water is too cold, too clean, too constantly renewed to feed anything. It has been caught from cloud and condensation and rain and led through stone until it is almost mineral water itself.

What lives here, in the loosest sense, is what can live on mineral and absence of light. In the deepest, dampest seams, under the lips of basins, inside the vaults where the inner rain falls, there are pale grey-white fungal films, a few finger-widths wide, slower than spreading. They blacken a copper joint here, whiten a lime course there. They grow millimetres in a season. They are not plants and they do not make the place look alive. Up close they look like damp shadow. From a little way off, other growths are more deceptive: constellations of white mineral crust where water has evaporated from a pan and left its lime behind, branching, rosette-like, in pools that have been left standing. They look, startlingly, like small flowers, or frost, or snow that has forgotten to melt. They are not. They are the water's record of itself.

The rules of the place are the rules of its water, and they are simple and slow.

First, water must never stop moving entirely, and must never pool dirty. A channel cleared by hand is a channel that will run clear.

Second, nothing organic is allowed to enter the system. Everything that is here was made here from stone, clay, lime, and a little copper. It all can be washed, and it all is being washed.

Third, everything must give way downhill, eventually, patiently. This is a sponge and a reservoir built on a height to catch what the sky gives — cloud, condensation, rain — hold it in its thousands of shallow pans and pipes and vaults, and release it in a slow, season-long seep to the terraces below. It fills over weeks. It empties over weeks. The stone itself is part of the storage; under the slabs the fill drinks and holds and weeps again.

You feel the slowness in everything. A drop forms, hangs, falls. A damp stain widens by the width of a hair in a day. A mineral rosette extends its points as the pan evaporates in the cool brightness. A shadow creeps across a basin and the reflected sky goes from pale blue to grey and back. Nothing here is in a hurry because nothing here can be. The gradients are weak, the air is cool, the water spreads by seep and sheet rather than by pour. Change is happening, but on a timescale that makes the fresh, sharp youngness of the place feel both new and permanent.

You walk on. The terraces do not end at any edge you can name. They step down and step up, basin and wall and channel and stair, under the same bright, wet, quiet air, the stone still sharp under your hand, the water still weeping from joints that were made by hand and have not yet learned to crumble.
