The first thing is that it is not dark.

You are below the surface — not far, perhaps fifteen metres of rock and soil overhead, but far enough that the outside world has gone quiet and close. And yet the chamber is bright. Not bright like a field. Bright in one place, violently bright, and then falling away into shadow within a few steps.

The light comes from above, through a single narrow shaft. It is a collapse chimney, no wider than a man's shoulders at the top, choked partway down with angular blocks, and it admits the midday sun almost straight down. In the morning it misses the floor entirely and strikes the upper wall. At noon it hits the floor like a poured column of white. The rock around the shaft is bleached pale where the light hits it, and in shadow it is the colour of wet slate. Dust does not hang in the beam — there is no dust to hang. The air is too wet for it. What hangs there is steam, and the steam catches the light and makes it solid for a moment, then thins again.

The second thing is heat.

It arrives before the sight settles. The air is thick and saturated, warm enough that sweat does not evaporate so much as condense. Your skin is already damp. The rock under your palm is warm, not merely sun-warmed, but warm from within: thirty-eight, forty degrees in the big slabs. Lower down, nearer the pools, it is hotter. You breathe shallowly without deciding to. The heat is not uniform. Near the shaft, where a thin draught moves, it drops by several degrees and feels almost cool by contrast. At the far end of the chamber, away from the draught, it pools and thickens.

This is a small place. A pocket, really. One room in the rock, maybe eight metres long, three high at the crown, less at the sides where the floor rises. You could, crouching, touch both walls at once. It is embedded in a larger system of dry passages that go nowhere useful and are already half full of their own fall, but this chamber is the only wet, living part, and the only part the light reaches. Everything that matters here happens inside this one enclosure.

It is young. That is visible everywhere once your eyes adjust.

The floor is not smoothed. There are no rounded boulders polished by generations of floodwater, no soft, grey, old cave floor. Instead there are fresh blocks, sharp as broken crockery, pale cream and pinkish where they have fallen from the ceiling in the last collapse that opened the shaft. Their edges would cut. Between them lie flakes of calcite and travertine, thin and knife-edged, not yet dissolved into softer shapes. The walls show the same freshness: thin stalactites a few centimetres long, still translucent, still growing fast enough that you can see the wet shine on them. Nothing has had time to become picturesque. Nothing has had time to become *old*.

And nothing is built. There is no trail, no chalk mark, no piece of wood, no rusted can left by whoever may have peered down the shaft. The water would have taken anything light, and the blocks dropping from above would have buried anything heavy. It is an untouched room, undeveloped in the absolute sense: rock, water, heat, light, and what little life can tolerate them.

The water is the main inhabitant.

It does not flood the chamber. It weeps. All round the walls, in no particular place, water beads and runs in slow, thin sheets down the limestone, joining in rivulets that darken the rock to almost black where they pass. In one corner a seep is constant, a film rather than a stream, making a soft, irregular ticking as it finds drip points. In the centre the floor dips and holds three pools, separate, each no more than a metre across, connected only by a damp skin of rock when the level is high.

The pools are still and mostly clear, with a faint opalescence. Their surfaces are intermittently disturbed — not by wind, for there is almost no wind here, but by drips from above and by their own heat. Every so often a bubble rises from the silt and breaks with a sound too small to call a bubble popping. Where a drip falls into a pool it makes a ring that expands and is gone. The water is hot: tepid at the margins where seep water cools it, uncomfortably hot in the centre, fifty degrees and more where it wells up from a crack that you can barely see. At that crack the water behaves in ways that are difficult to hold steady. It is near the point where heat and pressure turn it from ordinary liquid into something that dissolves silica like a solvent and carries it, then drops it as a glassy skin the instant it cools a few degrees. The edges of the pools are rimmed with that skin — white, then translucent, then faintly orange where iron has come out of solution. It builds little dams, millimetres high, which divert the next rivulet, which builds a new dam. This is fabrication, but no one is fabricating it. The temperature gradient across a few centimetres of rock is doing, continuously and without moving parts, a piece of high-temperature chemistry we can only reproduce in vessels that melt and corrode. The chamber out-engineers us at this.

Because of that, there is no green thing anywhere.

That is the most surprising absence, given the wet and the light. Ordinary caves in wet places grow moss at the entrance, algae in the sunbeam, ferns where soil has gathered. Here there is no soil. The light, where it lands, is too hard and too hot. The water, where it lands, is too hot and too alkaline and too laden with minerals for a seed or a spore to establish. The rock is too new, too sharp, too frequently flushed and re-coated with sinter. A plant would need a quiet, cool, stable place to root. This room offers none.

What life there is, is simpler and sparser.

In the cooler rim of the main pool, where the water drops to the low forties, there are thin films: grey-white, slightly gelatinous, wrinkled like wet tissue paper. They are bacterial mats, oxidising sulphur and iron in the water, building structure out of chemistry instead of sunlight. They have no colour to speak of. They are easy to miss, and they cover perhaps a square metre in total, in patches. Scattered much more thinly are amphipods — eight millimetres long, translucent, eyeless, flicking sideways in the shallows. You might see two or three in five minutes of watching. You might see none.

Higher up, on a dry ledge just out of direct drip, near where the shaft air is cooler, there are springtails, white specks that jump when the rock warms. And very rarely — once, perhaps, in an hour — a cave cricket strays down from the dry passage above and sits motionless on a block, antennae moving, before it climbs back toward the dark. That is the whole fauna. No bats roost here, the heat and light drive them off. No frogs, no fish. The food chain, if you can call it that, runs from dissolved minerals to bacteria to these few grazers, and it is balanced with a great deal of emptiness.

For emptiness is the other dominant condition. Most of the floor is bare, wet rock. Most of the walls are bare, wet rock. The formations are isolated, not a forest: one cluster of aragonite needles like a small frozen bush in the corner, one low travertine dam across a rivulet, one orange crust where iron precipitates. Between them, metres of nothing but slick stone and a shallow sheen of water. The sparse-ness makes the few active spots stand out more — the crack that bubbles, the seep that ticks, the mat that slowly shifts colour as a pulse of warmer water passes through it.

Sound follows the same pattern: mostly quiet, then a small event, then quiet again.

You hear your own breathing, and the soft tick of cooling mineral as a block contracts. You hear water finding its way: not a stream's rush, but a trickle over sinter steps, a drip that takes a long time to form, swells, falls, rings in a pool, silences. Occasionally, when a particularly hot drip hits a particularly hot part of a pool, there is a faint hiss, like a comma of steam escaping. The shaft above lets in almost nothing from outside — no birdsong, no traffic, no wind. The chamber makes its own muffled acoustics and then stops.

And it is active, despite the quiet. The activity is not movement you can watch so much as change you can infer. The sun moves across the shaft and the bright column moves with it, heating rock that then radiates back overnight. The heated pools evaporate. The vapour condenses on the cooler upper walls and on the undersides of the fresh blocks, and runs down again. The seep in the corner strengthens after a hot morning and weakens after a cool one. A rim of sinter that was yesterday at the pool's edge is today under water, and a new rim is building a centimetre beyond it. Nothing here is static except, at any given moment, the rock. Even that is precipitating, dissolving, re-precipitating.

Nothing here is strange, in the end. No glow from below, no impossible shapes, no life that breaks the rules. It is a hot, wet, young cave pocket doing exactly what hot, wet, young cave pockets do, but doing it in a small, sealed, sunlit room where you can see the whole cycle at once. Water comes in mineral-laden and leaves as vapour. Heat comes from the sun through a hole and from the earth through a crack. Light comes from above and is wasted as heat. Rock falls, dissolves, rebuilds itself in miniature. Bacteria live where the water is just cool enough. Everything else stays away.

You could sit here, if you could tolerate the heat, and watch the pools for an hour and see almost nothing happen. And then a drip would fall, a bubble would rise, a crystal would tick as it cooled, and you would understand that the room is not resting. It is working, slowly, in its own confined way, building itself fresh, block by sharp block, in the dark below a bright sky.
