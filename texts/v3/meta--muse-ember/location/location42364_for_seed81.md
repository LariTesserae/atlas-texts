First it is only the height that tells you something is wrong.

You climb out of the lower country for a day and a half, expecting the air to thin and cool, and instead it thickens. The plateau is broad and foundered, a high table of dark basalt broken into terraces, and every step you take is warm. The sun is somewhere behind a ceiling that never lifts, a pale smudge in a sky the color of dirty pearl. It never brightens. It never quite sets. Under it the air is heavy, saturated, and hot — not desert hot, not dry, but boiler-room hot, the kind that settles on your skin and will not evaporate. You sweat and the sweat does nothing. It joins the moisture already in the air.

There is no soil here, and nothing green.

The ground is mineral all the way down: black glassy stone polished by water, grey travertine steps, pans of shallow water that catch the dim light and hold it flat. Between the slabs, everywhere, water wells up through cracks and stands in sheets a few inches deep. It is warm to the touch. It shimmers with heat. At its edges it leaves a crust of white and rust-colored salt, like frost that has learned to build. If you step in the shallows your footprint fills at once and stops shining — the water closes over it, but the mark of your weight remains in the mineral film below.

It does not rain here in any ordinary sense. The water comes from underneath, near the surface, pushed up by the hot rock. It seeps. It weeps.

You begin to see it on the rocks first: dark faces with damp, clean streaks running down them, as if the stone itself is sweating in slow lines. Then on the first structures, where the streaks are wider, more deliberate. The place is not a ruin and it is not new. It is settled. Everything has come to its own equilibrium and stopped pretending to be sharp.

The city — when you finally find it, and you find it long after you have known it was there by the steam columns — is not a city in the way a town is a city. From the ridge it spreads open across the whole basin, valley to horizon, multiple districts laid out on a grid that the water has partly reclaimed. But the density of it is almost nothing. The buildings are enormous and they are far apart.

A factory hall may be half a kilometer long, roofed in sawtooth ceramic tiles glazed black-green, and the next hall may be two kilometers away across an empty pan of warm water, connected only by a raised causeway, a pipeline, a conveyor spine on pylons. The air between presences is vast. Inside the halls it is vaster still — hollow, echoing volumes where the machinery sits small in the middle, humming very slowly, and moisture drips from the rafters in a steady, irregular rain that has nothing to do with weather.

What is built here is industrial on a scale that no longer needs people. Stack fields stand like organ pipes. Condenser terraces step down toward the pans in broad, empty frames. Cisterns wider than lakes are roofed in copper sheet gone verdigris, and their sides are streaked where water overflows the joints and runs, perfectly neat, all the way to the ground. Conduits run everywhere at knee height and at head height, parallel, banded, sweating. Their surfaces are wet even where the paint has long since weathered to powder. The metal rusts, but it does not flake and fall apart. The rust has thickened into a stable skin, in balance with the damp. Concrete has slumped a fraction and then fixed itself there. Brick has darkened and polished. The erosion is not dramatic. It is complete. Nothing here looks fresh, nothing looks freshly broken. The city has been in this state for a very long time.

That is one of the rules of the place: the water does not leave.

Heat drives it up out of the stone. The air cannot hold any more of it, so it hangs as a permanent low canopy that turns the light to dusk and keeps the temperature high even at altitude — geothermal heat trapped under its own steam. Then, on the highest and coldest surfaces — the tops of stacks, the outer faces of the condenser roofs, the unused glass of the control houses — the vapor condenses first and runs down the outside, soaking back into the ground pans, to be heated again. It is a closed loop, sideways and vertical at once. The city lives inside its own exhalation. You can stand in a building and watch water climb a wall in a thin, trembling sheet, reach a seam, and fall. Or run, inexplicably, a few inches uphill inside a pipe, ticking as the metal expands.

The light stays dim because the cycle never breaks. There is a day in the sense that the pale blur in the sky grows a fraction brighter and the stacks exhale more strongly, and the pans rise perhaps half a meter over a long morning, then fall back toward evening as the stone gives up its heat differently, and condensation thickens on every surface like a second skin. No hard shadows are ever cast. Edges soften. Distance does not look far, it looks muffled, as if the whole basin were under glass that has fogged.

Sound is the same. For all the machinery, the place is mostly quiet. A pump somewhere pulses once every few seconds, too distant to locate, like a slow heart in another valley. Metal ticks as it cools and warms. Water drips, constantly, but the drips do not form a rhythm you can follow; they are too many and too far apart, scattered across the empty volumes. Your own voice comes back flat and shortened, swallowed by the wet air before it can travel between buildings. The conveyor spines move, but at a pace that is almost still — a sorting arm lifting, pausing, lowering, mineral crust flaking off in white plates that dissolve in the pans below.

There is almost no life in the usual sense, and what life there is works at the edge of recognition.

No plants take root in the hot, saline shallows or in the slag and alkali between the halls. There are no roots because there is no soil to hold them and no direct light to feed them, and every flood is mineral-laced and warm. What grows instead is the place’s own mineral ecology: calcite reefs building up in the gutters where water boils over, salt flowers in the joints of the concrete, thin grey-green bacterial mats in the quietest pools that look like oil and heal themselves when you disturb them. Inside the pipes, in darkness and heat, fungal mats thread through the rust, feasting on iron and old grease, growing in slow, blind ropes.

Fauna are rarer still. In the sumps and wide cisterns you may, if you wait long enough standing still, see them: pale, eyeless, eel-like filterers that cruise just under the warm surface, stirring the salt crust in lazy spirals. They do not come up for air. They have no need to. Once in a very long while — on the rising part of the cycle, when a thermal lifts off the pans — something crosses high overhead, a shape with a broad skin wing and no feathers, riding the updraft without flapping, so silent that you notice it only by the change in the light where it passes between you and the pale smudge of the sun. It is not hunting. Nothing here hunts, really. There is too little to hunt. Predator and prey have collapsed into the slow exchange between water, stone, and machine.

The machines, in their way, are the fauna. They patrol the causeways at night-cycle — not robots in any dramatic sense, but low, box-like sleds that ride the rails between halls, lifting mineral plates, clearing a gutter, sealing a joint, then stopping for a long time with a single amber light on. No one tends them, or if anyone ever did, the footprints on the causeways are all filled with water now, identical and old. The city is urban in its scale and its organization, empty in its occupancy, and it continues anyway because the loop continues. Industry here is not production for a market. It is maintenance of the loop: catching water, moving it, condensing it, letting it fall, catching it again.

If you stay, your sense of time loosens. Nothing decays quickly and nothing decays dramatically. The city does not crumble; it encrusts. Where a pipe leaks persistently, a new mineral growth bulges from the joint, rough and white, slowly armoring the metal it is in the process of preserving. Where a road sags, water settles in the sag and polishes it to a mirror. The buildings are not returning to nature — there is no nature here to return to. They are becoming part of the geology they are built on, hot rock and wet stone and iron all settling into one damp, steaming equilibrium.

That is the strange heart of it, held together: high and hot, wet and dim, built and empty, industrial and silent. A plateau city that never dries, never greens, never quite breaks, and never quite wakes. Under the pearl-colored sky the pans go on reflecting it, the stacks go on breathing out steam that becomes the sky again, and somewhere far across the empty water a pump pulses, once, and then again, and the drip from a rafter falls into a hall big enough to hold a weather system, and makes almost no sound at all.
