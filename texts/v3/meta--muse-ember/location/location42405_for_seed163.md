First it is the light, and the noise. Neither arrives gradually.

You come up and over a bare rock shoulder, about as high as weather gets, and the plateau opens out in front of you below a sun that has almost no air to soften it. It is bright in the way high places are bright — white off wet stone, white off still water, white off the sky itself until the shadows of the hummocks go black and sharp-edged. In the depths, in the morning, there is mist held in the hollows, but the sun takes it in minutes and leaves it as condensation: a sweat on every cold surface, beading on orange-painted steel, dripping from rock ledges long after any rain.

And under that: a deafening. Not an event, a condition. The wind has nothing to break against up here. It crosses kilometers of open, low, flat ground and hits the rock benches and comes back, so you hear the wind hit the rock *and* the wind arriving. Braided streams run everywhere over bedrock with no banks to speak of, spilling, ticking, hammering down little steps. Sedge and cotton-grass heads shiver together and make a static that never drops. And laid over all of it, from somewhere you cannot at first locate, is the industrial tone — a transformer hum, a pump cycling on and off in a basin half a valley away, a small wind turbine turning too slowly for altitude. Sound travels too well here. There are no trees to swallow it, no deep soil. Everything is hard, wet, and plant-thin.

This is a high, cool marsh that is still building itself.

You do not stand on earth. You stand on plant. The whole plain — valley-size, several kilometers of it, cupped between two ridges and dropping away at the far end into country that is already below the cloud — is covered, or in the act of being covered, by a living substrate. Sphagnum moss in pale, almost luminous green, flattened sedges, cushion plants pressed tight to the stone, moss again, fine grass roots knitted into a mat a hand's thickness thick. Where the mat is complete, it is springy and waterlogged and gives under a boot with a soft sucking sound, and water rises up around the sole. Where it is not complete — and that is much of it, in great pale stretches — you see the rule of the place.

The geology is raw and unfinished. The rock benches have not been softened or rounded. Frost still shatters them at night into angular shards. Streams cut no gullies; they wander across bare slabs, separate, rejoin. The plant mat is advancing across this stone in slow, visible fronts. At the edge, sphagnum is a bright green fringe a few centimeters wide, blistering up over clean granite. Behind it, the mat is older, darker, sagging. Ahead of it, empty wet rock weeps and shines. That is the whole landscape, really: a frontier of vegetation, patiently laying down soil where there was none.

Erosion, in the usual sense, has barely begun. Nothing here looks worn away. Things look newly laid down and not yet finished. The bog itself is a shallow dome, raising itself a millimeter a year, which is fast for this place. Water is never far below the surface — two centimeters, five — so every footprint fills. After a rain, and it rains often, though the sun is almost always back by noon, the whole plain becomes a field of shallow mirrors reflecting that white sky. The water is dark as tea from the plants, icy cold even in full sun. The air in the sun burns your face and neck, but step into the wind and it is cool enough to make you zip your jacket. Breath fogs at dawn. Metal pipes sweat.

Because the ground is plant, the plants are also the architecture. The hummocks of moss are the only hills. Sedge roots thrown across a channel make a living bridge you can step on. Cotton-grass, in its thousands, lifts a white haze a foot above the mat in summer. There is almost no relief that is not made by living tissue, and no soil that is not made of dead tissue underneath it. Scrape at it — no one does, for long — and you will find water first, then a black peat that is barely peat yet, then stone.

And yet it is sparse. That is the second surprise. Total vegetation does not mean crowded. The mat is low, uniform, and immensely empty. Between one stand of taller sedge and the next there may be twenty meters of open moss plain, open water, open rock. Between the human presences there is more. This is not a populated place, and not a rich one, either. One species of moss dominates for ages, then one sedge, then one cushion plant. Little competes, because the cold and the water and the altitude forbid almost everything else. Life, animal life, is rare to the point of absence. No birdsong, because the noise is already deafening and there are almost no birds to add to it. An occasional ptarmigan exploding up out of a hummock, startlingly loud for one second. Frogs in the pools, clicking. A handful of dragonflies over the water on the warmest afternoons. A sheep track from down below, old and refilling. That is the census. The ecosystem is plants, fungi working in the dark wet mat, microbes, water, stone, and — very thinly laid over the top — machines.

The built world is scattered almost to invisibility. Three huts, low and orange and bleached by the sun, half a kilometer apart, with roofs weighted against the wind. A relay mast on the highest bench, its guy-wires humming. A generator shed with a door that will not shut right because the moss has heaved under itsfloor. A pipeline, bright when it catches the light, running in a straight line across a landscape that has no straight lines, going down the far slope to a pump house you can hear but barely see. A small, half-finished concrete retaining wall at a channel crossing, already being undermined by living roots. Rusted drums. A weather station on a pole, solar panel tilted up. Tire ruts that the bog takes back within a season. These are industrial things — pumps, transformers, telemetry, a turbine ticking around slowly — but they are not *in charge*. They are outposts.

They are here, as best as anyone can tell, to measure the place or to try, very cautiously, to drain a little of it without killing it. It does not work quickly. The pumps run, the water level drops an inch, the sphagnum holds its sponge and in a week is wet again. The peat-cutter sits parked at the edge of an experimental cut, which is already softening at the sides, moss creeping back over the black face. Rust here is slow and patient. Everything is slow. The turbine turns. The moss grows. The channels shift after a freeze. The light changes from white to a slightly less white as the day goes on, and the mist comes back into the hollows before dark, and the wind, and the water over stone, and the hum from the basin, continue without variation into the night.

Nothing here is weird. That is perhaps its most normal feature. All of it can be explained, piece by piece: altitude, cold, water, plants colonizing fresh rock, people putting small machines in a loud, bright, empty place to watch it happen. But standing in the middle of it, deafened by wind and water and machinery, dazzled by sun on a plain made entirely of living ground — wet, cool, high, and still coming into being — you get the sense that you are standing on something that has just started, and will take a very long time to finish, and does not need you at all.
