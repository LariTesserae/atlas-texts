It is not a place you arrive at. You sink into it.

For a very long time nothing changes, and that is the first thing to understand. This is not a young landscape still being made, with cliffs falling or valleys cutting themselves fresh. It is old ocean floor, far below the surface of the sea, far below any light that could make a surface visible, smoothed and settled into its final form by currents that have run the same way for tens of millions of years. The crust here is ancient oceanic crust, basalt that rose once from a mid-ocean ridge and has since traveled far from it, cooling, cracking, and then being buried. Volcanic hills remain, but only as broad, drowned swells hundreds of kilometers across, their edges rounded to the point where they are less hills than the backs of sleeping animals. Between them the plain runs almost flat to a horizon that is not a line, but a gradual thickening of black. Nothing rises sharply. Nothing, in the big shapes, is new.

The material underfoot — if there were a foot — is mud. Not sand, not rock, but a silt-clay so fine it feels like flour suspended in water, calcium carbonate shells of microscopic plankton and red clay carried down grain by grain from elsewhere on Earth. It lies in a thin but everywhere-continuous skin, centimeters to meters thick, packed down by its own weight and by the pressure of four or five kilometers of water above. It does not crumble. It holds a mark for a while and then slumps, almost imperceptibly, back. Under that skin is basalt, dark and hard and utterly indifferent, and on top of the skin, in some places, there is another layer of building that is far slower than any tool.

That is where the density starts to assert itself. Because at first this place looks empty, and empty is a lie made by darkness.

Mile after mile the floor is covered, shoulder to shoulder, with black potatoes. Ferromanganese nodules, iron and manganese oxides accreted around a shell fragment, a shark tooth, a core of old mud, a millimeter at a time over millions of years. They lie packed so tightly there is almost no mud visible between them, a cobble-field with no gaps, each one lumpy, rust-black, faintly metallic. Beside them, in the water above, the same packing continues in another form. Every liter of water is crowded with marine snow: the dead, the discarded, the fecal pellets, the molts and houses of plankton from the sunlit world far overhead, falling. It falls not as a storm but as a constant, unending precipitation, flakes and strings and pellets tumbling past each other, turning slowly in the current. You cannot put your hand through a cubic meter of this water without displacing thousands of particles. The water itself is packed. Cold, dense, salty water pressed by its own depth into a slow, heavy medium where molecules, particles, and pressure leave no real emptiness.

And yet the living things you would recognize are rare. That is the second lie the darkness tells — that packed must mean busy in the way a forest is busy.

It is cold. Two degrees, perhaps four where a little heat still bleeds up from below, cold not as weather but as a permanent condition that seeps into everything and slows it. No photosynthesis happens here. No leaf, no frond, no green thing lives on this floor. The nearest vegetation is hundreds of meters overhead, in the last dim blue, and what reaches here from it is only debris, already dead, raining for weeks. The food web is built on that rain, and because the rain is dilute and the cold is great, the animals that eat it are scattered across distances measured in hundreds of meters, kilometers.

You might cross an entire field of nodules and see nothing moving except snow. Then, a pale shape sitting tripod-like on the mud, still as a stone, a fish propping itself on long fin rays to face the current and catch what falls. Further on, a rattail eel gliding low, or a sea pig, a cucumber with legs, plowing a slow, looping track through the mud that will persist for days before the current erases it. A brittle star no bigger than a coin, curled around a nodule. A sponge, white and glassy, using a nodule as its only rock in a world that otherwise has none, growing as slowly as the nodule itself. These are not communities. They are single presences, isolated by cold and hunger, and between them the sediment is where most of the life actually is — bacteria in unimaginable numbers, packed cell to cell in the top centimeters of mud, breaking down the snow, fixing what little chemistry there is to fix. At a few cold seeps, where old sediment releases methane, pale bacterial mats spread like felt, grey-white and sparse, chemosynthetic, the only color that is not black or brown. No built thing interrupts them. No artifact, no wreck, no tool mark older than a sea cucumber's track. Nothing made has ever been made here except shells and nodules, and both are made primitively, by accretion, without hands.

There is no atmosphere here to carry sound or light. That is also not quite true — water carries both better than air, but it takes them apart. Light from above does not reach at all. It is a complete, functional darkness, black not because a cloud covers the sun but because the sun is simply too far away and too scattered to matter. Vision, if you had eyes, would be a few meters of murk; beyond that, even the nodule field dissolves. The only light produced here is made by the rare animals themselves, a brief blue flash when a shrimp-like creature is disturbed, a dim glow along a fish's flank — and because animals are rare, even that is rare. Mostly, black is black.

Sound is the same reduction. The surface world is loud with wind and surf; here there is none of that. The turbulence that is present — and it is present everywhere — makes almost no noise. A bottom current, cold water sinking from the poles and sliding along the floor at twenty or thirty centimeters a second, moves in braided rivers and eddies. It does not roar. It sighs through the gaps between nodules, it lifts the snow and drops it a meter away, it makes ripples of mud migrate slowly across the plain like dunes under a slow wind, it tumbles a particle end over end around a nodule's lee side in a tiny, chaotic vortex that never repeats. At the scale of centimeters, the water is turbulent and unstable, chaotic, forever resuspending what had just settled. At the scale of the landscape, nothing has changed in an age. The turbulence is the old, settled state.

So you might hear, if you could hear, only the most attenuated things: the tick of a snow pellet striking a nodule, the low groan of deep water moving across a continental-sized floor, a sound from a whale a hundred kilometers away arriving flattened and distant through the deep sound channel, then gone. Silence here is not absence of motion. It is absence of resonance. Water swallows impact. Nothing breaks.

That is the rule that governs everything else: everything sinks, and nothing hurries except the water that carries it. A flake that died in sunlight will fall for a month before it feeds a worm. A nodule that is a fist-sized now began when the ancestors of humans were still apes. A current that looks turbulent now was turbulent when dinosaurs swam overhead, and will be when the plain itself has subducted and been remelted. The world is normal in its laws — no part of it violates physics, no light glows without a chemistry, no animal grows without food — and because the laws are normal and the time is long, the strange thing is how little has been disturbed.

Openness here is not grandeur. The plain extends past any immediate horizon in every direction, regional and beyond, plain to rise to trench, plain to plain, always the same materials, the same darkness, the same cold pressure. You cannot see the edge of it because there is no edge to see, only a continuation. And that continuation is crowded at every scale but the one we notice. Nodules packed against nodules. Particles packed against particles. Bacteria packed against bacteria in mud that looks empty. Water packed against water until it is almost a solid you sink through.

In the end, what emerges is not a desert and not a forest, not a calm sea and not a storm. It is a submerged, ancient floor where the surface has finished eroding and the water above it has not finished moving; where silence is total despite constant motion; where darkness is total despite constant falling snow; where life is both everywhere in the sediment and almost nowhere you can point to. It will outlast any observer. The snow will keep falling through the black, the current will keep lifting it, the nodules will keep growing a fraction of a millimeter, and the plain, already settled into its oldest form, will keep being exactly what it is.
