At first there is only water, and it is not a thing in the landscape — it *is* the landscape.

It is cold enough to ache through everything, so clear at the surface and so black a little way down that the two lose their edge. Rain falls without pause out of a low, close cloud ceiling and meets the water in a continuous, hissing wall, so you cannot tell where sky ends and flood begins. If you lift your head — if there is air to lift it into — there is only more of the same wet dark, mist thick as a substance, pressed down by the height of the place. For this is not the bottom of an ocean. It is high ground gone under.

Beneath the water the country still rises and falls as country does. It is a high, cold plateau country, a whole system of ridges and hollows and old valleys, drowned to the same patient level. You feel it before you see it: slopes climbing out of the black in every direction, then falling again into basins that go on further than sight, then climbing once more. On a map this would be a region of many features — successive ranges, each one softened at the crest, each valley a long, blind trough of still water. And yet the water makes it one. It hums through the gaps between the ridges, it covers the divides that once would have been heather and bog and dwarf trees, it has come up and up until only the highest knolls, and the stones on the highest knolls, remember being dry.

The erosion here is not dramatic. Nothing is fresh. No cliff is new, no shore is raw. Long submersion has settled everything into its own state. Edges have gone round. Silt has draped itself like cloth over rock, over peat, over the old riverbeds, filling and smoothing and filling again until the channel lines are only suggestions in the colour of the mud. Granite that must once have been sharp is now a dull, close-grained dark, furred with slime and moss. Amber peat still lies under it in pockets where the high moorland used to be, and when the current stirs it, the water clouds briefly the colour of weak tea and smells of stone and iron and rotting roots. It is an established place, weathered into balance — which makes the movement in it all the more constant. For all its settled form, it is not still.

The sound is loud, and the loudness comes from the water itself. Sound carries far in it and there is nothing else to make a sound. In the narrows between two drowned ridges the current puts its shoulder down and booms, a low, throbbing note you feel in your teeth. On the open silt flats it sighs and drags, moving pebbles, sorting them one by one. Deeper groves of vegetation rustle as a single mass, stems rubbing, leaves like wet paper. Overhead, far and muffled, the rain keeps a static on the surface, and now and then an ice pan — some of the far reaches of this high sea freeze even in the dark — cracks with a report like a shot under water. There are no calls. There is no birdsong from above the rain, no splash of a fish, no whale-note through the black. Only water on stone, water on plant, water on itself.

And then, in the emptiness, the plants.

They are lush beyond any reasonable expectation for cold and dark. There are meadows of them, forests of them, and the space between them is vast — great blank plains of silt and bare rock where nothing grows but a scattered stone, a drift of gravel, a single dark boulder sitting in the current. Grove after grove, then nothing for distances you can swim and swim and still be in nothing. But where they take hold, they take hold completely.

Moss carpets the slopes a hand's breadth thick, in blacks and deep olive and a pale, almost white green where the little light touches it. Kelp-like ribbons rise from the silt and do not so much grow upward as stream, sideways and up together, in slow banners that fold and refold in the current. There are fern-things grown too large, their fronds translucent, their stems corded and woody, reaching for the surface they will never reach. There are root-mats that have come loose from the peat and drift, slowly, in the deep basins, still growing, still green, turning end over end like lazy animals — except there are no animals. There are bulb-like growths anchored to stone that open and close with the pulse of the water, taking in dissolved minerals. There are even trees, or the ghosts of trees: pale trunks from the old high forest, sodden and banded, now used as scaffolding by climbing mosses and by vines that hang from their branches like hair in the current.

None of them flower. There has never been a pollinator here. They spread by fragmentation, by root and creeping stem, by clouds of fine spores that hang in the water like dust in a sunbeam and settle miles away on a suitable slope. Untouched, ungrazed, untrampled, they have become architecture. A grove is a place. You could get lost in one, and the current will carry their scent — green, cold, chemical — through the empty water between.

Because there is no animal life here at all.

That absence is the strangest rule of the world, stranger than the darkness, stranger than the high sea. No fish in the meadows. No shoals turning in the basins, no darting things under the kelp. No birds above the rain, no insects at the surface film, no mammals hauled out on the one or two stones that break the water in a brief, wind-blown moment. Nothing swims, nothing flies, nothing crawls. The water is too cold and too dark and, by some chemistry of stone and peat and long glacial time, too barren for gills and lungs and blood, while being precisely rich for roots — laden with what the rock has leached out over ages. The plants do not photosynthesise in any ordinary way in this lightless place; they absorb, they store, they glow faintly when the current is strong, a phosphorescent pallor along their edges in the blackest water, as if each grove were making its own ghost-light and lending it to its neighbours. A place can be lush and still be starved. This one is.

The primitive works of hands lie scattered through it, sparse as the groves, and they are the other evidence that the emptiness is not primeval. On the submerged knolls, where the water is shallowest and the silt thinnest, there are small dry-stone enclosures, walls of unworked granite piled without mortar, knee-high, roofless, long since grown over with moss to the very lip. There are V-shaped fish weirs in the old river mouths, converging walls of stone leading to an empty pit, built by people who used hand-tools only — chipped stone blades, wooden stakes driven into peat, nets of woven plant fibre now rotted to lace. There are cairns. There are hearth-stones blackened with cold fire. There are a few low huts, rectangular, their doorways facing away from the current, their floors still strewn with flakes of worked stone and the split ends of sodden wood, as if someone had set down a tool and walked out of the water and never come back.

It was an early technology: hide and fibre and stone and wood, a manual craft barely altered in generations. But it is all remains now, partial and faint and occasional. A hut here. A weir a valley away. A cairn on a crest with nothing under it but silt. No smoke. No footprints in the mud that would hold. No bones. Whoever built the traps built them for fish that do not exist and never, perhaps, did — as if the builders came up to this high, cold country expecting the flood to bring life with it, and waited in their stone walls for a harvest that never rose to meet them.

And yet the place keeps moving. The water level breathes almost imperceptibly with the season of melt that comes from the high icefields beyond the cloud, rising a finger's breadth, falling again. Currents comb the silt plains and move the drifting root-islands from one basin to the next, where they ground and take hold and become a new grove. Cold upwellings stir the deep water and bring up a brief plume of peat-brown, then settle. In the depths of the troughs the temperature drops another degree and the vegetation thins to pale threads. At the surface the rain never quite stops, and the ice pans, where they form, turn slowly in the wind, grinding against submerged summits with a sound like distant quarrying.

The light, such as it is, comes from no single source. The polar night is long here, high and cold, and what little day there is is filtered through cloud, through rain, through metres of black water, until it arrives not as light but as a lessening of dark — a blue-grey, then a faint green wash when the aurora is strong far above, shining down through the surface like something remembered. In that wash the groves become visible at great distance, not green so much as luminous, their edges picked out, floating in the black like low clouds in a night sky. Then the aurora passes, and they are gone again, though you can still hear them moving.

Stay long enough and the scale settles into you: open, regional, endless in a way a valley should not be. You are not in a lake. You are in a country that happens to be under water, a high cold country that has been taken over by water and remade without being destroyed. The ridges still divide. The valleys still lead somewhere, if only to another drowned valley. The moss still knows where the slope faces. The old peat still remembers being a bog. Everything is in its own state, weathered, in balance, and everything is in motion — the current finding the narrows, the rain finding the surface, the plants finding the silt, the silt finding the stones.

It is a strange, quiet, loud place: a sea at altitude, in darkness, full of lush green life that no animal will ever eat, strewn with the patient stonework of a people who are gone, roaring with water that has nowhere else to go.
