It is not on the surface. You get to it by going down, and the going down is the first boundary.

A stair cut in limestone — first concrete, then older brick, choked between treads with moss — drops you perhaps twelve metres below the ground around it. The air gets warmer as you descend, not stuffy-hot, but warm in the way a greenhouse is warm at closing time: humid, still, breathing out moisture. By the third flight your sleeves are damp. By the bottom, water is audibly everywhere.

The place is small. From the bottom you can see the far wall, all the way across. It is one hollow, a single sunken basin, maybe eighty paces from end to end, walled on every side by a steep, weeping face of pale limestone. Limestone that has been there long enough to settle into its own shape. The streaks on it are not scars. They are established routes: dark, damp lines where water finds its way down year-round, smoothed into shallow runnels, green at the edges with algae and liverwort. If you put your hand flat against it, your palm comes away wet and cool, and water wells instantly into the imprint.

The floor is where you expect stone and find something else. It slopes very gently to a sump in the middle, where the water table sits just below the surface. Dig anywhere with a heel and seepage fills the hollow. The solid ground is there — old quarry bench, poured concrete, brick paving — but it has been capped, in most places entirely, by a living mat. Moss, fern, sedge, watercress, creeping aroids with leaves like wet spades. It is springy underfoot. In places the mat is ankle-deep, in places it has built up into hummocks that never quite dry, black with peat made of its own dead leaves. There is nowhere bare to stand.

That is the rule that becomes clear the longer you are down here: plant is not decoration on top of the works. Plant is the substrate. Everything living in this hollow is plant, and plant is filling the space that any other surface would occupy.

The walls of the built parts tell the same story, only at a different scale. The hollow is urban, but urban in a packed, hamlet way: three tiers of buildings pressed shoulder to shoulder against the limestone, stepped back as they rise, so the roof of the lower tier is the floor of the next. A mill house, a boiler house, two warehouses, a terrace of small workshops with doors numbered in chipped enamel. Between them are lanes no wider than a doorway, paved with brick slabs that have sunk and tilted under root pressure. Every drain, every gutter, every handrail and pipe has been claimed.

Roots come out of mortar joints and push on in slow, patient arms. They have not broken anything dramatically. That is the erosion here — established, in balance. Over decades the roots have found the cracks, widened them a fraction at a time, let water in, let more roots in, and then stopped, because there is nowhere more to go. Bricks bulge slightly. A lintel sags under a tangle of stems that has become, in effect, load-bearing. Walls are soft where they should be hard. If you lean on one, it yields a little, and smells of wet earth and iron.

Above you is the other half of that architecture. The top of the basin is not fully open. Where the pit was left, trees on the rim sent roots across, and vines and banyan-like aerial roots stitched themselves into a partial roof. It is not a solid canopy — light still finds seams — but it is enough to keep the whole place dim. What daylight arrives is filtered twice: by depth and by leaf. It is green-grey, diffuse, without direction, as if the sun were behind a wet muslin. In the shadowed corners, under the tiers, it falls to a true brown dimness, and there the plant mat is paler, a little etiolated, leaves stretching blindly toward the lamps.

The lamps are part of the works. Old industrial fittings, sodium-orange, bolted to walls and hung from pipes, most of them still on. Their pools of light are small and underlined by steam. Condensation beads on their housings and drips off in a steady, amplified ticking. Every metal surface either rusts at the seams or has gone green with patina from the constant humidity. Copper pipes are verdigris. Steel doors are orange-black at the bottoms, permanently wet to a line about twenty centimetres high. The machinery in the lowest tier — pumps, a ventilator fan as wide as a cart, transformers, belt drives, a small hydroelectric wheel turning in its own channel — is slick with oil and water both. It runs. Not hard, not fast. It has been running in this slow cycle for a long time: a pump kicks, labours for a minute, rests; the fan turns with a deep, patient whoomph; the wheel creeps and creeps in the diverted flow.

That, with the water, is the noise. And it is loud. Not a sudden loudness. A loudness that fills the hollow completely because there is nowhere for sound to go. The limestone walls throw it back. Pumps thud through the floor. Water falls — from the rim, from pipes, from the hanging roots overhead, from the weeping rock — in dozens of separate drips that merge into a constant percussion, like rain on a roof that never stops. A loose chain clanks itself against a railing every time the fan pulses. Electricity hums at a pitch you feel in your teeth. On top of it all hangs the warm, wet smell: limestone, wet brick, rotten leaf, machine oil, and a faint metallic tang from the water that keeps the sump sweet enough for plants and too chemical for anything else.

Which is what you notice next, once your eyes adjust. There is no animal life.

Not a bird. The canopy over the top would hold them if they came, the walls would hold their echoes, but there are no nests in the rafters, no droppings on the sills. No insects in the light, despite the lamps, despite the warmth. No frogs in the standing water, though the water is everywhere and the temperature is right. No fish in the channels, though the channels are clear enough to see your boots in them. No rats in the warehouses, whose doors stand open on stacked crates now sprouting seedlings through the slats. The mud of the plant mat has no tracks. The only movement is plant movement and machine movement: a leaf unfolding, a root tip searching, a belt turning, water falling.

It makes sense in the way that normal places make sense, not in a mysterious way. The hollow is too enclosed and too loud, night and day, with no true dark and no true quiet for an animal to rest in, too respiring with damp and industrial runoff. Animals pass over the top, perhaps. They do not come down. What is left is a complete, closed, plant-only ecology, fed by seepage and lamp light, decomposing slowly into its own ground.

Change here is slow, to the point of stillness. No cliff has recently fallen. No flood has torn new ground. The water level in the sump rises and falls a few centimetres between pump cycles. Silt settles a millimetre a year on the lower paving. The plant mat thickens by one leaf-layer each autumn and does not quite rot fast enough to keep pace. Rust deepens. Roots widen. The machines wear in the places machines wear, and are worn smooth. Everything looks weathered, saturated, fully occupied — there is not a square hand of wall, roof, floor, or crate that has not been taken up by moss, by fern, by a deliberate line of creepers following a pipe like a vein.

That is the world-rule of the hollow: water is never scarce, light is always scarce, space is never empty. Where a machine must move, it keeps a small bald circle of heat and vibration clear. Where it rests, plant closes over it in a season. Lamps are tended, after a fashion, by timers; plants lean toward them; the plants shade the next tier; the shade keeps the air warm and wet, which keeps the condensation coming, which feeds the roots in the walls, which hold the old masonry in its settled, beautiful, ruined balance.

Stand in the middle, in the sump’s shallow margin where the mat meets the canal, and you can take the whole place in at once: the weeping walls, the stepped roofs, the hanging root-curtains, the orange lamps in green dimness, the slow wheel turning and turning. It is a small town that has been given back entirely to damp and green, without ever being abandoned by industry. Populated, saturated, loud, and warm — and empty of any creature that walks, swims, or flies.
