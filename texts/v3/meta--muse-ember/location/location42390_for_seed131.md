You go down into it by a built-in ladder, and then the outside world is finished for you.

That is the whole landscape, and it fits in one feature. A single sunken hollow, sixteen metres across and a little under four metres deep, below flat low ground. From above it is hardly anything at all — a rusted hatch and the wet rim of a pit in coarse grass, behind a service fence, in the low land behind an industrial estate. From below, once you are over the rim, the rim is the horizon. There is nothing else to see.

The terrain is one vast, almost perfectly flattened bowl. The floor is not earth. It never was. It is concrete, laid as a floor and a wall all in one piece, poured for a water tank and then left to the water. And the water has had the last word for a very long time.

Everything here is made, and everything here is ancient. The corners that were once sharp have rounded off to a soft radius, as if by the sea. The steps of the ladder are worn concave in the middle where feet have come down for decades. The exposed aggregate in the concrete — little grey and brown stones in the mix — has been polished until the whole floor feels under your soles like river stone or a doorstep worn down by generations. The painted waterline on the wall, the stencil of a number, the bolted flanges, the cast-iron drain grating set into a shallow channel around the perimeter: all of it remains, all of it faded to a ghost and smoothed. This is not a ruin falling apart. It is a built thing at the end of its life, settled into its final, oldest-stable form. It has stopped decaying because there is nothing left to lose. It lies in the landscape like a primordial sink, except that it was manufactured.

Across that floor, for metres at a time, there is almost nothing. That is the strangest and most persistent impression after the sound: the emptiness. The volume of the hollow is enormous compared to what is in it. Stand in the middle and you are ten paces from any object at all. The water is a sheet only a centimetre or two deep, unmoving except where it is struck. It holds the normal daylight flat and grey, and makes a mirror of the concrete. In the perimeter channel the water is deeper, twenty centimetres, and paler. Elsewhere it just wets.

The wetness is everywhere and constant. The walls weep. Condensation gathers in beads on the upper concrete and runs in thin, silver threads, exactly as it must have run for years, falling at an unhurried, unchanging interval onto the floor below. The air is warm — low ground, sun on concrete, warm water coming in from a sun-heated header somewhere else in the works — and it is saturated. Mist does not drift through here so much as hang, low and still, in a layer you walk through and which closes behind you. The place smells of wet stone, algae, warm iron, and the faint, industrial sweetness of the water itself.

The sound is deafening, and it is part of the wetness.

On one side of the bowl, high on the wall, a large industrial pipe comes through the concrete with a flanged, bolted joint and a valve wheel frozen smooth by rust and handling. The valve is not shut. It cannot be shut any more. Water pours from it in a constant, white, tumbling cascade, three metres down the face of the wall, onto a splash-polished apron in the floor. In open air that would be loud. In a concrete bowl it is total. It is a roar with a hiss in it and a boom underneath it, reverberating off the opposite wall and back again, shaking the soles of your feet and your teeth. You cannot hear your own steps. You could not hear someone shouting. Plants shiver under it, but do not move. And it does not vary. The head of water behind the pipe is constant, the crack in the valve is constant, the drain in the channel is silted to exactly the level at which outflow equals inflow. So the roar is the same at noon, at midnight, in winter and in summer. It is just there.

Below the roar, if you could isolate it, is the quieter music of the place: the tick of drips, the soft plink where the perimeter channel water falls over a slab edge, the faint wash of the sheet water moving, millimetre by millimetre, toward the drain. But the cascade drowns all of it.

And because of that endless wet, the growth is lush — but lush in islands, and islands far apart.

There is no soil here, really. There is only silt, ground concrete dust, leaf litter blown in from above and washed into cracks, a centimetre thick in places. Plants have taken what they can get from that. Moss grows in thick, velvet-black-green cushions wherever two slabs meet, or wherever the wall meets the floor, thriving in the constant damp. Around the splash zone, where the pipe feeds the bowl, there are clumps of rushes and sedges and water-mint, their roots wedged into the expansion joints, their stems stained green with algae. In the quiet half of the channel, where the water is still, duckweed makes a complete, ordinary green sheet, and algae makes the concrete underneath look deep green and brown. Maidenhair ferns unfurl against the lower walls, buttercup and creeping things root in the silt pockets. From the rim above, roots hang down the wall in search of the water — a tree outside, putting out white, seeking threads that never quite reach the floor because they have found enough condensation to live on.

It is dense where it is present, and that makes the bare stretches more bare. Between a moss cushion and the next fern there may be three metres of polished, water-filmed concrete with nothing at all on it, its only life a faint slime that catches the light. No pebbles except chips of the concrete itself. No fallen branches. Nothing that was not put there by an engineer or blown in.

Nothing lives here in the way you would expect such a warm, wet, green place to live. That is because few things can get in, fewer can stay, and the roar drives away the rest. There are no webs in the corners, no bird on the rim, no dragonfly over the duckweed. The walls are near-vertical and slick, the ladder is the only way out. Once, perhaps, something slipped in. Now there is, so far as you can see, a single toad sitting motionless on a ladder rung, its skin the exact grey-brown of the concrete, eyes open, not croaking, not moving — as if it too has reached equilibrium. In the silted shelter of one crack there are likely a few water beetles, dark commas under the surface, and water-striders making minute dents in the sheet water far from the cascade. That is the fauna: rare to the point of absence. The place is biologically quiet precisely because it is mechanically deafening.

The rules of the world are simple, and they enforce themselves. Water in equals water out, so the level never rises and never falls. The plants grow only where roots can find a joint, so they do not spread over the open floor. What dies feeds the next growth in the same crack, nothing is carried away, nothing accumulates. The concrete neither freezes nor dries; it is always warm and always wet, so it does not crack further. The light is ordinary daylight, filtered grey by the overcast and the mist, falling straight down into the pit for the middle of the day and none at night. Nothing glows. Nothing is strange. It is all completely normal — green plants, grey concrete, brown rust, white water — only arrested.

That stillness is what makes it feel older than the factory that made it. A marsh at the edge of a low field would change with the seasons. A riverbed would flood and shift. This has found a form and stopped. The cascade has polished its own apron to a perfectly smooth, permanently wet stone. The plants have grown to exactly the size their cracks allow. The drips fall at exactly the rate the warm air condenses. If you left and came back in a year, you would find the toad in the same place, the duckweed covering the same corner, the water just below the same painted line on the wall, and the same roar in your ears on the way down the ladder.

It is not a landscape in the ordinary sense. It is a single, hidden, built hollow that water has turned, over a very long time, into a warm, lush, empty marsh-room — deafening and motionless at once, ancient and entirely made, and complete.
