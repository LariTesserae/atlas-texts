**The Elder Table**

It does not rise so much as continue. From a distance it looks like a tabletop left out in the cold — one immense, level country, higher than the weather that makes it, its far edges lost in a plain blue distance. When you reach it, there is no dramatic ascent, no cliff of black rock, no jagged crest. The ground simply keeps going, and keeps going, at an altitude where the sky is ordinary and pitilessly clear.

The light here is normal light. A proper sun, white-gold and unspectacular, coming down through thin, high air. It warms the stone under your boots, bleaches the lichen, throws sharp shadows at noon. Nothing glows. Nothing is tinted. That ordinariness is what makes the place unsettle you later, when the shadows begin to behave strangely and the day still looks perfectly fair.

The cold never leaves. It is not the cold of a winter, with a season to end it. It lives in the stone. You can stand in sunlight with your back cooking and your shaded side going numb in minutes. Your breath remains visible at midday. A metal tool left on a wall overnight is stiff with frost by dawn, and a puddle in a hollow may keep a thin skin of ice around its edge while the centre, black and perfectly still, reflects the sky. The cold is quiet, too. There is almost no sound except your own steps on stone, then the soft, surprising hush when you step off the stone and onto the moss.

Because it is so vast, it has weather of its own. Clouds do not arrive from some outside world and pass over; they are born here, in vertical stacks over the cold rises, fatten, split, and die within sight. You can watch a storm assemble a hundred miles away — a dark smear on the horizon that grows for a day — while directly above you the sun goes on shining, and another smaller storm, forgotten by the first, rains itself out into nothing between ridge and ridge. A wind can circle the Table and come back to you changed, damp where it was dry, smelling of peat it has passed over an hour ago. Distances are not measured here in miles. They are measured in weather: one storm away, two thaws, a whole forgotten season of cloud.

And everything is old beyond the point where old has meaning. This is not a young mountain range being worn down. It has been wearing down for so long that it has finished. It is settled into its final form: a broad, high dome of bare country, all its angles softened away, its cliffs rounded to the consistency of old bone. Rain has not cut it into gullies; it has polished it. Rivers would have run here once, and where they ran there are now long, shallow troughs worn into the stone, smooth as the inside of a bowl, dry except when the damp finds them. A carved step in a doorway is not sharp, it is dished in the middle from ten thousand feet. A wall is not ruined, exactly — ruin implies a fall from wholeness — it has simply slumped, layer by layer, into a low, patient mound that is still recognisably a wall if you know where to look.

The stone itself is pale, cold grey, mixed with ochre and iron red where the damp sits longest. When wet it darkens and steams faintly in the sun, though the air stays cold. Under your hand it feels warm on top from the sun and cold through to wherever the moisture lives inside it. It ticks occasionally of its own accord, a small sound like a knuckle cracking, as it cools and contracts. In hollows, it gathers other things: grit, fallen leaf, soil no deeper than your palm, and then the green begins.

The green is the great contradiction, and the great fact of the place. It is lush — violently, impossibly lush — but it does not cover the Table. It pools. For hours you may walk over bare, worn stone, lichens the only colour, a yellow crust here, a dull green smear there, frost still silvered in the lee of the tiniest rise. Then you come over a swell and the ground drops perhaps three feet into a damp hollow, and suddenly you are in a thicket. A clump of moss as deep as a mattress, studded with knife-ferns taller than you are, their fronds arching and heavy; grass whose blades are thick as reeds, carrying bells of grey-blue flowers that close tight against the cold and open only in the warmest minute of the day; low birches and tallow-trees, stunted by altitude but dense, their trunks wrapped in hanging moss like old hair. The air in these places is close and smells of wet earth and decay that has never hurried. Nothing eats this. Nothing carries it away quickly. Leaves fall and lie, pale and recognisable, for decades. The ground builds up under them in spongy peat, so that your step lands soft and leaves a footprint for a week.

The water is like that: not abundant, but always arriving. There are no great lakes, no broad rivers. There is condensation. It beads on the underside of stones overnight and drips at dawn in slow, regular ticks. It forms on the inside of a carved bowl and runs down in lines. It mats the moss until the moss gives it back. Rain comes not as pours but as events: a sudden sideways mist for eleven minutes, beading on your cloak, hissing faintly on warm stone, then gone, leaving the stone dark and steaming. Springs appear in fixed places, clear and very cold, rising through cracks that have clearly never changed shape, and in three days they may shrink to a damp stain with a collar of bright green. In a few hollows, true pockets of water persist, still and black and shallow, ringed with frost and moss both. You can drink from them. The water tastes of stone and of lichen, and it is always, somehow, slightly out of date.

Between the green pools there is a great deal of nothing, and the nothing is part of the design. The Table is sparsely inhabited in the way an old cathedral is sparsely inhabited: the presences are few, enormous in their spacing. You can cross a fern-clump in an hour and then walk for six days over open stone without seeing another living thing taller than a boot. The horizon does not change. The same low swell repeats. At night the stars are sharp and severe, and there is no sound at all except the wind finding the tiniest edge to complain against.

That absence is complete. There are no birds. This takes a long time to notice, because one expects a bird, a distant cry, a wing. Overhead there is only sky and the weather being born in it. There are no insects over the flowers, no fish in the black pools, no worms in the peat, no tracks in the frost except the ones you are making, and the older ones, pressed into a town’s mud, which are not animal tracks. In the towns there are no dogs sleeping in doorways, no chickens, no flies around the meat — there is no meat. The stillness is not the stillness of a sleeping world. It is the stillness of a world where animals were never, or are no longer, part of the terms.

On the fifth day — or the seventh, depending on which way the weather moved you — you find the first town.

It is unmistakably a town, and it is unmistakably dense, which is the other half of the contradiction. From far off it is only a dark interruption in the stone, a low clustering. Up close it is walls on walls. The Table’s towns are built in layers, because the stone is too worn and too cold to cut easily and because no one here builds in a hurry. A street is a pavement of flat slabs, and under those slabs, where a corner has lifted with frost, you can see an older pavement, and under that, the cobbles of a road before that. Houses are made of megalithic blocks, doorways low and narrow against the wind, roofs of woven grass and turf and copper sheet hammered thin by hand. Courtyards are walled against the wind. Channels of carved stone catch the condensation from the roofs and lead it to cisterns sunk in the earth. There are granaries with stone doors, a kiln cold now, looms in open workshops, racks of hand tools: stone hammers whose heads have been worn to a shine, copper awls, needles, knives of simple iron. Nothing here is new and nothing is quite broken. Everything is repaired by hand, forever, by people whose idea of repair is to lay the same stone back in the same place until the place remembers it.

The people are not animals. This is not a figure of speech, and it is the first true weirdness, though they do not seem to think it strange. They stand in the courtyards and at the looms, wrapped in woven cloaks of moss-fibre and grass. Their feet are not visible; they are rooted, politely, in the deep soil of the planted beds that run along the walls, in the way a tree is rooted, with dark taproots visible where the soil has washed away. Their hands are jointed and woody, with bark split over the knuckles, and they move them with great patience and surprising delicacy over the looms and the stonework. They grow, slightly, in the summer and stand perfectly still through the long cold snaps, moss sprouting on their shoulders until they shake it off in a slow, green rain. They speak very little, and when they do it is in low, dry clicks that carry almost nowhere in the quiet. They are the gardeners of the towns, and the towns, in turn, are gardens that have learned to be urban.

It is in the towns that the breaking shows most clearly, because a town is supposed to be the same place yesterday and tomorrow.

A storehouse you were shown in the morning has, by evening, an extra room at the back. The door is the same door. The new room is empty, well-built, and the lichen on its far wall is in a pattern you recognised at your midday meal, on a stone a mile from here, and then again, hours later, frosted on the inside of a window. A street you entered from the south deposits you, after one turn, facing north across ground you have not crossed, with the same town suddenly a day behind you. The rootfolk do not seem dismayed. They simply add the room to their maps, or they redraw the maps, or the maps redraw themselves in ink that smells faintly of damp and is still wet in the morning.

The Table is too old to erode any further forward, and so, in places, it erodes backward. On a night when the temperature drops below its usual cold and holds, you can watch it happen if you know to look. A step that was worn hollow in the morning is shallower at dawn. A crack in a wall seals itself with a soft ticking, and the wall stands straight for perhaps an hour, proud and impossible, before the sun returns and it sighs back into its slump. A fern that unfolded yesterday curls tight into bud in the frost, and then, impossibly, opens again from the bud, as if yesterday had been a practice run. The rain that fell on Wednesday is still, in the cisterns, falling. The bell in a distant town is rung before the hammer that forges it is struck, and your echo off a cliff arrives a fraction before you shout. A shadow at noon points, for ten minutes, exactly opposite to where the sun requires it to point, and the rootfolk’s shadows, when they have them, are cast by trees that are not there.

These are not hauntings. There is no malice in them, and no wonder, really — only the heavy, settled logic of a place that has reached the end of its life and cannot quite stop. It has been worn to its oldest stable state, and to stay that stable, it must keep repeating the patterns that made it: the shapes of its first hills, the courses of its first rivers, the floor-plans of its first towns. New things that do not fit are gently, inexorably, revised until they do. A flower appears in two hollows at once because there were once flowers in two places and the Table remembers the plural. Paths move if they are not walked, and reappear, worn, if they are.

And still, for all that finality, everything is in motion. The processes are quiet but they never rest. Frost heaves the pavements a hair’s breadth and the moss fills the crack and the frost heaves it wider. Clouds are born and unborn all day. The peat thickens under the untouched leaf-fall. The rooted folk grow a finger’s length, then prune it, then grow it again. A town adds a storey in the night and loses it by afternoon, and in the losing, a basket that was on the upper floor is found, carefully, on the ground, as if someone had taken it down. Water condenses, drips, evaporates, condenses again, in a cycle so small and local that a single stone may have its own weather for a thousand years. The whole Table ticks and settles, ticks and settles, like a vast, cold animal turning in its sleep.

You leave the town the way you came, which is not the way you came, into the green pools and the long bare stretches between them. The emptiness opens on every side, and the horizon, which is only weather, moves out to meet you. Behind you, the town does not fade. It simply becomes, at a certain distance, a pattern you can see in the lichen under your boots, in the frost on a stone that has not yet rained. Ahead of you, the Table continues — high, cold, damp, worn to the bone and still growing hair on the bone, still building its layered towns by hand, still forgetting and remembering itself in the same slow breath. It is not ending. It ended a very long time ago, and has been, ever since, practising how to go on.
