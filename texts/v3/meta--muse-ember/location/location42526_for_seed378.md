The plateau is high enough that the sky forgets its color.

At noon it is bleached to a hard, blue-white. The light does not come only from above. It comes back at you off wet salt, off white sinter, off the flat roofs of the heat plants, so that shadows are short, violet, and scarce, and you are lit from below as if standing inside a lamp. The horizon is not a line so much as a glare that never quite resolves. It extends in every direction — open, regional, a country of it — ridge and basin and vent-field and ghost of a town, each a day's walk from the next, each weathered long enough to have settled into its own shape.

The heat is not only the sun. It is the ground giving the sun back. The air over the crust shimmers, then stacks itself in visible layers. The stone radiates through the soles of boots. Metal left in the open is untouchable. In the hollows, where water gathers, the air is thick and close and smells of hot rock, sulfur, salt, and wet concrete. You learn quickly to do everything in shade, and shade here is not made by trees.

There are no trees. There is no grass, no scrub, no shrub to break the white. Nothing green grows on this surface at all. From a distance you will think you see a forest — tall, regular, casting long thin shadows in the morning — and as you get closer it resolves into banks of radiator fins, condensing towers, pipeways on stilts, the vertical ribs of heat exchangers standing in rows. They are the vegetation of the place, made of steel and aluminum, and they do not move. Under them, where the ground is perpetually damp and a little cooler, there are pale films of fungus in the cracks and orange-black mats in the shallows. That is as close to plant life as this world gets.

The ground itself is the first thing to understand. It is not soil. It is crust: broken pottery of sinter terraced by old water, salt polygons, basalt and shards of black volcanic glass polished by runoff, orange-stained concrete where a plant has bled rust for fifty years. Erosion here is not violence. It is established. Channels are braided and permanent, vent mounds are rounded and stepped like rice paddies, roadbeds follow the old waterlines because the water never left. Everything has been here a long time and has come to terms with everything else.

And water is everywhere, because the plateau is wet in a way that has nothing to do with coolness.

Water is near the surface. It weeps from rocks in thin clear sheets that evaporate before they have run a meter. It seeps up through cracks in the crust and makes the ground squelch warmly underfoot. It sits in perched pools, dozens and then hundreds of them, steaming steadily even at midday, their surfaces mirror-calm and perfectly clear. Condensation is a daily weather system: at dawn every cool surface is beaded and dripping, every pipe and plate and shell, and by ten the beads are gone, burned off, and the low clouds that formed at night over the vent fields dissolve into the white sky. In the afternoon a warm rain sometimes falls from those clouds — it does not cool anything. It adds to the pools. It is already heated by passage through the steam.

You cannot drink any of it. It tastes of copper and salt and boiled mineral. High altitude thins the air so the pools are already near a simmer; they bubble at the edges without wind, they skin over with yellow sulfur and white carbonate, they change color with the chemistry of the vent below them — milky, amber, pale green from the mats, black where the glass is thick. The water is abundant and useless, a surface condition, a breath held and released.

The city is built into this breath.

It is urban, after a fashion, but it is sparse to the point of loneliness. It is not a downtown. It is an archipelago of industrial complexes spread over miles of empty crust, each one covering acres of low, sprawling plant — smelters with their mouths shut, heat-exchange fields, sorted yards of copper ingots, sulfur blocks, salt, coolant reservoirs, and long elevated pipeways that carry hot water from vent to tower and back again, humming no louder than a refrigerator if you are close enough to hear it. The complexes are connected by raised tracks and roads white with salt dust, and between them there is often nothing at all for an hour's walk except more pools and more crust and the next glare.

The buildings are layered. The oldest foundations have sunk a foot into silt. Newer pipes run over older pipes. Paint is chalked to powder. Concrete is crazed and stained in permanent maps. Nothing looks new, and nothing looks ruined either. The plants are not abandoned so much as dormant in the heat of the day. They cycle: they take heat at night, they shed it at dawn with a plume that immediately condenses and falls back as a fine, warm mist over their own roofs, they rest when the ground is too hot to work safely. They have learned the plateau's rhythm because they were built for it.

Which is why there is such silence.

A place with this much industry and this much animal life should be loud. It is not. The thin, steam-laden air carries sound poorly. The machines are designed to work without vibration, to expand and contract in the heat without groaning. The fauna, as you will see, do not call. The only sounds that reach you are the quiet ones that have to happen: the drip of a weeping rock, the tick of cooling metal in late afternoon, the soft patter of warm rain on a metal roof that stops as soon as it starts, and sometimes, so low you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it, a vent sighing somewhere in the basin as pressure finds its way out. Silence here is not an absence. It is a condition, like the heat. It presses.

And yet the place is active, in multiple ways at once, all of them almost invisible until you watch.

The vents do not erupt randomly. They breathe in sequence across the plain, one mound taking its turn while its neighbors rest, sending a slow column of steam up that bends and dissolves. The pools evaporate and refill. Crust grows a millimeter where mineral rides up with the water, and dissolves where a new channel cuts. The machines cycle. And the animals move, constantly, between the warm and the wet.

At first glance the plateau looks sterile. That is the trick of it. The life is abundant, but it does not live on the land the way a forest lives. It lives in the water and in the heat and in the structures the city left for it.

In the pools, if you crouch and let your eyes adjust, the clear water is full of movement. Glass shrimp, translucent as ice, flick through the shallows in clouds, grazing the bacterial mats. Blind scuttlers with hard, etched shells walk the bottom on too many legs, turning the sediment, leaving no tracks because the mud fills instantly with hot water. Slim, worm-like filterers pulse open and shut along the terraces. In the deeper vents, larger predators circle slowly, blind, feeling the water's temperature with their whole bodies.

On land, they gather where it is damp and shaded. Under the radiator banks and the pipeways — the artificial trees — the ground is a crowded country. Heat crabs and blind grazers nest by the thousands in the warm pipes, where condensation drips steadily. Their shells are white or glass-clear; pigment is wasted in this light. Under the plates, where a shadow actually persists, they bask in the reflected warmth, dozens to a square meter, jostling, cleaning each other with forelegs, entirely silent. When the sun shifts, they shift with it, moving from shadow to shadow like a tide of small pale bodies.

Above the vent fields, in the updrafts of steam, there are gliders. They have no business on a mineral plain, but they ride the thermals all day in slow, silent circles, wings so thin you see the sky through them, dropping suddenly to snatch a shrimp from a pool's surface, then rising again. They never land on the ground if they can help it. The ground is where their food is, and also where their predators wait.

The food web has no plants in it. It starts with chemistry. The mats eat sulfur and dissolved metals in the hot water, growing in slime-orange and soot-black sheets. The shrimp eat the mats. The scuttlers eat the shrimp and the dead. The grazers under the pipes eat the fungal films and the mineral crust that grows where the pipes drip, and are eaten in turn. The gliders eat the shrimp. Nothing needs a leaf. Everything is powered from below, by heat, the way a deep-sea vent teems without sun — except here the sun is merciless and the vents are on top of a mountain.

That is one of the strange rules. The animals and the machines share the same resources and the same shelter, and neither seems to mind the other. The warm pipeways are animal highways. The cooling towers are cliffs for gliders to roost on when the steam is thick. The reservoirs are ponds. The city was built to harvest heat, and the fauna were already harvesting it, and the plateau is large enough for both harvests to continue, sparse enough that they rarely cross.

There are other rules you learn by being there.

Shade is the only cool place, and every source of shade is contested. Water is everywhere and drinkable nowhere. The ground is wet and will scald you if you sit down in a hollow. The light is so bright that most animals are translucent, and the brightest hours are the quietest, when everything that can hide has hidden, and everything that cannot hide has gone still. At dusk the condensation returns, the machines wake for their night cycle, the gliders come down, and the pools, which have been steaming all day, begin to reflect a sky that is finally blue. For an hour the place looks almost gentle.

The weirdness is not in any single thing. It is in the accumulation. A hot, high, wet desert with no plants and more animals than you can count. A city with no people on its surface, its factories sleeping through the day. A silence that is louder than noise would be. A landscape that is old and settled and eroded into balance, and yet is changing every minute — crust growing, pools migrating, vents taking turns, ground slowly rising where mineral deposits, so that the oldest pipes are buried a little deeper every year and the buildings sink in proportion.

Stand on the scarp at the plateau's edge in the late afternoon, when the glare finally breaks. Below you the plain falls away in steps. Above you the steam from a hundred vents rises to meet the low cloud and becomes it. The radiator forests throw their long shadows west. The pools shine. The animals come out from under the plates one by one to drink the air's moisture and graze the mats in the cooling water, silent, abundant, pale, moving as if the whole plateau is breathing through them. The machines tick as they cool. A rock weeps somewhere behind you, drip, drip, into a channel that has been in the same place for a thousand years.

It does not need you to make sense. It is in its own state, and it intends to stay that way: hot, wet, bare, bright, open, and alive in spite of it all.
