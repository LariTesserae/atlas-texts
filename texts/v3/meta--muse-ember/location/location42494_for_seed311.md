It is called the Pocket, though from inside, it does not feel small.

It sits low, in a single shallow basin tucked beneath a larger town — two blocks across, five storeys deep, roofed and sealed from the world above. From the street you would see only a service hatch, a glass canopy clouded with condensation, a warning light. From inside, you cannot see the whole of it at once. The water bends it. Buildings lean over canals, bridges cross bridges, and a street that loops through a reflection comes back to you three storeys higher, wearing a different name.

Everything begins submerged. Water is not an element here so much as the medium, the road, the second sky. It covers the basin floor completely and climbs halfway up the towers, so that the lower city is a drowned lattice of streets, doorways, dock faces, and empty parking bays, all still perfectly legible under twelve to fourteen degrees of clear, cool water. The surface is never still. It breathes in and out with a scheduled tide, rising and falling the better part of a metre twice a day. At high water the ground floors vanish and hydrofoil traffic takes the place of pedestrians. At low water the plaza mud appears — fresh, pale, sharp with new stone — and walkways and ramps emerge, slick and steaming, until the next tide politely takes them back.

The light, impossibly, is normal. This is not the green twilight of the deep sea. The canopy above is a lens, the water is shallow and kept sterile, and at noon sunlight reaches the bottom with ordinary force, casting ordinary shadows. You can read a sign at the bottom of a canal. Colours are true: the rust-red of a dock crane, the algae-green of a wall panel, the warm yellow of a lit window in a tower whose lower half is underwater. There is no bioluminescence, no permanent glow. Night comes, and the city makes its own daylight from lamps, and dawn resets it. Cool mist hangs where warm air pockets meet cold water, beading on every rail and hand.

The terrain is young. That is the first strangeness. A place this wet, this crowded, should be softened by time, furred with barnacles, rounded by silt, rotted at the waterline. Here nothing has had time to decay. The basin itself was cut and flooded only recently, and the city built into it is being built still. You can see the striations of its printing in every tower, layer upon layer of composite concrete and ceramic and glass, edges still sharp enough to cut. Saw marks sit beside formwork. A tower will end abruptly mid-floor, its last storey a field of rebar and print-arms, and below it, fully finished, a bootstrap of shops and transit tubes hums with traffic. The city is perpetually about eleven years old. Anything that cracks is reprinted that day. Rust is scraped before it can stain. It is construction that learned to operate, and operation that never stopped constructing.

Density is absolute. There are no gaps. Buildings share load-bearing walls; balconies interlock; a rooftop is the foundation of the next tower and the tram stop for the one after that. Pipes do not run behind walls, they run in braided bundles across facades, hung with signs, valves, laundry lines for machine cloth, planter boxes, and cables as thick as masts. Canals are barely two boat-lengths wide where they pass between faces of dark glass. In a pocket this size, the crowd of presences becomes itself a weather system: heat shimmers off equipment, water drips in predicted places, and the air — what little air there is, held in pressurised domes and bubble-streets — tastes of ozone, wet stone, machine oil, and minerals.

The built world is layered and urban in the full sense. There are districts, though the names are older than the buildings: the Docks, where submersibles nose into berths that were godowns; the Lattice, where the towers pack so tight the sky is only a slot; the Gardens, a terrace of floating planters deliberately placed where the light is kindest. Traffic signals blink underwater. Zebra crossings are painted on canal floors. A ferry route crosses a pedestrian bridge at a different level, and both are used constantly. Everything is infrastructure, even the decoration — light-pipes curve up facades like white vines, and wind-scoops breathe for the dry pockets.

Those dry pockets are the second strangeness, and where the technology leaves modern engineering behind. Streets of held air exist inside the water like inverted ships’ hulls, their walls transparent, their floors warm. You can stand in one, dry, while a builder-bot swims past the window at eye level. Transit tubes bolt through the basin without wakes, bending around corners at impossible angles, carrying pods of parts. Some canals flow slightly uphill, persuaded by fields in the walls. Doors announce themselves a moment before your hand reaches them. Walls are phase-set: solid to pressure, yielding to a maintenance drone with the right resonance. Materials remember. A broken pane does not stay broken; it pales, shivers, and re-knits.

The sound is loud, and it is the sound of the place being alive without anything living in it.

Pumps thump in a slow, quadruple rhythm from beneath the basin, a heartbeat you feel in your teeth. On top of that: the constant clank and sigh of print-arms, the whine of drone flocks making their shift-change circuits, bells for the tide — one clear note for the turn, answered by three — water slapping tower legs, air hissing in and out of dry streets, and a low electrical hum that pools in the canals and echoes back days older. In water, sound carries far and returns late; sometimes a clank from the lower docks arrives at the upper gardens a full second after you see the arm finish its swing. At high tide the whole basin rings, like a struck bowl filled to the brim.

Vegetation is sparse, deliberate, and almost entirely useful. Long dark strands of engineered kelp are braided into fences and moorings, swaying in the pump-currents. Mats of salt-grass on floating planters bob at the waterline, maintained not by gardeners but by trimmers that move along rails. Moss and biofilm green only the north-facing walls, only where the builders have decided a little softness is permissible. Most surfaces are bare. The clarity of the water is the proof: nothing clouds it, nothing swims in it.

For there is no animal life here. No fish under the bridges, though the docks are built for them. No birds in the slot of sky. No insects on the warm handrails, no rats in the docks, no swimmers. It is a complete urban ecology with the animals removed and machines slipped into every niche. Builder-bots are the detritivores, consuming rubble and old print. Repair bots move like fungus through the walls, eating damage. Sensor swarms travel in murmurs that look, from a distance, like starlings, and they make the same wheeling turns. Submersible traffic uses the canals exactly as fish would. Nothing eats anything else. Nothing is born except buildings and algae. Nothing dies except by being reabsorbed.

The dynamics never rest. Twice a day the tide rearranges the city, drowning and uncrowning whole streets. All day, towers extend a storey and retract a beam, cranes make a gesture and hold it. Weather happens inside: condensation forms on the canopy and falls as a fine, cool rain that does not reach the water, evaporating on warm equipment halfway down. Drone traffic peaks, pauses for the tide bell, peaks again. It is predator-prey translated into logistics — workshops hunt for parts, parts hunt for workshops, builders hunt for empty water to fill. The pace is biological even if there is no biology left to pace it.

Three rules seem to govern it, though no one wrote them down.

First, everything submerged must be buoyant, sealed, or busy building itself. Second, breakage is not decay; it is an instruction to repair. Third, water is the calendar. High water means docks, low water means streets, and the bells are allowed to argue with the sun about which matters more.

Stand on the upper gallery at noon — the one place where the pocket can almost be taken in whole — and it looks deceptively ordinary: a flooded building site, a working city, a sunny basin. A second later the reflection in the canal shows the same scene from the last tide, a storey deeper, with a tower that no longer exists wearing the light. The kelp nods. A print-arm strikes a new floor into being with a sound like a distant weld. Somewhere below, a dry street bubbles past, carrying a pod of glass on its way to a window that has not yet been built.
