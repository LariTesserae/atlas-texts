The first thing that ends is the sky.

You do not look up into it. You look up into a ceiling, and the ceiling is only a few metres above your head, and it is made of leaves. Beyond the leaves there is a thicker dark, and beyond that, other leaves. The world is low and it goes on. It goes on past where fog can hide it, past where a day's walking could cross it, past any horizon a low plain should have. It is not a forest in a landscape. It is a landscape that has become forest, all the way down and all the way out, to a scale where the fog above it is not weather passing through but the weather it has made for itself and kept.

At midday, if midday can be said to exist here, it is dark. Not the dark of night, which arrives and leaves. A permanent, saturated darkness, softened only by the plants themselves. Everywhere in the layered mass there are small glows — pale green points in the roots underfoot, amber beads in the stems at waist height, bluish pinpricks where the canopy knits shut overhead. They do not illuminate so much as indicate. They pulse, or hold steady, like lamps in a factory seen through wet glass. By their light, when your eyes have learned it, you can see that you are standing on almost nothing that is not alive.

The ground is a weave. It yields underfoot like a trampoline made of root and pulp and old bark pressed flat, springy and warm and perpetually slick. In places your step finds a firmer place: a fused bundle of stems braided tight as cable, a platform of flattened trunks grown together. You do not walk on soil. Soil, where it exists, is a thin dark film between plants, never exposed for long. You walk on the plant-world itself, and it bears you because it was built — not built, grown — to bear weight, to channel, to hold. The wetness rises through it. Your shoes, if you have any, come away black and coated. Your hands come away smelling of resin, of hot iron, of fruit that is fermenting somewhere inside a sealed chamber and will never fall.

There is no empty space to stand in. Density here is not crowded in the way a jungle is crowded, with gaps between things. Here everything is near-touching. A floor of roots, a middle storey of trunks fused into arches and vaults and leaning towers of bark, a hanging storey of curtains and tubes and lianas that do not sway, and above that the sealed canopy. Between them, still more: shrubs in the crotches of the arches, moss like felt on every northless surface, floating leaf-mats on every film of standing water, plant matter in the water and water in the plant matter, translucent organs filled with clear fluid that slowly turns and turns inside them.

Even rock is not allowed to be rock alone. Where the young earth breaks through — and it does break through in sharp places, in low ridges and cracked plates of fresh basalt, edges still glassy and clean enough to cut, unrounded by any river, unweathered by any age — the plants have grown directly onto it, drinking it. Those stones weep. Warm water beads on their faces and does not run. It gathers in beads, grows larger, hangs, and eventually drops a few centimetres onto a leaf below, which takes it in. There is no stream. There is no flow downhill, because there is no downhill, and no gradient to drive anything. Water is near the surface everywhere, and above the surface it is in the air, condensing. The air is full of it. Your breath fogs. Leaves sweat. A fine mist, continent-wide, hangs exactly where it is. It does not drift. It is the atmosphere of this place, held in place by the canopy that holds the heat that holds the air.

It is warm the way the inside of a closed, breathing thing is warm. Not hot. Body-warm, evenly warm, with no cold draught finding you and no sun-warmed patch. The heat comes up out of the young floor. The earth below is still giving up the heat of its making, and the plants are built to take it. Their broad, thin leaves are not catching light — there is almost no light to catch. They are radiators. They pass the earth's warmth out into the dark air and keep it there. That is why the fog never lifts. The whole vast plain is a heat-exchange surface, and it has come to equilibrium with itself.

There are no animals. None at all. No birds in the ceiling, though there is room for a sky of them. No insects at your ears, no worms in the root-mat you are walking on, no fish in the shallow, plant-choked pools, no microfauna chewing the edges of things. The absence is not a lack within the place; it is the place. Everything that lives here is plant, in every form plant can take, and so all the work of the world — eating, building, breaking down, carrying — is done by plants, by themselves, in a closed loop. Nothing falls and rots in the animal way. Nothing is hunted, or pollinates, or carries seed in a gut. What is made is reabsorbed where it is made. What is shed is taken back up through the same root it came from.

So it is silent. Not emptily silent. Layered, wet vegetation drinks sound the way it drinks water. The only sounds are the quiet ones the industry of the place makes, and even those are mostly felt. A drip, somewhere, not falling onto stone but onto a thick leaf, a soft tick of cooling resin, a low peristaltic thump that passes up through the root-floor into your feet a second before you hear it, as if the ground were breathing through a wall. Then nothing. Then the drip again. Your own breathing seems loud in it, and then even that is swallowed by the fog.

For it is an industry. That is the strange thing that reveals itself slowly, the longer you are held in the dark between the near-touching stems. It is not there in any made thing. There are no buildings, no roads, no tools, no artifacts at all — nothing a hand has shaped, nothing a mind has constructed and left behind. If you are looking for a factory, you will never find its walls, because the factory is the vegetation.

Look closer at a trunk. It is not merely thick. It is jointed, with collars and splices where one stem has grafted itself perfectly onto another, the seam melted smooth. Tubes run along it in straight, engineered lines, too regular for a vine, crossing over and under with the logic of plumbing. Inside those tubes sap moves under pressure, up and down, in a circulation with no beginning. Nodes swell into chambers — round, smooth, faintly glowing bulbs lashed to the main stems like vessels — and inside them something is being done. Minerals stripped from the young rock by acid roots are brought up, refined, separated, stored as pure, bright, useless things in hollow organs: glass-clear resin, metal-like crystals, concentrated salts. Leaves wrap themselves as insulation around cold pipes. Roots work as pumps, squeezing in waves. Petioles flex as pistons, slow and immense, driving fluid from one chamber to another. The whole mass is a mechanised system, scaled up not to a building but to a continent, running on geothermal heat and dissolved stone and recycled water, electrified in its own way by the slow potentials that make its indicator-glows.

And it produces nothing that leaves. That is the weirdness at its centre. This is an industrial ecology with no outside. The products are not shipped, not eaten, not burned. They are made, stored inside the plant body, and endlessly re-made, re-dissolved, re-separated, in a perfect cycle. It is a factory that has achieved total efficiency and therefore total uselessness, working forever at making the same intermediates, in the same order, in the same places.

Which is why it never changes.

It arrived, or rather it emerged. The earth here is young — recently lifted, recently cooled, still sharp. For a moment, geologically speaking, it was bare and new. Then the plants came, and came, and filled every gap, until there was no gap left to fill. Growth reached exactly the point where any further growth would have to be at the expense of an equal amount of reabsorption elsewhere, and so it stopped. Not died. Stopped, in balance. A leaf secretes wax at the same rate its twin on the other side of the world dissolves it. A chamber fills and empties in the same motion. The fog condenses and evaporates in the same place. The drip falls and is taken up. Nothing progresses. Everything is active. Nothing is different from one moment to the next, or from one year to the next, or from the first year it was complete.

It is a landscape caught in the act of existing, and held there. The young rock will never weather, because the water never runs over it. The plants will never age, because nothing is allowed to grow old and die. The dark will never lift, because the canopy that makes it is complete. The warmth will never cool, because below it the earth is still young and giving up heat, and above it the plants are still giving that heat away.

You can stand in one place until your eyes ache, watching a droplet swell on a leaf, and it will swell, and hold, and swell, and never quite fall. You can press your palm to a glowing node and feel the slow pulse of its work, and it will pulse the same way, forever, without ever finishing the job. Your footprints in the root-mat close behind you, absorbed, not filled in by rain — there is no rain — but taken back into the weave. The place does not erase you. It simply continues, unchanged, at full saturation, vast and low and warm and dark and wet, a single held breath of vegetation the size of a world, industrious, silent, and complete.
