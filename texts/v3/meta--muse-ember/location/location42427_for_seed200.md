There is no view, at first.

The heat arrives before any landscape does. It comes up through the soles of your feet from the mud, and down on your skin from air that has nowhere to go. It is not the clean heat of sun. The sun, when you search for it, is a smeared coin behind a lid of steam and leaf, giving almost no light at all — a dim, greenish twilight that does not brighten or fade. The heat stays anyway. It is trapped and made here, by the earth below and by the sheer respiration of living things, and it has nowhere to escape to because the land is low.

You are in a bowl, though you would not know it from the ground. The whole basin is almost flat, only a few hand-widths of rise and fall across soft silt, and it opens out past the next line of trees and the next, region-wide, marsh to marsh. The horizon is not a ridge. It is just more of the same wet green going on and on until steam erases it. No hill to climb out of it. No valley wall to cast a proper shadow. The water does not run away downhill, because there is hardly any downhill. It sits, and seeps, and rises elsewhere at the same rate. A sheet of warm, black water a finger deep covers the peat in one place, then thins to saturated mud, then to a puddle so shallow it is only a shine on the surface. Rocks do not form cliffs or boulders; they are lumps, young and unfinished, pale grey and orange with iron, crumbling at the edges, warm to the touch, and weeping constantly — a slow mineral sweat that trickles down their sides and beads and falls, feeding the puddles around them.

This is land that has not finished being made. The silt is still accumulating, the mud still bubbles in blind pots that swell and seal and swell again, the stones never quite harden into stone. It is emerging, perpetually. Nothing has had time to carve a channel or wear a bank smooth or lay down a firm plain. Edges are blurred because there is no old, solid edge anywhere. The ground underfoot gives and remembers your weight for a second and then springs or slumps back. There are no paths, no clearings worked by anything, no structure of any kind. Even where animals move most, the vegetation closes behind them within the hour, not because it is fast in a dramatic way, but because it is already growing there, dense to the point of touching.

That density is the second thing you learn, after heat. You cannot move without brushing something alive. At ground level, ferns unfurl over ferns, broad elephant-ear leaves overlap like shingles, and a root curtain drops from a mid-story branch, finds the mud, and takes hold, becoming a new trunk in a pillar of green. Lianas hang to the floor and root where they touch, making low walls you have to push through with your shoulders. Above that, a shrub layer, a small-tree layer, and then the canopy itself, which is not a ceiling so much as another crowded floor of leaves, roped together, furred with epiphytes, spiders’ webs beaded with condensation, hanging nests, hanging fruit. Light gets through in patches, but it has been filtered and re-filtered until it is the color of water in a leaf. It is dim enough that leaves grow wide and thin and pale underneath, reaching for it, and lush enough that even the dimmest corner is not bare. Moss on moss. Seedling on fallen leaf. A flower and its rotting twin on the same stem.

The air is as full as the vegetation. That is the third thing, and the one that never lets up.

Fauna here is not a feature of the place. It is the place, overwhelming in number more than in kind. The air over the puddles is worked by dragonflies and damselflies in a constant, lacy patrol, by midges in clouds that part around your face and re-form, by moths in the dim light that have no need of dusk because it is always dusk. In the water, tadpole shoals move like shadows under the leaves, small fish flick in the sheet-water where there seems barely room, snail trails cross and recross the mud. On every leaf underside there are frogs — glassy, speckled, no larger than a thumbnail — sitting in the drip, and on every stem a procession of ants carrying more than themselves. Birds call from the canopy in layered, overlapping phrases that never quite finish; small mammals rustle and squeak in the root tangles; frogs start from the mud with a wet slap and land again two inches away. It is not that any one species dominates. It is that the volume is saturated. Breathing, feeding, breeding, dying — all of it happening at every level at once, in contact, near-touching. You feel it on your skin as a faint, constant crawling presence even when you see nothing, and you hear it.

The sound is loud, but not in bursts. It is a wall built of drips. Water falling from leaf to leaf to water, indefatigable. A frog chorus from the marsh edge, metallic and throbbing, answered by another from further out, and another. Insects high and low, cicadas if they are cicadas, a metallic shimmering that rises when you disturb the air and settles but does not stop. Birds, frogs, drips, the soft boom of something plopping in deep water, the rustle of a thousand small movements in wet leaves with no wind to cause them. There is almost no wind. The bowl holds still. Air moves only where a body moves through it. That stillness is part of the strangeness. Leaves do not tremble. Mist does not drift away. Steam rises from the mud pots in straight, slow columns and condenses on the nearest leaves, where it gathers, swells, and falls again as warm rain that never came from the sky. Your own sweat does not evaporate; it condenses and runs, and the place tastes faintly of minerals on your lips.

Smell follows sound: hot mud, sweet rot, the green bite of crushed leaves, white flowers, and underneath it all a faint sulfur tang from the young earth. It does not change through any hour, because there is no hour to speak of. Light does not shift. Temperature does not drop at a supposed night. The frogs that sing like night and the birds that sing like day sing together, in shifts so overlapping there is no silence between them.

And yet, for all that noise and crowding and heat, nothing here is changing. That is the rule that holds the rest together, and the hardest to hold in your head while you are dripping and being brushed by wings.

Decay is as fast as growth. A leaf falls and is skeletonized by insects and fungi before the next leaf has finished expanding above it. A flower falls already fruited; on the same tree, ripe fruit and hard green fruit and blossom hang together, always. An animal dies — and there are many deaths, you notice the small bodies in the water, the quiet frog under a leaf — and it is covered, cleaned, incorporated within a day, and a seedling pushes up through the exact richness where it fell. Populations do not climb. They do not crash. Every birth is matched, not in some distant field, but here, in the same square of mud, by a death. Water level does not rise after the leaf-rain. It does not fall. The mud pots bubble and the ground gains a finger-width in one place and slumps the same in another. The weeping rocks lose their sweat and precipitate it back as crust. The land is emerging and eroding at once, and the two cancel so precisely that the basin looks finished and unfinished at the same time — soft, raw, embryonic, never becoming anything else.

Nothing leaves convincingly, either. Seeds do not blow away; there is no wind to blow them. They fall where they ripened and root in the wet skin of the place. Frogs spend their lives on the same lily pad cluster, their children on the next leaf over. Insects born in that condensation on that leaf may never travel farther than the next branch down. It is a closed, self-warming loop, unusual in its tightness: a whole climate made by its inhabitants, heat made by breathing, rain made by steam made by heat, so that the place sweats and drips itself forever without weather from outside.

If you stand still long enough, your sense of time frays. There is no dawn to orient you, no cooling to tell you to rest. The dim light stays dim. The chorus stays loud. A new fern unfurls overhead while, at your feet, another is being eaten to lace. Behind you, the place you pushed through is already itself again; no trail remains, no footprint holds in the mud for more than a breath. It does not welcome you and it does not drive you out. It simply continues, dense and hot and wet and dim, overwhelmingly alive and perfectly balanced, a low, open basin still in the act of being born, and for that reason never changing at all.
