You hear it before you see it, and you never stop hearing it.

That is the first rule of the High Pavement. The sound is not an event. It does not rise and fall with weather, it does not pause for night, it does not vary in pitch or volume from one hour to the next. It is a wall — wind forced up a single tilted slope and through a single field of stone, amplified in fissures a hand's width deep until the pavement itself becomes an organ. Some cracks whistle a low, steady note. Some scream. Over that, with no silence between, is the chorus of the cliff colonies: thousands of small, long-winged birds nesting in holes that erosion has mellowed for a very long time, calling in a rotation that never rests. To think here you must shout, and to shout is to be instantly drowned. The deafening is not loudness that spikes; it is loudness that stays.

The light belongs to the same unchanging order. You are above the cloud-sea. From here the world below is a white, slow-moving plain under your feet in all directions, and the sky above the plateau is not blue but black-blue at the zenith, so thin and high that it has no softness in it. The sun is bright to the point of violence — 2.8 out of any scale you might try to invent — and it rebounds off pale stone until shadow itself is luminous. A boulder will burn the left side of a leg turned toward the sun while the right side, in its own shade, stays at winter temperature. The air is cool, always cool. It scrapes the lungs, dries the eyes, and cannot hold heat, so no matter how brightly the stone glares, the breath of an animal or a person makes a brief, thin mist even at midday. There are no cool mornings that warm into hot afternoons, and no seasons coming to undo this. The day, such as it is, repeats itself exactly. The year adds nothing.

This is extreme elevation made into a place, not a peak. The Pavement is a regional table, perhaps forty kilometres across and then, beyond a first break, another table, and then a ridge like a broken spine, and then air. It extends past the immediate in every direction, multi-featured and open: pavement, scree-field, shallow basin, hanging trough, nunatak ribs. Because it is so high and so open, you can stand in one place and see the curve beginning at the horizon. Other summits rise out of the clouds below like islands, distant enough to have no detail, close enough to be weather of their own.

The ground is mostly not ground at all in the usual sense. It is stone at the surface — pale quartzite and schist, ash-grey slate, rust-black desert varnish, wind-polished granite — smoothed into interlocking plates. The erosion here is established. Nothing looks fresh. There are no sharp, newly-fallen cliffs, no raw scars of recent avalanche, no rivers carving new canyons. Glaciers passed long ago and sanded everything down; wind since then has only polished, sorted, and settled. Stones are rounded in the hand, crevices are mellow and deep, scree has been sorted by size into patient, in-balance fans. Boulders do not roll. They sit where they have sat for an age, lichen-painted, varnished on the windward face, hollow white on the lee. It feels less like a young mountain than like an old floor that has been in use for ten thousand years and is now, finally, at rest with itself.

Density is the paradox you learn to live with. Per volume, it is nearly a void. You can walk for an hour across pavement and pass three plants, two stones larger than a house, and no sign of anything else. The spaces between presences are vast, clean, and empty — a kind of hollow grandeur. And yet life is abundant, if you stop demanding that it be large or obvious.

Water is damp, not wet. There is no river, no permanent lake, no stream with banks. Moisture arrives as condensation: cold stone meeting air rising from the cloud-sea below, beading on the undersides of plates, weeping into clay pans in the basins. In a pan you may find a film a centimetre deep, black and perfectly still except for the tremor the wind-sound puts on its surface — a mirror that reflects not less sky than the sky itself. It does not deepen, does not spread, does not drain. Its edge evaporates at exactly the rate its centre condenses. Rain, when it comes, is a brief fine drizzle that beads and rolls off the pavement in bright drops and is gone within minutes. It never soaks in. Nothing pools. The pockets are the whole hydrology: intermittent moisture, held in hollows, always the same size.

Where those pockets hold, vegetation clings. It is sparse — cushion plants no taller than your boot, hair-grasses in the crack-lines, rosette herbs pressed flat to stone, lichens in pale green, sulfur yellow, and a startling stained red. Most of the plateau is bare. A plant here is an event, a tiny tough dome that flowers once and then seems to become mineral again, growing so slowly that no eye can catch it changing, balanced by grazing as fast as it grows. Between the cushions, the soil is not soil but a thin biological crust, dust and cyanobacteria and fungal threads, visible only if you kneel. The vegetation does not sway or green or die back in any season you could name. It persists.

Fauna makes up for it in the small and the dispersed. On the ground, solitary sure-footed grazers move across the region — goat-like animals with thick wool and huge territories, never in herds larger than a family, one per valley of stone. Predators are rarer still: a great eagle-analogue that circles for hours without flapping, riding the constant wind. But under every plate that can be lifted with effort, there are thousands of snow fleas hopping in the damp film, rock mites grazing lichen, springtails like grey peppercorns. In the hollows of the pavement, the crust teems. In the air, above the colonies, midges and snow-insects drift up from below on the updraft in a perpetual fallout, food for the birds. Abundance here is not density on the surface; it is a thin, region-wide film of life that covers everything if you look long enough, and then, in a few narrow bands of cliff and crevice, concentrates into a deafening, obvious multitude.

Nothing here builds. That, too, is a rule. There are no structures, no shelters, no walls, no paths worn into permanence, no artifacts left to weather. A footprint in the fine dust of a basin is erased by the wind before a cloud passes below. If a nest is made, it is made of nothing — feathers and down wedged into a hole that stone already provided. The only technology is primitive, momentary, and hand-held: the snow-crows that rule the crevices carry small sharpened pebbles in their beaks to lever lichen from rock and to crack shells, dropping them when done; a rodent uses a short stick to probe for insects under plates, and leaves it there. Tools do not accumulate. They are invented for a single use and abandoned to the pavement, indistinguishable within a day from the wind-sorted stones around them. A fire could not be kept — air too thin, fuel too sparse, wind too constant. Metal would be an absurdity here. Stone and bone are enough, and even they are not kept.

The unusual is quiet, then. Not monsters, not marvels, but things that do not quite behave as lowland things should. The water in the pans reflects sound as well as light; lean over one and the wind-chord seems to come up out of it. Some lichens smell faintly of rain after rain that has not fallen for weeks. The cushions are hard as stone to the touch and, on a still — though there is no still — examination, have not moved. The birds call in darkness and in blinding light with the same intensity, because for them there is no other instruction.

At the end of a day here — if you can call the turning of that unvarying light a day — you realize the place has not changed at all since you arrived. The melt-pockets are the same size. The colonies scream at the same pitch. The pavement sings the same notes through the same fissures. The grazers have moved, but the number of grazers has not. Deaths have been precisely replaced. Uplift, if there is any left, exactly balances the little the wind can take. This is erosion in its final state, ecology in its final state, weather in its final state: equilibrium so perfect it feels intentional.

You leave, if you leave, the way you came — down through cloud, into softer light and softer sound. Behind you the High Pavement continues to shout at an empty sky, bright and cool and damp only in its hollows, showing no sign of having noticed you were ever there.
