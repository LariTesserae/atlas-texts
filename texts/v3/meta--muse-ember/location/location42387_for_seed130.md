The first thing is the sound, and it does not let you find anything else.

It is not a noise in front of you. It is a wall behind your ears, a low roar that lives in the ground and in your teeth, in the sternum. A waterfall somewhere and a factory somewhere else and a wind through metal somewhere else, all braided together into one unbroken hum that rises and falls without ever stopping or getting louder in any way you could track. You cannot speak over it. After a while you stop trying to listen for variety in it. The deafening is the weather.

It is dark. Not night-dark, exactly. A pressed-down, cool, low-ceiling dark that comes with the mist. The sky, if there is sky, is too low and too wet to see. Light does not come from above. It comes in small, mean pools from the machines themselves — a sodium lamp drowned in fog, the orange throat of a furnace a quarter-mile off, the blue-white bite of an electrical arc that flares and dies without anyone tending it. They illuminate almost nothing. They mostly make the damp visible.

You are in a low place. A basin. Water lives close to the surface here, as if the land has forgotten how to drain. Cold air hangs in a sheet a few feet thick over everything and does not lift. Your breath fogs. Your gloves stick to cold iron. Condensation beads on any metal and runs in slow vertical tears, and even on the undersides of things, where it should not be able to gather, it gathers and drips upward in behavior if not in direction. Everything, in time, is wet to the touch.

And the ground is not ground.

At first your feet think it is a plain. Flat, open, extending past the immediate — ridge to ridge of sameness, a regional sheet of it, valley-sized and more. Then your foot finds an expansion joint, perfectly straight, filled with black water. Then another, and a rivet head, and a seam of tar. The plain is made. Slab on slab on fill on slag on foundation: poured concrete, asphalt softened and re-poured, ceramic tile, brick, ballast, chewed gravel that was once the top of something. There is no natural substrate down here to find. If you dug, you would only find another built layer, older, settled. The horizon itself is built: low berms of crushed industry, tank farms, spoil heaps worn into long, featureless swells.

It is all made, and it is also all ancient.

That is the strangeness that holds it together. Nothing is new. Nothing is sharp. Corners have been taken off by a thousand years of wet. Roofs have subsided into their buildings until walls and ceilings are one smoothed curve. Stacks lean a degree and have leaned there long enough that the lean is their architecture. The concrete weeps — it takes in the basin water and lets it out again through hairline cracks, so that interior walls sweat in winter and in the dark they seem to breathe. This is not ruin as collapse. It is ruin as settling. The oldest-stable-state. The end of life, arrived at so slowly that the place has become primordial again, geology made out of factories.

Between presences there is a great deal of emptiness. Sparse. You can walk for ten minutes, twenty, over the flooded plain with nothing to your left and right but shallow sheets of water a centimeter deep over concrete, pipes crossing the ground like long, low spines, a dead relay box, a valve wheel seized open. Then a structure. Then emptiness again.

The structures are industrial in the way a fossil is industrial. A pumping hall with its doors rusted to the frame, still housing pumps that turn in slow cycles, lifting water from one tank only to let it spill back into the next. A gantry crane frozen mid-reach over an empty trough. A row of cylindrical tanks, some split, all of them beaded with condensation, sighing a little when the pressure shifts. Conveyor galleries, pipe bridges, substation yards humming with a higher electrical note inside the lower roar. Everything electrified, everything mechanized, and nothing attended. The machines do not work efficiently. They work endlessly, at the slowest pace that still counts as motion.

That is the dynamic of the place: not fast, but multiple, overlapping, unstoppable. Water rises and falls a fraction in the troughs. Fans turn. Pressure builds in a sealed line and is released in a shriek every few minutes, somewhere off in the mist, and the shriek is swallowed by the roar. Rust creeps. Concrete spalls in small flakes when it is too wet. A belt moves a load of wet ash from nowhere to a pile that has been in the same place for longer than anyone here can remember, and the ash pile drips.

The water is always post-rain and never quite rain. Sometimes it comes down as fine, needling drizzle that does not splash — it lands on the sheet water and the sheet water does not ripple, or it ripples long after, as if the splash were delayed. Sometimes it does not fall at all, and instead the air simply condenses into mist that thickens until the far structures are ghosts, until you can see your own hands dissolving at the wrist. Pools reflect lights that are not above them. A shallow flooded slab will show a perfect, trembling rectangle of white light that has no source you can find, and when you step closer, it breaks into the dark water it always was.

Life, such as it is, has learned this made world.

There is no animal life. None. No birds overhead in the dark. No fish in the pools, though they are deep enough and still. No insects around the lamps. The absence is not clean; it is total, and it makes the mechanical noises seem more alive than anything should. If something moves that is not a machine or water, it is very slow.

Vegetation is sparse, and it lives only where the made world has failed to be perfect. Moss in the joints, grey-green and spongy, that holds water long after the concrete dries — which is to say, forever. Lichen like pale coins on the north sides of tanks. A wet film of algae and bacteria in the runoff troughs, rainbowed with oil. Occasionally, heroically, a clump of reed or a stunted, blind-pale weed pushes up through a crack in the asphalt, no more than a hand high, saturated, doing the patient work of being the only green thing for a hundred meters in any direction. Fungi bloom on the damp timbers inside the halls. That is the ecology: not predator and prey, but seep and seal, oil and microbe, wet and wetter. Things eat the place, very slowly, from inside.

The rules follow from that. Nothing dries, so nothing quite rots in the usual way; it softens and mineralizes instead. Light does not warm. Sound does not travel through air so much as through the slab under your feet, up through your boots. Water is never contained for long; it finds the joint, the crack, the weep-hole, and returns to the sheet. Machines are never fully off; they idle, they cycle, they wait for a signal that might be the water level itself. And the ancient erosion works on everything equally — on a concrete wall, on a steel pipe, on a slag heap — rounding it all toward the same final, flattened form, as if the basin is slowly reabsorbing its own construction back into land.

Walk far enough and the pattern repeats without repeating. Another hall, another tank farm, another flooded extent of nothing, another lamp drowned in mist. Because it is open, because it extends past sight, you get the feeling, uncanny and calm, that the plain has no edge except where the mist and the roar finally agree to hide it.

It is cool, and wet, and dark, and loud, and it has been this way for so long that loud and dark and wet are no longer weather. They are what the place is made of.
