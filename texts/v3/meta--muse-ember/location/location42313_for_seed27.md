It looks at first like a desert, and the desert is what fooled you.

You come up out of the low country by a switchback of air, and the air gets colder and thinner with every turn until your ears ache and your breath shows white in the middle of the day. At this height the light never quite commits to being day. It falls sideways, pale and green-grey, through a high haze of dust, so that long shadows lie flat and everything looks smoothed at the edges, as if it had been left out for a very long time to settle.

There is no water. Not a puddle in a hollow, not damp in a crack, not a river valley, not even dew at night. Your tongue dries. The soil — and there is soil, but not as you know it — is cold mineral felt that crunches slightly underfoot, then gives, then holds you. It smells of dry loam, cold iron, and faint ozone, like a storm that will never rain.

Slowly the desert resolves into something else.

The ground is alive. Not in patches, not in a forest that you could enter and leave, but everywhere, as substrate. A continuous skin of anom moss, blue-grey and dusty-green, covers plateau, boulder, and ruin with the same matte felt. Where it is thin you can see old stone through it; where it is thick it is inches deep and springy, and if you kneel you can feel it pulse. It has no flowers. It has no need of water to have done this. Its cells do not run on sap. In place of water it pumps a clear, silica-rich fluid that hardens into glassy scales when it reaches the surface, which is why the moss has that faint, dry sheen, like frost that never melts. It feeds not by drinking but by a slow negotiation with air and stone: at dusk, when the pressure drops with the cold, it opens millions of microscopic mouths and takes in gas; at dawn it un-roots minerals from the rock beneath it, patiently, grain by grain, turning stone into itself. That is the only erosion there is. No rain has ever carved this place.

The plateau is ancient in the way a final form is ancient. High as it is — you can see for a hundred kilometers to a horizon that curves away — it is almost flat. The mountains that must have raised it were worn down long before anything with a name walked here, and then worn smooth again, and again, until wind and the creeping moss ground them down to broad, rounded shoulders and shallow swells. A boulder here is not angular. It is a worn tooth. A dead tower, left from the first builders, has been softened the same way. Everything shares the same old, settled outline. Nothing looks young because nothing is allowed to be sharp for long.

Out of that flat skin rise the plants that are not the moss. Sparse, at first you think terribly sparse, so much emptiness between them that you could walk for an hour hearing only your steps and the wind. Then you realize the spacing is the pattern.

The Stanions stand a kilometer apart or more, each one a hundred paces tall, trunk thick as a house, hollow, banded with rings so wide you could lie inside one. Their bark is petrified in places, living in others, and their crowns at the top are not leaves you would recognize but vast, translucent fans that open and close in a slow daily rhythm, catching the dim light and spilling it back in a green wash. Between them are smaller thickets, reed-beds of hollow stalks, and root-fields where the moss thickens into low, thatched domes. If you weigh a hectare of this place, ninety-nine parts in a hundred are plant. The rest is noise.

The fauna are abundant, but they are not separate from that. That is the first strange rule.

At dawn the whole plain makes a sound, a vast creaking and sighing as the moss contracts in the cold, a crackle like ice breaking under a weight, loud enough to rattle your teeth. Then the leaf-flocks come — broad, jointed kites of living leaf-tissue, hundreds of them, screaming with a metallic chatter as they tack over the Stanions in search of spore. Underground, in the aerated soil, root-eels surf blind on air currents, making a low drumming you feel through your boots. On the surface, browsing pods with six sprung legs and bellows-lungs crop the moss in slow, silent herds, and behind them, patient as a closing hinge, follow the mimics: trunk-shaped hunters that stand so still the moss grows over their feet, then fold and snap. They are all, if you watch long enough, polyps of the great plants. A flock detaches, flies for a season, lands, and roots again as a new thicket. A hunter is a Stannion that decided to walk, inch by decade, toward its prey. The ecology moves, but it moves on plant time: predation here can take a year to complete, and reproduction is a sporefall.

Spore-snow is the weather. Once in a while the pressure falls too fast and the air fills with a fine, dry fall of seeds that ticks against resin glass and piles in drifts against the Stanions. It is not rain. It is the nearest thing to a season. The wind that carries it is the only storm there is, and when it comes it plays the hollow stalks like organ pipes, a chord that lasts for hours and carries from one city node to the next.

For there are cities, though they do not crowd. The built world here is one of the second strange rules, and it is layered into the plants rather than set upon them.

Every few kilometers, where a Stannion is especially large or where two root-fields meet, a ward has grown. They are urban in scale and in organization — markets, workshops, archives, dwellings — but they grow as habitat. Houses are not built so much as cut from a living dome and then coaxed, over years, into shape: walls of woven fiber fused with ceramic ribs, roofs of resin bubbles that thicken and thin with the light, doors that are valves of petal tissue. Under the moss you can see the older strata in a broken sidewalk: cables of fired clay, conduits of dark metal, glass pipes — all now infiltrated, reclad, and held together by entangled mycelium that remembers the shape it is supposed to be. That is the third rule, and it is where the technology exceeds anything current physics describes, and knows it.

The wards are tended by caretakers — and by machines that are not quite machines — who tune the local constants the way a gardener tunes a trellis. Gravity in the Stanion towers is fractionally stronger, enough to keep a hundred-meter hollow trunk from swaying in the high wind and to let a petal roof hang open to the sky without tearing. Time near the rootways is fractionally slower, so that a wall grown a century ago does not age. Light is carried underground in root-fibers, a faint, amber pulse that becomes the ward lamps at night because the sun, filtered through dust and the fans of the crowns, is never bright enough to be trusted. And through quantum-entangled nodes, in the heart of each Stannion, a ward can share a memory instantly across a kilometer of empty moss: a plant in one district will shed its leaves an hour before the wind arrives in another, because the news traveled by a route that does not pass through air.

You walk the rootways between wards because there is no other road. They are living tubes, warm and humming, just wide enough for two people, running just under the moss surface in a lazy regional loop. Transit pods — soft, pneumatically driven sacs — sigh through them, and the sound of their passage is part of the plain's constant music: a low industrial hum underlaid by the cracking of the contracts, the chatter of the flocks, the bellows-breath of the herds, the organ-note of the wind. It is loud here. Not human-loud, but landscape-loud, the noise of a big, dry, living body breathing at high altitude.

At night it is dimmer still, but the moss bed itself glows faintly in patches where the silica scales catch the root-light from below, so the whole plateau looks veined, as if you were walking on the inside of a leaf. Cool air pools in the swells. The Stanions close their fans, one by one, with a sound like a book being shut very slowly. 

There is nothing here that rots. What dies hardens into fabric — the same felt, the same stone-like wood — and remains as architecture, as soil, as a place for something new to root. The builders are old, the wards are middle-aged in a way that makes them look permanent, the plateau is older than both and shows no intention of changing. It has reached its final shape and is now, internally, violently active: heaving, sporing, hunting, migrating, remembering, translating light into mass and mass back into stone.

Stand in one of the empty gaps, halfway between two wards, where the moss is thin and you can see the rounded plateau roll away to where the haze swallows it. You will hear, all at once, the distant hum of the city, the drum of the root-eels, the sigh of the whole skin as it cools, and the high, endless chitter of something winged that you cannot see in the dimness. The desert was a lie. The forest was a lie. What is true is this: a cold, high, waterless plain that is entirely plant, entirely old, entirely awake, and that has learned — or been taught — how to make a city grow out of itself without ever softening its edges.
