**The Selan Basin**

First it is flatness.

Not empty flatness, but a low, warm plain that seems to have been pressed flat and then left alone for the first time in the history of the world. The sun is a pale disc high overhead at midday, a coin seen through dirty glass. It never gets brighter than late evening in other places, and it never sets properly. It pales, and the haze thickens, and the light goes a little more amber-grey, and then it pales again. The air holds the heat anyway, held in the ground and in your skin, as if warmth here has nothing to do with light.

The ground gives the first rule away. It is young.

Not young as in fertile, but young as in just-made. Broad plates of pale mineral stone lie tilted a degree or two against each other, their edges still razor-sharp. Between them run seams of black glass, fresh fractures that catch the dim sun in hard lines. Cracks have not yet been softened by wind or water. There is no dust in them, no lichen, no rounded pebbles. The soil, where there is soil, is black and crumbly and smells of warm stone and unfinished chemistry. In places the plain is still lifting, a few millimetres a year, and you can occasionally hear it settle with a dry tick, like a knuckle cracking far off.

You can see a very long way in Selan, because there is so little to stop the eye.

The basin is open, town-sized and larger — valley-sized, if a valley could be this low and this wide. To the north, east, and south, on the heat-haze, at least eleven separate clusters stand out against the pale ground. They are not suburbs of one another. They are islands. Between any two of them is an hour, or three hours, of walking across bare plates broken only by a lone tree, or a perfect oval pool, or a single standing wall that has decided to be by itself.

That sparseness is the second surprise, because this is unmistakably a city. Up close, in a cluster, it is urban past anything modest: layered, built, rebuilt, filled with infrastructure you only notice when you step on a plate and it bears your weight a fraction differently, or when you look down a fresh-cut opening and see three storeys of hollow chambers below ground that have no doors and no weathering. But the clusters themselves are scattered like ships at anchor in a shallow sea of stone, and most of what the city owns is the emptiness between them.

When you reach one, lushness arrives all at once.

A cluster is a sudden, almost violent greenness. Broad-leaved vines climb the bases of the towers, leaves the size of hands, overlapping, wet-looking, drinking at a rate that seems too fast for the dim light. Trees grow out of plazas, out of tower roofs, out of the sides of walls, their canopies not so much branching as unfolding — a lattice of filaments and leaf-plates that opens wider every time you look away and back. Flowers never fully close. They keep a low, lamp-like glow in the grey light, as if pollinators might come at any hour. Wherever there is a cluster, the plants have taken it for a lung and are breathing with it: roots disappear into seams in the walls, leaves sweat visibly in the morning and the sweat runs *upward* into fine capillary veins in the ceramic.

The buildings themselves are the reason the land can stay so new. They do not sit on the ground so much as they precipitate out of the air above it.

At their cores they are solid — grown ceramic, translucent as shallow water, warm to the touch, scored with a fine weave that looks like woven light. Their edges are sharp, echoing the sharp fractures of the basin, and they feather out where the solid stops. A tower may end not in a roof but in a dissolving fringe, particles of itself hanging in the air, catching light and then letting it go.

And a whole second city exists in mist.

When the air is damp, which it is for long stretches of the warm, dim day, you can see faint structures standing between the solid ones, and sometimes standing alone on the plain far from any cluster. They have the same geometry — arches, spires, flat, folded slabs — but you can see through them, and the haze moves through them. As the afternoon dries them, they thin, they lose definition, they become only a suggestion of cool air, and then they are gone, leaving no shadow. At dawn, with condensation, they come back, solidifying from the fog inward, as if the city inhales.

You learn not to trust a map of Selan for more than a day. The mist-buildings are not illusions. If you lean against one while it is wet, it holds you. If you scratch a solid wall, a faint silver film spreads over the scratch and closes it while you watch, without heat, without sound. Repair here is not done by workers. It is done by the buildings themselves, by swarms at a scale too small to see that edit matter in place. That is why the stone under them never gets compacted, why the fractures stay sharp, why nothing erodes. The city is in a permanent state of being finished for the first time.

Water, in Selan, is mostly not there.

There are no rivers. There is no rain as a weather event, only a daily cycle of dampness that passes through everything. At night every vertical surface sweats. By dawn the towers are beaded, the leaves are beaded, the bare plates are cold, and the pockets fill.

The pockets are the only free water, and they are unnaturally still. Perfect ovals, no larger than a room, set into depressions in the stone. They do not flow. They do not ripple when a bird crosses them — birds rarely cross them, because birds are rare — and they reflect the grey sky so exactly that they look like holes cut out of the world. Kneel, and the water is clear and mineral-cold and not very deep. It smells faintly green.

Then the sun, dim as it is, dries the air. The beads shrink. The mist-buildings fade. The leaves, which drank all night, open wider and give the water back as vapour, and for a while at midday the basin smells of warm wet stone and crushed leaf and something sweet, like a greenhouse that has been left shut. In late afternoon the cycle turns again. Mist rises not from pools but from the plants themselves and from the skins of the towers, a slow exhalation. Sometimes that mist falls, not as drops, but as a fine, sighing condensation that settles on your shoulders and on upturned leaves, a rain you can hear only if you are very quiet.

And it is very quiet.

Sound does not carry here the way it should. The air is full of a dust so fine it never settles, the same dust that dims the sun, and it takes sound apart. Your own footsteps make a clean, dry ping on the plates and then stop, as if the plain swallowed the echo. The plant growth makes a soft clicking, especially at night, like fingernails. Condensation dripping from a tower roof at dawn will tick for an hour, each drop a small separate weather. Beneath all of it, if you stand still long enough, is a low hum you feel more in your teeth than in your ears — the mist-buildings changing phase, the repair swarms working, the ground lifting by fractions.

Life is mostly plants.

Fauna is the rarest thing in the basin, and the silence seems to be designed for them. You may walk a whole morning and see nothing moving that is not a leaf or a building in the act of appearing. Then, at a pocket, you will see one animal drinking: tall, pale, almost translucent, with a neck too long for its body and legs that make no sound on stone. It does not startle. It lifts its head, looks at you with an eye like a polished bead, and steps backward into the green of a cluster and is gone. There are birds, but they are slow motes high up, crossing the basin once a day, wings beating with a deliberateness that makes them look like they are thinking about it. There is one kind of insect that matters — a small, iridescent beetle that visits the lamp-flowers — and where there is no cluster, there is not even that.

Everything is active, despite the quiet. That is the dynamic of the place: nothing rushes, and nothing rests.

The condensation builds and is used and is given back. The trees grow a hand's breadth overnight and then, in the dry hours, pull themselves back into tighter spirals. The solid towers shift, not in any way you could measure, but their fringes re-weave. The mist-buildings appear in one place in the morning and, a week later, in a slightly different place, as if the city is walking in its sleep. The ground cracks a little more. The swarms close it a little more. In the depths of a cluster, a gardener — you will see perhaps one human figure in a day, far off, tending a vine wall — moves with a slowness that matches everything else, and then you look away and the figure is no longer there, not because it left, but because the mist thickened.

The weirdness is not in any one of these things. It accumulates. A shadow here does not move quite with the sun; it lingers a heartbeat after you have stepped out of it. The pools reflect not only the sky and the towers that are there, but sometimes the mist-buildings more clearly than you can see them with your eyes, standing solid in the reflection while they are still only fog in the air. Distances play tricks: a tower that looks two hundred metres away across a plain can take twenty minutes to reach, and the plain between feels longer than the plain behind you, as if the space itself were stretched thin by roots and fields and quiet. At dusk, when the light is at its most amber, the whole basin can seem to have a second horizon underneath the first, made of buildings that will only exist tomorrow.

That is perhaps the truest way to describe Selan. It is not an old city on an old land, wearing away. It is a new land that a city has claimed so completely by technology that does not obey the physics we know — by structures that grow by precipitating, repair by rewriting, exist by dampness — that the land is forced to stay new with it. Lush where it is lush, utterly bare where it is bare. Urban, but with the confidence to leave miles of emptiness between its houses. Warm and dim, damp and dry, silent and busy.

You leave it the way you came, walking between far-off towers that shimmer and fade, stepping over sharp, unweathered stone that has never been walked on before you in any meaningful sense, and the basin goes on condensing itself into being behind you, one breath at a time.
