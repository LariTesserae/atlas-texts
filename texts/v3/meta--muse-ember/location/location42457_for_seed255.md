It begins below.

You go down by a wide ramp, not a stair — wide enough for three sledges abreast, packed clay and cut stone still tacky underfoot, joints between the slabs beaded with moisture. The air drops as you go. Cool, and then cool in a different way, the cold of stone that has never seen the sun, settling low and heavy. Your breath shows. The cold is not from outside weather. It is the temperature of the mass itself, damp and unchanging, leaking up through the floor you are walking on — if floor is the right word. There is no ground here, nowhere. What looks like ground is the top of a built thing, mortared, tramped, re-laid. Scrape a boot and you get brick grit, not soil.

The ramp opens.

For a moment the scale of it simply extends away from you and keeps extending. A low, broad hollow, valley-sized under a low hill, has been hollowed, lined, and filled in tiers. Across and above and below you the work continues in every direction. Rows of kilns, their mouths orange, far off. Stacks of houses built crate upon crate. Stables and stores and workshops whose roofs are the floors of the next level. Scaffolds of lashed poles and stone piers. Long halls braced by pillars that are themselves still being faced with brick. Even through the dark you can see lamp-dots and kiln-glow receding until they become a separate weather at the far end, and then beyond that, more construction, unfinished, fading into unlit dust.

Darkness is the default. Light is something made, carried, and lost. Small oil lamps in niches, hand torches passing shoulder to shoulder, the sullen flicker of kiln fires, and — once you are used to it — a faint greenish phosphorescence where pale fungal films grow thick on the damp walls. They do not brighten a room. They just make the shapes of dampness visible, like a slow tide-mark of glow where the condensation gathers.

Water here is never a river, never a lake. It is a dampness everywhere in small amounts, and that is more relentless. Condensation forms on the cold vaulted ceiling and falls in slow, irregular drips that find a bucket, a channel, a person's neck. Seepage threads through mortar joints and makes dark veins down every wall. Floors are perpetually wet in the low places, where puddles gather in the unfinished basins of cisterns and in the ruts of the sledges. In the one unroofed shaft — the Sky Well, where the builders have left a hole to haul spoil up — rain occasionally finds its way in as a sudden cold shower, hissing on kiln-stone and flooding the catch-basins for an hour. Then it is gone, and the drip-cycle resumes. People carry water in jars and skins not because it is scarce underground, but because it is rarely where you need it, and always where you don't.

Everything is made, and most of it is being made again. The material palette is narrow because it must be: fired and unfired clay, soft cut stone, lime mortar still pale and soft in the seams, rope, basketry, animal hide, a little copper and iron for edges. No wood for building except charred scraps and what has been hauled in and hauled out again — nothing green grows here. No soil is ever exposed; no plant can get a hold. The tools are hand tools: stone hammers, adzes, picks, scrapers, sledges, levers, ramps. The work is not done by machines but by lines of people passing bricks hand to hand, hauling, pounding, chanting the count to keep the rhythm. Pounding thresholds until your teeth ache. Dragging a cut block that leaves a wet score in the floor behind it.

It is dense past the point of comfort. Corridors pass over corridors. A doorway opens at shoulder height onto scaffolding over a drop. A kitchen sits directly above a kiln, its floor warm while the cistern below it sweats cold. Houses press against workshops press against solid masses that are not rooms at all but fill — entire towers built as solid brick cores to give an outer wall its shape, never to be entered, their interior a strange stratigraphy of alternate brick, rubble, and rejected fragments. You learn to read a wall by its dampness: where condensation beads and runs, there is an empty space on the other side breathing cold air; where it stays dry, the wall is solid all the way through.

The sound is a physical thing here. In stone halls a hammer blow does not end; it travels. There is a constant, irregular percussion — picks in the new tunnels, ramming in the floors, the scrape of sledges, the clatter of baskets, the creak of rope under load, bells rung hoarsely for shift changes, men shouting brick-counts, water dripping into jars with a hollow note out of all proportion to the size of the drop. Above it all, at intervals, the deep crump of a wall being taken down somewhere, deliberate, to free its bricks for a wall going up somewhere else. The noise ebbs and surges with the work, never quite stopping, muffled in one hall and deafening in the next depending on which vault catches it.

Life that is not human is rare, and it lives on the margins of the building. Pale fungal mats are the real vegetation of this place — sheets and crusts and orange-black molds that spread over damp clay and mortar, fed on dust, starch, waste, and the bodies of the dead insects. They are farmed in dark rooms, scraped off walls in grey ribbons, cooked into a flat, sour bread-stuff that is most of what anyone eats. In the cisterns there are blind fish, slow and translucent, that survive in the cracks between tanks. On the warm kiln walls, minute pale lizards flicker and vanish. Moths swarm so thickly around the lamps in the main haul-halls that they become part of the light, and are eaten, in turn, by the few swallows that have learned to nest in the upper vaults, the only birds, and rare even so. It is an ecology of leftovers: fungus eating mortar-dust, worms eating fungus, people eating fungus and worms, and the whole system kept moving by heat from the kilns driving moisture from the low, wet places to the cold ceiling where it rains back down again.

That is one of the rules here, and others follow from it. Nothing is left as found, because nothing was found. Nothing is finished, because a finished thing would soon be in the way of the next thing. A hall built last month is a barracks now; a barracks last week is a kiln; a kiln tomorrow will be pulled down for its bricks to face a new ramp. Entrances appear overnight, cutting through what was yesterday a solid wall, discovered by lamplight and rumor. Passages get narrower as facing is added, then wider again when someone cuts through. The place has no patina. Thresholds are not worn smooth, corners are not rounded by use, mortar has not darkened with age. Even the oldest parts look newly slumped into shape, raw, the clay still shrinking into fine cracks as it dries, the edges sharp enough to cut a hand. It is in a state of permanent emergence — not ruins, not a completed city, but construction that has become the landscape itself.

You navigate, if you live here, not by map — a map would be wrong by morning — but by sound, by smell of wet lime and smoke and fungus, by the temperature of a wall under your palm, by the newest scaffold, which you know will be gone or built over by the time you return. Strangeness is not in monsters or marvels. It is in this: a ceiling that drips, a floor that is a roof, a door that opens onto air, a tower that is solid, a city that is always being born in the dark, cool and damp and loud, eating its own past to make its immediate future, and having no outside at all to grow into.
