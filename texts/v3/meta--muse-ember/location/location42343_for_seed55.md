It is cold before it is anything else.

Not air, and not a metaphor for air. Water — deep enough to be a world, shallow enough that the floor never quite leaves your reach — takes the whole body at once. It is near freezing and it does not vary. It does not warm in a current or clear. It presses evenly, it slows every movement, it carries a suspended snow of fine silt that falls and falls and almost never arrives. Sound is taken from you the moment you make it.

And it is dark. The light that finds its way down through the water column is the light that finds its way down through ice, through a long polar season, through water thickened with its own sediment and with the dust of dead vegetation. Ten steps, perhaps. Fifteen, if you stir nothing up. Beyond that the dark is not empty; it is filled with the suggestion of more dark, green-black and blue-black, the colour of deep shade under still water.

The floor, when you can make it out, is low and flat. Elevation here has no drama in it at all. Nothing rises. The greatest relief in view, if you walk for an hour, might be the width of a man’s forearm. And yet it is not flat in the way a plain is flat.

You find the regularities first with your hands.

A straight edge, almost buried. A line of stones, each one too smooth and too evenly sized to be chance, set in courses that step and turn at a corner. A groove in the ground a pace wide, running farther than you can see, flanked by low banks. A slab of packed clay, square, its corners rounded by a very long time, its surface softening, slumping, settling into the silt around it. You press a thumb into it — the cold mudbrick yields a little, and there, under a film of algae, is the faint hollow of another thumb, a man’s, from before any of this was wet.

Nothing underfoot is natural. That becomes, slowly, unanswerable. The substrate is made. Stone has been brought and laid by hand, unevenly, one piece at a time, without mortar. Clay has been dug and puddled and formed into platforms and bunds and walls. Timber has been cut to a point, driven down in rows, and lashed with woven fibre. Mats of reed have been laid as roadbeds over soft ground. Even the “soil” is made: it is the fine silt that was trapped, year by year, behind dikes, mixed with composted weed and root, and trodden flat. If you dig, you do not find rock. You find more work — a lower course, an earlier floor, an abandoned stake — and below that, more packed earth that once held water back.

The work is primitive and it is total. There is no metal to speak of, no machined edge. Every tool mark is a hand mark: hammer-dents in a boulder meant to be squarish, stake-holes spaced by an arm’s length, bricks whose sides bulge a little where fingers held them while they dried. And there is no place where the work gives out into a natural shore, a natural bank, a natural hill. The largest features are simply the biggest versions of the smallest: terraces a few paces square tessellate into fields; fields are cut by canals; canals meet at junctions roofed once with timber and now choked with silt; fields are enclosed by long, low dikes that run in straight lines until, because of the curvature of the dark and the silt in the water, they vanish. Then they continue.

This repeats. That is the sense of scale. It is not a city, exactly, and not a farm. It is larger than either and laid out with the patience of both. You can walk all day over the same pattern — platform, canal, low ridge, platform — and the pattern does not simplify or end. Occasionally a wider canal opens, perhaps ten paces across, its banks more heavily stoned; occasionally two dikes cross and make a small square basin, a reservoir, a pond within the pond. The whole basin in which all this sits is vast, low, and because it is vast and low, the water over it has a weather of its own, though a slow one. Patches of slightly clearer water drift overhead over the course of days. Patches of silt-laden water hang and turn, almost imperceptibly, in circles miles wide. The ceiling of the world — ice, seen from below, a dull grey-white that never brightens to day — moves at the pace of a season.

Over everything, and through everything, is vegetation. It is the only living thing here.

It is lush in the way a thing can be lush when nothing eats it, nothing disturbs it, and time is long. Algae lay down a felt over every stone and brick and timber, a black-green skin a finger thick in the ditches, a forearm thick where it has accumulated in the lee of a wall. Eelgrass-like ribbons rise from the platforms and hang from the lashings, pale where the light fails them, a dirty yellow-green where a little more finds them, translucent and rubbery and very cold to the touch. Moss climbs the sides of the dikes in layered cushions. Filamentous threads trail in the canals and bridge them, root to root, until a canal a pace wide is roofed over in living matting through which the water moves, if it moves at all, as if through a sieve.

None of it is vigorous. None of it grows fast. In this cold and this dark, growth is a matter of millimetres in a year, of creeping rhizomes under the surface of the mat, of leaf that unfurls and stays unfurled for a decade. But because it has had an ancient, undisturbed time to do it, the coverage is complete and layered — mat on stone, mat on mat, fallen leaf on living leaf, root woven into the silt that the dikes caught. The built world and the vegetable world have become indistinguishable at the edges. A wall is a mound of algae. A platform is a garden bed. A timber stake, black and soft as fruit, holds its shape in the cold anoxious water and is green with beard.

There are no animals. No fish. No crabs picking through the weed, no birds above because there is no above to fly in, no insects, no grazing snails to keep the mat cropped. The absence is not dramatic; it is simply the condition that allows the lushness. Dead vegetation does not vanish. It sinks a little, it softens, it becomes silt, it feeds the next layer. The ecology is one storey high. Water, built earth, plant. That is all.

And the built world is ancient, not in the sense of being old with sharp edges preserved, but in the sense of being finished. Erosion here has done its work down to the last softening. Corners are rounded to the radius of a hand. Courses of stone have slumped toward each other over centuries until a wall two metres high is now a ridge thirty centimetres high, its capstones tipped and vine-entangled. A canal dug three metres deep is a shallow trough holding water a shade darker than the water over the fields. Timber lattices have settled into the mud they were meant to bridge. Everything has sunk a little into everything else. The whole place has the look of a thing that has reached its oldest stable state: not ruined violently, but settled, silted, near-flattened into its final form — primordial in the way a drying puddle is primordial, the way the bottom of a long-undisturbed pond is.

Density follows from that. There is nowhere that is empty. Within arm’s reach there is always another edge, another stake, another mat, another layer. The presences press on each other. You cannot put a foot down without displacing weed; you cannot see a metre of floor without seeing the work of a hand.

It is mostly quiet. The sounds that exist are the sounds of slow settling: a pebble dislodged by your own passage ticking down through weed; the faint sigh of timber under silt taking a new load; the muffled thud of a slumped brick somewhere, far off, where a lattice finally gives. Your own movement is the loudest event in the place, and even that is swallowed. Occasionally, from far above, where the ice is, there is a distant crack, a sound like a very slow continent clearing its throat, and a fine rain of ice-spores and silt drifts down and does not hurry to settle.

Change, in general, is of that order. The dynamic of the world is patient to the point of stillness. Water exchanges through the canals at a rate you would not notice if you watched for a year. A footprint pressed into soft brick will hold its shape through a season and fill, grain by grain, in the next. A mat that is torn will grow back over the course of a human lifetime, perhaps. The dikes, which held back a higher water, which were then abandoned, which were then drowned when the basin subsided or the melt came or the sea came — no one has kept the record, and the record would be ordinary if they had; this is a drowned raised-field system, a drowned aqueduct plain, nothing stranger than a very large, very old, very wet piece of manual labour — the dikes now hold back nothing but the silt they themselves have become.

Stand up, if you can, in the middle of one of the larger reservoirs. The water above you darkens to black and then to a dull, uneven grey. Below you, in every direction, the pattern continues until the dark takes it. Platform, canal, ridge, mat. Platform, canal, ridge, mat. The cold comes up through the soles of your feet from the made earth. The vegetation brushes your ankles, unmoving except for the minute sway your own passing imposes on the water. Somewhere west, at a pace you will never verify, a wall is a little higher than the rest; somewhere east, a canal is a little wider. That is the extent of the geography.

It is not weird. It is not enchanted. There are no lights in the water, no voices, no things that should not be. It is exactly what cold, dark, still water, a total built environment made by hand, and a very long time with no animals to interfere would produce: a vast, low, submerged, lush, silent plain of work, now grown over and smoothed and sunk, holding its shape under its own weight, in the dark, almost motionless, visible only as far as you can stir the silt.
