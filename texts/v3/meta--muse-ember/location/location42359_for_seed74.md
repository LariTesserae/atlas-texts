Cold settles here first, before light does.

It pools in a low hollow where the land barely rises at all — a shallow basin hemmed by a low ridge, by hedges and old iron fences trained into the hedges, so that from the ridge you can see the whole of it at once. The light is ordinary, flat daylight, grey in the morning and pale again at noon, but the air is cool enough that breath shows, and even on a rainless day everything is beaded. Condensation forms on cold iron, on window glass, on the undersides of leaves, on the black stone of the drains. It gathers until it drips in a slow, irregular rhythm, fills the shallow hollows between stones, then is taken up again by the ground. Rain, when it comes, comes briefly and without force — a ten-minute drumming on leaves — and is gone.

The ground is not, properly, ground. It is a mat. Underfoot is root and moss and decayed leaf pressed into a spongy, springy substrate that gives a little with each step and dampens your shoes through the sole. Where paths have been kept, the mat has worn away to settled stone, weathered concave by generations of walking. Where they have not, it has closed over in weeks, softly, like skin over a seam. Drainage rills cut across the hollow in straight, engineered lines, but they run mostly empty, merely damp, their edges rounded and green. Puddles form in the lowest places and vanish by afternoon, not evaporated so much as absorbed.

From the ridge the place looks smaller than a town and more arranged than a grove. That is the first of its confusions. It is a hamlet, a single feature, bounded on all sides. You could walk its length in fifteen minutes. Its streets form a loose grid, its blocks are numbered in paint that has long since weathered to grey, it has a power shed, a water tower, a schoolhouse with a bell, a row of workshops, and a long low factory at its heart. And yet between those presences there is a great deal of nothing — thirty paces, forty, of open moss glade, of empty plant lawn, between one house and the next. The buildings are scattered like seedheads. Infrastructure, by contrast, is everywhere in lines: wires sagging between pillars, steam pipes running buried and then surfacing along the streets, overhead lamps set at regular intervals, drains, conduits, railings. The town is dense in its routes and sparse in its occupation.

Vegetation is not in this place. It *is* the place, in the way that soil or stone usually is. Some of the plants are trees, trained since planting into straight trunks and right-angled branches, holding up wires and roofs and bridges as naturally as columns. Many are not trees at all. Foundation walls are woven root, thick as a fist, bound with living vine. House walls are stone at the base and woven plant above, with window openings cut and then kept open by pruning — the vines grow up to the frame and turn, precisely, at ninety degrees, and frame it again. Roofs are mats of house-moss and leaf that are cut back for harvest and grow thick again. Even the streets are lined not with decoration but with structure: hedgerows that are also fences, root-pillars that are also telegraph poles, canopy vines that shade the pipes. The factory floor is plant too. Its conveyors are broad bands of living fibre that carry cut plant-stuff from one machine to the next and, when torn, knit themselves shut overnight.

The factory is the heart, and it explains the strangeness. This is an industrial hamlet that makes things out of what grows. Looms weave rope and coarse cloth from pipevine and loom-bindweed. Pulp presses turn leaf and stem into stiff panels for walls and boxes. Oil is pressed from seed and feeds the engines. The engines themselves sit in brick towers clothed in ivy, their exhaust warming the vines that insulate them, their drive-shafts passing through root-bellows — great hollow bulbs of a gourd-vine that swell and contract with the heat of the machines, slowly pumping air through the hall. Iron is everywhere: black when wet, rust-streaked, brass valves gone green with age. It is heated less than you expect. Nothing here runs hot for long. The cool air, the stone, the living insulation keep everything at a working temperature, and the machines run accordingly — not roaring, but turning. Steady, cyclical, unhurried. A loom clacks, pauses, clacks. A press sighs. A piston ticks in its slowed rhythm. It is activity without noise.

That, too, is a rule of the place. Sound dies here. Moss, damp root, wet leaf, and the deep bellows of plant absorb it. What remains is mostly quiet: the low electrical hum of the lamps, the drip of condensation from a pipe, the creak of a root-pillar shifting its weight, a distant clank that comes every minute or so from the factory and is never quite the same. There is very little animal life to add to it. Fauna is rare — not absent, but rare, as if the plants have quietly outcompeted everything else for the right to live here. No birdsong; birds pass over the ridge but do not settle. No insects in the air except a small, pale colony of moths that gathers around the warm lamp-glass at dusk. A frog appears in a drain after rain and is gone by morning. A single cat walks a fixed patrol between the workshops, stepping only on stone. Spiders occupy the corners of the loom hall. The few human caretakers — and there are only a few, walking the sparse blocks with pruning tools and oil cans — are mostly noticed by their effects: a path kept clear, a valve turned, a vine trimmed back from a window.

The activity is constant, though. It is just not the kind that announces itself. At dawn, condensation gathers and drips; roots drink and swell. By mid-morning the machines are turning, the bellows are pumping, spores lift in faint clouds from the fern-banks along the pipes and settle again. At midday the growth on the south-facing walls visibly leans toward the sun and toward the warmth of the engines. In the afternoon a short rain passes. In the evening, as the light thins to its normal amber and then to blue, the whole town exhales — moisture rising off the root-mat, off the warm iron, off the leaves — and by night everything is beaded again. The cycles repeat, slightly altered each day, and no one cycle overwhelms another.

Weirdness here is not dramatic. It is orderly. Seedlings spring up in perfect rows along the buried cable runs, where iron salts have leaked into the soil in a straight line. Leaves on the factory wall grow in a pattern that echoes the window frames behind them. A moss that should be chaotic instead forms neat rectangles around gravestones and doorsteps. The plants have learned the geometry of the town and reproduce it, and the town, in return, has learned to build with plants that want to grow that way. Nothing is wild and nothing is ruined. Everything has reached a settled state after a very long time: stone has sunk and settled, iron has rounded, paint has worn away, roots have grown around foundations and through fence-lines and have made their accommodations. It is old, weathered, in balance — but the balance is alive. A machine left still for a season will find a root curled around its axle, using it as a trellis. A path left unwalked will be softly reclaimed. A building left unrepaired will not collapse; it will be held up, increasingly, by what grows through it.

At evening the lamps come on along their straight lines, pale against the green and the wet stone. The factory ticks and then stills, the bellows deflating slowly. Condensation begins to form again on the pipes, on the iron gate at the boundary, on your own sleeves as you leave. Behind you, the hedge-fence closes the hollow. In fifteen minutes you will be over the ridge, and the whole place — its scattered houses, its plant walls, its humming engines, its empty moss glades — will fit behind you in a single view, still working, still damp, still growing in its own established way.
