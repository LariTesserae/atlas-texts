You hear the Bowl before you reach it.

Not birds. Not insects. A slow, deep percussion, as if something large is dripping inside a hollow — *bong ... bong ... bong* — rolling off rock and back again, never quite fading. Then a thinner ticking underneath it, thousands of small drips on wood and clay and stone, and the knock of loose things in the wind. The sound arrives while you are still on the stair-path cut into the cliff, still a hundred paces below the rim.

The path itself is new. That is the first thing you understand about this place: everything is new.

The Bowl is small. You can walk its whole floor in twenty minutes, and from anywhere in it you can see the wall that bounds it on every side — a hanging cirque high up, well above a sea of cloud that sometimes fills the valley below and sometimes does not. The air is thin and cool enough that your breath shortens on the climb, and cool all day after, a damp cold that gets into wool and stays there. It is not frozen high country. It is sheltered. The rim catches the fog off the peaks and holds it in.

Inside the rim the rock is raw. Pale limestone and fresh granite faces, sharp as if split yesterday, unsoftened by weather. Here and there you can see the pale scars where terraces have been cut with stone mauls and bronze chisels, the chips still bright at the edges. Below that, the floor is not yet finished either. It is a thin skin of peat and black mud over scree — a marsh still coming into being. A bog being born in a stone cup. Water stands an inch below the surface everywhere; poke a reed into it and dark water wells up. It has not rained today, and the rocks are still weeping.

They weep in threads. Fine lines of seepage run down the cliff face and gather and fall, one drip at a time, each bead taking a long, patient minute to swell. Mist condenses on the cold stone even in normal midday light — a plain white daylight, unremarkable, blue in the shadowed corners, white on the wet roofs — and runs off the eaves of the houses in dark streaks.

The lushness is absurd for this height, and it is everywhere the water can reach. Because the bowl holds moisture and the fresh rock has not yet been leached, the peat is rich. Sedge and cotton-grass stand knee-deep in no particular order, waving in a slow green tide. Dwarf birch and willow crowd the margins, soaked and heavy. Ferns grow out of wall joints and out of the unfinished streets. Moss makes a thick, spongy carpet on every north face, on roof timbers, on the rims of pots left out to catch rain. Vines have already begun to pull at the scaffolding. Everything green grows faster here than anything built.

And this is, unmistakably, a town. A city, in intention.

The plan is laid out large and confident, far larger than the people present to fill it. There are four terraced levels cut into the slope, like steps, each with its own wide street — not paths, streets, broad enough for carts that do not yet exist here, paved with flat stones fitted by hand, edged with low retaining walls, crossed by drains and by log bridges over the channels. On the lowest terrace, nearest the marsh, is an enormous empty reservoir, its clay walls still red and unweathered, its floor dry except for a shallow black pool that never quite drains. Above it, a market square of beaten earth and gravel, empty. Above that, rows of houses in various stages of beginning.

Most of them have no roofs yet. Or half a roof, a thatch of straw and reed lashed to pine beams that are still pale and resinous, smelling sharp in the cool air. Some are only frames: squared timber posts, wattle panels half-woven, a doorway with no door, a hearth with no fire. A few are lived in — maybe a dozen households in the whole Bowl, the builders and their families who came up for stone and water and stayed to raise a city. Their smoke is thin. Their voices, when you hear them, are spare and practical, and then they are gone around a corner and the spaces they left are very wide.

That is the other truth of the place: between any two things, there is mostly nothing.

The density is hollow. A granary stands open to the weather, its bins empty. A communal bath-house has great clay tubs sunk into the floor, empty. A smithy has a cold forge and a rack of bronze chisels and stone hammers, tools laid out as if the smith had stepped away mid-morning and might return any century now. A pottery kiln squats on the top terrace, its mouth packed with damp clay. Distances across the Bowl are ruled by this emptiness — yards and yards of gravel street, of marsh grass between houses, of fog hanging over an unbuilt lot where a street corner has been marked by two stakes and a string. The city exists mostly as its infrastructure: steps, drains, channels, walls, cisterns, bridges. The life has not yet caught up with the plan. Or the plan intends life a hundred years from now, and the builders are content to let the streets wait.

The infrastructure is all for water. In a wet place, even a new, primitive town understands its one resource. Hollow pine logs are lashed together as aqueducts, carrying spring water from the weeping cliff into the top reservoir, leaking at every joint in a fine, needling spray. Clay pipes, some no wider than your thumb, run along the walls, down the staircases, through the floors of the unfinished houses, ending in carved wooden spouts that drip without pause into vessels set below. Rain chains of bent bronze rings hang from eaves, so that roof water tinkles down them like beads. Hand pumps of wood and leather stand at intervals, their handles tied up with rope. Nothing here is metal beyond bronze and a little iron in the tools; everything else is stone, clay, pine, reed, hide, and human muscle. Stones are rolled on logs, levered with poles, carried in woven baskets. Walls are raised a course a day.

All of that mattering water is why it is so loud.

The Bowl is a resonator, and the town has filled it with drums. Every empty cistern is a drum. Every sunken tub, every clay pot left under a spout, every empty reservoir is a drum. A drip falls from a height of three meters into the hollow bell of a cistern and the note it makes goes *BOOM* and rolls off the cliff and rolls back, and rolls again, slower each time, until it is swallowed by the next drip. In the market square you can hear a single overflowing gutter from the far terrace as if it were beside your ear, and a drip in the nearest cistern as if it were a mile away. On still days the sound has a physical weight in your chest. Loose shutters clap. Unfinished roof shingles, unweighted, lift and slap in the wind that pours over the rim. Scaffolding poles creak and knock together rhythmically. Water hammering in a blocked log pipe makes a wooden *tok-tok-tok* that goes on for an hour.

Against this, the animal silence is complete. Fauna is rare to the point of notice. No birdsong, because there are almost no birds — now and then a single heron picks its way through the marsh on long legs, silent, and is gone. A few frogs in the black pools, cold and slow, that croak once and stop. Small, pale things in the wet moss that you see only if you look directly at them. The builders keep no stock animals up here; it is too cold and too wet for them to thrive, and too new. It is a loud place with almost nothing alive making the noise except plants leaning and water falling.

The weirdness is quieter than the noise. The moss, in places, grows not randomly but in straight lines, following the lines of the unfinished streets where feet shuffle the spores but do not crush them. Condensation forms in thin vertical threads on the inside faces of walls, as if the stone were breathing out. The central pool in the reservoir never ripples, even when the wind is up and the marsh grass is bending; it only reflects, perfectly, the scaffolding above it upside down, so that the town seems to be built twice, once in air and once in black water. And the drips — if you watch one fall into a cistern, the bead above it swells for so long you think it will never fall, and when it does, the boom takes so long to die that you have time to count your own heartbeats inside it.

Everything moves slowly here. That is the rule that holds the rest together. Water seeps rather than flows. Peat deepens by a fraction each year. A wall that would be a week’s work in the lowlands is a season’s work up here, because hands are cold and stone is hard and the builders must stop to clear the vegetation that has grown over their work in the three weeks since they left it. The town is not failing. It is beginning, at the speed of geology. The cliffs have not eroded. The streets have not worn. The marsh has not yet become a marsh. Both the land and the city are in the same embryonic stage, raw and unweathered, trying each other out.

By late afternoon the fog begins to think about coming back in over the rim, and the stone weeps harder, and the drips increase their tempo just slightly, as if the Bowl were clearing its throat before night. The light stays ordinary to the end — grey-white, then amber on the wet granite — and does not go out, it just thins. In the empty market square, rain that fell hours ago is still ticking down the bronze chains into a row of clay jars, each jar sounding its own note. In the reservoir the pool sits black and still. The houses stand half-roofed. The scaffolding stands waiting for hands.

It will be a city one day, the builders say, when the peat is thick enough to trust and the children who are not yet born are old enough to finish the roofs. Until then it is this: a small, high, cold cup of raw rock and new mud, lush with grass and moss, wet on every surface, empty between every building, and loud — so loud — with the sound of water falling into empty vessels that are waiting, patiently, to be full.
