There is no sky here, and there has never been. You come down into it by a shaft that is too narrow for ceremony — a ladder, a lift-cage, a slope of cut rock — and the air changes before the light does. It gets warm. Not hot. Warm as the inside of a closed hand. Warm all the way down, and dry all the way down, until your mouth is paper and your eyes sting faintly, as if from dust that has never learned to clump.

No water has ever been here. You feel the lack of it not as thirst, which is a wanting of something, but as an absence of everything water would have done. Nothing drips. Nothing rusts. Nothing rots. Nothing has ever made mud. The dust under your boots is an anhydrous grit — crushed mineral, metal filings, salt that blooms in white, dry frost on the walls but never melts, never runs. It powders your shoes and does not cake. It rises and settles in the same instant, because there is no humidity to hold it in the air and no wind to take it away.

And under the dust, the floor is not soft. It is young.

That is the first strangeness to get used to: how new everything looks. The chamber — for it is a chamber, you understand this within perhaps a hundred paces — is cathedral-small. A single vaulted pocket, bounded on every side, perhaps eighty meters long and thirty across, its ceiling twenty-odd meters up in the middle and sloping low at the edges. Below you is rock, above you is rock, thick with it, kilometers of it, pressing faintly on your ears. But the surfaces of this pocket have not been aged by the world above. No rain has rounded them. No river has polished them. No freeze and thaw has loosened them. They are sharp as a thing broken yesterday.

The floor is slabs and lances of fresh precipitated mineral — anhydrite, basalt glass, plates of a pale crystal that split into edges like paper cuts. Stepping carelessly will open a boot. The walls are teeth. They rise in unweathered spires and shelves, still glistening faintly where the last crystallization left them, still unoxidized, still perfect in the way of a newly formed seam. In a desert on the surface, the wind would have worked at them for a million years. Here nothing has worked at them at all. They have stayed new, and stayed new, and that is why they still look new.

The light is dim, and it does not come from above. It comes in small, scattered points from within the pocket itself: amber lamps in cages wired to the walls, the slit-windows of machines, thin lines of bioluminescence that live in the dust and pulse where the current passes. It is enough to see by, never enough to feel cast by. Shadows are short and uncertain. Color is mostly the color of hot metal and bone — grey, ochre, dull copper — because there is daylight nowhere to announce itself.

But the sound is loud, and it fills the whole room equally. That is the second thing to learn. From the moment you enter, you are inside a roar: the deep chord of an engine, the whine of fans, the steady clank-clank of something turning on a fixed cycle, the electrical hum that you feel in your teeth. It is not distant. It does not get louder as you approach its source and quieter as you move away to any extent you would recognize in an ordinary hall. It is in the floor under your feet, in the air, in the metal of the walls. And after a while you realize it is not changing. A machine in a normal factory sounds alive because it varies — power surges, loads shift, a belt slips. Here the pattern repeats without variation, bar for bar, like a song that has been played so many times the player no longer hears it. You can set your breathing to it. You can hear, under the roar, smaller roars.

The source is at the center, because there is only one center. A kiln-engine, squat and vertical, riveted iron banded with fresh crystal, dominates the middle of the vault like an altar and a chimney and a heart at once. Pipes radiate from it in all directions — thick as your thigh, thin as knitting needles — up to the walls, down into the floor, over the vault in layered bundles. Cables hang in festoons between anchor points, so dense overhead that in places they form a second ceiling, a black canopy through which the amber lamps shine. A conveyor runs in a perfect, useless loop from the kiln to a wall and back again, carrying nothing, or carrying the same ingots around and around. A fan turns, stirring the same warm air. A lathe turns the same piece of metal, shaving the same curl, which falls and is picked up by a floor-runner and... you will come to the runners in a moment.

This is industrial in scale, in intention, in smell — ozone, hot oil, burning dust — but the buildings are only scattered. The original settlement was never large. There is a small control cabin with glass gone blind with dust, a row of lockers, a lean-to of corrugated sheet propped against a wall, three or four huts no bigger than sheds, a bunk room whose beds are still made, a shrine or a station shrine with a single wrench laid upon it. Partial remains everywhere, but preserved by the dryness as if wrapped. Tools lie where they were dropped, their edges still bright. Because nothing corrodes here, and nothing decays, work stops exactly where it stopped.

Against all this density — machines, pipes, crystals, dust, bodies — the vegetation is sparse to the point of being a rumor.

Only along the seams where current bleeds from the cables, and only in the warm outflow of the kiln, grow the stone-grasses. They are not plants in any wet sense. They are black, wire-thin filaments, five or ten centimeters high, branching like miniature winter trees, and they conduct. When the current passes, they light faintly from within and sway in the fan-wind, but they do not grow. They have not grown taller since the first footsteps scuffed dust over them. They eat electricity and heat. They excrete a fine metallic powder. There are perhaps a few hundred tufts in the whole vault, in lines along cracks, in circles around bolts — a sparse, deliberate lacework against the crowded floor.

The floor, and the air, and the walls, are crowded with everything else.

The fauna are abundant, and they are the reason the place feels saturated even though it is empty of people. They live in layers, near-touching, because the chamber is small and the population is enormous and nothing leaves.

At your feet, constantly, are the runners. Blind, elongated, dog-sized, armored in overlapping plates of a dull, iridescent mineral, they move on six jointed legs that tap the sharp floor without cutting themselves. They have no eyes, but their antennae are tuned to vibration and electric field. They graze. Not on the stone-grass, which is too balanced to eat, but on the dust itself — on the metal filings from the lathe, on the pellets the other creatures drop, on the white salt blooms. They pick it up, they process it through a gut that runs hot as the kiln, and they excrete it again as a finer dust. They have been doing this in the same circuits for as long as the footprints have been sharp.

Above you, hanging from the cables and the low vault, are the gliders. Bat-like, but their wings are not membrane. They are thin sheets of crystal on a frame of bone, and when they shift they ring, very faintly, under the roar — a glass chime you feel more than hear. They eat from the air: motes of dust and oil mist and the tiny swarming things that live in the lamplight. Those swarms are a fauna in themselves, thousands of insect-things with bodies of dark glass and legs like needles, that never land, that orbit the lamps in dense clouds, that serve as food and as other things' food, and that are themselves eaten by the runners when they fall.

And there are others still, things that live inside the pipes, things that cling like limpets to the kiln and pulse with its heat, things that you will never see directly because they are the color of the wall and the size of a hand, appearing only as a ripple in the dust.

None of them drink water, because there is none to drink and none to need. Their blood, if you cut one — and the runners do not get cut, their armor is proof against the floor that would cut you — would not be red and wet. It is a molten salt, a clear oil that carries heat through their bodies and crystallizes when they die, so that a dead runner becomes a small, perfect statue of itself on the floor, and remains there, and becomes part of the density, and is distinguished from the living only by the absence of tapping. The gliders breathe through their wing-sheets. The swarm-things live on electrical discharge. All of them run on the waste heat of the kiln and the waste current of the grid, which is, in this sealed pocket, a river that never flows out.

That is the ecology: a closed loop of dust, heat, noise, and bodies. And a closed loop, here, means something more literal than in any surface place.

This place does not change.

Things move enormously — it is loud, it is busy, it is crowded — but nothing accumulates into difference. The conveyor carries its ingots from the kiln to the wall and back, and the ingots are the same ingots. The lathe shaves its piece and the shavings fall and are eaten and become dust and are eaten again. The runners breed, or something that looks like breeding — a runner splits its armor and two runners walk out, identical, and the population does not grow, because somewhere, at exactly the same rate, a runner stiffens into crystal-statue and is left where it fell, and the dust reclaims it. The temperature is warm, but it is warm everywhere, perfectly, despite the kiln in the center. Stand next to the engine and stand against the far wall, and your skin cannot tell you which is closer. The heat that should make a gradient is somehow worked away by fans and by bodies and by the stone itself, until equilibrium is a kind of temperature: one warm, unvarying atmosphere.

The footprints of the first workers are still on the floor, sharp as when they were made, beside your own, which are also sharp. No dust settles into them. No water softens them. No new footstep erases the old, because the density is such that new steps fall in the same grooves as old ones. You can follow a trail that is both an hour old and a hundred years old, because here those are the same length.

The sound never decays, either. An ordinary cave would echo and the echo would die. Here the roar is trapped by the walls and by itself, and the echo returns exactly as loud as it left, and joins the roar, and the roar is therefore always the same. You begin to hear it not as noise but as a place: a continuous present tense.

It is a strange place, in the way that a perfect circle is strange. It was made young — by a fault opening in fresh mineral, by miners driving a chamber into a new seam, by a kiln installed in a pocket that had never known air — and then it stopped, all at once, at the moment of its making. It has been stopped ever since. The youth is a kind of preservation. The sharpness is what happens when time is allowed to begin and then forbidden to continue.

Stay long enough and your expectations adjust to it, which is the strangest part. You stop waiting for the clank to miss a beat. You stop expecting the lights to go out for night, because there is no night, only the dim that is always dim. You stop expecting to get hungry in the old way, because the runners are eating the dust around your ankles and the dust is the floor and the floor is the world. You walk the hundred paces from one wall to the other, and the kiln is always in the center, and the lamps are always in the same places, and the stone-grass is always five centimeters high, and the gliders ring above you as they shift on the cables, and the roar is in your teeth, and it is warm, and it is dry, and nothing — nothing at all — is different from when you arrived, except that you are now part of the pattern of movement that produces no change.

The chamber will be exactly like this tomorrow. It was exactly like this yesterday. It looks as if it was just made.
