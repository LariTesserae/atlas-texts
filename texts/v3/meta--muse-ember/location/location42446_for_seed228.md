It is not, at first, anything but a cave.

You go down — not far down, a slope wet underfoot and a few minutes’ stair cut into somewhere, and then the passage opens — and the air hits you before the place does. Warm. Not the dry warm of a hot day, but warm and saturated, so that your skin stays wet whether you are sweating or not and your breath comes back to you as mist. The sound hits next, and it is too loud for a place this size. A constant roar of moving water, amplified too much by stone, with a low hum underneath it and the irregular *boom - drip - boom* of something falling a long way in a hollow place, and the hiss of spray breaking somewhere out of sight. You can hear your own boots move pebbles. You can hear the far wall.

And then, third, the light. Bright. Day-bright, white-gold, noon-bright, with no sun to account for it and no opening in the roof you can find.

That wrongness unfolds in layers, because the place keeps revising itself.

The chamber is small. That is the first true thing about it, once the noise settles into a background. A grove-or-room scale, an oval perhaps sixty meters long, thirty across, vaulted maybe twenty high, and entirely bounded. You could cross it in two minutes at a walk, if the floor let you. Tunnels leave it at low points — dark mouths where black water runs out — but they all seem to sigh back into the same room, or the mist closes them off before you can be sure. Mist is everywhere, in slow sheets, and it steals the far side and gives it back again.

The floor is not dry stone. It is stone under a film. A shallow sheet of clear water, centimeters deep in most places, deeper in polished hollows between terraces of white sinter, runs over a plain of wet clay and gravel that has been softened into smoothness. Every step makes a thin ripple and a soft splash. The rocks weep. All of them. The walls are dark, scalloped limestone and basalt, and they sweat in curtains. Rivulets start nowhere near the ceiling and run down, stop, start elsewhere, braid and recombine. The ceiling itself drips steadily, a rain made entirely of its own condensation, which falls six feet and is caught by the updraft and becomes mist again. It never really lands. It circulates.

This is an old place. That is the second true thing. Nothing here is sharp. The erosion is not the fresh violence of a flood or a collapse; it is settled, in-balance, long-existed. Stalactites have blunted to nubs. Edges have been licked away by water until the whole room feels handled. Even the scattered built things — and there are only scattered built things — have been taken into that softness.

Around the periphery, where the water is not quite so deep, you find them, sparse as the density of everything else: a half-disc metal door sunk to its waist in sinter, its wheel gone; a tiled bench, three tiles missing, the rest sealed under a crust; a length of railing that runs a few meters and ends in air; survey marks painted on rock in a pigment now mostly mineral, now mostly gone, where a white drip has not covered them. A glass bead. A bracket. A boot-print preserved in a terrace that fills with water and empties again. Faint marks, partial remains. No building stands. Whatever was built here was built small, and lightly, and the place has worn it down to artifacts the way it wears the stone.

And yet the technology is not worn down. That is harder to see at first, because it is not built in the human sense.

Look at the light again. It does not come from lamps. There are no fixtures, no cables to the surface, no windows. It comes from the stone and the water themselves, in a diffuse, almost daylight spectrum, brightest where the water is moving fastest over the sinter terraces. Where there is spray, the spray glows faintly. Where the mist thickens, the light pools in it like it is being held. Dust-motes — too many, too regular — drift in the updrafts and hold the brightness longer than dust should. You begin to notice small silver threads in the shallow water, no thicker than hair, moving against the current, pausing over a crack in the terrace, brightening, and the crack … not healing, exactly. Reprecipitating. A new lace of mineral over it, overnight-fast.

The place is engineered, but the engineering has dissolved into the medium. Water that is not just water. Stone that is not just stone, but a manufactured geopolymer that weeps on command and holds light and lets it go. A machine that has no center and no outside, that runs the chamber as a closed loop: evaporate, condense, precipitate, dissolve, circulate. It is well past modern, and it is mostly invisible, which is why the built things look abandoned. The building was the clumsy part. The rest was poured in.

Vegetation should be lush in a warm, wet, bright room. It is not. That is a surprise you have to get used to. There is sparse vegetation, and only in transient niches: a mat of pale moss on a rock that is sprayed but not scoured, a clump of stiff, reed-like shoots where the water slackens at the edge, each stem no higher than your knee and bent permanently by the wind of the chamber. A fern-form gripping a wall seam, its leaves bleached to a colorless green by the artificial day. They appear, they are covered, they move. Patches that were green this morning are white with fresh sinter by afternoon, replaced a meter over. Life here cannot afford to be settled either; it has to be quick.

Fauna are rarer still. You may go the whole crossing and see nothing alive that moves on its own. Then, in a pool that catches extra light, a school of small translucent fish turns all at once in the shallow water, blind or indifferent to the brightness, and is gone under a terrace lip. In the spray near a vent, a cloud of midges hangs in the light and disperses when you approach. Once, a pale amphibian, longer than your hand, clings to a weeping wall and pulses there, breathing through its skin, until a pulse of water knocks it loose and it swims, very deliberately, for deeper water. That is all. Unspectacular, watchful, rare — animals that have learned that loud, turbulent water gives you no cover, so you do not stay long in any one place.

Because the third true thing is that nothing stays. The erosion is old and the form is stable, but the moment to moment is turbulent, hyperactive, a storm that never leaves the room.

Every few minutes a wave moves across the flat. Not a big wave. A change in the sheet-water, as if pressure has shifted underneath, that lifts the surface a hand’s breadth and pushes it, rippling, up the lower walls before it falls back. The mist answers in a surge, rolling low across the floor, thinning the light to a pearl grey, then clearing so fast the bright returns like a switch. Bubbles rise in the pools without boiling, burst, raise a brief crown of spray that hangs, startlingly, before it falls. Sometimes a rivulet on the wall runs upward for a second, a thin sheet climbing two meters against gravity, then breaks and rains. The humming under the roar rises and falls, and the stone ticks and cracks in small, sharp reports, settling, re-settling. A gust — self-made wind, no outside to come from — flattens the reeds, lifts the mist into a vertical column at the center of the dome, and drops it again.

It is chaotic, but it is a chaos inside a boundary. The walls do not move. The terraces grow and dissolve in the same places. The light pattern repeats, the water tide repeats, the boom-drip echoes and echoes again in the small hollow. Over a day the room would look identical. Over a minute, it never looks the same twice. Solaris-like in miniature: constant creation and small destruction — mineral laid down, mineral taken away, water up, water down — balanced into a state that can go on indefinitely.

So you stand in the middle, in warm, condensing air that smells faintly of wet stone and iron and ozone, with the water at your ankles looking impossibly clear and impossibly bright, and the sound filling the whole small volume of the room until your own breathing seems amplified. There is vast emptiness here, for all the loudness. Between the pool and the door and the reed clump there is mostly just wet plain, and mist, and distance that is not very far but feels, in that light, very far.

It holds together. That is the unusual thing, held at a low, comprehensible strangeness. A cave that should be dark and is bright. A machine that should be built and is weather. A storm that should break the place and instead is the thing that keeps it in its own, long-existed, settled shape. A place below the surface that feels, stubbornly, as if it were trying to remember what the surface is like — the warmth, the rain that does not fall, the daylight that does not come from the sky — and has remembered it just well enough, and just wrong enough, to be itself.
