**Kaldreach — the Held Plateau**

At first there is nothing.

Not a mountain, with a summit you could claim, but a tableland so large it ceases to be a landform and becomes a country in itself. You stand on it at a height where your breath comes short and the air tastes of cold iron, and the horizon does not end so much as thin out. Eastward, the moss plain runs for days. You can see a smudge of weather sitting over the far side — grey, immobile, raining on a place you will never reach — and over the near side a clear sky of the most ordinary daylight, white and high and indifferent. Between them, nothing resolves. The wind, such as it is, does not gust. It does not bring the weather to you. The weather stays where it is put.

That is the first rule of this place. Everything is put somewhere, and stays.

The edges are why you know it is high. The plateau breaks off in fault-scarps that are still young. The rock is black basalt and pale granite split so recently that the faces have not yet learned to be round. Scree at their feet is angular enough to cut boots, each shard sharp as a newly broken tile. No river has had time to soften a valley here, or rather, no river is allowed to. On a vertical wall the moss will grow thick right up to an edge and then stop, as if clipped. You can put your hand flat to the stone and feel it ring faintly through your palm. It has not weathered because it has not been left to weather. Fresh, immense, and held.

The top of the plateau is wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain. The water table lives just under the surface, and the surface is a sponge. Every step you take in the low ground sinks a centimetre and fills. Pools are everywhere, perfectly still, mirroring the sky with the fidelity of glass, not rippling even when you stand in them. They are not lakes. They are seepages. The cold is a constant, penetrative cold — not a snap of frost but the kind that lives in your fingers, that rimes the rivets on a pipe while the moss beside it is soaking. Condensation forms on everything. On the underside of leaves. On the inside of your coat. On steel, it beads and then, impossibly, runs *up* a little, toward a seam, toward a waiting pipe.

The rocks weep. All along the scarps and in the clefts, water emerges from the stone in thin sheets and in slow drips, darkening the rock in vertical streaks. A drip will fall, run ten centimetres, and vanish — not into soil, but into the rock again, or into a concealed channel at the base of the cliff. At the foot of every weeping wall there is a narrow, dry rim, salt-pale, where the water is collected and pumped back up into the mountain in a closed, industrious loop. So the stone is always wet and never erodes. The scree stays sharp. The pools stay the same depth. It is a marsh that never drains, a post-rain landscape that has been in post-rain for longer than anyone has been here to measure.

And the vegetation is lush. That is the second astonishment, and the one that makes your mind snag. In this cold, at this height, on this wet black ground, the cover is almost complete. Moss in cushions and deep carpets, black-green and spongy, hissing faintly underfoot. Sedges and rushes in hummocks that sway — not in wind, of which there is hardly any, but in a slow, synchronous bend, as if listening. Ferns low and tight, cushion plants like green loaves, lichens in sheets of acid yellow and rust red over rock and concrete alike. Nothing grows tall. Nothing needs to. The plants are all ages at once on the same stem: green shoot, mature frond, brown curled leaf, side by side, because there is no season to tell them to die back. They flower when they please, which is to say, constantly, in small white bursts. They seed by fragmentation and by being carried on the tracks of machines. They have no need of animals. They have never had one.

You hear the place long before you see what makes the sound.

It is loud, but it is not varied. A low roar under everything, felt as much in your molars as heard — turbines, pumps, smelters, the great water-lifts working the cliffs. Over it, at exact intervals, a drip. Over that, the clank of an empty freight car, the whine of a crane, the tick of a conveyor junction. The sounds repeat. Not roughly. Exactly. If you stayed an hour, you could set your watch to it, and the hour would not surprise you. The moss seems to grow *into* the sound, thickest along the pipe-lines and cable-runs where the air vibrates. The sedges bend on the beat.

The city — and there is a city — arrives as a glint at a distance so great that it seems not to be a place but a weather of its own. Then another glint. Then another. The density of Kaldreach is emptiness. Districts are islands, a day's walk apart across the moss, with nothing between them but wet plain and the straight iron lines of pipes and rails humming faintly. You can walk for hours with the horizon empty, save for the fixed storm and the fixed sun, before a smokestack resolves itself, hanging a column of steam that neither rises nor disperses but spreads into a perfect, stationary disc.

When you reach one, it is industrial in the old, heavy sense. Terraced factories cut into the fault-scarp and layered one above another like strata. Housing blocks on stilts over marsh, their windows lit. Smelters with orange mouths. Cranes swinging in their endless arc. Tram lines looping. Everything built of galvanized steel, concrete gone pale with frost, and glass filmed with condensation. Oil and wet stone smell together. The heat from a smelter mouth will scald your face and the cold from the open plain will bite it a second later. Machines burn skewed lengths of cable with a regular hiss. Elevators go up and come down. Doors open and close. No one uses them.

There is no one. No footprint in the moss that is not made by a track. No laundry, no market noise, no dogs. The cafeterias have trays stacked clean. The offices have chairs pushed in. Clocks on the walls all show the same time, and the same time again when you return. The factories are producing — you can see sheet metal being rolled, pressed, stacked in perfect piles, carried away by conveyor to a point where it is unstacked and fed back in again. The city is manufacturing for the pleasure of its own loop. It maintains the plateau: it pumps the weeping water back into the rock, it clips the moss to a prescribed thickness along the roads and returns the clippings as mulch, it blows spores across the plain on a schedule so the lushness never thins, it keeps the scree from sliding, the cliffs from splitting further. It has made a young landscape old enough to build on, and then arrested it exactly there.

That is the strangeness that settles over you slowly, like the condensation. The sun is up there, doing its slow crawl — ordinary light, ordinary brightness, the kind of light you would expect at midday in a northern place — but its shadow has not moved perceptibly since you arrived. You could, if you were staying longer, watch a whole week and see it barely shift. The storm at the horizon rains and rains in its place. The pool by your foot does not evaporate, does not freeze, does not ripple. The fern beside it does not senesce. The crane does not stop. Nothing here is changing because change would be a kind of waste, and this place was built — by whom, for what, you cannot tell — to waste nothing.

Hence the absence you eventually feel as a physical thing. After an hour, you notice you have not heard a bird. The sky is big and normal and empty. The pools have no frogs. The moss has no insects rising from it, no beetles under the stones. Even the harsh, industrious vibrations that fill your ears have kept animals out — or animals were never let in when the loop was closed. A living nervous system needs a night, a season, a silence to orient itself by. Kaldreach offers it a noon that never ends, a drone that never stops, and a world that returns, instantly and politely, to how it was a moment ago. Plants can bear it. Plants, in fact, seem to drink it — their leaves turning toward the factory glow, their roots threading through warm pipes, their ferns unfurling in the direction of the hum.

You walk on, because there is nowhere else to go, across sponge and sedge toward the next glint, which will be the same as this one and also different in the arrangement of its stacks. Your boots are wet through. Frost is forming on your laces. The roar is behind you now and also, somehow, still with you, because the pipe you are following carries it. At your feet, a pool mirrors the sky. On its surface, your reflection does not move unless you do.

Kaldreach does not go on living. It goes on *being* — vast, high, wet, cold, lush, loud, sharp, and empty — a plateau held at the exact pitch of its own making, weeping and humming and growing forever in the same well-lit, unchanging afternoon.
