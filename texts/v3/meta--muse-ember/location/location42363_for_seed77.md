The horizon does not end. It goes on until the heat takes it.

It is low country all the way — not hills, not a valley you can descend into, just a shallow, continental flat that rises and falls by a meter or two over distances you could walk all day without noticing. Any rise is not rock. Any hollow is not a riverbed. The whole of it, as far as weather can be seen, is made.

Under a normal sun, in a normal blue sky, the light at noon is flat and bleaching. The air shimmers above the ground in broad, slow sheets. Far off, a rain band can be standing dark over one part of the plain while the part you stand in remains hard and bright, and the wind between them carries only dust and the smell of hot straw. The weather here is its own, because the place is large enough to make it.

If you kneel, the ground is not soil. It is a sheet — warp and weft, over and under — of stiff stems and roots locked together and packed with silt and sun-baked clay. In places it is as thin and flexible as a thatch mat; in places older layers have been pressed by their own weight and by the heat into something like a dull, ochre brick, pale where the sun has bleached it, rusty where clay shows through, grey-green where a seam has stayed damp. It cracks in polygons when it dries. It ticks and creaks faintly as it cools at night and contracts. It holds footprints for a while, then the wind erases them.

There is nowhere to step off it. Dig — if you could — you would not find a natural substrate. You would find another layer of the same woven, packed material, and another under that. Everywhere is artifact, but there is no tool-mark on it, no cut edge, no straight line that a hand or a machine would make. The joints are irregular. The thickness varies from a few fingers to an arm's length. The edges feather and fray. It was not built at once. It is still being built.

The living part of it is sparse to the point of loneliness.

What lives here does not grow up. It grows sideways. Low, spreading plants, tough-leaved and waxy, send out hard stems that root where they touch, then send out again. Their roots do not delve; they spread in a dense, shallow basket, level with the surface, trapping whatever the wind brings — dust, grit, seeds, fragments of dead stem. When a stem dies it does not rot quickly in this heat and with nothing to eat it. It dries, it is packed tighter by the next season's silt, and it becomes part of the sheet. The next plant grows on top of it. In this way a patch thickens, inch by inch, year by year, and where patches meet they lock together.

You can walk for kilometers across finished mat with no green thing on it at all. Then, in a shallow depression, a cluster will be working: a loose tangle of live stems at the frayed front, a few small flowers open to the wind — for there is nothing here to pollinate but the air — and beyond them, behind the front, a paler expanse of stems already dead and bound into the new ground. The living edge is always a thin line. The dead work is the landscape.

Water is present, but it is not abundant, and it does not run as rivers do. Rain comes intermittently, sometimes a hard, short downpour that drums on the mat and vanishes almost as fast as it fell, soaking down through the seams into the layers below. It reappears days later, or it does not. In the deepest made hollows it gathers into shallow, still pockets — clear, warm, rimmed with dark damp where the mat has swelled. Those pools shrink under the sun. They are the wettest places, and the builders gather there, because their baskets can seal a bottom and hold the moisture a little longer.

At dawn, before the heat returns, condensation does more than the rain. The woven sheets cool enough that dew beads on the upturned stems and drips, one by one, into the pockets and seam-gaps. For an hour the air smells faintly of wet fiber and warm clay, an earthy smell without earth. By mid-morning it is gone, and the surfaces are too hot to touch where the sun strikes them. The heat radiates back up through your feet. The mat stores it and gives it back slowly through the evening.

There is no animal life. No tracks cross the mat. No nests, no calls, no buzzing, no grazing, no burrowing. The silence is not absolute — wind moves through the live stems with a soft shushing, dry stems tick against each other, a drip falls and is absorbed — but it is a silence without voices. Nothing here eats the dead material except sun and wind and the next plant. Nothing here moves across the plain except the wind and the slow advance of the living fronts.

And it is always moving. That is the paradox of the place: its stillness is busy.

All day the panels are expanding and contracting with heat. Dust lifted from a dry stretch settles into the basket-work of a growing patch a field away. A rain pulse swells the seams, then the sun bakes them tight again, opening fine cracks that the wind immediately fills with new silt. The living plants push their roots into any gap that holds moisture after a rain, then stop, waiting. Seeds blown far and wide germinate only if they land in a seam that stays damp for three days — most do not. Those that do become a new, isolated building patch, hundreds of meters from the nearest other, which will thicken slowly until, perhaps in a human lifetime, perhaps in ten, it touches its neighbor.

Erosion, in the usual sense, has barely begun. There are no gullies, no canyons, no rounded slopes worn smooth. The forms are fresh and undeveloped. New work is loose and unfinished, with gaps you can see shadowed hollows under, and warp ends sticking up like straw. Older work is polished only by sun and wind, its edges still sharp in the way of sun-dried fiber. Rain does not carve it; it soaks into it. The plain does not wash away. It accretes.

The rules are simple and they hold everywhere:

Nothing grows except where a surface has already been made to hold water.
Nothing is made except by a living plant at its edge.
What is made, stays, until the plant that made it is covered by the plant that follows.
Dryness is the default. Damp is a pocket, a seam, an hour at dawn, a rain that passes and is gone.
Heat is the medium in which all of it is cured.

So the whole vast expanse is a single, continent-sized construction still under construction — a mat woven of silt and stem, laid out flat under an ordinary sky, hot, mostly dry, occasionally damp, silent except for wind and dripping water, alive only in scattered, sparse lines of growers at the fronts, and active everywhere in the slow, ceaseless work of swelling, drying, cracking, trapping, and growing over what has already been made.
