It begins as water over stone.

Not a lake, not a river, not quite a marsh. A low basin, sunk far below any proper horizon, where the land gives up and lets the water sit. You stand ankle-deep, then sometimes shin-deep, in black water so still in places that it holds the sky — a flat, colorless smear, dim as late afternoon in a cold winter, and just as permanent. The light never really rises. It thins, thickens, thins again behind low cloud and fog, and leaves everything in that wet blue-grey that makes distance useless.

The water is everywhere, but it is never clean, and never quite cold. The air bites. It is just below freezing, and your breath fogs before it leaves you, and the fog does not disperse — it hangs, condenses on your eyelashes, on the back of your neck, on the stones. Yet in patches the water is lukewarm against your legs, and the stones beneath it are warm. Somewhere below, heat is rising through everything.

The rocks weep. All of them. Low outcrops, no higher than a crouching person, flattened and smoothed by an age that has erased anything sharp, stand in the shallow water and sweat. Water beads on their sides and runs in slow, dark streaks, as if the stone itself were full and had to let it out. The whole plain is like that: a surface that cannot hold its own moisture. Rain falls, but it is a kind of precipitation of the air itself — sleet that melts halfway down, fog that thickens into drizzle, drizzle that freezes on the windward sides of things in a skin of clear ice, even where the rock below is warm to the touch.

You expect, in a wet place like this, grass. Reeds at the edges. Moss on the wet stone, algae in the pools. There is none.

That is the first wrongness. There is no green here at all. No leaf, no stem, no fine root holding the mud. The soil, if it ever was soil, has been ground away. What remains is bedrock worn to a polish, ironstone dark as wet bark, basalt rounded like river stones enlarged to the scale of houses, salt-white crusts in the hollows, and a thin, dead-looking film of grey slime where the water is slowest. Slime, mineral, fungal-smelling — that is as far as plant-like life has gotten, or perhaps as far as it ever will. Life here took another road, long before plants were an option.

At first the plain looks empty in the way a flooded stone-field looks empty. Black stones to the horizon where the fog cuts them off. Then, when you have been standing a little too long, one of the stones breathes.

It lifts, just a fraction, and settles. A seam opens along its side and exhales steam. Rivet-like bumps along its flank catch the dim light — they were not rivets, you realize, they were knobs of bone or grown metal, raised in regular rows. And then another stone, and another, out across the water, doing the same slow piston-motion.

The ground is not ground. The ground is them.

They are packed so tightly there is no between. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, plate overlapping plate, they cover the basin from the fog in front of you to the fog behind you, a continuous, living paving. Where the water is deeper they are stacked, two and three high, the upper ones perched on the backs of the lower to keep their breathing slits above the slush, building little ridges and hummocks that look, from a distance, like cairns or low walls. Where the water is shallow, they lie just under it, and the surface is broken only by the slow heave of their backs and the bubbles that rise in rank, sooty streams.

Nothing here is built. There is no road, no wall, no footprint of a maker, no ruin polished by the same age as everything else. And yet everything works.

Up close the basal animals — for they are animals, in the final sense that they feed and move and breed and die — are machines made without a machinist. Each is broad, low, and squat, a dome of overlapping plates the color of wet iron and rust. The plates are not laid on; they grow, and they grow in the overlapping pattern of boiler plate, each edge tucked under the next so water runs off. Between the plates, tendons pull like cables. Along the underside, hidden in shadow, a seam glows a dull, furnace orange, pulsing in time with a slow intake of water through a mouth that is really a filter. They take in the black water, they take in gas seeping up from below — a sour, sulfur-and-ozone smell on the cold air — and what they give off is heat, sound, and waste.

The waste is the other part of the cycle. A thin, oily runoff that sheets across the water in rainbow films. A fine, metallic dust that the smallest of them sneeze into the fog. Crystals of salt and iron that precipitate from their exhaust in the cold and fall back onto their own backs. They are refining the place as they move through it, slowly, in a wave that crosses the whole basin like a peristaltic band. For hours a section will be still, idling, the hum deepening in their chests. Then the ripple comes — you feel it through the soles of your feet before you see it — and each animal in the band lifts, clanks against its neighbor, shifts a hand-span forward, and settles, breathing out a plume of steam that instantly fogs and freezes on their plates.

They reproduce the way a production line would, if a production line had learned to eat. A flank will split, the plates separating with a sound like a tearing rivet, and a smaller dome will push out, soft and steaming, its plates still unhardened, and inflate with water until it hardens into its place in the packing. Old ones molt. The emptied shells do not fall away and rot; they remain in the mass for a while, still warm, still ticking, and the small interstitial things — a second kind, no bigger than your fist, all legs and rattling joints — live in the seams between the big ones and scuttle from shell to shell, making most of the higher noise, the constant clattering like loose change in a dryer.

A third kind rides the water itself. Overlapping discs, black and glossy, they raft across the surface in mats so dense you cannot see water between them, each pulsing a faint electric blue along its rim, like a running light. They swarm the warm patches where the big animals exhale, and they break apart and rejoin without ever leaving a gap.

Above it all, in the low fog, there is a fourth presence: a haze of micro-fauna so fine it looks like industrial dust or snow that will not fall, tiny winged things with engine-bright thoraxes hovering on the updrafts from the warm backs below. The air is as packed as the ground. There is no empty space in this place at any scale you test it. Stand still for more than a few minutes and condensation will bead on you; stand still longer and the nearest animals will begin, unhurriedly, to grow over your feet. Stillness is colonized. Stillness is eaten.

The sound is therefore not an event. It is the condition. A low hum, fifty cycles deep, that you feel in your teeth. On top of it the rhythmic piston-breath of thousands of chests, out of phase with each other so it never settles into a beat. The clank of plates. The suck and bubble of water forced through filters. The ticking of the small ones. The occasional high, metallic scream from the discs, cut off as if the water took it. It is loud, but it is not chaotic. It is a factory floor that has been running so long it has become weather.

That age is in the land itself. The erosion here is not the erosion of a young landscape being carved. This is an end-state. Whatever mountains once fed this basin have been worn down to these low, weeping lumps. Whatever soil once covered the stone has been ground between animal and rock until there is none left to find. Every edge is rounded. Every line is softened. The place has settled into its final form and is now only polishing itself, grain by grain, animal by animal.

And it is open. That is the cruel part. The basin extends past any point you can make stand. You can walk for a day — and the walking is slow, through water and over warm, shifting backs that resent your weight — and the packed plain continues, the same shallow water, the same dim sky merging with fog at exactly the same height. The low rim that must exist somewhere is never reached, only suggested by a slightly darker band in the mist. The scale is regional, a whole province of this one texture, and the repetition makes it feel longer than it could possibly be.

Reality begins to come apart out here, not all at once, but the way ice breaks on a lake — a crack, then a pattern.

You hear the clank before the animal moves. Not by much, sometimes by a whole second, and sometimes the other way around. You step forward and the water ripples outward from where your foot will be. The foam forms, then the foot lands in it. In the black water, the reflection of the dim sky sometimes shows a sun — a pale, clear oval — that is not in the sky when you look up. The discs on the surface appear to be facing away from you in their reflection, while facing you in themselves.

The pattern of the plates repeats. Count them if you like. Seventeen domes, then the same seventeen, in exactly the same tilt, with the same scar on the central one, and then again. Look away, look back, and the familiar weeping rock that was to your left is now to your right, though you have not turned. Distances do not hold. A stretch you crossed in ten minutes will take an hour to recross, and the animals along it will have advanced a full pace toward you, or away from you, or both.

A molted shell in the seam beside you hatches before it splits. A small animal pushes out while the seam is still closed, and then, after it has scuttled clear, the seam opens on nothing. The water, in some places, visibly runs uphill, sheeting up the warm sides of the domes to bead at the top and freeze there, although the stone is warm and the air is not cold enough, and then the ice melts and runs down again, and the cycle begins without a cause you can point to. The fog in front of a dome darkens and lightens in a flicker, like a fluorescent tube in a workshop, although there is no tube, no workshop, no electricity except what the animals make along their rims to signal to each other in the dim.

None of this stops the processes. That is what makes it a place rather than a trick. The dynamic here is mid-paced and relentless: weather cycling in minutes — sleet, thaw, condense, freeze — the slow migration wave crossing the basin over days, the breeding, the molting, the hot seams brightening and dimming with the cold. The animals do not notice the rules breaking. Or perhaps, from inside the packing, the rules have always been this: sound before motion, growth before splitting, the same rock on both sides of the sky. The breakdown is only apparent to something standing outside the system, trying to keep its footing on a floor that is alive and working and old and new at once.

By the end of the first day — if it is a day, under a light that never changes enough to tell — you stop trying to find the edge of the water or the line where the plain stops and something else begins. There is no edge. There is water and stone and animal and fog, packed and weeping and humming, extending past the immediate in every direction, running like a factory that was never built, in a cold that was never quite frozen, under a light that was never quite day.

You walk because standing still means being covered. The wave comes, you feel it through your feet, and you shift your weight with it, piston-like, without deciding to.
