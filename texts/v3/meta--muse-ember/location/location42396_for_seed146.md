It is warm before it is anything else.

Not sun-warm. You feel it through your soles first, and then all over, as you come down out of the throat that leads here and into the first vault. The air is thick and dry and does not move toward you, it is simply *there*, already heated, pressing evenly against skin and eardrum. Above you, out of sight and out of reach, are miles of rock. There is no sky in any direction. There is only the floor below, the ceiling above, and the long, dark room between them.

And “room” is too small a word, in time. The vault is vast enough to have its own weather, and the weather never arrives. Far off, heat rises in a slow, standing column from a field of vents and falls back along the distant walls in broad sheets no one can see. Particulate — fine mineral dust, sulfur haze, the shed flakes of ten thousand living things — hangs in layers at certain heights, never thinning, never dispersing. It is a permanent overcast made of stone too fine to settle. If you stand long enough, your mouth becomes coated with the taste of hot iron and oil. There is no water here to wash it away.

No water at all. That is the first rule, and it explains almost everything else. The crust above is sealed. No rain has ever found this level, no river, no seep. The water that was once in the planet was driven down and then driven off by heat, so that what remains is a dry mineral chemistry: vapors that condense as waxes and salts, fatty films that weep from warm rock pores, crystal growth as a kind of metabolism. There are basins in the floor that look like dried lakes, salt fields white and blistered, polygonal in their cracking — except nothing has dissolved in them for as long as they have existed. You can walk across their crust and hear it crunch, dry as bone, and the sherds you crush will still be there, sharp, years later.

Because there is no water, there is nothing to soften anything. The whole level looks young. Geologically young, newly made, newly broken. The floor is not a smooth plain. It is a shattered pavement of plates — basalt glass, sintered ash, clear quartz-blades — tilted up at their edges, overlapping like roof shingles, fractured into fresh planes that still show their bright interiors. Stalactites do not drip. They *grow* down as needles and daggers, dense as a thicket, and their points are not worn. Where a ceiling slab has fallen, it lies where it fell, its break faces clean and glittering in the little light there is, unweathered, unrounded, as if the fall happened yesterday. In a normal place, time would dull this. Here time has stopped at the moment of breaking and decided to stay.

The darkness is not total, but it is close. 0.4, if you meant light by numbers. There is no sun-path, no moon. What light exists is made here, locally, and grudgingly: a dull amber bloom along seams where rock is hot enough to glow; a spilling blue-white along the flank stripes of the biggest fauna; brief sparks where glass-bodied things rub together while they feed. Your eyes do not adjust to it so much as learn to work in absence. Objects are found by heat and by touch and by vibration, smelled long before they are seen.

And it is mostly quiet. That is the second rule. Sound does not carry in this thick, still air. The fauna do not call across distance. They tick, click, and scrape against stone in a way you feel through the soles of your feet more than you hear through your ears — a faint, irregular metronome transmitted through the floor-plate. Once in a while, far off, a rock-plate pings with a clear tone, a single note, and then silence returns for a long time. The ping will come again, at the same interval, from farther than you can guess. It is not weather. It is equilibrium, sounding itself.

Life is abundant, and nowhere is it plant-like. There is no green here, nor anything rooted that reaches for light. No leaves, no grass, no moss, no trees of any kind. The colors of the place are black, rust, tarnished copper, bone, amber. What grows — if growth is the word — is a thin, fungal crust that mats over warm plates in gray velvet. It is not vegetation. It does not photosynthesize. It eats the mineral vapor as it condenses, and the fauna eat it.

The fauna are strange because they do not need what surface life needs, and they are dense because they have nowhere else to be.

On the warmest plates they are packed near-touching, shoulder to shoulder, a living shingle layer of Plate-Clingers. Each one is flat, broad, armored in overlapping segments that are part chitin and part accreted stone. They are no larger than your hand, but they cover the floor in interlocking drifts for hundreds of paces. They scrape the fungal mats with mouths like files, and when they move, it is only a shuffle from one heated seam to another, a few inches, then still again. They are arranged, it seems, by temperature.

Hanging above them, on the ceiling, is the other half of the world. Stalactite forests are draped with the Sky-Clingers, blind, pale, translucent things that hang upside-down by hooked limbs and trail long fibers into the mineral haze to catch falling flakes. In the depths, where the vault ceiling dips low, they are so dense that their fibers interweave into a second ceiling, a veil. You must push through it, pushing warm, soft bodies aside, and they do not flee. They grip tighter.

Between floor and ceiling wander the Glassthings — long, jointed, worm-like, with no eyes but with heat-sensing pits along their sides. Their bodies are partly glass, clear as a bubble, and when the light-stripe fauna pass near them they flare faintly from within. They tunnel, not through soil, but through the powder of the salt basins, leaving behind perfect, sharp-edged burrows that never slump, because there is no moisture to make them slump. You find their trails everywhere, young and fresh, as if they were made an hour ago. They were made years ago. They look the same now.

And there are larger things, slow, six-limbed, stone-backed, that move on paths so regular they have polished the floor plates to a dull shine along their routes. They do not hunt. They breathe in sulfur vapor, metabolize it in chambers in their bellies, and excrete it back as molten wax that cools into the fungal mats. They are, in effect, the digestion of the place — waste becomes food becomes floor. When they die, they do not rot. They dry, they lithify in place, their backs becoming new plates, their limbs becoming new needles. That is part of why the floor looks so recently fractured. It is, continually, being re-made out of its own dead, and the break is always bright.

The builders are scattered through this like punctuation. You can walk for a very long time — hours, under a ceiling that never appears and a floor that undulates gently on a continental scale — and see nothing made. Then, suddenly, a semicircle of stacked stones no higher than your knee. A pit lined with glass shards, faintly warm from a geothermal tap. A single standing wall, one stone thick, primitive, its mortar a wax of mineral fat. These are not ruins in the sense of decayed cities. They are marks, caches, windbreaks, shrines, made by a lean, long-limbed fauna with clever hands. They use hand tools: chipped obsidian knives, hammered copper needles from surface ore, drills of sharpened quartz. Their technology is simple-fabrication, manual — they knap glass, they beat metal, they weave cords not from plant fiber (there is none) but from the sliced sinew of the stone-backed grazers and from the binding threads of the fungal mats. Their structures are small against the vault, and they are few. One wall here, one cache there, a ring of white bone-stones at a vent. They do not build for permanence against weather. There is no weather to erode them. So they stay, sharp-edged, faint, exactly as left.

That is the deepest rule, the one that pulls the rest together. This place is not changing. Not seasonally, not diurnally, not over years. The thermal columns do not move. The aerosol layers do not drift. The Plate-Clingers shuffle and return. The Glassthings retrace their tunnels. The builders rebuild the same wall in the same place, generation after generation, because the plan for it is stored not in an idea but in the arrangement of the stones themselves. Birth and death happen constantly, but the *system* does not. It is a frozen equilibrium at around forty degrees, rich and warm and still, below the surface of a sealed world, non-aqueous, plantless, and young-looking forever because nothing here has the means to make it old.

If you stay, you begin to understand that the vastness is not empty. It is full, but full in a way that does not announce itself. Density here is vertical and personal: layers upon layers, near-touching, of mineral, fungus, beast, fiber, and hot vapor, all occupying the same volume without pushing each other out. At any given spot, something is clinging to the floor, something else is hanging above you, something else is threading between them in the dark. You are the only thing out of place, and even you, after a while, stop feeling like a visitor and start feeling like another presence in the density.

You do not hear rain. You do not see growth. You do not see a horizon, only a floor stretching away into black, a ceiling stretching away into black, and between them a permanent, standing weather of warm air and noise-less industry.

The place does not become older while you are there. It does not become newer, either. It simply continues to be, in the same sharp, hot, dark, crowded way, as it was when it broke open newly below the crust, and as it will be long after the last builder's wall is covered by the slow accretion of the stone-backed dead.

The Still Deep does not pass time. It holds it.
