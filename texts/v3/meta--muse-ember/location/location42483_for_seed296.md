It is called Sarn-Veth, and the first surprise is that it is not new.

The plateau it sits on is extreme in height — four kilometres up, where the air is thin enough that a kettle boils lukewarm and a staircase leaves you breathing hard — and yet it is old to the point of exhaustion. Erosion has finished here long ago. There are no sharp peaks. The whole region is a table of domes, whale-backed ridges and broad, U-shaped hollows rolling to a milky horizon in every direction. From the rim you can see for a valley's worth of distance and there is nothing to stop the eye except more worn stone, losing itself in mist.

The land has settled into its final form. Stone that was folded before there was life on earth has been planed, soaked, and rounded until it feels almost soft. Paths follow the contours because they have nowhere else to go. The roads do the same. Nothing is cutting a new gorge. Nothing is raising a cliff. Geological change, here, is memory.

And so all the movement has moved up, onto the surface.

You come into Sarn-Veth not over a river — the rivers died out of this porous plateau tens of thousands of years ago, sinking into the stone and never surfacing again — but through weather. The city occupies the warmest, least-windy hollow, where the plateau dips a little and shelters itself. From a distance it is not a skyline so much as a thickening. Low, densely packed blocks lean into one another for warmth, cut into the grey basalt, built over and over on top of their own ruins. A house here might have a steam engine in its cellar from the first industrial age, three storeys of riveted stone and iron above it, and a roof garden of ferns above that, where the rain that never quite falls gathers and drips through into the kitchen below.

That is the rule of the place: nothing dries, and nothing stands alone.

The temperature stays cool even at noon, 4 to 10 degrees in the deep of summer, and the light never quite commits. You are above the low cloud sea that fills the valleys below, and below a high, thin mist that catches the sun and spreads it grey-green, like light through moss. For twenty minutes around midday the sun is a pale disc that fails to warm stone. In the shadowed alleys, in the north-facing courtyards, frost is still on the iron in July. Electric lamps, strung on sagging wires between frost-heaved poles, come on early and stay on late. The dimness is not night; it is permanent shade, altitude, and damp.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no lakes, no streams wide enough to name. Instead the city lives on intermittent moisture: a fine rain that comes sideways and leaves the pavements damp but never soaked, fog rolling up the dome-slopes in a slow tide every hour, and above all condensation.

Cold stone sweats. Iron sweats. The night air, drawn up from the cloud sea, hits the warm walls of the foundries and the cold funnels of the water towers and becomes rain on metal. Every eave is a gutter, every pipe a drain, every tunnel roof a catchment. Children collect the drip from industrial condensers into cisterns under the tramway. Moss holds the rest in a spongy felt several centimetres thick, so that the ground always gives a little underfoot, like walking on a wet mattress over rock. Your boots are never dry. Your wool is never dry. The smell of the place is wet wool, ozone from the tram lines, rust, mushroom, and the sweet green breath of ferns.

Vegetation here does not grow tall. At this altitude and age, height is a waste. It grows thick. Everything low is lush to an almost indecent degree: knee-deep mosses in the hollows, ferns as tall as a person crammed into the sheltered lee of a boiler house, alpine grasses, lichens the colour of old pennies and sulphur, flowering shrubs that wedge themselves into mortar joints and flower in defiance of the cold. It grows on roofs, on the sides of moving trams, in the joints of bridge girders, inside empty engine casings where the condensate pool never evaporates. Rust feeds it. Iron oxide blooms as a kind of flower, and whole rusty gear-wheels in the scrap terraces are upholstered green.

The city is industrial, but it is an industry muffled by all this damp and softness. Foundries still run, though their furnaces are small and their chimneys cough more steam than smoke. Condensation distilleries hum day and night, separating drinking water from air. Tunnel mushroom farms, peat-moss works, rope and glass shops, a tram loop that ticks around the hollow with a patient, metronomic clack. Everything is electrified because fire is difficult in thin air and damp stone — stoves are hot plates, lights are everywhere, cranes move slowly and surely, powered by the ceaseless wind funnelled between the buildings.

And it is strangely quiet. For a settlement of this density — thousands of presences pressing wall to wall, layer upon layer, the noise should be formidable. But the plateau's stone is porous, the moss is a blanket, the fog is a curtain, and everything damp absorbs sound. A foundry two streets away sounds like someone clearing their throat. The tram is a sigh. The loudest, most continuous sound in the city is dripping. Drip from a ledge. Drip from a wire. Drip from the underside of a leaf into a metal cup left out to catch it. The city speaks in drips, counted out by the slow cycle of condensation and release.

Animal life, in contrast, is rare to the point of absence. The ancient, stable, thin-aired plateau offers little to large animals, and the young, active life has settled lower down where the rivers are. What remains is specialized and strange: stone-coloured marmots in the quarries, slow and almost silent; blind cave fish in the cisterns, pale as uncooked dough; furnace moths that live their whole lives in the warm flues of the engines; rust-eating beetles that travelled here in the first iron and never left. At dusk, if you are very still, you may hear something else — a single, hollow call from above the rim — and then nothing for days.

That stillness is deceptive. Under the quiet, the place is in constant motion. Fog banks advance and retreat over the domes in a matter of minutes. Rain arrives as a temperature change rather than a storm; frost forms on a copper pipe, melts, runs down into a planter where a fern is unrolling a new frond overnight. The industrial cycles turn: boilers breathe, trams loop, condensers fill and empty. Fungi push up through a pavement in the morning and collapse by evening. Mosses open and close like tiny lungs with the humidity. The ecosystem is not peaceful; it is busy at ground level, at fungal speed, at machine speed, at weather speed — everything but geology. The land cannot change. So the city must, endlessly, building over its own eroding edges, re-lining a tunnel before the damp takes its rivets, re-planting a roof before the moss drags the tiles down.

There is a strangeness to that bargain. The old plateau wears everything very, very slowly, and the city wears itself very, very fast. An iron bridge built a generation ago is already a garden. A house built this year will be thick with green by next winter. Sometimes, in the enclosed courtyards where warm air from a kitchen rises into cold air from a shaft, water will condense and fall *upward* for a few seconds, mist beading on the underside of a roof and raining back onto the warm stones below. The inhabitants do not remark on it. They wear hats, they carry cups for the drip, they speak more quietly than the fog requires.

At evening the lamps come fully on, not bright but amber and hazy in the damp, and the cloud sea below the rim turns the colour of ash. The city lights float in the hollow like a constellation that landed and decided to stay, dense, layered, damp, and lit. Above them the domes go dark first, then the mist. Nothing breaks. Nothing rises. The ancient stone holds its final shape, and on its back, for a little while longer, life and iron and moss keep remaking each other, one quiet, wet, cold day after another.
