It is high, and it is dark, and those two facts are fighting each other.

You come up out of the low country by a road that was never really finished, and the air gets thinner and warmer at the same time, which shouldn't happen. The plateau spreads out and does not stop. Not in the way a plain does not stop — in the way a weather system does not stop. It makes its own. A clear spell can be happening three kilometers away while above you a rain is falling straight down out of a black sky, warm as bathwater, and somewhere else again the mist is so thick you walk as if inside a lung. You learn quickly not to ask where the plateau ends. The horizon is a rumor. The fog eats it.

There is no sun to give you the lie of it. The sky is not night, exactly. Night implies a day somewhere else, a turning. Here the dark is a material, like wet felt. It presses. It has weight on the top of the head. There are no stars to puncture it; the cloud deck sits too low and too thick, forever condensing and breaking and condensing again. What light there is, is made.

That is the first rule, and everything else grows around it: nothing shines here unless it makes itself shine, or something else makes it.

The rock is basalt and old loam, settled. This is not a young landscape tearing itself up. The cliffs have slumped and smoothed, the valleys have stopped arguing and wide channels run in marsh-edged flats where water sits just under the surface, never deep enough to be a lake, never shallow enough to dry. The stone weeps. You can hear it before you see it — a fine ticking, a slow leak from the pores of the rock, rust-colored streaks running down faces that have been weeping, patiently, for longer than the machines have existed. Erosion here is not a catastrophe. It is a habit. Outcrops are rounded like sleeping animals. Even the rust on man-made things has moss on it. Everything has reached a truce.

And it is wet. Always wet. Condensation beads on your skin despite the heat, beads on the leaves, beads on hot metal and then runs off in sheets. The temperature is warm in a way that has nothing to do with noon — the ground itself is warm, from below, from the engines below that. Put your palm on a stone and it gives back heat. Put your boot down in the mire and it steams a little. The air smells of wet earth, of iron, of fertilizer and ozone and sweet rot.

Despite the dark, it is lush to an almost indecent degree.

The vegetation does not look like anything that needed the sun. The leaves are black-green, huge, waxy, made to drink whatever light falls. They grow not only in the valleys but up the sides of culverts, in the cracked seams of cooling towers, over the warm pipes where the factories exhale. Ferns the size of umbrellas; vines that net the ground in layered, near-touching mats so dense you cannot see soil for weeks; orchids that hang from cables; fungi in sulfur-yellow and corpse-pale sheets that glow faintly, a cold blue-green, along every wet log — not bright, but enough that your eyes learn, after an hour, to navigate by the green smear of a fungal bank. Some plants have finished their negotiation with the machines: they turn their faces, slowly, hour by hour, toward the nearest smelter, timing their opening to its pulse. Their flowers light up in sequence, amber, then white, like signal lamps. Pollinators come for the heat as much as the nectar.

It is dense here in volume, not just in cover. The air is crowded. Spores. Mist droplets. Seeds on the wind. Midges and larger biting things in clouds that move like a single animal. From the ground to twenty meters up is canopy, understory, vine-layer, root-layer, and beneath that a black, moving mulch full of worms and beetles and burrowing things you hear more than see. From twenty meters to the sky — if you can call it that — is a second traffic: birds that navigate by echolocation and by the blue work-lights, bats, moths the size of hands, things that swim through fog on leathery wings. Nothing is far from anything else. You brush past another creature every few steps, and it brushes past you.

The fauna is abundant and it is loud.

It never gets quiet here, even by the standards of a place with no traffic. The ground has a heartbeat. Deep, slow, industrial — a smelter or a pump-house or a row of generators, miles away, thumping through the basalt into the soles of your feet. Over that, the high whine of cableways. Over that, water: dripping, running, plashing into metal cisterns. Over that, the living chorus that starts at no particular time because there is no morning to start it: frogs bellowing from inside a cooling tower, from inside the rim of a silo, from flooded tire ruts; howlers in the upper canopy trading notes; a herd of something large moving through the mire at speed, splashing, grunting, their eyes — two green dots apiece — bobbing and vanishing and bobbing again. The sound carries absurdly far in the wet air and the hollowed basins. An echo can last a full minute. You hear a factory shut down in one valley while animals answer it in another, as if it were a call.

The built things are scattered. That is the second surprise. For all the industry, there are no cities. There are no towns. There are single buildings, far apart, days apart in the parts where the marsh and the fog conspire against you. A smelter. A pump station. A row of electrified silos. A small, bright, almost domestic hut with its generator and its antenna and its laundry line limp in the damp — one hut, then nothing for days. Each one is a small constellation seen from a ridge: sodium orange, work-light cyan, a string of perimeter bulbs flickering in sequence. Each one is half-sunk already, in its own way. Vines through the window. Ferns in the intake vents. Rust weeping down the painted walls in the same streaks as the basalt. The machines are still running — many of them autonomously, tending themselves, sending out squat harvester crawlers on wide tracks and long-legged walkers that cross the mire on stilts, mechanized and tireless, electrified at the joints with a blue arc when they step through water. But they are not dominating the place. The place has incorporated them, the way it incorporates a fallen tree. A factory is a warm rock now. Animals nest in its flues. Plants specialize in its waste heat.

The third rule: heat is rain. The engines warm the air, the air rises, it cools against the high cold stone at the plateau's rim, it condenses and it falls again as warm rain, half a kilometer away, feeding the very marshes the pumps were built to drain. The whole system turns over on a scale of hours. A harvester crawls through a field of engineered grain that has gone partly wild, glowing faintly along the seed-heads. Behind it, a flock of rat-like grazers follows, eating the spillage, and behind them a predator you never quite see, fast and wet, that uses the harvester's headlights to hunt by. Nothing stays still long enough to be only one thing. Births in the mist. Deaths in the runoff, which carries a faint chemical tang that the fungi love. A river that forms after a vent blows, runs for two days, and vanishes into a crack that steams.

It is strange in the quiet way of old, settled places that have had time to develop habits. The water in the upper marshes flows slightly uphill under the pull of the condensation towers. Some animals live only in steam: translucent, blind, fat with condensation, hanging in the white columns above the vents and dropping, one by one, to drink. The plants near the oldest smelter have learned to mimic its rhythm, opening and closing in perfect time, as if photosynthesis were a factory shift. And at night — which is to say, always — the bioluminescence and the work-lights do opposite things: the living glow is steady and low, the industrial glow is harsh and pulsing, and shadows fall the wrong way, because there are too many sources for them to agree on.

You get used to walking by sound more than sight. The plateau teaches you. A low hum means a pump-house ahead and dry footing. A sudden metallic ping, repeating, means a cableway swaying overhead with a load, and you should not stand under it — things fall off. A thickening of the frog chorus means the ground ahead is flooded and warm. The smell of ozone means the electrified fence is live and the animals are migrating along it, using it, stupidly and cleverly, as a guide-wire through the dark.

It is vast in a way that breaks your maps. You can be in weather that wets you to the bone while, on the next rise, a herd stands in still, warm air under a hanging sheet of mist that glows amber from a factory you cannot see. Distance does not guarantee sameness. A day's walk can be a change of climate, an ecology, a language of birds. The plateau does not have edges you can point to; it has transitions, and it makes them over and over.

And it is alive, actively, impatiently alive. Predator and prey in constant negotiation. Machines and moss in constant negotiation. Rain and heat and rock. The truce of erosion is not peace; it is a long, slow motion — a slope creeping, a rust flaking, a root splitting a pipe, a seed splitting a silo door. Everything is weathered, and everything is growing back over the weather, and everything is being eaten, and everything is being made again by industry a little farther on.

By the end of the first day you stop looking for the sun and start looking for sources. A green glow on a bank of fungi: footing. An orange flicker through the black leaves: a building, shelter, danger, warmth. A blue headlight moving, low and steady, through the mire: work, and the living things that follow work. The dark is not empty. It is full of made lights, and made sounds, and things that have learned to live inside both, dense and lush and wet and warm, up high where the air is thin and the rain makes itself, on a table of stone so old and so large that it can afford to have its own weather, its own night, its own industry, and go on — weeping, humming, blooming in black — without ever being finished.
