### The Underplain

You do not enter it so much as sink into it, over a long time. There is no cliff and no gate. The surrounding plateau softens, slopes down for hundreds of metres in a curve worn smooth as a hand-polished stone, and keeps going until the sky is no longer sky but roof.

It is a plain that has fallen in. A regional basin, perhaps twenty kilometres across, a few hundred metres below the surface that covers it. Above you is not open air but a vaulted ceiling of limestone and packed earth, and cutting through that ceiling, descending like the slow roots of a second world, are the roots of the trees that live up there. They hang down ten, twenty, thirty metres, gnarled and branching, festooned with moss and nests and vines. From below they look like an upside-down forest. From the floor, trees rise up to meet them. The two forests almost touch. In the middle, there is a dim, greenish dark that never quite gets light and never quite gets black.

This is the oldest shape in the place. Erosion here went further than in most places, and then stopped. Everything has been smoothed, settled, flattened toward its final form. The rim of the basin is a low swell, not a ridge. The floor is near-flat, with gentle rolls and broad terraces that were once something else. There are no sharp edges anywhere. No fresh scars, no new collapses, no quarry cuts. A stone you pick up is rounder than a river pebble, though no river has ever run here. The strata in the walls show through and then melt into lichen. To walk here is to walk on something that has been left alone for longer than any living thing can remember, and has become quiet in its bones.

The light is dim, and it comes in shafts.

It leaks through cracks in the roof, through the gaps between the surface roots, through a few wide fissures where the ceiling is thin. They are pale, amber-grey, dust-coloured columns, and they do not move like sunlight. They crawl. Over the course of a day they shift perhaps a tree's width. Over a season, a shaft may cross an entire district and then leave it again. Most of the Underplain is in a perpetual twilight of reflected green and grey; very little of it is in direct light at any one time, and nothing is ever in full sun.

Because the light is weak, the air stays cool. It is the cool of deep shade, of stone that never warms. Your breath shows faintly even at midday when you are standing in a shaft. Jackets would be unnecessary, and then suddenly necessary, and then unnecessary again. The cool, and the dim, are the governing mercy of the place: nothing evaporates quickly here. Nothing burns with energy. Everything lives slowly.

That is also how it is lush without water.

There is no free water. No rain ever falls — how would it? No streams, no lakes, no puddles after a storm, no well you could dig and find a drink. If you are thirsty, you find nothing to drink. The ground, where the light hits it, is dry mineral dust. Water exists here only in other forms: bound in the sap of a plant, in the body of an animal, condensing at night as a thin dew on the broad waxy leaves and on the cool stone, and then being drunk back in by root and leaf before the next shaft can dry it. A plant may go its whole life without ever standing in liquid.

And yet, because the air is cool and the light is gentle, a plant needs very little. The vegetation is not jungle. It is a lush skin. Every trunk, every wall, every mound, every fallen stone in the shaded parts is covered in a felt of moss that lives on dew, in fern-like fronds that unfurl a hand's width a year, in shrubs with thick, laurel-like leaves and in fat, slow succulents that store a decade's worth of moisture in a stem. The trees are sparse — you can walk a hundred metres between trunks — but each tree is enormous in its needs, sending roots out in a shallow, invisible web across the mineral plain, gathering dew from an area twenty times its crown. Between them the ground is pale, empty, scattered with pebbles and leaf-litter that has sifted down through the roof cracks like very slow snow. The emptiness is part of the lushness. It gives each green thing room to be entirely green.

And built through and under all of it is a city.

Not a human city. There is no metal here, no glass, no worked tool, no fire. Nothing in the Underplain uses a device. The city is grown, not made, and it was grown by patience.

Its builders are social insects, and the birds and small mammals that build upon the insects' work, and the plants that build upon theirs. Over immemorial time, termite colonies raised mounds in the clearings where the light fell. The mounds joined. Walls rose, a millimetre a year, of mud and chewed fibre and mineral cement. Avenues formed between them because the insects marched the same routes each generation, compacting the dust into paths. Plazas are the open, trampled clearings at the centres of the light shafts, where the roof is thinnest. Towers are fused mounds that have been added to for so long their original shape is gone; they now look like eroded hills with tunnels. Around them, birds have woven twig scaffolding into the upperworks, raptors and flocking seed-eaters roosting by the thousand in the hollowed chambers, and small burrowing mammals have excavated terraces and granaries — dry storehouses of seeds and dried leaf — deep in the mound-walls.

It is urban in layout, empty in feeling. A district may occupy a clearing the size of a village, its buildings low, broad, and soft-edged, its streets now grassy swales where the moss has taken them. Then there is a kilometre of open plain, of sparse trees and stone, before the next district rises. You could walk for a week across the Underplain and still be crossing it, passing forest, mound-field, ruined tower, root-bridge over a dry gully, and long stretches where there is nothing but dust, lichen, and the faint vibration under your feet. The density is low. The scale is not. It extends past any immediate horizon, which here is never a horizon at all, but the far, hazy wall of the basin and the interlaced canopy above.

Everything in the city has been eroded into the landscape. The walls are humps. The gates are gaps between mounds where the vegetation has grown thick. A tower is a hill that happens to have holes in it. Nothing is new, and nothing is recent, and that is the point. The building has finished. The slow work of termites still adds a skin here, a repair there, but the city as a form is settled. It will not noticeably change in a lifetime, or in a hundred.

The sound is the opposite of the stillness.

At first, descending, it is just a hiss. Then you realize the hiss is layered, and getting louder, and it does not stop. The Underplain is full of animals, animals in proportion far beyond the sparse look of the place. The noise is not made by crowds. It is made by multitudes spread very thin, each making a little sound, in a stone bowl that holds sound the way it holds cool air.

Insects are everywhere: crickets in the moss, beetles in the leaf-sift, ants and termites in the walls, their wings and legs a continuous dry trilling, like rain that never arrives. In the clearings, flocking birds rise and settle in the root-canopy, calling in unison, thousands upon thousands, wheeling up the light shafts and dropping back into nests in the mounds. In the shaded forest, slower creatures move and grunt and cough and snap: tortoises shifting through litter, lizard-like reptiles basking in the shafts, moles and shrews tunnelling under the avenues, arboreal mammals hanging among the descending roots, chewing and muttering. The hollow towers hum when air moves through the roof cracks. The whole basin is a resonator.

At the height of the day, when the largest shaft is at its fullest and the flocks are most restless, the chorus becomes deafening. Not a single scream, but a pressure. It gets into your teeth. You have to shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away, and then give up shouting. The animals do not hurry — they are cool-blooded and dim-adapted and unbearably slow in their movements — but they never stop making noise, as if sound were the one energy they could afford to waste. At night, when the shafts fade to almost nothing, it drops only a little. The insects take over completely, and the basin rings with a high, endless tone, long after the bird that made it has gone quiet, because the stone keeps it.

Ecologically, it is a closed, patient web. Plants feed on dew and on the slow rain of debris from above — leaves, spores, and dust sifting down through the cracks over years. Fungi, immense and unshowy, break down the litter and the dead wood of the city. Termites eat the wood and the fungi and build the ground everyone else lives on. Birds eat insects and seeds and carry seeds in their droppings to the moss under the roof roots. Small mammals eat everything: seeds, insects, grub, fallen fruit from the succulents. Reptiles eat the mammals. The big slow tree-dwellers eat leaves from both canopies. Nothing dies and is wasted; it is eaten, or it becomes wall, or it becomes soil, very slowly.

Nothing here is strange in the way of magic. No light glows in the dark, no plant walks, no animal speaks. It is all explicable, ordinary life carried to an extreme of patience. That is its rule: in the Underplain, water is never free, light never reaches everywhere, nothing is built quickly and nothing is broken quickly, and sound, once made, stays a long time in the stone.

You could live here for a year and find, in your notes, that the tallest tower had grown a hand's width, that a tree had put on one thin ring, that the light shaft over your camp had moved thirty metres, and that the city had settled a fraction deeper into its final shape. And all the while, deafeningly, uninterruptedly, the place would have been singing around you — lush, dry, cool, dim, ancient, and full.
