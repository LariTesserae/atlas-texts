You are standing on a floor that is not quite a floor.

Up close it is stone, but already it is refusing to stay stone. Under your boots the dust gives in a dry, pale sigh, and everywhere the dust ends and the grey begins, it is hard to tell where one starts. A slab the size of a table, rounded at every edge as if it had been held in a hand for a billion years, turns out to have a seam down its middle and a slow, almost imperceptible rise and fall along its back. It is breathing, very faintly, through plates of mineral.

This is the Still Shelf.

It is high. That is the first thing your body knows before your eyes do. The air is cool in a way that has nothing to do with day or night — a clean, thin chill that comes up through the soles of your feet and stays there. It carries no damp. Breath does not fog. The smell is of cold rock and salt and a faint metallic tang from broken glassy stone, and nothing else, ever. There is no meltwater, no spring, no rain, no dew, no wet hollow, no stream carving a green line through the grey. Water, in any form you can find, simply does not exist here. What looks like a pool in a shallow basin is a crust of white salt, perfectly dry, cracking underfoot like thin ice that has never melted. What looks like a riverbed is a dry groove in the stone, worn to a final smoothness and then left alone.

The Shelf is a plateau at the extreme top of a world, and it is also, impossibly, almost flat. Eons have had their way with it. Every peak that ever rose here has been worried down, every cliff softened, every sharp edge rubbed away until the whole surface has settled into its last shape. From where you stand, the land undulates in enormous, tired swells — a rise that takes a week to cross, a basin so shallow you walk down into it without noticing — and then, in every direction, it goes on. It is open in a way that makes the word wide feel small. The horizon is far off and slightly curved, blue-white where the thin air thins out, and beyond it are other tables like this one, floating in clean, ordinary daylight, their sides dropping away in smooth, ancient slopes into a lower, darker country you cannot quite make out. The light is normal. The sun is white-gold and honest, the shadows are black and sharp, colours are true. Nothing about the light is alien. What is alien is that it never changes.

You notice this slowly, because the place discourages noticing. There is almost no sound. Your own steps are muffled by dust, your blood is louder than anything around you, and once in a while, far off, a shell somewhere ticks as it cools and settles, a small stone click with no source you can find. There is no wind to rush past your ears, no birdsong, no buzzing, no creak of vegetation. The air above the plateau is so still that dust you kick up hangs for a moment in a pale cloud and then settles straight down, as if gravity were the only weather allowed. The flying things — for there are flying things — do not beat their wings. They hang.

### The pavement

The Shelf is not empty. It is overwhelmed.

Fauna cover it, ground and stone and air, in densities that at first look like pattern, and then like geology, and then like nothing else you have a category for. The dominant beasts are low, domed, and armoured, from the size of a bread loaf to the size of a large dog. Their backs are made of overlapping plates, grey-brown and rust-red and salt-white, as mineral as the rock they walk on. They have no visible eyes, no mouth you would recognise, no legs you would call legs — just a fringe of short, jointed limbs that move with dreadful slowness, or do not move at all. They eat dust. Not metaphorically. They press their blunt fronts into the pale powder, dissolve the silicates with a dry chemistry that needs no water, and excrete clean pebbles behind them in a perfect, regular trail. They take their energy from light through the translucent rims of their shells, the way a plant might, but without any of a plant's need for rain. Their bodies are not wet inside. They are fatty, salty, and crystalline. They do not drink. They do not sweat. They do not rot.

And there are so many of them that walking is an negotiation. You cannot put your foot down without deciding whose back you are stepping over. They lie near-touching, belly to shell, head to tail, in loose herds that stretch to the horizon and then re-form on the other side of it. On the larger slabs, smaller cousins cluster like barnacles, crustlings no bigger than coins, in layered fans of dozens. In the lee of a rise, a shallow haze of mote-drifters hangs motionless in the air, suspended where they hatched, translucent bladders full of dry spores, making no sound, casting no shadow, drifting neither up nor down. The volume of the place is saturated with presences: under the slabs, on the slabs, between the slabs, in the dust, above the dust.

When one dies — and you come to understand that death here is a slow hardening, a shell gradually becoming stone while still occupied — it does not leave. Its plates join the pavement. Its children, if it budds a copy of itself, bud it in exactly the same posture, in exactly the same place, with exactly the same chips and scars. So the population looks, at a glance, like a single impossible carpet, and at a second glance like a single impossible individual, repeated until repetition itself becomes the terrain.

That is the strangeness that grows on you. The clusters repeat. A group of seven beasts arranged around a pale salt patch, the largest with a cracked plate on its left side, appears again a hundred metres on, and again on the far rise, and again in the distance as a pattern in a much larger arrangement of the same seven. The stone slabs repeat their rounded forms. The basins repeat their shallow arcs. It is as if the world, having been worn down for so long, has run out of new shapes and has begun to copy the old ones, endlessly, at every scale. The fauna are both alive and rock, both one beast and ten thousand, both moving and never having moved. Their tracks are worn into the stone in the same places their grand-beasts walked, because there is nowhere else to walk. You realise you cannot reliably tell a sleeping beast from a boulder, or a boulder from a beast that has finished — and that the beasts themselves, blind and slow and silent, may not be able to tell either.

### The sparse green

Against this overwhelming animal multitude, vegetation is almost an afterthought, a rumour. Search a slope and you may find, one to a hundred paces, a tuft of needle-grass in a crack: pale, wiry, stiff as wire, a dull straw colour that does not brighten in the sun. Rarer still, a stone-flower, a small rosette of glassy filaments that opened some immense time ago and has never closed, never seeded, never wilted, because nothing here can wilt without water to lose. These few plants root in dust, drink nothing, and are ignored by the beasts, who prefer stone. A single individual may be older than any cairn, any tool, any human mark on the plateau. It simply persists, adding a millimetre of dry growth in a century, in a place without seasons to mark it.

### Marks of hands

People have been here. The marks are scattered, faint, and strangely preserved — because nothing decays here either. A cairn, hand-stacked from three flat stones, stands on a rise, and a near-identical cairn stands on the next rise, and the next. Flaked tools of black obsidian lie where they were dropped, edges still sharp enough to shine in the normal light, half-buried in dust, with a huskback's shell resting against one as if it were a companion. There is a single shelter, low and circular, built of stacked slabs and faunal shells, its doorway open to the palace of dust inside, where a simple awl of early metal and a bundle of mineral fibre lie on a stone table. Whoever built it understood, quickly, what you understand now: there is no water to carry in, no wood to burn, no soil to plant, no change to farm. One could leave a mark. One could not stay. The beasts have long since moved in through the doorway and arranged themselves inside, in the same quiet, identical postures as everywhere else, and the shelter is now just another rounded shape in the repeated pattern of the land.

### The rules of a finished world

The Shelf does not change. That is not a figure of speech. The sun hangs in the same place in the morning, and in the afternoon, and whenever you think to look again. Your shadow points the same way it pointed when you arrived. The beasts shuffle forward a fraction of an inch over the course of a day and shuffle back, or shuffle forward and are replaced, in the same place, by a copy of themselves that is exactly as old. Footprints you make in the dust do not fill in, do not blow away, do not soften. They remain, clean and sharp, in a place where nothing else leaves a trace of passing, and after an hour you find yourself following your own prints back to where you started, though you are certain you walked in a straight line across an open field.

Life here has reached equilibrium and stopped. Births are matched by hardenings. Eating is matched by excreting. The erosion that once shaped the plateau is finished; the stone is in its final form, and the fauna are in theirs, and the two have become indistinguishable. It is an ecology with no waste, no decay, no growth spurt, no season, no weather — a dry, cool, silent loop closing on itself at the top of the world, under a perfectly ordinary sun that will be in the same place tomorrow, and the day after, and for as long as anyone has been watching, which is perhaps why no one can tell how long anyone has been watching.

At night, or at what would be night if the light ever moved, the sky over the Shelf is a deep, clean blue, and the pavement of beasts stretches away in every direction, motionless, layered, and overwhelming, a living floor for a world that has no water, almost no green, and nothing left to become.
