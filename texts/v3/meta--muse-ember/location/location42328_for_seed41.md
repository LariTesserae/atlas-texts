It is hot before it is dark.

You enter by sinking. The opening is no more than a leaning fissure between warm stones, wide enough to shoulder through if you turn sideways, and it slopes down at once, not steeply, but steadily, so that the daylight does not cut off — it thins. Above your head, a root or two crosses the rock. Below your feet the ground changes from dry leaf-litter to something softer, darker, and already sweating in the heat.

This is a small place, and that is its first rule. From the entrance chamber to the farthest pool is perhaps a few minutes' walk, and you can walk the whole of it and begin again. But it contains multitudes in the way a room can contain a crowd. There are four chambers, really, linked by passages that pinch to a crawling width, and even the largest — a vaulted hall the size of a village chapel — feels crowded because almost nothing in it is alone. Fern touches ceiling. Moss touches stone touches root touches a lizard. Life is layered, near-touching, shoulder to shoulder.

The heat is not the sun's. It comes out of the stone itself. The limestone is porous and old, established into a settled form over a very long time. The walls are not sharp or new, nor are they crumbling. Stalagmites have broadened and fused into thick, rounded pillars; hollows have been worn smooth by water and by bodies; the floor is a layered cake of mineral crust, black humus, flattened leaf, and fine grit, weathered into balance. Whatever change happens here now happens inside that form, not to it. The rock radiates warmth up through the soles of your feet and into your palms when you steady yourself on a wall. In the deepest pocket the air grows hotter still and thinner with life, and there a single species of pale millipede rules, slow and by itself, marking the boundary. You can feel where the world ends by that thinning.

Light is dim, in the way of water seen through leaves. One narrow crack, high in the entrance chamber, admits a grey-green shaft that never quite reaches the far hall. It moves a little through the day as the sun moves above, but it is never bright, only less dark. The rest of the illumination comes from below: in the still pools, a faint algae glow lifts like cold stars under water, and when the animals stir it, it brightens for a moment and fades again. Your eyes adjust, but they never fully succeed. Shapes become textures. Greens become a brighter darkness.

Water here is a matter of pockets, not a river. It is damp rather than wet. Hot air rising from the deep stone meets cooler upper rock and condenses, leaf by leaf and stone by stone, until droplets gather and fall — not in a trickle you can follow, but as isolated, intermittent events. An hour may pass without one. Then the ceiling seems to exhale a fine mist, and a fern tip beads and lets go. The drips land in shallow basins scooped in the floor, in the cups of old stalagmites, in the folded hearts of moss. Those pools are still, warm, and mirror-like, never flowing away, never quite evaporating either. The damp lives in them and in the moss cushions that stay wet to the touch even in the dusty hot corners, where the floor is dry enough to hold a footprint.

Because it is that kind of damp, and that kind of warm dimness, the vegetation is lush without being jungle. Near the light-crack, ferns unfurl in broad, patient fronds, so green they seem to give off a smell. Every ledge is cushioned in moss, thick as velvet, in some places waist-high. Roots hang from the ceiling like ropes, some long-dead and mineralized, some living and questing downward for a pool. Mushrooms do much of the work here: clusters of white bells, orange cups, and soft puffballs that open and close in slow pulses, feeding not on sunlight but on fallen matter and animal waste, releasing their spores in invisible sighs. A few strange succulents, pale and waxy, store the damp in their leaves and flower downward, their blossoms hanging under the plant, pollinated by crawling insects, not by anything that flies. Many of the plants grow toward heat as much as toward light. Where the far wall is warmest, leaf and root lean into it, flattening against it, as if pressed there by an invisible hand.

It is the silence that makes the abundance strange. Sound is  almost absent. The porous stone drinks it. The moss, the humus, the soft bodies layered everywhere drink it. And the animals themselves have learned, or been shaped, not to make it. There are no birdsongs, no buzzing, no stridulation, no calls. A frog inflates its throat and it makes no sound. A lizard crosses the floor and its feet are padded. What you hear, if you listen long enough, is not a noise so much as a series of nearly-nothings spaced far apart: a single drip, a soft rub of a fern against rock when the warm air in the deep chamber slowly expands and pushes through the tunnel, a faint tick of a millipede's legs, a leaf settling. Then silence re-seals itself.

And yet the place is abundantly, densely alive, and constantly in motion in its slow way. Under almost every stone you lift — and the stones are already warm — there is a committee: isopods curling, many-legged worms, beetles with glassy shells through which you can see a dark gut working. Glass lizards, eyeless and lidless, press themselves flat to the walls in the warm shaft, breathing in slow, visible pulses, navigating by heat on their skin. Translucent frogs sit on fern fronds, their hearts beating visibly. Blind amphibians with feathery external gills fan themselves in the pools, never needing to surface. Snails with clear shells trace the undersides of leaves. Millipedes go in migrating columns across the floor overnight, eating the moss line by line, changing the shape of a chamber by morning if you knew how to see it. Tiny fungus gnats drift rather than fly, blundering from leaf to leaf in the dim light. Mud is tunneled by a mole-like animal that swims through it like water.

Predation does not hurry. A long, blind, eel-like animal waits half-buried in the pool chamber, its tongue flicking out to taste the air and the water, eating the frogs and salamanders that come too close in the dark. Lizards eat millipedes and beetles. Beetles eat fungus. Everything eats the damp and the dead, and the dead feed the mushrooms, and the mushrooms feed the gnats. The cycle is tight and complete and small; nothing leaves, nothing much arrives except a leaf or a root from above, once in a while, through the crack. The whole ecosystem is active, but its activity is measured in centimeters and hours. That is why it can be both abundant and silent, both dynamic and still.

There are scattered signs of building, old and now reabsorbed. In a side niche of the central hall is a low crescent of stacked stones, no mortar, half-collapsed, its interior filled with moss — a fire ring, perhaps, or a nest, or a seat. In the entrance chamber a shallow bowl has been carved into a pillar, too regular to be natural, and worn smooth by use. On a lower wall, five short parallel scratches repeat in three places, faded almost to nothing. A small dome of mud and woven leaf, once inhabited, now shelters isopods. No metal, no tool but stone and bone and fiber ever touched them, and whatever made them has been gone long enough that the place has settled around them and made them part of the floor. They are not ruins in the grand sense. They are hints, easily mistaken for lumps of rock until symmetry betrays them, and then easily forgotten again under a fern.

The unusual thing, if you stay, is how completely you are noticed by contact rather than by sound or sight. You feel the heat first on your back. Then the damp on your skin, where sweat does not evaporate so much as bead and stay. Then the density — every surface is within reach of something breathing, growing, waiting. The plants do not rustle. The animals do not startle away loudly; they withdraw in a slow, collective ripple, frogs sinking deeper into leaves, lizards flattening further, millipedes pausing mid-step, all of them watching your warmth pass. The place seems to be listening with its surfaces.

To be here is to understand that a world can be complete at the scale of a house. You can learn its four chambers, its one light, its few pools, its warmest boundary. And yet because everything touches everything else, you can never take it in all at once. The hall is at once cathedral and grove and burrow and nest. Heat lives at the bottom, damp lives in the pockets, green lives near the crack, and blind pale life lives everywhere else, in layers.

It does not need you to stay quiet. It is already quieter than you can be, and it will absorb your footsteps and your breath and go on condensing, dripping, growing toward warmth, eating and being eaten, for as long as the stone holds its long, settled shape.
