**The Rootworks**

You do not so much arrive in the Rootworks as step down into it.

The slope is not really a hill. It is a root-slope, a slow green-black incline into a hollow where the air has pooled and stayed cold. There is no horizon and no height to speak of — everything is low, sunken, held. All around the place is a boundary you can see if you stand in the right spot: a continuous palisade of black-stemmed ferns and thorned saplings, close as a fence, beyond which the dark is the same dark as inside. The whole of it could fit inside a cathedral. From the shallow mound of roots at the center you can take in the entire town at once — the ring, the lanes, the few roofs — and yet the town will take you a long time to cross.

For the first surprise is the emptiness.

A town should be dense. This one is planned like a town and occupied like a wilderness. Streets exist, numbered and named in cast iron screwed to bark: FERMENTATION ROW, HOUSING A, CROSSING 4. There are drains, hydrants, traffic lights blinking green for no one, power lines sagging from trunk to trunk. But between each presence there is a vast, damp interval of nothing much — an acre, sometimes more, of unbroken plant. A single house, then a whole plain of moss. A single mill, then an empty intersection with only a signpost in the middle. The infrastructure has been laid out with industrial optimism and then left to enormous distances, as if the town were drawn on paper at city scale and then shrunk, and the distances forgot to shrink with it.

Underfoot there is no ground, not in any ordinary sense. The substrate is living. Ankle-deep mats of liverwort and moss, fern-fronds stiff as knives, low crawling vines that root and re-root as you watch, make a springy, wet floor that muffles your step. Where the lanes run, the mat has thickened and flattened under whatever traffic there once was, but it has not been paved. It has been *persuaded*. Plants lean together to make a road, then relax. No soil shows except in the raw pits where the engines have dug, and even those fill quickly with pale roots. Vegetation is not in the town. The town is in the vegetation, and the vegetation is in the town, and after a while you stop trying to decide which is the frame.

The houses are the clearest example. They are not reclaimed houses. They were never built by hands and then overgrown — though they look like both. A house here is a hollowed trunk with a brick core still soft as unfired clay, its outer wall a layer of living wood that is, on the north side, brick again, and on the south side, leaf litter, and on the inside, a smooth, breathing skin. Roofs are canopies trained to tile themselves. Windows are clearings in the wall, not glass. At night some of them glow faintly from within, not with a lamp but with the phosphor of fungus that fruits in the damp of the walls. Others are dark and will stay dark. They are not finished. Some are barely more than a thickening of stem and a suggestion of a door. Some are already rotting back into the mat they came from, shedding bricks like seeds. They exist in both states at once, embryonic and decaying, because here construction runs backward: a thing must first be allowed to fall apart before the plants will grow it properly.

The factory district is the largest presence, and the only loud thing in spirit, though it makes almost no sound. It is built around a single enormous trunk — the Engine Tree — whose bark is plated with riveted iron, whose limbs are cradled in steel scaffolding, whose hollow heart has been turned into a mill. Chimneys are not chimneys but lianas trained upward, exhaling. Inside, the industrial process is legible and wrong. Fermentation vats bubble slowly, an acid-sweet smell rising from them, not steam. Turbines turn on pressure from sap and on the drip of condensation falling from cold iron onto hot iron. Conveyor belts carry root-cuttings, not parts. Looms weave cable out of vine and copper bark. The machines are not powered to make products; they are powered to make *conditions* — heat, vibration, moisture, a steady hum below hearing — under which the plants will know where to grow the next wall, the next pipe, the next street. What comes off the line at the end is not finished. It is a seed, or a raw brick, or a beetle still soft in its case, hatched into the substrate to do the work of assembly. The factory produces the town, and the town, when it dies, is fed back into the factory to be unmade into raw material. Matter and cause keep a very loose hold on each other.

Water is everywhere and never accumulates. That is the rule of the damp. The air is cool enough that condensation beads on every cold surface — on iron, on window-clearings, on your own sleeves — and falls in slow, intermittent rain that seems to start not in a sky but in the undersides of leaves. Gutters of folded bark carry it to shallow pockets in the lanes, lens-thin pools that hold frogs, blind fish, and the reflections of things that are not above them. Then, for no reason you can see, the pockets are gone, drunk by the roots beneath. Sometimes the rain runs upward: you watch a thread of water climb a root toward a leaf, gather there, and fall again, a small weather all contained within a single plant. The riverbed that should cross the town is only a damp line in the moss, a pre-river, an idea of a channel that the water has not yet committed to carving. Erosion here is still embryonic. Shapes are soft. Nothing has been fully worn into form, because nothing has finished forming.

And it is dark in a way that has texture. There is no sun and no day. The darkness is not absence; it is a medium, cool and faintly sweet, like peat water. The plants photosynthesize it. Where the ferns are thickest, the dark thins, eaten down to a pale, bruise-colored glow, the afterimage of light. Where you stand, the darkness closes behind you. The only true fire is in the Engine Tree, a furnace-heart that blooms orange when the vats are working, and the only other lights are biological: fungus on a wall, the belly of a frog, the green thread of a caterpillar that crosses a lane at your feet. Light is a waste product here. It is exhaled by things that have eaten too much night.

For all the quiet, the place is teeming and in motion. The quiet is a trick of the substrate. Moss eats sound as efficiently as it eats footsteps. A conveyor can run all day and make less noise than a drip. A root can push through a brick and not creak. Under the mat, and over it, fauna are abundant to the point of overwhelming if you crouch and look. Springtails like drifting snow. Frogs with tongues that flick and do not click. Blind newts that swim in the gutters. Beetles in their thousands, farming spores on the undersides of leaves, hunted by gnats, the gnats hunted by bats that are themselves only a rustle and a pressure change in the dark air. Larger browsers move at the edges — shaggy, slow things with root-mouths that trim the houses and the Engine Tree, leaving them cleaner. Nothing is still. Roots travel. Vats ferment. Rain starts and stops. Migrations cross the empty lanes along routes no one marked, compacting the moss into next season's streets. Predator and prey, grower and grown, are all active at once, a whole ecology in motion, muffled to a hush.

That is where the reality of the place properly breaks.

You learn, walking it, that distance is grown rather than measured. The mill looks close from the central mound. Walk toward it and the plain between you and it lengthens with fern. Look away and it shortens. The town is small — you can see its ring — and it is also larger than its ring, because the intervals between presences keep expanding. You learn that a wall watched steadily will both grow a leaf and shed a brick, and that you cannot tell which process is the building and which is the ruin. You learn that the houses breathe at night, faintly, and that when one finally dies it does not collapse; it opens, and a cluster of frogs spills out, and the frogs are the next house, in miniature, already rooting. You learn that the factory's finished products are root materials that do not yet exist, and that the town at its center is its own origin, still under construction, still pre-formed, still being dreamed by its own machines and plants in a loop with no first morning to break it.

Do not try to fix an image of it. It will not stay. The town that is coming into being is the same town that has already rotted and will rot again. The damp that never pools is the same water that is always raining from the leaves. The darkness is food, the plants are architecture, the architecture is animals, the animals are workers, the workers are machines, and the machines are growing next season's town out of this season's ruins.

You leave, if you leave, by stepping up the root-slope into the same dark you came from, only now it seems thinner, as if something behind you has eaten a little more of it. Behind you, in the hollow, a house you passed unfinished has put out a new stem. A vat has bubbled. A frog has eaten a gnat that ate a spore that will become a wall. The Rootworks is not finished being described, because it is not finished being made.
