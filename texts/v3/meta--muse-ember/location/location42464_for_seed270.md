From a distance it does not look like a place at all. 

It is a low, flat expanse worn down to its final form — clay the colour of old ash, stones rounded until they have no edges left, salt blooms in the hollows where water must have once sat. There is no water now, not even in the deepest crack. No green anywhere, no root, no leaf. The light is ordinary daylight, cool and even, and it shows everything truthfully: grey, rust-red, washed yellow. The wind is cool even at midday, and it carries no smell but stone and distant iron. The plain is very old. Whatever was here has been smoothed, settled, flattened by time until it could go no further.

And then, far off and very low against that flatness, a sound. A muffled hum, a clicking, a clank repeating at intervals, as if something large were breathing inside the earth.

It is a hall. Or rather, the remains of one. A long industrial shed — engine hall, grinding mill, group of stills and presses — half-sunk into the settled ground, as if the plain had slowly flowed around it and decided to keep it. Its roof has slumped to the angle of the land. Its chimney is gone, only a stump worn smooth like a broken tooth. Pipes run out of its sides and vanish into dust. Everything metal has pitted and softened into a mineral texture, like basalt. The paint has gone. The concrete foundation has been eroded by grit and by time into rolling, almost organic curves. It has been here so long that it no longer looks built. It looks eroded, primordial, the oldest stable form an industrial thing can take.

You would walk past it if not for the noise.

The door is warped shut by dust. The gap underneath is just wide enough to crawl through, and the dust pours in a fine stream like sand in an hourglass. Inside, the cool hits you at once — not the cool of outside, but a held, metallic cool, the thermal mass of concrete and steel that never warms. The air is dry. It makes your throat catch. There is no condensation except a faint beading on the coldest steel at dawn, and no drip, no puddle, no moss in any corner. Light comes in through broken skylights in hard, normal shafts, and through chinks at the eaves. It is enough to see colours true, and enough to make the dust visible.

And the sound is loud.

It is not quiet, ever. It is a layered roar: the dry rustle of a million legs, the whirr of wings that never quite stops, the click of mandibles, the groan-tick of metal as it cools, and at intervals a heavy relay snapping, a motor coughing over, a conveyor belt jerking a foot forward on a timer that has been running, unattended, for longer than anyone can remember. The rhythm is industrial even now.

The whole world is inside here. That is the strange intimacy of it. The hall is perhaps forty paces long and twelve wide, vaulted, and from one end you can see the other. The far wall is your horizon. The low concrete floor — worn completely smooth, dished and undulating by a century of grit and feet and bodies — is your plain. In the middle, dominating everything like a mountain range, is the engine: a six-cylinder diesel, a press, a mill, all chained, piped and bolted together into a single massif. You can climb it by its handrails and catwalks. Its pipes are ridges. Its valve wheels are stone-like outcrops. Around it, scattered small, are the other structures: a row of lockers like apartment blocks, a workbench island, a small control cabin with spider-webbed glass, a cage of tools, a rack of ledgers. They are half-buried. They are villages in this landscape, already being pulled down into the ground.

There is no vegetation here, and the absence is total. No weed cracks the concrete. In place of plants there are other blooms: orange fungal mats spreading over oil-stained felt, pale grey mould on leather belts, rust-flower crusts on steel, and the dust itself. The dust is the soil. It lies in dunes against the engine’s base, in drifts in the lee of the lockers, in deep valleys between the press feet. It is mineral grit, yes, but also shed skins, frass, pulverised bodies, insulation fibres, worn rubber — a dry, rich, moving ground.

Because the ground moves.

Fauna are not in this place. Fauna *are* this place, overwhelmingly, densely, to the point of near-touching. They are in layers.

The floor layer is a living crust, incandescent with motion. Mites no bigger than pinheads turn the dust over like ploughs. Beetles the length of a thumbnail, oil-slick and blind, burrow through grease that has congealed in the cracks and polish the concrete with their bodies. Moth larvae eat the rockwool insulation from inside the walls, leaving tunnels that tick faintly. Everything that dies is eaten within hours, and the eating is audible.

The wall layer is different. Centipedes and spiders, pale and long-legged, hang in strata up the concrete, holding artificial cliff faces of web and cast skin. Cockroach-like runners course in commuting lines along the pipes, indifferent to the heat or the cold. The lockers are black with them, inside and out.

The machine layer belongs to the specialists: larvae in the felt pads of the engine, beetles in its oil sumps, wasps that parasitise the moths and lay their eggs in the warm felt of the conveyor. They live on what the industrial world left behind — fats, waxes, rubber, rope, leather, paper, wood, processed food long since turned to powder. It is an unusual diet, and they have adapted to it in unusual ways. Many are eyeless or half-eyed, navigating by vibration through metal. Many get their water not from drinking but from the chemistry of eating fat, or by licking the dawn condensation off cold steel. A few have gone translucent with generations indoors.

And above them all, the air layer: a permanent weather of winged things. Midges, moths, flies, parasitic wasps, in a cloud no thicker than a fist at most times, hanging in the middle of the hall like a second ceiling. When something disturbs them — a motor jerking, a predator wave, you — they lift all at once and it rains. It sounds like rain on the roof, though there is no rain, and it falls as a living storm of wings that blots out the skylight for a moment, then settles again, layer upon layer, into its strata.

It is active. The hall is in constant motion, a complete little ecosystem turning over on itself with no outside input. Predators pursue prey in ripples across the floor crust: a big predatory beetle charges, the mites scatter like a flock, spiders drop on lines to take the runners. Parasitoids track the moth clouds. Cannibalism is common. At night, when the light through the skylights thins to that same cool grey, the migrations change — the air layer drops, the wall layer descends, the floor goes into a frenzy of mating and feeding, and the sound gets louder, a dry thunder of chitin.

And every few hours the machines wake. A fan turns, powerful enough to send the dust into a slow horizontal storm and the winged layer tumbling. A press sighs and settles a fraction. An electric panel buzzes and a strip light flickers on for no reason, giving that normal, flat industrial light at midnight, and the fauna adjust instantly, neither startled nor pleased, merely continuing. The built world is still functioning in its slow, pointless cycles, and the living world has built its own cycles right over the top of it.

This is the end state. Nothing new enters. The sealed doors, the lack of water, the lack of any living plant to photosynthesise — the whole system runs on what is already here, slowly consuming the hall grain by grain, turning steel into dust, dust into bodies, bodies into dust. The erosion that smoothed the plain outside has smoothed this inside too, but here the eroders have legs. The edges are all soft. The forms are all final.

You can walk the entire world in two minutes. You could, in theory, know every corner. And yet it is overwhelming, a million presences in the volume of a room, layered floor to roof, humming loud enough to hear through the walls from the plain outside. The quiet, empty, ancient land has nothing on it. Everything that lives, lives here, in this hidden pocket, cool, dry, mineral, and ceaselessly busy — a landscape that is not on the land at all, but inside a single worn-out machine, and inside the lives that have taken it, utterly, for their own.
