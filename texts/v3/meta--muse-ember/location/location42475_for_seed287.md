**The Turbine Fernery**

You do not arrive at it from above. You descend toward it, and the descent is the first change in the world. A narrow concrete throat, cut long ago into warm rock, narrows and then opens, and the air gets thicker with every step, not cooler. Heat rises from below to meet you. By the time you reach the bottom the sweat is already beading and evaporating off your skin in the same breath. This is a place below the surface — thirty, forty meters down in basalt and filled ground — sealed in a single vaulted chamber, cathedral-long and not much wider. You can take it in at a glance, and yet you cannot take it in at close range. It is small, but it is crowded to the point of suffocation.

The chamber is an old geothermal hall, or something that pretended to be one. The ceiling is a curve of stained concrete ribs, close enough overhead that the warm air sits on it and condenses. The walls are stone that has been lined with panels, pipes, and machinery until the original rock is only seen in glimpses, in corners, glazed and softened. Everything here has been here a long time. That is the first rule of the place: nothing is new, and nothing is sharp.

Erosion here is not dramatic. It is settled. Heat and damp have worn every edge down to its own equilibrium. Paint has blistered and slumped into a second skin over steel. Rivets have bloated with rust and been sealed over by mineral sinter, white and glassy as candle-wax. The floor — a mixture of grated metal, poured concrete, and packed earth where the grating failed — has been polished by a single, endless drip to a dark gleam in one spot, and left rough and felted with dust everywhere else. Dust does not blow here. It settles and stays, layering like sediment in a still pond. Tools, lockers, a small control booth with cracked green glass, a tiled washroom with its fixtures fused by vines: the scattered built things are all partial, all faded to faint marks. Numbers painted on pipes have dissolved into rust-colored ghosts. No one has built here in a very long time, and no one has cleared anything away.

And so the plants have built instead.

They are everywhere, in layers. This is a dense place, a near-touching place. At floor level, ferns crowd between turbine bases and spool under transformers. Their fronds are not the soft green of a surface woodland; they are thick, waxy, blue-green, hot to the touch, and divided into segments like radiator fins. They have grown to shed heat as much as to catch light. Above them, broad-leafed things climb the pipes, their leaves overlapping like shingles, trapping damp and dripping it back down in slow threads. Vines have insulated the steam lines in a living lagging, braided tight around the metal, and where they pass over electrical cable they have fused with it — you can trace a vein of green that pulses, very faintly, in time with the room. From the ceiling ribs hang other plants entirely, upside down, rooting in the condensation on the concrete and unfurling toward the warmth below, never needing the sun that was never meant to reach them.

Their leaves are immaculate. That is the second rule, and the strangest thing to register at first: there is no animal life here at all. No insects have chewed a single margin. No birds, no bats in the rafters, no webs strung between the ferns, no droppings, no footprints in the damp dust. Everything that lives here is plant, and plant alone, and therefore everything that falls does not get eaten or carried away. Dead fronds drop straight down and become a spongy, black peat under the machines, sweet and mineral-smelling, slowly turning to soil in place. Spores drift down in the hot, still air and land in oil sumps and grow in the grease. Pollination, if you can call it that, happens by proximity and by current. Some flowers open only when a machine cycles, gaping toward a transformer as if to drink its field. Some leaves, when brushed, give a tiny crackle and a spark, and are smelling faintly of ozone after.

The light is dim, and it does not come from the sky. It comes from below and from the machines themselves. Furnace slits in the floor breathe a dull amber up through the grates, back-lighting the fern canopy so that the undersides of the fronds glow for moments before the heat shimmer takes them. Sodium service lamps hang at intervals along the ceiling, most of them dead, two still burning with a tired orange that makes the wet metal look bruised. The youngest leaves have their own faint luminescence, a greenish phosphor along their veins, brightest right after a drip falls on them. You learn to see by that indirect light, by reflections in condensation and in the curved shells of turbines.

Water is only dampness here. There is no river, no pool worth the name, except for stagnant black pockets in the sumps where oil and water have separated and lie still under a skin of fern moss. The chamber’s water is in its intermittent form: it arrives as vapor from the hot rock below, rises into the thick air, and condenses on everything cooler than itself — on the upper runs of pipe, on the turbine blades that were designed to be cooled, on the undersides of leaves. Then it falls. Not as rain, but as a patient, irregular drip, drop by drop, from a dozen different heights, each with its own rhythm. One drop every seventeen seconds from the same joint for longer than anyone has recorded has carved a shallow, perfect groove in the concrete beneath it. The drops are warm. They bead and hang and fall and vanish into the peat, and the place smells of wet iron, wet stone, hot copper, decaying leaf, and the faint acrid tang of electrified air.

The machines are still working. That is the third rule. This is an industrial place, mechanized and electrified, and it has not stopped — it has only slowed to the pace of the plants that have taken it over. A great turbine turns at a pace you can almost miss, one revolution taking minutes, its shaft sighing through a bearing that the roots have packed with living grease. A conveyor belt, half-buried in fallen fronds, creeps forward at a rate that would move a leaf’s width in an hour, carrying a single pressed bundle of vegetation toward a press that closes with a soft, enormous sigh every three days or so. Transformers hum, low and loud enough to feel in your teeth. Control panels blink their green and red eyes in a slow binary, indifferent to whether anyone reads them.

All of it is loud. Not with commotion — with constancy. The sound fills the small chamber completely, as if the room itself were tuned to a chord: the bass drone of the turbine through the floor grates, the periodic hiss of steam finding a loosened valve and then sealing itself again with mineral scale, the clank of a counterweight, the tick of cooling metal, the drip-drip-drip that never quite harmonizes with any of them. Because the chamber is enclosed and dense, the sound has nowhere to go, and because nothing living makes a sound of its own, the machinery is the only voice.

Time here is not measured by day and night, for there is neither. It is measured by cycles that are too slow to notice changing. A fern is unfurling now that began unfurling years ago; if you returned in a season you would see that a frond that was a tight fiddlehead cinch is now a spread fan wide enough to shade a locker, and that the vine that was kissing a pipe is now a braid a hand’s thickness around it. The condensation gathers, falls, gathers again. The peat under the turbines deepens by a millimeter. A light that flickered for a decade finally steadies. Nothing hurries. Nothing collapses. Everything has reached a balance of heat, damp, dimness, and growth, and maintains it with a patience that feels, in a human timeframe, eternal.

And the weirdness grows out of that balance, the way moss grows out of a crack. Some of the “plants” are not entirely plants. One stand of what looks like broad grass near the far wall is in fact a bundle of insulated cables that have sprouted leaves along their length, each leaf a thin copper-green plate that warms when current passes through it and cools when it does not, as if photosynthesizing electricity. Some of the ferns grow in exact fractal copies of the piping above them, leaf branching for pipe branching, in a pattern too deliberate to be accident. In the hottest corner, where the floor grates glow almost red, there are pale, flowerless stalks that never open, that only lean, minute by minute, away from the heat and then back again, following the slow breathing of the furnace.

If you stand long enough, you begin to understand that this is not a ruin that has been reclaimed. It is an ecosystem that has incorporated its ruin as a skeleton. The hot, below-surface dark; the damp that never becomes wet; the dim amber light; the dense, layered green; the scattered huts and humming engines; the complete absence of any creature that walks, flies, crawls, or swims — all of these are conditions of each other. The plants drink the heat the machines make. The machines are kept from seizing by the moisture the plants transpire and condense. The drip feeds the peat, the peat insulates the pipes, the pipes warm the roots.

It will be like this, unchanged in its essentials, for as long as the rock below stays warm and the old current finds its way down. A cathedral of ferns around a sleeping engine, growing slower and slower into stone, loud with a sound that never ends, and utterly, completely, still.
