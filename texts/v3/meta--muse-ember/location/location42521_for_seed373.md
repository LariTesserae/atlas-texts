It is heard before it is understood.

You come up through a narrow notch in the rock, lungs thin and burning, ears popping, and the place comes at you as pressure first. A deafening, layered roar that is not like wind or water or machinery alone. It gets into your teeth and the soles of your feet. You cannot hear your own boots. You cannot hear yourself think aloud. The sound has no source you can point to, because it is everywhere at once.

And then, in the ordinary light of afternoon, you see how empty it is.

It is a single high bowl, not much bigger than a cathedral nave laid on its side — perhaps two hundred steps from one rim to the other, and you can see all of it at once from the centre. That is the first strangeness. For all its emptiness it feels enormous, because there is so much *between* things. You walk and walk over bare stone, minutes of it, with nothing.

The ground is dry mineral through and through. Pale grey-blue slabs and pillows of basalt, cracked in a fine, dried pattern like the bed of a sea that never held water, flooded now with a crust of frost that behaves like salt. It crunches underfoot and does not melt. There is no stream, no puddle, no damp earth, no wet smell. The air tasted of cold stone and ozone. But the stone is not new and jagged. It has been worried for a very long time into settled forms — edges rounded as if by hands, hollows worn smooth under overhangs, cairn-like stacks that have found their balance and stayed. It is an old place, weathered into its own state. In shadow, even at midday, frost stays in the cracks and your breath shows. You are high up, cool, above other peaks that drop away below into a hazy void. The sun is white and normal, the sky a thin, hard blue, colours true — that ordinariness makes the rest harder to believe.

Then, in sheltered seams, you find the lushness, and it makes no watery sense at all.

Plants grow here without water. What looks at dawn like dew is a thin, volatile mist that is not water — it beads clear on leaves, smells faintly coppery, tastes metallic, and is gone by mid-morning. It condenses in the cold nights, in the lee of rocks, and around a few impossible objects. The vegetation drinks it with wick-roots and does the rest with stone.

Between the emptiness, clumps flare up thick and impossibly alive: paddle-leaves as thick as skin and cool as river stone, storing not water but a mint-scented oil; whip-reeds, hollow and jointed, that grow in tight, feathery tufts and sing; metallic moss that is not moss but a mat of living filaments, silver-green, inching over the slabs; ferns that unfurl ten centimetres overnight and then tear themselves apart by noon, scattering spores that glitter like dust. They grow upside down under overhangs, their roots etched into the rock with their own acids, feeding on the mineral itself. Where they grow, they grow *lush* — dense, overlapping, vivid — and then there is a long, empty walk to the next pocket, ten, twenty metres of bare, ringing stone. The lushness is localised. The emptiness is not.

Life is abundant here in the same way. Not in volume, but in motion. The sky is rarely empty for long.

First there are the Listeners. Thousands of them, small fliers with huge translucent ears fanned behind their heads, blind, navigating entirely by the roar they themselves make. They turn as a single ribbon, then shatter, then re-form, screaming in a chorus that rises and falls like a tide of bells. On the ground, flat, stone-grey grazers crawl with a rasping grind, their bellies sealed to the rock, scraping minerals and mist for food, leaving polished trails. Hoppers go by in silence except for the *thock* of their legs — they hear through their knees, through vibrations in the slabs, and freeze, tilting a leg to the stone, listening. Under the overhangs, where the plants grow down, things skitter and nest and burst out in sudden, hyperactive swarms. Nothing rests. The whole bowl is an anthill of energy in constant, turbulent exchange: a plant detonates its spores, a flock veers, a gust reverses itself mid-air, a grazer is mobbed and stripped to shell in minutes, a new shoot is already in its place.

That turbulence is the settled state. It is not a storm passing through. It *is* the place. Erosion here went on for ages not despite the churn, but because of it — everything flung against everything else, polished, worn, rearranged, and always returning to the same bowl-shape. Growth and destruction cancel out in a din.

Part of the din, you learn after a while, is not animal at all.

Scattered — very scattered — are the built things. Not houses. Remains. A single small shelter of fused stone and smoked glass, half-sunk into the rim, its door open on one cold chair and nothing else, and still, impossibly, faintly warm inside. Three needle spires of an alloy like bone and wire, leaning together, vibrating at a pitch you feel in your chest more than hear. A great broken ring, tilted, buried to its hub, still turning slowly against the wind, holding a little constellation of stones aloft in orbit around it for no reason you can find — they have been there, levitating with millimetric precision, long before you. Dust near it falls, very slightly, upward. Near the ring, and near the spires, the mist condenses thicker, beading into a clear, bright fluid that plants lap at and animals drink. It is the tech the place runs on: engineering for which your physics has no tidy word, nanoscale sculptures of glass that repair their own scratches when you look away, filaments too thin to see that catch the wind and turn it into that harmonic under-roar. They are not maintained. They do not need to be. They have become part of the erosion, part of the ecology. Faint marks cross the stone where something once walked in a spiral, etched almost away, and the grazers follow that spiral still.

All of it contributes to the sound, and the sound contributes to all of it. The whip-reeds hollow in the wind. The Listeners scream to stay together. The spires hum. Stones knock and roll a few centimetres and knock again. Your own voice is lost. You learn to communicate by laying a palm on a slab and feeling what the grazers hear, by touching a companion’s wrist, by signs.

At the rim the world stops abruptly. The moss and the plants are cut off in a straight line, as if by a wall you cannot see. One step beyond, the roar drops away to almost nothing and the air is still and thin and you can hear your own heartbeat. The bowl is bounded like a room. Outside it is only drop and other mountains and the normal wind. Inside it, it rings.

Stay long enough and you stop trying to parse the noise into sources. It becomes a weather inside your head — cool, high, dry, bright, empty, and deafening with life. A small, old, settled hollow where nothing is at rest, where lush green paddles drink copper mist off dry stone, where flocks tear the air apart and stones hang in the air, and all of it, endlessly, destroys and remakes itself in exactly the same shape.
