It is, at first, empty.

You are at an extreme height — not on a peak, but on top of one, in the sense that the whole country has been lifted and left there. A tableland four, five thousand meters up where the air is thin enough that your breath comes short on even a gentle slope, and the sky at noon is not pale blue but a deep, hard blue overhead, softening only at the far rim. From here the world drops away in every direction, and you can see for days: folded, worn ranges below, other tablelands like yours floating at the same level, and between them weather moving like a slow, deliberate procession.

The light is ordinary daylight. No strange color, no perpetual dusk. A clean, white-yellow sun, shadows short and sharp at midday, a little blue haze gathering in the low places far under you. The warmth is the surprise. For all this height, the days are warm. The sun heats the thin air quickly and heats what is under it even more, so that at afternoon the ground itself holds warmth against your palm. At night it sheds it fast, and the cold comes in as a clear, mineral bite, but the days belong to heat.

And the ground sheds the first impression of emptiness, if you kneel.

There is no soil. Not in the usual sense. There is no exposed earth to be eroded into valleys, no sand, no scree slopes of fresh stone. What your hands touch, what your feet sink a hand's breadth into with a soft, springy give, is plant. A continuous mat, moss-thick and grass-fine in places, fleshy and fibrous in others, woven of rhizomes and fine roots and fallen leaf that has never quite rotted. It covers the plateau entire — plant as substrate, plant as ground, plant as the only living mass there is to walk on. Where the rock should show through, the mat has grown over it in ancient, rounded swells, so the whole high country feels like a skin stretched over bone. It is green in a thousand aged greens, ochre where it is dry, almost black where the damp sits longest. It smells green and faintly sweet, like wet hay left in the sun, and metallic underneath, from the stone far below.

This is why it looks empty while being, in another way, total. Because the mat is flat. There is no forest. Vertical things are rare and very far apart. A single stalk-giant may rise a hundred paces from the next, its trunk a column of living wood wound with vines, its crown a parasol of huge, broad leaves, then nothing again but open mat to the horizon. Those giants are the architecture of the place. Their hollows shelter things. Their undersides catch condensation. Their shade is a small, intensely sought country. Between them — nothing but the spongy, breathing carpet, wind-combed into slow waves, and sky.

The rock that does emerge does so as an elder. Erosion here is not a process in progress, it is a conclusion arrived at long ago. Every outcrop is smoothed as if by water and time together, domes and low blisters and long, slumped swells with no sharp edge anywhere. The plateau itself is the settled form of something primordial: a mountain range worn down to its final curve, then lifted again, then worn again, until nothing young is left. Dust on the mat is fine and old, the color of bone. There is no fresh cliff, no new river cutting, no landslide. Everything has been here for an age and has learned to stay still. That stillness is what makes the life on top of it so startling.

Water is only damp. There are no lakes that last the day, no rivers. Rain comes as occasional, warm, passing curtains far off — you can watch them cross the far tablelands, hear them maybe an hour later as a soft hissing — but most moisture arrives another way. At dawn the plant mat condenses. The whole country sweats. A thin shine covers every leaf and filament, puddles form in the shallow, ancient hollows of the mat and are gone by mid-morning, drunk back into the sponge. Inside the giant leaves, water collects on the underside and falls in slow, irregular drips, tick-tock against the mat. Animals lick it from surfaces. The builders — of whom more later — catch it in woven cups. By afternoon the air is again dry enough to crack your lips, steam rising faintly off warm green. Dampness here is a pulse, not a permanence: overnight wet, midday warm and close, then wet again.

And then there is the sound.

It is deafening. Not a roar from a single source, but a totality you feel as much as hear. The high wind is constant, moving across the open mat in long, low rushes that comb the filaments flat and then release them with a sighing crackle. Under that, and through it, is the chorus. It comes not from the sky, though things pass through the sky, but from the ground itself. You stand on plant and plant is crawling, humming, chirping, rubbing, sawing, drumming — millions upon millions of small lives in the sponge beneath your boots. Choir-beetles in the rhizome hollows. Mites that rasp like tiny files. Burrowing things that send slow ripples of vibration through the mat, which the plants themselves carry and amplify. At times the whole plateau seems to resonate, a low, insectile thrum that gets into your teeth and your sternum, so you must shout to be heard even by yourself and the shouting is lost.

From a distance it looks empty of fauna. Up close it is overwhelming. The mat is an animal. Its underside teems. Its surface hosts grazers: slow, long-legged, shaggy beasts the size of houses that move like walking architecture, eating the mat standing up, leaving a clean, pale swath behind them that slowly greens again. They are preyed upon, openly, in the full light, by things with wings the size of sails that can be seen for kilometers as they stoop — and because the scale is regional and there is almost nothing to hide behind, a hunt can cross a whole valley in view, a slow, stately chase across the sponge. Twice a day, or sometimes not for days, the sky darkens as a migration passes: not birds as you know them, but swarming flyers in numbers that block the sun for minutes, their wings a second wind, their calls layered over the ground chorus until the total is a physical pressure on the ears. Then they pass, and the emptiness returns, with only the constant hum from below to say the place is still full.

That is one of the rules here: bodies are not separate from the ground. Nothing is allowed to die and lie. A dead grazer is eaten where it falls, first by the mat itself — fine white roots thrust out to drink it — then by the beetles, then by the flies. Nothing touches bare stone for long; the mat covers, the fauna consume, the dust is old. The builders understand this. Their few, scattered structures obey it too.

Built things are rare to the point of rumor. You may walk for two, three days with nothing but mat, giant, giant, wind, and chorus, and then find a single hut where a stalk-giant has died and its hollow trunk stands dry and open — a round shelter woven shut with living vine and dead fiber, warm inside where the sun touches it. Or a cairn of smooth, hand-placed stones on a rise, each stone polished by handling. Or a fire-pit, long cold, lined with ash and a few charred sticks. The tools left behind are primitive in the most literal sense: chipped obsidian and flint, bone awls, axes of stone hafted with cord, woven baskets of plant fiber, a simple loom frame. No metal but what small bits have been hammered and kept. No wheels, no pottery that lasts — clay is scarce where plant is ground. The people who made these things are small, dark, and live largely inside the hollow giants and under the mat's edge, moving by vibration and signal because of the deafening sound, appearing and vanishing in the vast spaces between presences as if the country itself were their house.

There are other rules that emerge if you stay. Movement is expensive at this height and in this sound; you learn to step lightly, to let the mat spring back. The giant plants open and close their leaves not with the sun alone but with the chorus: when the subterranean hum rises, they lift; when it falls to its baseline thrum, they settle. The warm damp of morning is a time of frantic activity — insects surfacing, grazers feeding, water running in braided threads no deeper than your thumb across the sponge. The warm dry of afternoon is a time of endurance: everyone and everything pressed into shade or into the mat, the sound narrowing to wind and a single, endless insect note. At night the light goes, the temperature drops, the condensation begins again, and the chorus changes key, deeper, slower, as if the plateau were dreaming in vibrations.

It has no edges you can walk to in a day. The tableland extends past the immediate into a region of other tablelands, other mats, other isolated giants turning their crowns in the wind. Rivers are below, out of reach, in a different air. What is here is high, warm, ancient, worn to final form, and yet actively alive in every layer: plant eating animal eating plant, weather crossing in visible processions, swarms arriving like fronts, dew arriving like a tide and leaving like breath.

It is not a forest, and it is not a desert, and it is not a mountain in any familiar way. It is a living skin at the top of the world — empty to the eye, overwhelming to the ear, damp at dawn and warm at noon, covered in green where there should be stone and in stone-smooth age where there should be youth, with only the faintest, most scattered marks of hands that have learned to live by not disturbing it too much.
