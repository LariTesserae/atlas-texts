It is high, and warm, and you know both at once. The air is thin enough that your breath comes shorter than you expect on the climb, and yet the ground under your feet is warm through your soles, damp-warm, as if the plateau itself is breathing heat up from below. You can see a long way — this is an open country, a valley-sized tableland that opens past the next rise and the next, feature after feature dissolving into low cloud at the horizon. Ordinary daylight. Ordinary blue. True colors. Nothing glows, nothing is twilight.

And it is wet in a way that is not weather.

Water is not in rivers here. It is everywhere near the surface, in the air, on every surface, beading and re-beading. Mist does not drift; it sits, condensing on itself. Every leaf weeps. Every stem sweats. The shallow sheets between things are never still for long, because a drip is always striking them, and the drip is always the same.

The ground gives first.

There is no soil. If you push aside the bright, spongy mat underfoot — a living weave of root-cord, stem, and flattened leaf packed so tight it feels like decking — there is no earth beneath it. There is woven green-grey, and then water, and then more woven green-grey, springing back. It is warm, saturated, faintly sweet-smelling, like a greenhouse floor that has been left forever at the exact moment after watering. Your footprints fill themselves almost as you lift your feet, not because the mat is soft in the usual marsh way, but because it is elastic and full, and it wants to be flat again.

That mat is the whole substrate. Vegetation is not in this place. Vegetation *is* this place. Everywhere you look, to the far clouded edge, the surface is plant. The pools have a skin of duckweed-thick green. The rises are green. The hollows are green. And because the green is all, it paradoxically feels empty.

Because the presences on it are sparse.

A few hundred meters out, across water that covers your ankles in a clear film, stands a tower. Not a tree. A house-shaped plant, twenty meters high, hollow, its walls braided from living stems that have grown in deliberate spirals, its roof a dome of overlapping leaves that have opened just far enough to let light in and no farther. Its skin is not bark. It is wet, smooth, pale in places where new growth is permanently new, with seams where one whorl meets the next, like basketry. There is a doorway — an arch in the braid, clean-edged but not cut. No cut ends anywhere. No tool marks. No dead wood, no stone, no metal, no chimney, no track, no wire. Nothing in this landscape was made with a device. It was made entirely, which is different: grown into shape and then held.

Another, farther still: an arch that almost meets. Two great braided ribs rise from the mat, curve toward each other over a narrow channel, and stop. The gap between them is a hand's width. It has always been that width. The tips are frayed into soft rootlets that curl, reaching, never arriving. Between the tower and the arch, nothing stands. Only the mat, the water, the drip-lines — thin living cables that run low across the ground from one presence to the next, sagging slightly, carrying beads of water along inside them, out of sight. You walk and walk between buildings that are plants, and plants that are buildings, and the distance is the main architecture. A town would be the wrong word. A scattered constellation, each single vast organism separated by its own small sea of green emptiness, each one visible from the next because the plateau is open and unobstructed.

Up close the construction is more specific, and stranger. Some of the presences are bridges: long, bowed spans of braided stem, hollow underneath, with a walkway of flattened, living leaves that do not wilt. Some are baskets — immense open cups with walls of latticed stem, maybe to catch condensation. The cup is always brimming. It never overflows and it never empties, because the wall leaks exactly as fast as the mist feeds it. Some are stairs. A hollow tower can be climbed by a staircase that is also a plant, each step a broad, stiff leaf arranged in a perfect spiral, leading up to a platform whose railing is a row of half-unfurled buds. The buds have been half-unfurled for as long as anyone — or anything — here has been able to notice. They are embryonic, and they will stay embryonic.

That is the rule that holds the place together, and the strangeness of it: nothing here is becoming. It is all *just* becoming, forever.

Erosion, in the ordinary sense, has not touched it. There is no weathering, no lichen crust, no rot, no fallen leaf litter, no bleached bone, no scar. Colors are raw because everything is fresh in a way that does not age. A new leaf is eternally new and slightly translucent at the edge. A broken-seeming fray is not damage; it is unfinished growth, held at the instant before completion. The arches do not close. The domes do not finish growing over. The platforms do not smooth. And nothing changes to finish them, because change itself is absent. The cloud bank in the east is the same shape it was an hour ago. The sun — if that moving light overhead is a sun — does not move appreciably; shadows fall the same way all day, though it is never quite noon. Water does not run downhill so much as it seeps, condenses in one place, drips, is taken up again by the mat, and condenses again, in a closed, warm, high-altitude loop. Everything transpires exactly what it absorbs. Everything leaks exactly what it receives. It is an equilibrium so perfect it feels arrested.

And yet it is loud.

The loudness is the third surprise, after the height and the wet ground. In a place with so much space between things, with no wind worth naming, you expect quiet. Instead, the plateau is filled with sound from the moment you arrive: a tapestry of drips striking hollow stems, water moving inside root-cord, condensation beading and popping off leaf surfaces, larger drops falling through the braided walls of the towers and ringing them like wet organ pipes. A tower is a resonator. Water enters its base and travels up inside the living wall and weeps out at the top, and on the way it sings — low sighs, high pings, a constant counterpoint of plinks that never rise or fall. One drip-line crossing the mat ticks the same rhythm, over and over, twenty times a minute, loud enough to turn your head, then immediately forgotten because it has not changed in the next minute either. It is not the loudness of a storm. It is the loudness of a mechanism that has no off switch and no variation, and because it never varies your ears cannot learn to ignore it. The plant-as-architecture is also plant-as-instrument, and the water-as-substrate is its player.

Ecology here is total and almost empty at once. The plants do the work of everything: they are ground, house, bridge, reservoir, roof, road, and instrument. There is no soil fauna, no burrowing, no grazing complement. Animals, in the sense of warm bodies moving across the mat, are rare to the point of rumor. If you walk for a day you will likely see none. If you are very still, very long, by one of the basket-plants, you might notice a pale, translucent thing suspended inside the hollow, filter-feeding on the condensation as it forms, hanging from a single thread of stem. It does not move toward you. It does not move at all, except for the slow, barely perceptible pulse that is its breathing, which is also always the same. It is not that it is hiding. In a world that does not require motion, motion has simply become optional, and almost nothing has chosen it.

The air tastes of leaf-water. It is warm on your skin despite the altitude; warmth rises off the mat in a steady, damp heat, so that your face is cool in the thin wind and your feet are warm in the wet green, and both sensations are constant. There are no seasons to wait for and no nights to relieve you. Light stays normal. Sound stays loud. The mat stays warm. The half-open buds stay half-open.

Standing between two of the distant presences, with clear water over green and mist beading on your arms, you understand that this is not a ruined built world reclaimed by plants, and not a wild plant world that happens to look built. It is a made world in which making was done by growth alone — a high, wet, warm plateau constructed entirely out of living structure, built once, sparsely, on a regional scale, and then fixed at the moment of emergence. No tools were ever used here. Nothing was ever cut, burned, or buried. Nothing grows up, nothing falls down, nothing finishes, nothing decays.

It is new, and it will always be new. That is why it is strange, and why, standing in the middle of all that green emptiness with the dripping ringing in the hollow towers around you, you feel as if you have arrived in the first minute of a world and will never be allowed to arrive in the second.
