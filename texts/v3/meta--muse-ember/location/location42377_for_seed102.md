You hear it first, and it is loud.

Not a noise that comes and goes. A whistle, thin and needling, that runs without a breath. Under it the scrape of legs on stone, thousands of tiny brushes, scrape-scrape-scrape in no rhythm you can follow and also in a rhythm you cannot escape. Under that, a boom so low it lives in your teeth and in the soles of your feet, and over everything a constant ticking and popping, like knuckles cracking inside a drum. It does not rise. It does not fall. It is exactly as loud in the far corner as it is at the entrance, as if the room itself were singing at a fixed volume and you had merely stepped inside the song.

The heat comes next, before your eyes have anything to do. Warm air presses against your face and does not move. No draught, no coolness, no change as you shift your weight. It is the warmth of a closed oven that has been closed for a very long time, the warmth of earth held against skin. Your throat is dry as paper almost at once, and the sweat on your neck vanishes before it can gather. Somewhere in this warm, still air is a lush smell — crushed leaf, hot wax, warm stone dust, something faintly sweet like burnt almond — and that is the first wrongness. There is no water here. No drip, no seep, no damp patch, no wet smell. The floor is powder-dry. The walls are powder-dry. You run a hand along a stone and it comes away dusty and warm, and you look for the place where the moisture must be hiding, because green things cannot live without it, and you cannot find it.

The light is dim and does not come from above. There is no sky. There is no day. Weight sits on your shoulders — the whole mass of the ground you have come down through — and the dark is not black. It is a bruised, violet darkness, the color of a deep bruise seen from inside. Your eyes adjust slowly, and then shapes begin to separate, not by daylight but by a phosphorescent glow that seems to come out of the walls themselves.

The place is a pocket. That is the right word for it. Not a cave system, not a canyon, not a field. A single, contained hollow, perhaps fifteen paces across, perhaps eight high at the crown of its dome, embedded wholly inside rock below a flat, dry world of ash and salt. From the surface you would never know it was there except for a faint warmth in the ground and a crack no wider than a hand, through which heat and noise somehow rise. Inside, the dome is curved and pitted, studded with crystal. The crystals are the lamps. Pale blue-violet, some greenish, some the color of weak amber, they glow with a steady, unblinking light. They do not flicker. They do not brighten or fade. Around each small cluster of crystals, the light falls off in a few steps, and beyond that the room returns to shadow.

And it is the crystals that explain the green.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not spread across the floor like a meadow. It grows in islands. A dozen islands, maybe fewer, scattered sparsely across a great deal of bare stone, each one ringed around a glowing outcrop as if the plants had planted themselves around a lamp and meant to stay there. Between the islands there is emptiness — broad, warm, polished panels of black stone, solitary pebbles, a single fern standing alone in the dark far from any light — and that emptiness makes the islands look more luxuriant by contrast, more deliberate.

Up close the islands are thick. One is a carpet of star-rosettes, each rosette no bigger than your thumb, mauve and felted, overlapping until the mat is as thick as a quilt and warm to the touch. Another is a curtain hanging from the dome, vines and hair-fine strands falling in sheets, dotted with hard pods like beads, none of them swaying. Another rises as a cluster of great stalks, translucent at the base, lifting fronds as wide as ears up toward the ceiling until they touch the stone and stop, as if they grew until they could grow no farther and then decided to remain. There are shelves of amber fungi stacked like steps, round bulbs in tight bunches, ferns whose plume-heads are feathery and dry to the brush of your hand, and everywhere a fine, powdery dust on the leaves, as if they had never been rained on in their lives. Which, in a sense, they have not.

They drink the air. You begin to see how it must work, because there is nothing else to drink. The leaves are built to catch, all feathery edges and waxy surfaces and fine hairs, to comb something invisible out of the still, warm atmosphere. The roots — if they are roots — are not in soil. They are mats of pale fingers spread across the stone, dissolving it grain by grain, feeding on mineral dust and on the faint light of the crystals. The sap, when a thorn of the rock scratches a stem, is not water but a warm, clear resin that smells of candles. Life here has learned to run without a liquid to carry it, on wax and vapor and stone, in a sealed loop that never lets anything out.

The floor teaches you the age of the place in a different way. Or rather, its youth. This is a fresh fracture. The basalt underfoot is broken into scales and shards whose edges are still sharp enough to cut a boot, to cut skin if you fell. Crystal faces in the walls gleam white where they were split, unsullied, not dulled by weathering or time. Nothing has been rounded by water, because there is no water to round it. Nothing has softened. A boulder balanced on a point is balanced still. The rock looks new, as if the pocket had only just been opened to the world — and yet inside it, everything is fully grown, fully occupied, as if it had been sealed and left to finish itself in the dark and then frozen at the moment of completion.

Because it does not change. That is the rule you feel in your bones after a while, even before you can name it. A dust mote hangs in the crystal light exactly where it hung a minute ago, and exactly where it will hang an hour from now. A leaf that has fallen — and leaves do fall, you can see them lying crisp and whole on the stone — does not rot. It lies there, unchanged, year upon year. A plant does not lean toward you when you pass. There is no wind to lean it. There is no growth you can perceive, no decay you can perceive, no season, no night, no gradient of heat from one side of the room to the other. The warm air is the same warm air. The glow is the same glow. The boom in your teeth is the same boom. You could close your eyes and count your breaths and have no way of knowing whether you had been here for an instant or for a very long time, because nothing here keeps time. It has reached an equilibrium and it holds it.

And yet it is full of animals.

They are abundant, and they are everywhere the islands are, and also in the empty places where you thought there was nothing. The ceiling is alive. Hanging things cluster there in the crystal light — blind, moth-like wings folded, long-legged things with too many joints clinging head-down, thorn-like creatures that at first you take for part of the rock until one of their antennae twitches. On the stone, pale centipede shapes move with a slowness that makes them seem part of the floor, placing one foot, then another, scrape — scrape — the sound of their passage magnified by the hollow dome into something that fills the room. Tiny flying things rise in a faint cloud around an island and settle again in the same place, a glitter of wings. Translucent hoppers sit motionless on broad leaves, their insides visible, a small light burning in each belly that matches the color of the crystal they sit beside, as if they had borrowed it. Some animals look like plants and some plants look like animals; a “fern” turns out to have a row of eyes along its stem, a “moss” lifts and walks away on a hundred hair legs when your shadow falls across it, and for a moment you cannot tell which of you is the intruder and which is the furniture.

They all breathe together. That is the source of the boom. Not perfectly, not like a machine, but with a slow, shared swell, in and out, in and out, a tide of warm air pushed gently against the walls, making the crystals hum and the dome resonate, so that every scrape and click is caught and thrown back at you louder than it was made. The room is a drum and the animals are inside it, breathing. When you hold your breath to listen, the breathing goes on without you. When you move, the sound does not follow you; it is simply there, at the same volume, everywhere.

The built things are scattered and faint, and they are not all built by the animals. Or not only by them. You find a low dome in the shadow between two islands, woven of plant fiber and resin, no higher than your knee, with a narrow opening. You find a perfect spiral trench cut into the dust, a neat circle of small stones, a cache of pebbles stacked like a cairn. Near one crystal island, half-buried in powder, is a tool: a stone flake struck only a few times, a bone awl, a length of cord twisted from fiber, a cracked clay cup no bigger than an egg. On the wall beside it, cut into a crystal face with the same patient blows, is a row of tally marks, identical, side by side, dozens of them, none different from the next. Someone primitive was here once, or something that used primitive means — hand tools, simple craft, a shelter, a mark to count a thing that never changed. They left the tools and went, or they stayed and became part of the stillness, and the room took their work exactly as it takes everything else and altered nothing.

That is the strangeness of it, held together by warmth and dimness and dryness. A sealed bubble in hot rock, warm as a body, dark as the inside of a bruise, loud as a held chord, lush with islands of wax-fed, air-drinking plants, crowded with slow, translucent, breathing animals, dusted with the fresh sharpness of newly broken stone, and marked here and there with the small, scattered work of hands. No water ever falls. No light ever shifts. Nothing grows visibly. Nothing dies visibly. The air is breathed and rebreathed. The nutrients go round — plant to animal to fungus to plant, dropped leaf to stone-eating root — in a loop with no beginning and no end inside a room that time forgot to enter.

You stay because there is nowhere else for time to go. The whistle runs on. The warm air presses. The crystals glow at the same pale distance, island after island, in the dark between them, and the plants hold their great fronds up to the dome, and the animals hang and cling and scrape, breathing in unison, and the room, hidden and complete, remains exactly itself — as if it had just been made, and as if it always would be.
