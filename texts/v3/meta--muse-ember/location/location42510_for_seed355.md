First there is the dark, and it is not the kind of dark that comes after light. It was here before you arrived, and it stays when you turn around. It has weight. It pools in doorways and in the bottoms of streets and along the lee side of walls, as if it were a heavier gas settling, and your hand can push it aside for a moment before it closes again behind your fingers.

You do not see far. The horizon is low and close, though the place goes on a long way — a whole valley, a whole low basin sunk below the level of the world you came from. The sky, if it is sky, is the underside of something black and close. There is one thin, dirty light in it, not from a sun, barely enough to show wet stone from dry. Everything else you find by touch and by wetness.

The heat is immediate and total. It comes not from above but from below, from the ground itself, radiating up through the soles of your feet and into the air so that the air is like a wet cloth held over a kiln. Your skin sweats and the sweat does not dry. It meets the condensation and runs together. Your breath steams though it is hot. Stone walls weep. Under your fingertips the walls are damp and warm, and beads gather and join and slide down in slow, bright tracks that vanish into blackness at the base, where the water is already standing.

Water is everywhere, but it is not a river and it is not a lake. It is a condition. The ground is saturated a few centimeters down, and every footprint fills. After a rain that you never see fall, the pavement holds a film that mirrors nothing and everything. In the lower streets the water comes to your ankles, black and perfectly still, except that it is never still. It creeps. It ticks against stone thresholds. It rises in one square while it falls in another that drains into it. It condenses on the cool side of every wall, runs, evaporates where the ground heat touches it, and condenses again somewhere else. The cycle is constant, audible if you listen: drip, drip, a long sliding sigh as a sheet of water finds a new crack.

This is a city.

It does not announce itself as one. There is no skyline. Low, broad buildings spread out of the dark at wide intervals, as if the city had been laid down and then the spaces between it had been pulled away. A block of houses, then a hundred paces of open flooded ground, then a wall enclosing nothing, then more houses. Density is a suggestion. The houses that exist are solid — basalt and black limestone, fired mudbrick darkened almost to charcoal, roof tiles still glazed and holding water in their curves like scales. Roofs have slumped. Corners have rounded. The pavement between them is organized, unmistakably: a grid, cut by drains, lined with low curbs, with squares and causeways, with an aqueduct course that is now only a stone channel carried on arches over a street that no longer leads anywhere. Layer upon layer is visible where a wall has eroded: an older pavement below a newer one, a doorway blocked by the floor of the room above it, a staircase that ends at a ceiling born later. Nothing is new. Nothing is sharp. The whole thing has had time to settle into itself, to find the exact angle at which it will crumble in balance with the exact angle at which water seals it again. It is ancient in the way a riverbed is ancient — not ruined, but finished at this moment, and still moving.

There is no technology here. No lamp is lit because there is no lamp. No wire, no engine, no wheel-track worn by vehicles. Even the drains are cut stone, even the channels are grown with salt. Wood has rotted to a black pulp in the lintels. Doors stand open on hinges that have seized with rust and dew. You walk, because there is no other way to move, and the city permits walking and forbids anything faster. Your footsteps make a small sound in the water, a soft splash that arrives a fraction late, as if the water remembers the step before the sole has lifted.

Vegetation is sparse, and what there is has given up on light. In the gutters, pale reeds push up in thin, determined clumps, their heads bowed under water. In the courtyards, a few trees — no more than a dozen across the whole district you can perceive — stand with waxy, dark leaves and thick roots that have split the foundations they were planted in generations ago. The walls are veined with lichen and with black algae and with fungal mats that live on damp mortar. Nothing is lush. Nothing reaches. It is as if the plants have learned the city's other rules and are growing sideways, toward moisture, toward sound, toward the heat in the stone, rather than upward.

There is no animal life. The silence is not an absence of noise so much as an absence of inhabitants. No bird crosses the black sky. No insect rises from the standing water to bite. No fish flickers under the films on the squares. Your own breathing is the most animal sound in the place, and even that seems to be absorbed, dampened, returned to you a moment later from a wall too far away for a true echo.

And yet the place is active. It is full of motion that has nothing to do with living things. The ground heaves in tiny, irregular pulses, so that a threshold that was level in the morning is a finger's width higher by evening, and the water that pooled against it now runs the other way. Stone ticks as it expands in the heat and contracts as a cold sheet of condensation runs down it. Salt blooms overnight on the shoulders of statues and on window ledges, white as frost, and dissolves by noon into brine that finds the drains. A whole facade can sigh, softly, and lean a degree further into the street, taking a year to fall the last centimeter. In one district the dripping is rapid, almost musical; in the next, a single drop falls every few minutes and each drop, when it lands, makes the puddle it lands in ripple outward from a point before the drop has arrived.

That is where the place stops holding together, though it never quite fell apart in the first place.

The water does not obey connection. Two cisterns joined by an open channel hold water at different levels for hours, and no amount of looking will make them agree. A puddle in the market square reflects a building that is not behind you. If you lean over it, the reflection leans with you a breath too late, and shows your face older, or younger, or with the hot sweat already dried. Rain, when it comes — and it comes constantly, though you rarely feel it as rain — sometimes falls upward out of the puddles, in a fine mist that beads on your upturned face and vanishes before it reaches the dark above.

The streets are not reliable in length. The same causeway, walked south in the morning, delivers you to a flooded plaza with a market hall whose roof has caved in. Walked north in the evening, it delivers you to the same plaza, whose roof is intact and whose interior is dry. Stairs ascend and arrive at the landing from which they rose, but the window at that landing looks out onto a different district each time — always wet, always dark, never the same. A door that opened onto a cellar, when you open it again, opens onto the room you have just left, though the hinges are on the other side.

None of this makes the city less solid. Your hand on a wall is warm and gritty; the mortar crumbles black between your fingers; the water at the base is cold enough to ache. The buildings are where they are. The grid is real. The erosion is real and settled, the slow equilibrium of a place that has been in its final state for so long that its final state has become a process. It is the distances, the reflections, the levels, the times that have slipped.

Time does not pass evenly here. The heat comes in waves that are not weather, as if the basin were breathing slowly through some deep vent, and with each breath the condensation thickens on the east walls and thins on the west. In some blocks the dripping keeps a brisk tempo while, a street away, the same water moves as if through honey. You will find a courtyard where the puddles have frozen in their ripples, the surface tension holding a single ring intact for as long as you watch, and you will be unable to say whether you have watched for a minute or an hour, because the faint light overhead has not changed and your sweat has not dried and the dark has not moved. The city does not hurry. It has no need to. It will go on weeping, cracking, blooming with salt, creeping down into its own bowl, long after the footprints you made have filled with water and lost their shape.

At the edges, the city thins and does not quite end. The last walls give onto marsh, onto low flats where the water stands in sheets between the sparse reeds, and the grid of pavement dissolves into mud and then into water again. Farther out you can make out more low shapes, more roofs, more arches, across a distance that the dark will not let you measure. It is a regional place, many features, one connected whole that extends past the immediate and past easy sight. You could walk for a day, perhaps for many, and still be within its wet, hot, unlit embrace, still finding the same quiet, the same organized emptiness, the same buildings holding their settled, slumped forms against the black.

You leave by the way you came, which is also a way you did not come, and the dark closes behind you as heavily as it closed before you. Behind you, water continues to rise and fall in disconnected vessels. A wall ticks. A reed bows. Salt blooms again. The city, with no life and no light and no tools, remains exactly as active, exactly as broken, exactly as itself.
