From far below, it is not clear what it is.

A green knot on a white crown of rock. A tree, perhaps, grown too large and too small at once. A mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain. If you climb toward it, which takes longer than it should, you begin to understand that the knot does not get larger in the way a tree should. It gets *denser*. The outline stays small — a bowl at the very top of a new peak, ringed by sharp stone spires, no more than a few hundred paces from wall to wall. Inside that ring, everything is touching everything else.

This is a high place, high enough that the peaks around it are floors far below, and the sky is wide and ordinary and bright. The light is normal daylight, true-colored, falling straight and clean, making the greens too green and the rock too white. Yet it is warm here, improbably warm for the altitude. The sun-heated stone holds the day in its fractured mass and gives it back slowly. The air is still, thin, dry, and warm against the face. Nothing moves in it quickly.

The rock is young. That is the first rule of this world, and everything else follows from it. The peak has recently risen and has not yet learned to be smooth. Every edge is a fresh fracture. Pumice, basalt, silica glass, pale salt-mineral that is not salt and never has been wet: all of it is angular, shard-like, unweathered. No canyon has been cut. No pebble has been rounded by a river, for there are no rivers. There is no rain, no stream, no spring, no lake, no puddle, no dew. Water does not exist here in any form that can be drunk, fallen, or flowed. It is a dry mineral world, sealed not by a lid but by an absence.

And yet it is lush.

That is the second rule, and the one that makes the place hold together. The vegetation does not drink. It cannot. It eats light and warm air and dust. From the deep warm rock beneath the bowl, a faint mineral vapour rises all day, invisible, steady as breath. The plants take it in through broad, thick leaves and condense it into a clear amber sap — not water, but their own enclosed fluid, heavy and fatty and slightly sweet-smelling when a leaf is bruised. They never lose it. They never spend it. They photosynthesize it into more leaf, more root, more trunk, as a slow thickening of themselves.

So the lushness is strange to touch. The leaves are enormous and overlapping and deeply green, layered five and six deep in places, but they are leathery, waxy, warm, and springy, like the pads of some enormous succulent. They slide against each other more than they rustle. Under them the ground is carpeted with dense mats of small, spongy spheres and filaments, a dry velvet that gives underfoot and then holds. There is no mud anywhere. There is no smell of rot, because nothing rots in the moist sense. A dead leaf falls, desiccates in an hour, and over years becomes a crisp husk, then mineral dust, which the roots reclaim. The cycle is closed and slow.

The grove, insistently, is a town.

This is the third rule. The vegetation has been tended, chewed, woven, and inhabited into a settlement, and the settlement has been grown, not built. There is no technology here at all — no tool, no wheel, no vessel, no fire, no metal, no pottery, no lamp. Nothing is made by shaping dead material with devices. Everything is organized, and everything is alive or lately alive.

At the boundary, the rock spires are occupied. Burrowing fauna — slow, grey, armored things with blunt stone-eating jaws — have hollowed the base of the spires into small, paired chambers, their entrances worn smooth only by the passage of bodies. Just inside, the wall changes. The first district is the Root Quarter: houses whose walls are not built but grown, thick trunks split and hollowed over decades, their doors made of a single leaf that opens and closes with the light, like an eyelid. Low root-bridges arch over powder-filled hollows and serve as streets, packed flat and hard by feet. The paths are narrow. In the dense parts you must shoulder past a living wall, and the canopy above you comes down close enough to brush your head, weaving from house to house in a thatch of living plates that serves as roof, shade, and common ground all at once.

Above that, the Canopy Quarter. Platforms of interlaced branch and leaf, suspended between trunks, hold the abundant fauna that live in the dusk of their own shade. Below ground, in the Under-Roots, a cooler — though still warm — warren of root cellars winds through the mineral soil, where eggs are kept and the old sleep. The districts are not separate by distance. They are stacked. A single trunk can be foundation, house, street, and tower. The whole city, wall to wall, can be crossed in minutes, and inside it, there is no empty space. It is a hamlet compressed vertically, dense to the point of near-touching, planned by growth habits and by the habits of its tenders over a very long time.

The fauna are everywhere, and they are silent.

This is the fourth rule, and perhaps the strangest. Life is abundant here — soft-bodied grazers the size of dogs, with waxy skin and color-changing flanks, who move through the Root Quarter at a patient walk, pruning the walls by eating them; beetle-like wall-runners in numbers like ants, flowing over the houses in dark shiny sheets, farming dust-mites in the cracks; downy gliders who slip from canopy to canopy without a wingbeat you can hear; burrowers; egg-tenders; leaf-nibblers no bigger than your thumb, living their whole lives on a single leaf. None of them vocalize. They have no throats for calling, no lungs for song. They communicate in body color, in scent-marks of sap, in pressure of foot and antenna and flank. The plants do not rustle in wind because the air is still and the leaves are thick. No water trickles. No birds cry.

The silence is not empty. It is close and complete, like being inside a sealed room that happens to have no roof. The only sounds, if you stay long enough to hear them, are the quiet ones a slow world makes: the faint tick and sigh of stone shards cooling after sunset, expanding and contracting in the heat; the soft creak of a trunk thickening by an imperceptible amount; a muffled footfall in powder-dust; the extremely slow inhalation of a sleeping animal; a leaf uncurling over the course of a morning, so slowly that the movement has no sound at all until it is done.

That slowness is the fifth rule. Nothing here hurries. A leaf-house takes years to grow a room. The city expands by one new wall, one new root-bridge at a time, eating into the sharp, empty rock of the bowl. A season — and the seasons here are long, marked only by the sun moving and the warm vapour rising a little stronger or a little weaker — a season is enough for a branch to become a beam. The fauna move slowly, mate slowly, grow slowly. If you watched for a day, you would think the place was still. If you watched for a decade, you would see that a street had become a house and a house had become a street, the same continuous tissue doing both.

In the morning, the leaf-doors open. In the heat of the day, the canopy plates spread a little wider. At night, the doors close, the gliders fold themselves into the warm crooks of branches, the wall-runners settle into their cracks, and the tenders lie down in the dust of the Root Quarter with their flanks dimmed to a sleep-pale grey. The rock ticks. The vapour rises. The plants condense a little more sap out of light and air and store it.

It is youthful, humid with life but absolutely dry, high and warm and small and impossibly dense — a city with no technology, a forest with walls, a garden that is also a town, sitting on a fresh, sharp peak that has not yet been eroded into a landscape. It does not drink. It breathes, slowly, light and mineral and heat, and thickens itself, leaf by leaf, wall by wall, into a place that will still be growing long after the peak has learned, at last, to be round.
