Warmth is the first thing, and the weight of the water after it.

It is not cool, it is not cold. It holds you at about the temperature of your own skin, thick and even, so that after a while you stop feeling a difference between inside and out. It does not move much. It presses, gently and from every side, and carries you with a slow drag when you try to turn. Darkness is the second thing, but it is a darkness you learn by touch. There is very little light here at all — a faint pulse now and then when a grazer is brushed, a scatter of mineral sparkle when silt is disturbed — and so you learn the shape of things by the pressure against your flank, by the way a wall stops your passage, by the damp, mineral taste of the water passing over your gills.

You are not in a wilderness. That becomes clear the moment your hand finds the ground.

Under your palm it is not rock. It is laid. Flat stones, none of them quite the same size, set close together and worn down to a common, soft level. Between them, smaller pieces, broken and re-set. Where a slab has lifted, silt has crept in and made a white, spongy crust. A little farther on the paving gives way to a mosaic made by hand: shards of fired clay in buff and dull red, shell fragments white as teeth, bone chips polished smooth by water. There is no soil. If you dig — and the animals here do dig, patiently, with their mouths and forelegs — you find only more rubble: ballast stone, broken pots, more bone, all of it packed tight as fill.

This is a high place. It took a while to understand that, because there is no sky to judge it by and no view. But the ground, for all its paving, falls away. The whole of it is a plateau, a domed summit only a short swim across, built up and terraced, with a retaining wall running around its edge like a spine. Beyond that wall the made ground gives out, stone by stone, down into a drop that goes on and on into the same warm darkness, colder and emptier the farther you fall. Up here, on top, the warm water sits like a shallow, still pool over the deep. You could, if you wanted, swim up and up into blackness for a long time and never find a surface that matters. Here, the place has edges in every direction, and you can cross it from one side to the other in a few hundred slow lengths of your own body.

Everything inside those edges is made.

It is dense with making. Low terraces step up toward the middle, each one crowded with walls. The walls share themselves, one chamber leaning on the next, so that there is never a wide empty space except at the very center. There, rising above the terraces, is the one large room — cathedral-cubed, vaulted, its ribs not of stone alone but of great bones curved and lashed and then plastered over with clay, so that the ceiling arches over you for perhaps thirty meters. Around it, packed shoulder to shoulder, are the smaller rooms: storage pits roofed with clay slabs, narrow tunnels just wide enough for a body to squeeze through, vertical shafts, cupboards, niches, chimneys. Nothing is natural and nothing is grand in the engineered sense. The bricks are uneven. The clay is finger-marked where it was pressed into place. The mortar oozes between stones in lumps that were never smoothed. The joints do not line up. You can tell, even blind, that these were hand-tools and patient hands: knapped flakes, bone needles, copper awls now green and bent, left in wall niches where someone set them down and never came back.

There is no wood here. There never could have been. There is no plant fiber, no timber brace, no grass rope, no wicker. What holds it all together is stone, fired clay, shell lime, bone, and sinew darkened to the color of tea. That palette is everywhere: grey-black stone, buff clay softened at the edges, ivory bone, white shell. No green at all. No sway. No weed has ever grown in this darkness, and none grows now. The walls are bare mineral, filmed with a matte, cream-colored skin of microbes and animal mucous, and crusted in places with warm-water scale, white and pitted, where the water has laid down its minerals over thousands of years. The absence of plants is the shape of the whole ecology. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing reaches for light because there is none to reach for.

It was not built for water.

You understand that by the inconveniences. Stairs that switch back on themselves too tightly for a swimmer, now used as ladders by crawlers. Doorways at hip-height that must be wriggled through sideways. Windows that look out on more wall. Hearths — great clay kilns in the side chambers, cold for longer than anyone can remember, their interiors now rookeries. Stone beds, single slabs on low plinths. Cups still sitting in rows on stone tables, each cup a perfect small house for a fan-filterer. The place was made to stand in, to breathe in, to fire pots in. Then the water came, slowly, over a time too long to mark, as the summit sank by fractions and the warm sea crept up its terraces. The high rooms were the last to drown. The builders are gone — not fled, not buried in any place you can find, just finished, eroded out of memory. Their marks on the clay have rounded away. The sharp corners of their work have softened as if held under a tap for centuries, edges like soap, inscriptions like smudged fingerprints.

Ancient, settled, final. That is the rule here now. Change happens, but at a pace that is almost stillness. A wall that has stood intact for a thousand years will sigh, tick, and settle a finger's width in an afternoon. A silt bank will build and unbuild over a generation. A filterer will open its fan for an hour, close it, open it again. The fauna — and there are many, everywhere — move as if the water itself were tired.

And there are many. The density is a thing you discover by brushing past them. At first you think the central vault is empty, only you and the slow drift. Then a pressure-wave tells you a hoverer has swung away from your face. Then dozens more. The swimmers are pale and blind and translucent, eel-like, eyeless, ribbed with a side-line that feels the ebb of another body; they hang in the middle water and move in slow, looping patrols, mouths gaping at particles you cannot see. The floor belongs to the crawlers, flattened and armored, with suction feet and whiskered antennae, traversing a wall's width in a day, grazing the biofilm off the tiles. The pots and tubes belong to the pumpers, cones fixed head-down in clay jars, their lacy fans beating in unison, so quietly you mainly know them by the faint current they make against your skin. The chimneys and kilns belong to the shrimp, translucent and tireless, carrying their young in shell houses they wedge into any cup that will fit them. The ceiling of the vault is not ceiling at all to them — it is a crowded underside, a forest of sticky feet and filter fans and egg-strings, layered three and four deep, near-touching, so that the stone you would expect to see is mostly alive and slightly moving.

They are silent. That is part of the density. No clicks, no calls. They speak, if you can call it that, by touch: an antenna laid against another, a puff of chemical from the gills that means *mine* or *move* or *stay*, a slow press of flank against wall that others read as a path is clear. Occasionally the whole place makes a sound, but it is a made sound: a low tick as clay contracts, a distant rumble as a stone settles somewhere in the deep fill, the far-off sigh of water moving through a broken tunnel. The artery of the vent under the center — the source of the warmth — gives a very low hum through the paving, more felt in the belly than heard.

The food web is short and closed and old. Warm mineral water brings up dissolved chemicals from below. Microbes use them and make a film. Grazers eat the film. Filterers strain the slow rain of dead things from the water above them. Small swimmers eat filterers and grazers. One or two large, slow hunters — cartilaginous, blind, almost featureless — patrol the retaining wall and the drop beyond, taking the careless. Nothing blooms. Nothing bursts. The populations hold at a high, packed level, generation after generation, because there is nothing to knock them out of it. No seasons. No day. Only the patient laying down of silt, the slow opening and closing of fans, the births in cups, the deaths that become rain.

The animals have begun, in their small way, to maintain the place. Not as builders. They wedge pebbles under a sagging slab with their mouths. They haul a shell fragment to block a tunnel that brings in too much current. They pile picked stones in a deliberate heap by a doorway, a cairn no larger than your fist, and add to it over years. It is primitive craft, hand-scale, the same tech left behind in the niches, continued by other hands.

So this is what it has become: a small, high, drowned hamlet of stone and clay and bone, warm and pitch dark under deep water, silent except for settling, ancient beyond any mark its makers left, so densely inhabited that every surface is a neighborhood, and with no plant anywhere in it. You can swim its whole length and touch its far wall and know you have seen all of it, though you have seen nothing at all. The water will be the same tomorrow, and in a hundred years, and the walls will be a fraction softer, the crust a fraction whiter, the fans beating at the same slow rate.

You rest your flank against one of the low terrace walls and let the current — such as it is — pass over you. A crawler crosses your wrist and is gone. A hoverer loops, unhurried, into the vault. The place holds.
