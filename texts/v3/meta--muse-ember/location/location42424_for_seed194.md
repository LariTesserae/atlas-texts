There is no sky here, and no night to replace it. The darkness is not something that falls. It is the inside of something.

You are lying — though you have not stood up yet — on a floor that is almost perfectly flat. Low ground, spooned out over a span too long to count. The air is cool, still, and dry, heavier at floor-level than at head-level, and it smells of resin and old paper and cold mineral dust. There is no damp in it. No trickle, no seep, no condensation on the walls. Your mouth is dry and stays dry. Nothing here has ever needed water to keep going, and nothing here is wet.

Under your hands the floor is not stone, not earth. It is plant, compressed and aged past recognition as a leaf or a root, soft with a fibrous give in some places, hard and pale as bone in others. It yields a little, then resists. Everywhere you press, you find the same thing in a different state of decay: pith like cork, bark like worn leather, dust like powdered spore. The whole solid world is one vast, living slow mass, and the dead parts of it are indistinguishable from the parts still doing their work.

That is the first rule, and it never breaks: if it is solid, it was or is plant. Stone, where there is stone, is not bedrock brought in from elsewhere. It is mineral concretion grown inside the plant body, lumps of gritty amber-grey matter the ancient inhabitants cut out and used. Even the tools are mostly this, and hardened sap, and woven fiber.

### The Town

You stand in a space that is empty enough to be confusing. Vastness between presences. You can walk for long stretches and touch nothing. Then, far off in the dark — far by sound and by touch, not by sight — a wall.

It is a town. Not a ruin of a town, not a suggestion. A single, contained town, embedded and hidden, built into a hollow. From the outside, if there is an outside any more, you would never know it was there. Inside, it has all the measures of a settlement: streets aligned to the veins of the plant, a plaza laid out in a shallow, near-flattened bowl, rows of houses with thresholds and steps and hearth-pits, storehouses, workshops with charred floors, places where fiber was loomed and resin was boiled down. Layer upon layer of it. The earliest houses are carved straight into the pith, their walls still showing the grooves of stone adzes. Later houses were framed in mineral stone and roofed in woven bark. Later still, the plant grew into the gaps and sealed them with resin, thickening walls by a finger's width in a century, until doors became low and lopsided and some entrances closed themselves entirely.

The builders were primitive in the oldest sense. Hand tools only: knapped stone blades, bone-hard sap awls, rope of plant fiber strung along the streets so hands could follow them in the dark. No wheels for moving goods any distance. No lamps — they never made light, because there was no fuel that burned well and no reason to try to push back a darkness that was not hostile, just total. Tally marks are cut into doorposts, worn down to smooth dimples. Simple metal, where it was used at all, is dull and dark, untouched by rust because the air has never held water to rust it.

And the town is urban in its emptiness. The plan is there, the density is not. Most houses are hollow shells. Whole blocks are inhabited only by fallen ceilings of woven leaf, sagged and split. You can stand in the middle of the plaza and listen and hear only yourself and the building. There are no footprints in the dust except the one set you are making, and even those fill slowly behind you as spore dust settles — not falls, settles, in air too still for weather.

### What Lives Here

Vegetation does not grow *in* this place. This place *is* vegetation, at the end of its life.

Above you, continuing for a distance your arms cannot measure, is a matted canopy of root and leaf turned to a dark, fibrous ceiling. Below you, the floor. Around you, the walls are the swollen interior of a single primordial rhizome, or a trunk, or a seed-husk of a size that stopped being a plant long ago and became a geology. It has been growing, and drying, and settling for so long that erosion has done its final work: everything is rounded. No sharp edge survives. Corners have been softened as if by river water, though no river has ever run here. Floors are concave where generations pressed. Projections have been absorbed. The whole town has been smoothed into its most stable, most ancient form. It will not collapse. It is already what remains when collapse is finished.

The plants that remain alive are not green. Color is a rumor here. They are pale, waxy, translucent in the way thick wax is translucent when you bring it close to an eye that cannot see it anyway. Their leaves are not for catching sun — there has never been sun — and their roots do not drink. Their chemistry is sealed and non-aqueous. Sap is not watery; it is oil and resin, sweet and sharp, that moves so slowly through them you would need a lifetime to watch it rise.

They feed, as far as anyone ever understood, on dust and on vibration. The town was built, perhaps deliberately, perhaps by accident of better building in resonant hollows, as an instrument to feed them. Hollow culms and chambered rooms carry air. Air moves when the great body above contracts in the cool. The culms sing.

That is why it is so loud.

### The Noise

The loudness is the most startling thing, because the place is empty and there is no animal life at all — no insects in the plant mass, no birds in the hollows, no worms in the floor, no webs, no droppings, nothing that lands on you when you stand still. And yet the sound never stops.

It is a low, patient booming, as if you were inside a cello that someone is very slowly tightening. It comes through the floor into your feet before it reaches your ears. Then, at intervals that have no rhythm you can learn, the plant cracks. A sharp pop, like a knuckle, like dry wood splitting, somewhere indeterminate and very close. A long creak as a wall thickens or a roof sags by a fraction. The slow sigh of air being pushed through a narrow throat of pith, rising to a moan and falling away.

Your own steps are loud. Stone floor rings. Pith floor thuds and then keeps ringing faintly, as if the whole town remembers being struck. Shout, and your voice does not die in the plaza; it comes back to you from the rounded walls, flattened and late, as if from a much larger room.

The plants grow toward sound, the builders seem to have believed, and whether or not they were right, the growth follows the resonance. The loudest chambers have the thickest walls. The quietest corners are thin, brittle, and dead.

### The Rules of the World

There are no seasons here, only cycles that take centuries. A slow drying. A slower swelling when resin moves. A release of spores so fine they hang in the air and do not drift until a booming moves them from chamber to chamber through the town's airways, where they settle and, in the right resonant hollow, take root in the wall. No pollinators. No water cycle. No sun cycle.

The town, having no water and no light, has no clock. The inhabitants — they are gone now, utterly, with no fauna to follow them, not even the smallest scavenger to pick their tools clean — must have navigated by rope and by ear, by the pitch of a room, by the smell of resin in the workshop quarter and leaf-dust in the storehouses. They built low because the hollow is low. They built thick because the cool never leaves. They left everything where it fell, because there was no weather to clear it.

Changes happen, but you will not see them. A wall will be a fraction thicker when you return after years. A street will be narrower by the slow push of a root. A house will have fused with its neighbor by a bridge of grown fiber that looks, in the dark, like a doorway that was never intended. The place is not dying quickly. It is at the end of life in the way old things are at the end: settled, final, hard to alter, still creaking under its own weight.

That is the landscape. A hidden, pocket town inside a single plant-body, under permanent darkness, on a flat floor of living and dead plant matter, cool and dry and almost empty, held together by primitive stone and fiber work that has been eroded and overgrown into one soft, ancient form. No water. No animals. No light.

Only the plants, filling all the space there is, and the sound they make as they go on eating the dark, quietly, loudly, for far longer than anyone will be there to listen.
