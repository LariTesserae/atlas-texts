Kersmill is small enough to hear whole, and you do hear it whole — long before you see it, long after you are inside it. It is a hamlet squeezed into a clearing in the low marsh, a quarter of a mile from gate to gate, and from the middle of it you can count its roofs. That is the first surprise. It is dense, and it is urban, in the way a factory town is urban: lanes and canals and pipe-bridges and wires laid out in a rough grid, everything within touching distance of everything else, no waste ground. But it all fits inside one view. Ten minutes of slow walking will take you from the outer levee to the far levee, and the swamp beyond is the same flat green in every direction.

You approach on a causeway that is not really a road. It is a mat of woven root, palm, and packed fiber laid over black water, raised a hand's breadth on piles. Under your boots it gives, very slightly, and springs back. On either side the marsh is total plant: reeds standing in water to their knees, palm clumps, bamboo thickets in different years of growth, water hyacinth locking the side channels solid. The water is everywhere near the surface. Dig a spade-depth and you are in it. After rain it does not run off; it stands, it seeps, it weeps from the sides of the levee and drips from every iron roof for a day after the sky has cleared. The air is warm and condensing. In the morning the whole place hangs in a low, white damp that lifts slowly. In the afternoon the warmth is a sweat on the back of the neck, and the canals hold warm water that reflects rust and green in equal measure.

There are no hills. The elevation is a table. The horizon is plant and smoke and a normal, diffuse daylight turned a little grey by humidity. Nothing glows. Nothing is strange. That is part of its character: a completely ordinary, completely working place, made entirely out of the material it stands in.

The ground itself is the settlement's main invention. Under Kersmill there is no mineral soil to speak of, only peat, alluvial mud, and generations of its own waste — pressed fiber, ash, chopped reed, mud from the canals, living root — laid down and trodden and grown over until it has become a buoyant substrate strong enough to build on. It has settled. The town has sunk a hand's breadth here, been jacked up there, and sunk again, until every building sits at the height the peat will allow. Houses are not founded on stone. They stand on driven piles of mangrove and palm, and their floors are rafts of woven root and timber that float a little with the water table. The walls are plant architecture without any metaphor in it: frames of bamboo and hardwood, infill of reed matting and woven rattan, plastered inside with clay and sap, thatch outside in double layers, the outer layer left for sedges to root in so the roof lives and insulates and sheds rain. A house leaning a degree to the south is not a ruin; it is in balance. A new root is grafted into its pile, the floor is packed tighter, and it steadies for another decade.

In the center, on the only truly permanent foundation — a raft of brick and iron piles that has been sunk and raised and sunk again for longer than anyone living there can remember — is the engine house. It is the one building that would be recognized anywhere: brick darkened to brown-black by damp and smoke, iron roof streaked, a short chimney banded in rust, great doors that never quite close against the wet. Inside it is one high room, almost cathedral in proportion if not in scale, where the steam engine turns at the slow, unhurried speed that everything here turns at. The flywheel does not rush. The belts do not snap. They creep, thick and greasy, carrying power to the fiber beaters and looms and dryers in the workshops that lean against the engine house on all sides. Electric motors hum off the same shaft, joined later by copper wire strung low between roofs. The town's industry is fiber: rattan, palm leaf, reed, the hyacinth pulled from the canals, cut, soaked, beaten soft, woven into matting, rope, packaging, and rough paper. The waste is burned for steam. Steam condenses on pipes and runs in beads down the walls. The smell never leaves: hot oil, wet peat, fermenting green matter, woodsmoke, and the clean sour smell of worked fiber.

Because it is plant, the vegetation is not decoration here, and it does not stop at the edge of the town. Inside the levee every gap between buildings is filled — with drying racks stacked with thatch, with fuel piles, with nursery beds where new root-mat is grown for next year's repairs, with filter beds of cattail and sedge through which the mill's wash-water is led slowly before it is released to the ring canal. Vines are trained over pipe-bridges to shade them. Living fences of thorn and bamboo mark the boundaries of yards. Along the canals the banks are not stone but woven root, holding the peat together, renewed as it rots. Outside the levee the same plants continue, less ordered, but you can see where the town's cutting and planting has shaped the swamp for a long way out. It is all one ecosystem, managed intensively in the middle and more loosely around it, and the economy is closed in the most literal sense: the marsh feeds the mill, the mill feeds the marsh, the roofs feed the houses.

Density is vertical as well as horizontal. At water level are the canals — streets, really, though no wheeled cart could use them — where pole-boats move fiber and muddy water at a walking pace and are moored in rows against the house piles. At the level of the causeway are the workshops, the market stall, the wash-houses where laundry hangs over the water to dry in the damp air. Above that are the dwellings, a second and sometimes third story of light plant construction, with verandas linked house to house by boardwalks, so that you can walk the length of the town without touching the ground. Overhead are the pipes, the wires, the vines, the drying lines. Nothing is far from anything else. A window looks onto another window close enough to hear a kettle. The town is established in its weathering: water stains stop at a consistent height on every wall, timber has silvered, iron has bloomed orange and then darkened again, moss has claimed the north sides of roofs and stopped there, as if a negotiation had been reached between rot and repair and neither side is winning. It does not look new. It does not look abandoned. It looks like a form that has existed long enough to find its own shape.

The sound is continuous and it is loud. There is no quiet corner, only corners where the machinery is slightly more muffled by thatch and wet walls. The engine thumps low, a slow heartbeat that you feel through the raft floors. Over it is the higher whine of the electric motors, the rhythmic clatter of the beaters, the hiss of steam at joints, water dripping from a hundred places, the splash of a pole in a canal. It does not vary much through the day. At night the human noise drops away and the machinery does not. The town hums in its sleep. That constancy, more than the volume, is what drives the animals away.

Fauna are rare to the point of absence. In a warm wet marsh you would expect birds in the reeds and frogs in every pool and insects in clouds, and outside the levee there are some, but inside the palisade of noise, smoke, and managed water there is very little. You can live in Kersmill for a week and see no animal larger than a moth. A gecko will take the warm wall by the engine house at dusk. An eel turns in the ring canal below the sluice. A heron will pass high over the clearing, not landing. The plants, which elsewhere would shelter a crowd of living things, here are mostly structural, harvested, or too constantly worked to host much else. The absence is noticeable precisely because everything else is so present.

Everything moves slowly. That is the second governing rule, after the first one, which is that nothing heavy can be built and nothing impermeable can be laid. Water rises a handspan in the wet season and falls a handspan in the dry, and the town rises and falls with it on its rafts. A roof that is growing its sedge layer gains an inch a year. A walkway is re-woven when it sags, not before. The engine, the boats, the looms, the people carrying baskets — all at the same unhurried pace, because the substrate will not bear hurry and the climate will not reward it. Change is measured in years: a house slowly settling until it is packed up again, a root dyke thickening until it can take a new pile, a canal bank slumping until a new weave is laid over it. Erosion has not stopped; it has been absorbed. The peat is always compacting, the piles are always rotting at the waterline, the thatch is always thinning on top and thickening with moss below, and the town is always being remade out of its own marsh at exactly the rate at which it decays.

So Kersmill holds together not by permanence but by continuity. A small, loud, warm, wet, plant-built industrial hamlet on flat ground, bounded sharply by its ring canal and leaky levee, dense and layered and entirely itself — engine house in the middle, houses and workshops pressed close around it, canals for streets, roofs for gardens, roots for walls, smoke and steam and rain always condensing on the iron, and the slow drone of the flywheel going on underneath it all, as it has for a very long time, and as it will, at the same pace, for a very long time more.
