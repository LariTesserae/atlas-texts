It does not start with rock.

From a distance — and the distance here is long, open, regional, a whole high plateau breaking into shallow valleys and rounded shoulders that go on past easy seeing — it looks like a plain. A green one, in the way a sea is blue. Only as you come closer does the plain resolve, and then continue resolving, into more and more of itself.

The ground is plant. Not plant *on* ground. Plant as ground.

Under your boots there is a felt. It gives, it springs back, it squeezes a cold dampness up around the sole. Layers, near-touching, dense as a woven thing: a skin of lichen no thicker than paint, in rust and ochre and a grey-green that almost disappears; then moss, in hummocks and hollows, cushion on cushion, saturated; then a knotted mat of creeping stems, hairy, wiry, rooting again at every joint; then larger leaves, waxy and thick to survive the cold, overlapping like shingles, like scales; then, rising through them, small, improbable towers of herb — stems as tough as cane, clustered rosettes, plumes that turn dark red at the edges, heads that nod and do not nod. Nothing is bare for long. Even the scattered stones, rounded by an age you can feel rather than measure, are skinned over. They are not landmarks in the green. They are lumps in it, half-buried, wearing moss like a coat that has grown into the cloth.

This is old. That is the first rule you learn, walking it. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is newly broken. The erosion here is not dramatic — no cliffs, no fresh scree — it is settled. Everything has had time to soften its edges, to be taken over, to be weathered into balance and then weathered again. The soil, where you can find it, is not soil in the usual sense but peat and root-felt and rotted leaf, black and spongy, holding water the way a sponge holds it: not flowing, not pooling in any great amount, but damp through and through.

Water here is intermittent and modest. It does not run. It condenses. In the mornings, and again when the light thins, every hair on every leaf is beaded. The leaves are built for this — fine, downy, fanning out to catch fog as it slides low over the plateau. It rains sometimes, a thin, cold sleet that need not be named rain, that wets the top inch and vanishes into the felt below. In the hollows, meltwater sits in small, still mirrors for a while, reflecting the sky exactly, then is drunk. You will not find a river. You will find moisture everywhere, held, released, held again. The air smells of it: wet peat, crushed green, a faint mineral tang, ozone from the high cold.

Cold is the other constant. It is not the white, dead cold of snowfields. The sun is normal, clear, high-altitude light — sharp, a little bleached, with plenty of glare off a bead of water — and the day warms enough to let the place work. But shade is always cold. The damp felt is always cold. Your fingers go numb if you stop moving. Frost persists in the lee of a hummock well into the day, not as a sheet, but as lace on the moss, as crystals the plants themselves extrude. Some leaves, when the temperature drops fast, push out frost-feathers from their margins, tiny clear combs of ice that sparkle for an hour and melt into the plant's own stores. The landscape glitters, quietly, in patches.

And it moves.

Not with wind. There is almost no wind at ground level, because there is so much plant to stop it. The air in the mat is still. That stillness is what makes the movement stranger.

All day the surface is churning. Frost-heave lifts a hummock an inch, cracks its crust, lets new roots in; by afternoon the crack has been felted over. A mat that has been creeping downhill — for years, tirelessly, a plant-glacier an inch at a time — pushes over a stone and collapses in a slow, silent slide, exposing dark earth that greens within days. Rosettes open like shutters when the sun hits them and close with a soft, internal pressure. Seed heads, swollen with stored sugar, burst without a pop, just a collapse and a cloud of down that settles on the next leaf and is sucked in. Leaf after leaf yellows, blackens, rots into the felt — and another leaf, already made in the bud below, expands to take its place in hours.

It is turbulent, in the sense of a factory at full tilt, a market, an anthill: too much happening at once, in too many places, for the eye to take in. Waves of growth travel across the plateau — not seasonal waves, daily and hourly ones, ripples of opening, of shedding, of root-tips pushing — like a field of wheat in an unseen wind, except there is no wind. The turbulence is internal. It is the place making itself and unmaking itself, continuously, in balance with itself because it has done so for so long.

That is the industrial heart of it. There is nothing built here. No path, no tool, no wall, no footprint of a maker. You could walk for a day and find no artifact that was not once alive. And yet everything is production on scale.

The leaves are solar mills, broad and angled, converting that clear high light into wax, into dye, into acid, into sugars packed tight against the cold. The stems are transport — hollow, pressurized, moving sap up and down in pulses you can almost feel if you press a thick stem between thumb and finger. The roots are mining belts, endlessly laying down new absorptive hairs, intertwining with fungal threads that carry messages and minerals laterally, hand to hand across the felt. The fungi, in their turn, are electrified: fine white networks that conduct faint bioelectric signals from a leaf unrolling in one valley to a bud swelling in the next, so that growth here is coordinated, mechanized, without a single hard part. Nodules on the roots fix nitrogen the way a foundry fixes metal; resin ducts lay down protective coatings; pigments — rusts, deep teals, berry purples, ember oranges — are not decoration but industrial chemistry, sunscreens and antifreezes and deterrents, flushed through the whole system on demand. When a plant dies, it is not wasted. It is stripped, rotted, reabsorbed by the mat that killed it. The factory floor is the landscape; the landscape is the factory.

Because of this, the colours shift. A hillside that is green at noon will be shot through with red as the day cools and anthocyanins flood the leaves. A hollow that was muted moss in the morning will be bright with new growth by evening, a different green, a yellow-green like fresh metal. Lichen flares orange after a wetting. It is not a pretty, pastoral wobble. It is signal and process, constant.

Animals are rare to the point of absence. You may walk an hour and see nothing that walks itself. No birds — the silence is too complete for them, the food too hidden, the cold too indifferent. No swarms. Once, perhaps, a dark beetle crosses a leaf at a pace that looks deliberate. Once, a springtail flicks across a bead of water. Once, something larger, indeterminate, makes the felt dip and is gone. The fauna are not the protagonists here; they are visitors in a plant world that has already filled the niches. The plants do the preying, the migrating, the building, the decaying. If you want movement with eyes, you will not find it. You will find movement without them.

And for all the churning, it is silent.

That is the most unsettling rule. Turbulence usually makes noise. Here, everything soft absorbs it. Moss deadens your step to a muffled press. Wet leaf damps leaf. The thick air, cold and still in the mat, carries almost nothing. You hear your own breath, a little too loud. You hear, every few minutes, a single drip from a leaf tip into the felt, a note so soft you feel it more in your ear than hear it. The frost does not crack audibly. The mats do not tear audibly. A collapse of a whole hummock, a landslide of greenery, happens without a sound, like something underwater.

So the eye is always working, the ear almost never. The landscape sparkles with condensation, shivers with root-tips, heaves and settles, greens and rusts, produces and consumes at a rate that would exhaust a temperate meadow — and it does it all in a high, damp, cold quiet.

By late afternoon the light lowers but does not fail. Shadows grow long and blue across the hummocks, but the high places catch the sun and look almost lit from within, the leaves translucent. The plateau stretches on the way it has stretched, valley after valley, all of it vegetated to the horizon, all of it damp, all of it working. There is no edge to it you can see, no summit bare of green, no river cutting it into sections. It is one continuous, layered, self-renewing surface, old and in its own state, making soil out of itself, making air out of itself, making more of itself out of light and cold and a little water — silently, industrially, turbulently, without pause, and without ever showing a single hard, made thing.

You leave your footprints in it. In an hour, the felt has risen around them. In a day, they are gone.
