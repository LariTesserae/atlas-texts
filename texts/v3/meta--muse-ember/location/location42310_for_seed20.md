**The First Water**

You do not arrive at this place. It arrives at you, through your ribs.

Before there is light, before there is shape, there is sound — and not sound as information, but sound as weight. A roar with no single source, layered and re-layered: the deep boom of water hammering stone, the hiss of a thousand small impacts, the ringing of fresh rock under pressure, the high tearing sigh of something boiling or bursting far below. It is deafening in the old physical sense. It presses the air out of your chest, rattles your teeth, makes your own pulse feel like a faint second heartbeat underneath it. You cannot think in words here for long. Thought comes in fragments between percussion hits.

And you are already wet. The warmth is the second thing. Not air, not quite. You are submerged, up to at least the shoulders, in warm water that feels thicker than it should — slick, mineral, faintly gritty on the tongue, warm as a bath left in the sun and then heated again from below. Steam rises where the surface breaks. Your skin prunes at once. This should be cold. You are high up.

That is the first wrongness you can hold onto, and it helps: this is a mountaintop that has drowned.

The place is small. That is the second mercy. The whole of it fits inside a single shattered bowl, a few minutes' swim across if the water would let you swim straight. The walls close around you in every direction, rising steeply, too steep. Cathedral scale, vertically. Near the top the rock narrows and darkens and disappears into a low, turbulent ceiling of rain and fog, so there is no sky, only a dim, greying membrane overhead, and far beyond the rim, nothing you can see at all — only, if you surface and crane your neck, a sense of immense height, of thin air and a cloud-sea far below the cliffs, of being on the crown of the world. The horizon is rock.

Most of the bowl is water, and most of the water is empty.

The stone is new. That matters more here than age usually does. The basin has recently split, recently lifted, recently filled — it has not yet settled into being a landscape. The cliffs and the floor are made of black, glassy rock broken into fresh planes and razor shards, edges that have not been worn by current or time. Pebbles are angular cubes, not rounded. There is no smooth beach, no soft silt bank where you could rest your feet; the ground drops in steps and splinters, and every few minutes a new splinter shears off somewhere and clatters down through the water with a crack you feel before you hear. Where the rock broke, it is still bright inside, almost metallic, weeping warm mineral water. Turbidity hangs in veils that never quite clear. Sediment has not learned to lie down.

Light is dim and green-grey, no matter which way you look. From below, the surface is a pale, roiling ceiling, far above and never still. From above, the air is a wall of rain that does not fall so much as exist — atmospheric water, a suspended south-facing storm that hangs over the bowl and never moves on. It is warm here too, oppressively wet, and full of sound: rain striking the rain, wind trying to find a way through the rim and failing, the bowl itself acting like an ear, cupping and amplifying everything back at itself.

And the water will not hold a shape.

This is not a lake at rest. The dynamic is constant and violent in a small way — turbulent not like an ocean storm, but like something alive and too confined. Currents spiral up out of fissures, reverse, collide, and spiral again in a different place. Warm upwellings boil through the surface in domes that collapse. Whirlpools form, travel, and devour themselves mid-bowl. The level itself rises and falls by handbreadths in minutes, lapping and overtopping the lowest notches in the rim, then receding, leaving black rock glistening and new. Islands of churned sediment and fresh rock bubble up, stand for a moment like a newborn ridge, then slump and dissolve back into the general roar. Creation and destruction happen at the same speed, in the same place, with no time for either to finish. You learn quickly not to anchor yourself to anything.

Between the turbulence, the emptiness is startling. Density here is low, scattered, balanced with void. Vast tracts of the bowl are only water and stone and your own muffled movement. That is why the few presences stand out so sharply when you find them.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of rumor. No trees, no grass — nothing that would belong on a mountaintop, and little that belongs underwater either, because there has not been time. In protected cracks in the rock you find films of pale algae, olive and translucent, that tear and regrow. In a few places above the main chop, thin hair-like strands trail from ledges, bleached almost white, trembling in the rain. One patch, perhaps. Then long, empty stretches. Then another.

Fauna is rarer still. In an hour here you might see nothing alive that is not plant, and then realize that one dark pressure in the water was an animal, and it is gone. There are blind, translucent swimmers that navigate by the pressure of the sound itself, riding the booms like slow weather, their bodies almost water, flashing only when they turn. There is a sessile thing fixed to a shard near the deepest point, a pale ear-flap that opens and closes with the percussion, filtering what the turbulence brings it. That may be all of them. The food web, if there is one, is still remembering how to be a web. Everything that lives here lives by listening, because there is nothing else reliable.

The built things are like that too — scattered, partial, and older than the water but younger than any tool.

You find them only when the current slackens for a breath: a low ring of flat black slabs, knee-high, half-silted, close to the eastern wall; a broken half-wall running into deep water, its top edge sharp and fresh as if it were snapped yesterday; a line of stepping stones that leads from nowhere to the middle of the bowl and stops; a pile of white shells fused into the rock, arranged — arranged, you think, but there are no tool marks, no metal, no glass, no plastic, no light, no device of any kind anywhere in the whole basin. Stone, shell, bone, silt — nothing else. Whatever made these did not leave machines behind, or left them so long ago that the new water has taken them. They are faint marks in a place that is still being made, and the roar goes through them as if they were ears.

That is when the place stops behaving like a crater lake and starts breaking.

Reality here is not broken all at once. It frays at the edges, loudest at the center where the water is deepest and the sound is greatest.

Up, for one thing, is unreliable. It tilts, slightly but unmistakably, toward whichever eddy is roaring loudest at that moment. Your sense of vertical follows the noise. Objects do not always fall; debris sometimes drifts upward in a loud current and hangs there, trembling, as if suspended from the sound itself.

The boundary between air and water is not a line. It is a zone several handbreadths thick where water climbs the walls in sheets and hangs in the rain, where bubbles rise and do not break, where you can take a breath and taste water and not drown, and surface and still be wet to the bone. Your reflection arrives a fraction before you do, mouth open in a roar you have not yet made.

Distance does not measure in meters here. It measures in seconds of reverberation. A thing on the far side of the bowl can be nearer in echo than a thing at your shoulder. Sometimes you hear the echo of a rockfall before the rock falls. Sometimes the same bubble passes you three times, in the same eddy, though the water is moving fast. Time stutters in the whirlpools. The bowl is small, but if you listen wrong it goes on for miles.

And the sound — the deafening, all-encompassing sound — is not a byproduct of the place. It feels increasingly like the mechanism. The rock rings like a bell when a current strikes it, and the ringing shapes where sediment settles. The rain hisses, and where it hisses longest, new algae films appear. The animals turn toward the booms. The built rings hum in sympathy, low and sweet under the roar. If the sound stopped, you suspect the water would not know which way to flow, the walls would not know how to keep standing, the warm high sea would forget it was a sea.

It does not stop.

So you stay, submerged, warm, and small, in a newly-born high water held in a sharp new bowl, dim-lit, mostly empty, with rare green films and rarer blind lives and a handful of stone remains, while the turbulent whole makes and unmakes itself around you in a roar that fills your body. The place has not yet learned its final shape. It is still trying out shapes, one loud minute at a time, and you are inside the attempt, listening to a world being assembled badly and beautifully at the top of everything, under a sky that is also water, waiting to see what it decides to become next.
