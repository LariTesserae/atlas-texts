The Cut is not built on the mountain. It is built inside a wound in it.

You would not find it from below, and from above you could walk straight over it. The summit station is a cluster of cable-pylons, winches and wind-braced sheds on a bald, high plateau of dry mineral — rock and salt and shattered ore, nothing green for as far as the light goes — and then, where the ground should simply continue, there is a slot. A single, blind crack in the peak, perhaps two hundred and eighty metres long, never wider than eighteen, cut straight down a hundred and sixty metres from rim to floor. That is the whole of it. The whole world, stacked inside one feature.

To get in you go through the shoulder. A service tunnel, bored by the company and then widened by everyone else, takes you from the thin, clean air of the summit into pressure that pops behind your ears and then settles there, heavy. The tunnel walls are still sharp. The rock has not yet learned to be old. Drill-marks show in white spirals; a fresh blast-face at the far end shines pale grey and rust-red where the ore changes; scree at your boots is angular as broken teeth. No soil has had time to form. No edge has been softened into a slope. Everything here is young in the geological sense — recently emerged, recently cut, still ringing faintly from being made.

When the tunnel opens, you do not step into a street. You step onto a bench.

The slot is a vertical city, and most of its traffic is up and down rather than along. Fourteen main terraces have been hacked into the walls, narrower than alleys in most places, and between the terraces there are more: ledges, bolted-on grating walkways, conveyor bridges that cross the void at different heights so that looking up you see a lattice of them instead of sky. At the widest point two handcarts cannot pass without one backing to a niche. At the narrowest, two people cannot pass without turning sideways.

The walls are the buildings. Dwellings are niches cut or blasted into the rock, fronted with riveted iron, corrugated sheet, slabs of poured concrete and, in the older south end, whole dismantled houses re-erected in iron and timber against the cliff. Pipes run in layers — air, oil, coolant that is not water, cable, waste — each at its own height, each humming or ticking at a different rate. Lamps are strung along railings in long dim threads. Work-lights, amber and blue-white, wash the haze. Only at midday does a blade of true sun come down the slot and rake the floor for perhaps twenty minutes, and then it is gone, leaving the blue shade again. The place lives in dimness by design and by geometry.

It is cool, still, near the top. The air up there is the summit air — thin, dry, biting, with a taste of metal in the back of the throat. The further down you go, the warmer and staler it becomes, until the bottom is a small, hot weather of its own. Heat rises in a permanent, fitful chimney between the walls. It carries dust up in slow spirals, carries smoke sideways, carries the smell of hot oil and crushed stone and baking fungus. Every hour or so the updraft strengthens, then falters, then reverses for a breath, so that curtains of dust billow one way and then the other, papers and feathers and loose tarps are lifted, dropped, lifted again. Nothing settles for long.

Down there, at the floor, is the reason for the Cut. The pit is both town and quarry. Crushers sit in a row, their jaws open and closing with a dull, muffled thud that you feel in your sternum more than hear. Concentrators, separators, a kiln whose mouth glows orange even in the morning. Conveyors climb the south wall in long black ladders, carrying ore to the summit cable. Cranes swing loads across the void with a patience that looks lazy until you realise they have not stopped moving since you arrived. This is scaled industry — not a workshop, but production — and yet the slot eats the sound. Thin high air carries it poorly, the walls scatter it upward and out of the crack, the packed layers of iron and rock absorb what is left. It is loud everywhere you can see, and quiet almost everywhere you can listen. You hear footsteps on grating, a cart’s squeak two terraces away arriving late, the sigh of ventilators, the clink of a coupling, a distant blast at the face, softened to a cough. Between machines, a silence that feels deliberate.

The face is at the north end, and it is always advancing. Twice a day, by a schedule everyone feels in their teeth, the rock there is blown and cut and hauled away, lengthening the slot by a few metres. The newest terraces are unfinished — raw, pale, still steaming faintly in the cold — and the oldest, at the south, have already been occupied, patched, inhabited, built up with an extra room here, a shrine of lamps there. When the face comes too close, a whole bench of houses is simply taken apart. Iron doors, hinges, window-frames, even the cut block of a front wall are numbered, lifted by crane, and re-erected a terrace higher. Markets follow. A bakery becomes a barber becomes a shrine becomes a bakery again, all in a month. If you drew a map of the Cut, it would be wrong before the ink dried. That is its turbulence: not a storm passing over a place, but a place that is perpetually being unmade and remade by the work that feeds it. Anthill-energy, but vertical.

There is no water to speak of. No rain enters the slot — storms break on the plateau and go around, or shed their snow on the rim and let it sublimate in the high wind. What little moisture exists is trapped, hoarded, recycled. At dawn, in the coldest upper niches, frost forms on the rock from breath and kiln-vapour and the slow exhalation of the mountain itself. Men and women go out with scrapers before the lamps change, and scrape it into jars. Condensers on the cool walls weep a few litres in a night. Coolant is oil and dry air and whatever the ore gives up when it is heated. A household’s water is measured, traded, boiled, reboiled. The air is always thirsty. Your lips crack. It is a dry-mineral chemistry, and everything here has adapted to it.

Nothing grows, in the way you would recognise growth. No garden, no weed in a crack, no tree on the rim. The city’s food is largely brought up by cable in dried, compressed form, and what is grown here is not plant but fungus — pale, grey-orange mats cultivated in warm wall-rooms behind the pipes, and wild mats spreading, uninvited, across abandoned niches where the heat and the dust are right. It is soft, sour, faintly luminous in the dark, and it is made into loaves, into soup, into a kind of pulp that is fed to almost everything else. The only green anywhere in the Cut is the greenish cast of old copper oxide on a fresh face, and even that is mineral.

And still, life is abundant. That is the second surprise, after the quiet.

At the rim, where the slot opens to the sky, the ravens live. Big, soot-black, fearless high-altitude birds that ride the updraft like lifts, coming down in a slow spiral to steal from open hands and to noses around the market bridges. Their nests fill the overhangs until the rock is whitewashed below them. Lower down, in the expansion joints and the warm cracks between iron plates, are the wall-skinks — flattened, steel-grey, quick as fingers — that live on moths. The moths are everywhere near the lamps: ash-moths, heat-moths, clouds of them rising from the kiln and settling on the fungal mats. Beetles the size of a thumbnail pick their way through cable-ducts, eating insulation and spilled food. Rats, introduced with the first supplies and now larger, hairless-tailed, grease-fed, run in established highways along the pipes, and are eaten, in turn, by the ravens and by the men who catch them for the pot. On the hottest walls, translucent dust-worms mill in the film of oil and fungus, more animal than plant, and a whole ecology of mites lives in their folds.

The residents make a kind of sense of it all because, in the Cut, the distinction between fauna and machinery keeps slipping. The little quadruped sweepers that patrol the lower terraces, eating dust and dropped fungus and learning the routes of the alleys, are tended like animals — fed, repaired, cursed when they die in a drain. The carrier-mules are engines, but they walk, they require loading and rest, they have names. If you watch long enough you stop asking which things are alive and start asking which things move on their own, and in the Cut that is almost everything.

People define position by heat and height more than by north and south. Uptown is colder, richer in air, poorer in warmth. Downtown is the floor, the kiln, the crushers — warmer, dirtier, louder with work if not with noise, and more prized in winter. Addresses are given as bench and niche: *Bench 7, Niche 41*. Everyone knows the blast times not by clock but by the brief, total stillness that precedes them, when even the ravens leave the walls.

It is, in the end, an unusual place rather than a strange one. Nothing here breaks the world. It merely lives at the extreme of it — at an altitude where lungs work hard, in a dryness where water is a rumour, in a pocket so small and so dense that a city of several thousand can be contained in a single crack and still feel, from the bottom looking up through layers of bridges and lamps and dust, as if it went on forever. Go back in a month and the face will have moved, three benches will have been rebuilt, a market will have migrated to a different wall, and the ravens will be nesting somewhere new. The Cut will still be there, dim and cool and crowded and quietly turbulent, breathing its dust up the chimney, waiting to be blown a little wider.
