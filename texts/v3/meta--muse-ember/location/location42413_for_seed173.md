It is not at the bottom of the sea. It is below the surface, which is a different thing.

You descend into it and the light goes first, then the air. At about twenty meters the blue becomes a green-grey and stays there, dim and unchanging, as if someone turned a filtered lamp on under water and left it. The water is cold — cold enough that your hands go clumsy in it after a while, cold enough that everything moves a little slower to compensate. It is fully, completely water. Not a flooded floor, not a leaking basement. You never stand up out of it. The only air left in the whole place is trapped in thin, trembling pockets under the ceilings of the overturned tanks, silver and unreachable.

And there is no ground here that was not made.

The whole area is a bowl, a hamlet-size caisson sunk into the harbor dark, maybe two hundred meters across, and that is its boundary. Its floor is poured concrete slabs laid in a grid, steel plating riveted over the seams, a road of asphalt that goes nowhere now, tiles in the corner of what was a wash-house. The walls that hold the bowl are retaining walls, vertical, barnacled to the waterline that never ends. Where you would expect sand or rock or mud, you find the same things again: concrete dust, shell grit, oil-stained gravel that is actually crushed brick. Even the cracks between slabs are filled with broken concrete. There is nowhere for a natural substrate to show. Life does not live on stone here. It lives on the built.

It is an old industrial place and it has been underwater long enough that the industrial has stopped looking industrial.

You can still read it — a turbine hall in the center, cathedral high and long, its roof a barrel vault of corrugated steel now softened by algae; around it, lower and scattered, a row of tanks, two crane rails that end mid-air, a conveyor gantry, a small office block with its windows punched out, a pump house with its doors swung open like a mouth. But nothing is sharp. Water and time have done their work on it for a very long time, in the way rivers do work over a very long time. Corners are rounded. Edges are gone. Paint has gone from yellow to a mustard stain, then to a smudge. Rivets have swollen and blurred. Concrete has pitted and then been bored by animals and then smoothed again by them, until it looks less like concrete and more like weathered limestone, ancient and settled. This is not a fresh wreck. It is not actively rusting apart or collapsing in drama. It has reached its oldest, stable state. It is deeply aged, near-flattened by process, end-of-life in the sense that decay itself has finished and what remains is a final form that will change only imperceptibly now.

The density is sparse. That is the strangest part for something so full of buildings. Between the turbine hall and the tanks there is a long, empty street of flooded asphalt. Between the tanks and the wall, open water. You swim across those gaps and there is nothing at all — just particulate drifting, just the cold, just the sound coming up through the slabs. The presences are scattered and low-saturation. A single crab on a pipe. A single buoy chain swaying. A flicker from a sodium lamp someone left wired to a tidal generator, still in its housing under glass, blinking red and amber through the dim in a slow, patient rhythm. The dimness is not total. It is the dimness of depth and of water and of failing electrics. Up above the vault, light comes down through a grated roof in green shafts, so little that it makes the algae glow rather than grow. Down here on the floor it is almost night.

And yet the fauna is abundant.

Because everything here is shelter, and shelter is plentiful, and nothing here is natural in a way that would limit it. The fish come through in schools — pollock and herring and small mackerel, hundreds at a time, turning as one body in the empty street, then scattering when they hit the shadow of the gantry. Bass hang motionless under the catwalks. Crabs live by the thousands in the pipework, in the turbine scroll, in the hollow legs of the crane, clicking and shuffling in the dark. Mussels and barnacles have incrusted every vertical surface until the steel looks furry. Sea anemones open on valve handles. Shrimp pulse in the silt that isn't silt but ground concrete and droppings and shell. Everywhere the built has become reef, and the reef is busy in a perfectly ordinary, biological way. It supports predators and prey, grazers and filterers, decomposers working on the old oil in the sumps. The algae — the only vegetation, and it is sparse — grows where it can: thin green films on the south-facing concrete, kelp-like fronds unspooling from pipe joints where a little light leaks, curtains of it waving in the current at the grated roof. No soil, no roots worth mentioning. It just attaches to metal and hangs on.

The sound is loud, and it has been loud for a long time.

Underwater, sound does not fade. The pumps in the pump house still cycle, autonomous and mindless, driven by the tide through the sluice gates in the retaining wall. Thump. Thump. Thump. A low industrial hum runs up through your sternum and through the concrete at the same time, like a note held on a piano. Valves hiss and vent bubble streams that rattle up to the vault and pop in the air pockets. Chains somewhere bang against steel as the current swings them. The whole turbine hall pings — a soft, metallic ticking — as the cold water contracts the metal. You hear schools before you see them, a sibilant rush. You hear crabs. You hear, every twenty minutes or so, the deep flush as the sluices open and a current moves through the bowl, carrying particles and algae and fish from one end to the other in a slow, active pour.

That current is the weather here. Twice a day the bowl breathes in and out. The dynamic is always on: the pumps working, the lights flickering in sequence, the schools migrating between the empty street and the shadowed halls, the algae swaying, the crabs emerging to feed on the mussel beds when the flow brings new food. Nothing is static, but nothing is frantic either. It is an ecosystem in motion at the pace of cold water.

There is nothing weird about it, and that makes it stranger than if there were. No ghosts. No glow. The water obeys pressure and temperature. The concrete mineralizes rather than rots. The fish need oxygen and get it the way fish do. The machines run on the same principles they always did, only now without hands to turn them off. A human divers would recognize it instantly as a flooded shipyard, a drowned cannery, a sunken power station, and would be able to guess its purpose from the labels still faintly legible on a tank: DO NOT SMOKE. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. Over the years the letters have eroded until they are just suggestions.

After a while you stop feeling walled in by the smallness of it. A cathedral-scale hall, a hamlet of tanks, a room of pumps — you can cross the whole place in a few minutes of swimming, and yet you will not see it all in an hour, because most of it is empty water and you keep getting caught by life. A school turns. A crab crosses your light. The hum changes key as a second pump kicks in. The light from the surface — that same dim green — shifts its angle almost imperceptibly.

It is a made world, completely made, that has been in the water so long the making has become geology. Cold, dark, loud, sparse, abundant, ancient, and still, impossibly, working.
