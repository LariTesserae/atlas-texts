It is smaller than you expect.

You climb and climb — through cloud, through thin air that makes lungs work like bellows — and then, at the very top, the world narrows to a bowl you could cross on foot in twenty minutes. A summit plateau. A hamlet of stone and ice. A single feature, ringed by a rim.

Below the rim there is no ground at all. Only a slow sea of cloud, white and moving, so the bowl feels like an island taken out of the sky. Above it, the sky is not pale. At noon it is a hard, dark blue, almost black at the zenith, because there is so little air left to scatter light. And yet the light that reaches the bowl is merciless. The sun is high and white and vertical, and the ground throws it back. Everything bleaches. Ash, pumice, frost-shattered rock — all planed down to greys and whites, to a chalky, sun-bleached palette. Shadows are the only color left, and they are blue-black, knife sharp on the sunward side of every stone.

You squint even through a shaded hand. Your eyes water. The glare comes from below as well as above, off snow that never quite melts and off ice lenses set into the rock like eyes.

The ground is crowded. That is the first surprise: for all its emptiness, it is dense. You cannot take a step without stepping over, around, or between things. Angular blocks the size of fists, shards of black volcanic glass that catch the sun and look wet, plates of pale rhyolite worn smooth, loose cinder crunching underfoot, then sudden patches of fine, sifting grit, then a slab of clear ice frozen into a hollow. They lie in layers. In the lee of a boulder, a drift of snow a hand's breadth deep, and under it a crust, and under the crust a tongue of ice, and under that more stones. The whole place has the feel of something long settled — not fresh lava, not a new cliff. The rims are rounded. The slopes are at their resting angle. Wind has polished faces flat. Frost has wedged and re-wedged the same cracks for centuries until the stones have found their own balance. Nothing is dramatic. Everything is weathered into place.

There is no plant life here, and no animal life to speak of. Nothing green. Nothing that burrows, flies, or crawls. Once in a while the wind carries something up from the cloud sea below — a dead insect, a seed — and drops it, and it lies there unrotted, frozen dry, until the sun turns it white. The only things that grow here grow without living.

At night, when the temperature falls far below freezing — it is cold here even at midday, cold in the breath that clouds and stings your nose, cold in your fingers despite the sun — moisture gathers. Not rain, mostly. There is not enough water for rain to soak in. But twice a day the bowl changes state.

In the coldest hour before dawn, every windward edge is furred. Hoarfrost feathers grow on the walls, on the glass shards, on the tops of stones, needle-fine, white as magnified snowflakes. In sheltered cracks, ice grows in blades and rosettes, crystal gardens that push into the air a finger's length, break off, and are replaced. Salts migrate up through damp stone and bloom as efflorescence, a pale crust. These are the closest thing to vegetation: structures that grow, compete for shadow, collapse, and regrow, but made only of water and mineral.

When the sun hits them, they do not melt so much as vanish. Sublimation. The thin, dry air takes them straight back to vapor. In the hollows, where the ground dips and the wind cannot reach, water does persist — in pockets. Small melt pans no bigger than a saucer, frozen over with a clear lid even at noon, or a lens of ice trapped under a stone, or a bead of condensation on the shaded north face of a block that will sit there all day in shadow, cold and damp to the touch while the sunward face is bone dry. A cloud will sometimes pass over the rim, and for five minutes droplets form on everything, a brief damp that darkens the ash, and then the sun burns it off within the hour. Water here never runs. It freezes, it evaporates, it stays put.

It is mostly quiet. The thin air carries sound poorly. You hear your own boots crunch — a dry, granular squeak on frozen cinder — and your own breath, too loud. Then, intermittently, the processes speak. A sharp *ping* as a stone contracts in the evening chill and splits a hairline wider. A faint chime, like a tiny bell, when an ice blade snaps. The wind, when it comes over the rim, not as a howl but as a hiss, driving grains of snow and ice dust in a dense, low saltation a few centimeters off the ground, scouring the windward side and depositing them in drifts on the lee. Then it stops, and the hiss stops, and the silence returns, complete.

And yet, in that silence, much is happening. This is an active place, just not a biological one.

Frost heave lifts stones a fraction at a time and lets them settle at a new tilt. Daily freeze and thaw — brutal at this altitude, where the sun can warm a dark glass shard above freezing while the air around it stays bitter — works the cracks like a slow wedge. The sun bleaches anything left exposed to paper white in weeks. Ice creeps in the pockets, expanding the hollows it fills. Wind balances wind: what is stripped from the crest is laid down in the bowl, so the surface is always arriving and departing, never quite the same, but never changing its shape.

Scattered through this dense, mineral cover are the remains of people, and what they could do with hand tools.

No metal to speak of — a single rusted nail, perhaps, in a wall. Everything else is stone on stone. Low, semi-circular windbreaks made of uncut slabs fitted without mortar, crouching into the ground, their tops slumped and settled like old backs. A hut, or the idea of a hut, barely shoulder-high, open to the sky, its inner face blackened once by a fire that seems to have been lit only a few times. Walls that led nowhere, or led in a line of small cairns, each cairn three or four stones high, spaced close together across the white so you could follow them even in whiteout — though you would have to guess which way was forward.

There are tools left where they were dropped: a smooth maul for pecking, flakes struck from the black glass, a few shards of crude ceramic cracked by frost. Pecked lines on a flat boulder, too weathered to read. Someone came up here with stone hammers and patience and tried to make a shelter from the mineral world, and the mineral world has been slowly taking it back, heaving the walls outward, filling the hollow with grit, frosting the hearth.

That is the rule of this place more than any other: things do not rot here. They bleach, they crack, they freeze, they are ground down and lifted again. A footprint taken at dawn is filled with rime by the time you turn around. A thing left in the open does not decay — it desiccates in the sun and UV, it is sandblasted, it is buried and exhumed.

At midday the bowl can feel almost warm on a black glass shard under your palm, and unnaturally cold in the one step of shadow beside it. At dusk the light goes oblique and suddenly everything is long and blue, the ice lenses glow from inside, and the ticking starts as the whole surface cools at once. The cloud sea below turns gold, then grey. The rim keeps its shadow circle complete long after the center has gone dark.

You could walk its whole extent before your breath settled. You could also lose your sense of distance entirely in the glare, where a white stone ten meters away and a white stone two meters away look identical, and black shadows lie so hard they look like holes.

It is not a dead place. It is a place where the living has been subtracted, and what is left — ice growing like a garden, water hiding in pockets, light too bright to look at, stone endlessly rearranged by cold — carries on, dense and quiet and busy, at the top of the world, in a room no bigger than a village, ringed by sky.
