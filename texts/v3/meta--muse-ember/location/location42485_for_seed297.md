It is a plain, and the first fact of it is flatness. Low land runs in every direction until the horizon, a few meters up and down in sharp, unfinished wrinkles, but never rising into hill or valley. The ground beneath your feet is not soil. It is fractured parent rock, still chemically young — pale mineral silt like flour mixed with sandpaper, plates of silicate glass, shards cut by recent tearing whose edges have not yet been softened by weather. There are no rounded pebbles, no washed gravel, no layers sorted by time. Cracks gape with fresh interiors. Crystals poke through the dust unpatinaed, as if they grew yesterday and simply stopped. It is warm everywhere, a steady, even warmth coming up out of the stone itself, held at the same temperature across the whole expanse. The air is dry, still, and metallic on the tongue. No humidity, no dew, no moisture on skin or stone. Nothing here has ever been wet.

And yet it is lush.

Vegetation is not in patches. It is the plain. It covers the fractures, climbs the little sharp mounds, and meets itself overhead in places, dense enough that you must push between stems to walk, near-touching, layered. At ground level there is a mat of thick, overlapping pads, copper-green and sea-glass teal and dusty violet, waxy to the touch and completely dry. Above it, knee-high and waist-high, stand fans of folded plates on stiff stalks, each plate ribbed like a miniature roof tile, each surface polished in a way that seems too perfect to be organic. Higher still, where the ground rises a meter or two, the fans interlock into a low canopy, thin sheets holding their angles to the sun. The palette is exuberant but un-Earthly: sage, rust, silver-blue, burnt orange, a deep bottled green. It is green in the way a desert succulent is green — concentrated, waxy, defensive — carried to excess.

There is no water to explain it, and the plants do not miss it. Their roots are not roots but anchors and mining arms, splitting rock to draw out phosphate, sulfur, iron, and trace metals directly. Their leaves are not built for drinking; they are built for inhaling. The atmosphere carries a permanent, invisible breath of carbon dioxide and mineral vapor — sulfur compounds, fine silicate dust — which never condenses, never rains, and is taken in molecule by molecule across the leaf surface. Inside, the “sap” is not liquid water but a warm ionic slurry, more like molten salt gel than blood, which flows when a stem flexes and sets solid when it rests. Cut a leaf and it does not bleed. It hisses, releasing a puff of compressed gas, and the wound seals as salt crystallizes in the air. They photosynthesize, they fix carbon, they thrive — without a single drop of solvent ever touching them. The surrounding ecology has no use for water either. Nothing rots. Nothing rusts. Fallen fragments do not soften into humus; they are reabsorbed by the living mat or revert to stone. The ground is not enriched by death. It does not need to be.

This is the second fact: nothing changes. Not slowly, not seasonally, not noticeably at all. The sun crosses a normal, yellow-white sky each day — normal light, hard shadows, clear air, no clouds ever forming — and the temperature at noon is the temperature at noon tomorrow, and the day after, to a degree you can feel underfoot. The plants do not grow toward the light; they are already at the precise angle. They do not unfurl, shed, fruit in waves, or heal. Mark a frond with a scratch and come back a year later and the scratch will be there, unweathered, the same pale line, the same color in the leaf around it. Seeds are abundant — dry, glassy seeds hang in clusters, spores drift in the still air like fine grey snow that never quite falls — but sprouts appear in the same places forever, as if germination has already happened and been fixed. The whole system is held in a kind of finished equilibrium, lush and arrested, a world after completion.

Sound belongs to the silence. Mostly it is quiet in a way that feels engineered: your steps make a crisp, dry crunch that dies immediately in the porous vegetation, with no echo, no answering insects, no wind moving through leaves. Occasionally, as a crystal plate cools a fraction, there is a faint ping, high and singular, from somewhere far off in the mat. Otherwise the air absorbs everything. You become aware of your own breathing, your own pulse in your ears, because the plain offers nothing back.

Fauna are rare in proportion to the plants. You can walk a long way — a kilometer, more — without seeing a track, a dropping, a sign that anything with a nervous system lives here. When you do, it is singular and unhurried: a low, ash-grey stepper, armored in mirrored plates, moving so slowly across the silt that you understand it has not moved since you first saw it, and will not have moved much when you look again. It does not drink. It grazes the mineral film on rock with a mouth built for scraping glass. There are smaller things, motes in the sunlit air, drifters without wings or sound, but they do not land. No birds. No burrows. No webs. The animal presence is a rumor in a plant world.

And the rules are wrong in quiet ways. The weirdness is not chaos; it is pattern pushed past sense. The same arrangement of stones and fan-plants repeats, exactly, two hundred meters on, and again, until you suspect the plain is tiled by a template that reuses itself. Some leaves are solid from one angle and translucent from another, without thinning. A frond casts two shadows, one true and one at a right angle, both sharp. In certain patches the light seems to come from slightly behind the horizon, and colors leave an afterimage in the air itself for a second longer than they should. Distance does not add up cleanly across the invisible seams in the vegetation — a cluster you counted as ten steps away can be fifteen, and the count will not repeat the same way. It is not hostile. It is simply a place where the local physics has been patched, and the patch shows.

For that is the final strangeness, and the one underneath everything else: nothing here was built. There is no road, no wall, no tool, no shard of metal anywhere in the plain. No artifact, no ruin, no sign of a maker. And yet everything works with a precision that current physics does not account for. Under a microscope — and there is no microscope here, only the hint of it in the leaf surface — each plant cell is not a cell at all but an interlocking lattice at the nanoscale, capable of tunneling molecules directly from air into interior chemistry, of repairing its own structure without waste, of exchanging signals across the entire mat without nerves or roots connecting them. The whole plain behaves as one distributed system, regulating its air composition, its temperature, its own frozen state, with no center and no machinery in sight. The engineering is not in objects. It is in the laws as they apply here: entropy politely declined, water made unnecessary, decay suspended, growth brought to a perfect halt at the moment of fullness.

So you stand in a dry, warm, flat expanse that goes on past sight, dense with life that never drinks and never grows, quiet except for your own steps, sharp underfoot with young stone, and utterly still. The vegetation touches you as you pass, dry and cool and waxy. The sun is ordinary. The horizon is heterogeneously green to its edge. And the place, for all its lushness, gives the feeling not of a wilderness in progress, but of a wilderness already finished — a reality that was corrected once, long ago or just now, and has been holding that correction ever since.
