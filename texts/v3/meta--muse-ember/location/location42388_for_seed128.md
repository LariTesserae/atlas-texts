Call it the Roost, though no map has it and the larger structure has no name for the humans who built it. It is not a room so much as a pocket left in the works — a cavity about seven meters across, four high, irregular as a pulled tooth, nested inside the stacked heat-exchanger plates near the top of a tower that rises a mile above a dry valley. From outside it is invisible. The tower is all vertical fins of sintered ceramic, copper, and black glass, sun-bleached and screaming in thin high wind. Inside a seam between two plates, if you know where the service iris is, you can crouch through a warm, damp slit and be suddenly, completely, elsewhere.

The transition is violent. Outside: cold, thin, bright, the valley far below in blue haze. Inside: warm — not hot, but a deep, incubating warmth that settles on the skin and stays — wet to the bone in a minute, and deafening.

Nothing here is natural. Even the ground is a construction: overlapping tiles of ceramic slag, each about the size of a hand, laid like scales over a honeycomb of ducts. The tiles were once sharp and dark. They are not anymore. In centuries of being sweated on, breathed on, walked on by things that never lift their feet, they have rounded, paled, and gone matte in the middle of the paths and glossy at the edges where bodies brush them. White, green, and faintly iridescent crusts bloom in the grout — salt, oxidized copper, dried biofilm — no two tiles the same. Where a tile cracked, a fine mineral vein has healed it shut, slightly raised, like a scar. The place does not look new. It looks *settled*, the way a very old engine looks settled: worn into its own shape, still running, in balance with everything it has grown.

Water is everywhere, and it is not contained. The ceiling is cooler than the floor, coupled through the plates to the high cold outside, so warm vapor rising from below condenses up there and falls again as a perpetual small rain — not a shower, a misting fall of droplets that seem to hang for a moment in the dim before they land. The walls weep in thin, continuous films that do not drip so much as slide, guided down grooves molded into the tiles. At the edges of the ankle-deep pools set into the floor, the water simmers without quite boiling; at this elevation the pressure is low, and heat from the deck keeps it on the edge of steam. Three states at once: steam in the upper air, liquid glass on every vertical surface, warm current moving slowly along the floor in braided channels under open grating. Your shoes never dry. Your hair never dries. Breathing feels like breathing through a warm cloth.

The light is dim and mostly not light at all, but glow. Behind frosted ceramic panels at floor level, old heating elements breathe a dull amber — the underfloor of the world. Deeper pools glow faint turquoise where the water is thin over pale tile, and green-blue where a microbial film lives undisturbed. The fauna carry their own pinpricks: amber dots of eyes, a constellation that moves. There are no windows. The only true light comes through the iris when it opens, a white slash from the high sky, and then it is gone, and the dim closes again like water.

And the sound. It is not one sound. It begins under your feet, a low thrum you feel in your molars from the core far below the tower, never stopping. Over it, the hiss of steam escaping through tile pores, the irregular *clang* of water hammering through pipes you cannot see, droplets plinking into resonant hollows and echoing too long. And on top of everything, the chorus.

The fauna are the density. It is not that there are a lot of animals in a room; it is that there is hardly any surface that is not already occupied, layered, near-touching. The walls are alive with thumb-sized runners, copper-backed and six-legged, smooth as wet stones, licking condensation as they climb, their feet making a soft, endless ticking like rain on a tin roof. In the air hangs a cloud of droplet-fliers so dense they shimmer — pinhead midges with wings that catch the amber — swarming, dispersing, re-forming around the steam vents in waves. In the pools, flat armored grazers paddle in slow files, scraping tile with mouthparts, leaving those polished tracks that are now part of the floor’s erosion. Under the grating, blind white things pulse in the warm current. And there is bigger life: a humped, dog-sized grazer that moves through the central basin with enormous patience, its shell plated in the same heat-pipe ceramic as the walls, scraping biofilm and mineral crust and leaving the tile bright behind it — an old, established route worn concave by its ancestors. Nothing here is rare. The walls move.

They are loud in the way a living engine is loud. The runners chirp through their feet, vibration through metal and ceramic, a sound that is less heard than felt in the soles. The fliers hum as one body. The grazers rasp. Every twenty minutes or so the whole pocket enters a different phase: a heat tide rolls up through the floor, the rain thickens, the chorus rises until conversation is not difficult but impossible, until your own voice comes back to you muffled and strange. Then the tide passes, the water hammer stutters, the clamor drops a fraction, and the fauna migrate — thousands at once pouring off the walls and into the pools, or the reverse, a living tide that redraws the room.

The plants are the exception, and that makes them noticeable. Light is too poor and the surfaces too washed and hot for much to root. In one cool trough near the intake pipe, where the inflow keeps a draft, a small patch of engineered silver ferns persists — maybe a dozen fronds, each leaf etched with fine conductors that carry their dew back to the roots. Dark moss-filaments hang from a few ceiling seams like wet hair. Fauna constantly crop them. They are sparse because everything else eats, cleans, or washes them away. The ecosystem does not run on plants. It runs on film, on mineral dust, on waste heat, on each other. What looks like dirt in the corners is made sediment: shed shells, dried biofilm, crushed crust, recycled over and over by mouths and current until the floor slowly builds its own terraces.

That is the rule of the place, as far as you can tell: nothing leaves, nothing is wasted, and everything heals. Heat that should be lost is walked sideways through the wall lattice and returned to the pools. Water that evaporates is caught by the tiles, which breathe it in and out by the hour. Cracks seal. The thermal cycle that makes the deafening groan — the whole chamber expanding a fraction, then breathing out — is not a malfunction. It is the heartbeat. The technology is well beyond anything you could maintain; it does not need you to. It has long since been adopted, colonized, and softened by the life that was seeded here or crawled in, and now the life and the machinery are the same system, eroded into each other.

You can stay only a little while. Ears ring. Clothes cling. The warmth gets into you. When you crouch back through the iris into the high wind and the brutal bright, the tower’s finned flank towers above and below you, and it is hard to believe that against the whole cold sky there is this one small, hidden, sweating pocket — dim, wet, warm, crowded, thrumming — continuing without you, in its own established weather, rain falling from its own ceiling, its creatures singing through stone, as it has for longer than anyone has bothered to remember.
