It begins as a subtraction.

You come over a low rise through a gap between two ridges, and the first thing the hollow does is take the world away. Not all at once in a visual way — there is almost nothing to look at in any case — but first by sound, and then by the sense of space in between things.

The sound is deafening. It is not the roar of a river, though your body wants a river to blame. It is a white, tearing wind-noise with a low note underneath it, steady enough that after a minute you stop hearing it as an event and start hearing it as a pressure, in the teeth and behind the eyes. Speech would have to be shouted into an ear. A bird’s call, if there was one, would be swallowed whole. Anything falling, anything breaking, anything living quietly out there would be lost under it. And yet, underneath the noise, the place feels empty. The horizon is gone. The sky is only a grey strip between the shoulders of the ridges. The ground falls away a few necessary metres, and then there is a long, shallow floor, no more than a couple of hundred paces end to end, which nevertheless feels much larger because there is so little in it to cross by.

The ridges themselves are old in the way of things that have stopped changing much. Nothing here is sharp. The stone has been softened into broad mammalian curves; pebbles are set in the peat like worn teeth; cracks in the north wall have been polished and rounded by long weather until they look hand-worn rather than split. Lichen grows in crusts that must be many decades established, and where moss has taken hold it has taken it completely, smoothing stone into green upholstery. This is not a new hollow. It is a settled one, a place that seems to have found the shape it can keep. A landslide would be an offence. A stone knocked from a cairn would lie exactly where it fell for longer than anyone would wait to notice.

The light never really arrives. The walls are high enough, and the opening narrow enough, that even at midday the basin is in deep blue-grey shade, as if under a thick forest canopy that isn’t there. In the depths of the season the day thins to a long dusk and never quite becomes day at all. The colours that survive are muted and saturated at once: peat black as wet leather, moss a cold green, stone the colour of wet ash, rust a small orange punctuation on an old nail or an iron awl. Frost feathers the sheltered sides of stones even when the air is a degree or two above freezing, because the cold air pools in the bottom and does not leave. It is a low place in every sense — low ground, low light, low temperatures — and the chill is not sharp, as mountain cold can be, but damp and patient. It gets into fingers and knees. Your breath shows. The stone is colder than the air that touches it.

Water is present the way damp is present in a good cellar. There is no stream, no pool, no runnel. There is a skin of moisture over everything. Condensation gathers on the undersides of stones and on the inside of the fissure and works its way down in beads. Now and then a single drip falls from a crack in the ridge — you can watch the bright thread of it against the dark wall — and lands on stone with a tick that the wind immediately takes away. Fine sleet sometimes comes sideways and vanishes into the moss. Puddles form only as films on flat peat, and the moss drinks them almost as fast as they form. Your hands come away wet from touching anything. Lungs fill with cold wet air. The soil is never dry and never flooded; it is a sponge that has learned to keep its water in.

And because of that — the damp, the low sun, the absence of heat or grazing or much disturbance — the vegetation, where it grows, is startlingly lush. It does not grow everywhere. In the middle, the wind has stripped the floor down to empty flats of grit and peat where a few stones sit alone at long intervals, each one ringed with a tuft of grass or a cushion of moss. But in the sheltered seams along the walls, in the lee of a boulder, in the hollows where the peat deepens, the growth is exuberant for such a cold dark place: ferns standing waist-high with fronds uncurling in slow green commas, moss deep enough to take the print of a hand and spring back over a minute or two, broad-leaved herbs hugging the ground, lichens hanging in grey festoons from the edges of overhangs. Dwarf willow and birch exist here, but as scrabbles and stools rather than trees, their energy spent low. It is not a jungle. It is a series of rich islands in a mostly bare sea, and the distance between islands is part of the design.

Eventually you find the source of the noise, or you find where it pretends to be. In the north wall there is a vertical fissure, no wider than a hand, its inner edges worn smooth as a riverbed. Air is being forced through it — through some long hollow under the ridge, some old wind-tunnel in the stone — and it emerges on this side as a concentrated jet and then unravels. The jet itself is only a few paces wide; step into it and your clothes snap, your hat would go if you had one, loose grit skitters. But the basin catches the sound and throws it back from wall to wall, so that the whole hollow hums like the inside of a drum. On still days outside, in here nothing is still. In the pulses of the wind, pebbles in the peat tremble almost imperceptibly, fern fronds shiver in unison, the hanging lichens swing. It is an ordinary phenomenon, nothing ghostly or miraculous, but it makes the ordinary feel enormous, and it makes silence feel like something that has been permanently mislaid.

Life, consequently, keeps its distance and its voice down. You will not see much of it, and what you see will mostly be sign: a fox track pressed into soft peat and already softening at the edges, weeks old; worm castings in the moss; one beetle, dark and industrious, righting itself under a stone you lifted and already regretting the light; a pair of small slate-grey birds that flush from a nest tucked into a crevice out of the jet’s path and are gone over the ridge before you have properly registered them. A slug crosses a moss cushion with the indifference of a creature that has all the time this place offers, which is a great deal. There is no swarm, no chorus, no grazing herd to crop the lushness down. The plants can afford to be lush because almost nothing is eating them, and the animals can afford to be rare because almost nothing is asking to be eaten. The hollow is not dead. It is sparsely, carefully alive.

The human traces are of the same order — scattered, small, and long since absorbed. A low arc of dry stone near the sheltered wall, a windbreak never high enough to stand behind, its top course subsiding into moss. A line of flat stones laid as a causeway across the wettest peat, now sunk to ankle-depth and vanishing. A wooden frame, black with damp, lashed with root-fibre that is stronger than it looks; a wedge split into a log; a bench slab beside a shallow blackened scrape where a fire was attempted and, one assumes, given up on. Near that slab, flakes of knapped stone and a bone awl, patiently sharp, left as if the owner would return in a season or a year. A rusted iron spike driven into a stone. A smear of ochre on a boulder, weathered to a blush. Nothing built to last, nothing built to be found, and the moss and peat have been quietly mailing all of it back to the ground ever since.

That is the rule of the place, in the end: slowness as a form of authority. The moss advances a finger’s width and keeps it. The ferns uncurl in the same places each year. The windbreak lowers by a stone every few winters. The fissure sings the same note through every season, louder when the weather presses, never silent. Cold slows rot, damp slows fire, darkness slows growth, and the noise slows anyone who tries to linger — you find yourself doing everything a little more deliberately, listening with your eyes. Nothing here is new, nothing here is finished, and nothing here is in a hurry to become anything else. It is a low, dark, wet pocket in the world, emptied out by wind and filled, in islands, by green; a place that has worn itself into a balanced hum, and keeps humming.
