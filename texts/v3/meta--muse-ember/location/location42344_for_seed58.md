**Sinderhold**

The way down is a stair cut fresh into black rock, and it has not had time to make itself comfortable.

It drops below the plain in a single straight flight, then another, then a landing where the air changes. Pressure settles in your ears. The walls on either side are close enough to touch, and they give back heat through your palms long after you take your hands away. The stone is young. It was not a cave granted by age and slow water, but a blister opened recently when the ground below shifted and a pocket of heat worked its way up and cracked. The basalt is still sharp. Edges have not been rounded by centuries of seep. Underfoot the gravel is not soil or dust worn smooth, but shards, splinters of glassy rock that bite through soles and are crushed finer with every shift. Here and there on the ceiling are the first infant knobs of mineral, no more than a thumb’s length, where drips have begun to lay down stone and have not yet learned to make stalactites. Everything is raw and recently emerged: unweathered, unsoftened, still ticking faintly as it cools in fits, a soft ping here, a sighing crack there, like pottery settling.

Sinderhold fits entirely inside that one opening.

It is small — a hamlet squeezed into a city. From the mouth of the stair you can see, dimly, the other end of it, four hundred paces away, where the vault rises to a dark dome and the hot air gathers and loses itself. You could walk it end to end in ten minutes, and many do, several times a day. But you will not easily cross it, because there is almost nowhere to walk that is not built over, under, or through.

The settlement claims the rim of the cavern and leaves the middle as a kind of steep, built canyon. Houses are three and four stories, stacked against the rock and against each other, brick made from the crushed basalt underfoot, faced with corrugated iron and smudged glass. A shop’s roof is the next house’s floor. A bridge-street at the third level crosses an alley below, so laundry and pipes and cables pass overhead in a second, braided ceiling. There are no empty lots except one. No gardens. No yards. The density is near-touching everywhere: balconies lean over doorways, stair towers bolt onto older walls, windows face windows across a lane too narrow for two carts and open onto the neighbor’s kitchen. The town has grown into the rock like a thicket, layered thick with presences until you look up and see more building than sky — and there is no sky at all.

That is the city’s nature: urban not by sprawl, but by saturation.

Heat is its foundation. It is never cool here. At the stair-head it is already 40 degrees, and by the middle of the canyon it is close to fifty, thick enough to drink. Sweat does not evaporate; it beads and runs and stays, salt on your lips, damp at the small of your back. Near the boilers the air shimmers. In the dwelling stacks, where the fans can reach, it is a degree or two less, which people call a cool room. Everything works because the heat is free and constant. The factories do not so much burn fuel as borrow temperature from the rock and from great iron boilers at the far end, and they spend it making things for the surface world — castings, furnace brick, glass vials ground from silica, machine parts hammered and filed and riveted — things that would be costly to fire above, where heat must be paid for.

The light is dim and almost entirely artificial. Amber lamps hang every twenty paces, strung on cables that run in dense bundles overhead, sagging with their own weight. Furnace doors glow orange in the shop fronts. In the working hours the lamps burn a hard white; in the quiet hours they sink back to amber, and the town dims with them. No sun has ever entered. Workers’ days are measured not by daylight but by that lamplight cycle and by the cavern’s own weather.

For Sinderhold has weather, confined.

Hot, damp air rises along the walls, meets the cooler vault, and condenses. Every hour or so the dome begins to weep. First the rock sweats in dark patches, then the moisture gathers and falls in a fine, warm rain that is not rain from a cloud but from the ceiling itself. It lasts ten minutes, rattling on iron roofs, filling gutters, spattering the open square. Then the great vents at the far end sigh open — you feel it before you hear it, a movement of air — and the excess is pulled up and out into a shaft that goes somewhere far above, into darkness. The air clears, relatively. Heat builds again. Humidity builds again. It is a breathing cycle, about ninety minutes long, endlessly active, and the whole town lives to its rhythm: washing, cooling machinery, clearing steam, watering the sparse living things.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no river, no well that matters. Only pockets and film and intermittence. Puddles lie black and oily in the alleys, reflecting the lamps upside down. Gutters run half-full with warm, alkaline water that smells faintly of sulfur and hot iron. In the one open place — the Cistern Square, not much bigger than a house plot, paved and walled and roofed half over with iron sheeting — there is a single pool, perhaps three meters across, steaming faintly and too hot and mineral to drink. Pipes sweat. Every wall feels damp to the touch. Condensation forms instantly on the cooler side of a boiler, on a water jar brought from the square, on your own forearms when you step back from the Engine Hall. It is damp as a held breath, not wet as a landscape.

In that damp, and almost nowhere else, vegetation has taken hold.

It is very sparse. No trees. No grass that a grazing animal could make use of — and there are no grazing animals to make use of it. In the gutters of the Square, in cracks where the pooled runoff lingers, in the damp shadow under the cistern, there are mats of pale succulents with fleshy rosettes, hairy ferns bleached almost white by the lamps, and a low, moss-like growth that blooms in tiny, waxy flowers only during the quiet hours when the fans have stilled and the heat ebbs a fraction. They are not planted in any proper sense, though a few were coaxed there long ago. They are colonists, seeded by the vents, clinging to moisture the city makes anyway. Everything green in Sinderhold is small, tentative, and pale. The town itself is the thicket.

The absence that sits alongside that density is striking, once you have been here long enough to notice it. There is no animal life.

No flies land on the food in the market stalls. No pigeons nest under the eaves, though there are a thousand eaves. No rats run the gutters, though the gutters are dark and warm. No dogs, no cats, no birdsong, no insect hum in the amber light. The heat and the dry steam and the chemicals in the process air have sterilized the place of anything that crawls or flies or runs. The people of Sinderhold — perhaps eight hundred of them, hurrying in work clothes in the narrow lanes — keep no pets and could not keep them. It makes the town unnaturally quiet.

It is, by the standards of a working industrial place, very quiet indeed. Or rather, it is quiet in layers. The rock absorbs sound, and the humid air muffles it further. You hear the drip of condensation counting itself from eave to gutter, the distant, rhythmic clank of a press in the Loom Shed, a belt slapping softly, a cart on its short rail loop moving parts from the Engine Hall to the square, the low murmur of the boilers breathing, and then, between shifts, a pause where even the machines seem to exhale and only the dripping and the ticking of the cooling stone remain. No traffic roars. No horns. The city’s noise is intimate, close, as if it were happening in the next room — because it usually is.

And it is always in motion. The rail cart trundles back and forth along its fixed line. Belts turn. Fans turn. Doors, swollen by damp, are planed once a year and jam again within months; buildings sit on screw jacks because the young floor still heaves and settles in fractions. When the vent cycle begins, paper on the walls flutters and the lamp flames lean. When a furnace is tapped, a brief, brighter light rolls the length of the canyon, catching the iron roofs, the wet stone, the undersides of ten thousand cables, and showing the whole small city for a second in sharp relief before sinking back to amber.

The Engine Hall at the far end is the one place that approaches true grandeur. It is cathedral-scale within the small whole: a vaulted chamber perhaps thirty meters high, filled with two great boilers like altars, their pipes rising and branching and converging in dense, orderly bundles, valves like wheels, gauges like eyes. Heat pours from them in visible waves. Men work the valves in pairs, because a single wheel is too hot to hold for long without gloves. It is the heart and the furnace and the lungs of Sinderhold, and everything else — houses, looms, forges, the cistern, the sparse pale plants in the gutters — is arranged around it as a body around a heart, close, warm, and dependent.

That is the unusual thing about the place, in the end: not that it is strange, but that it is a complete world turned inside out. A town with no outside. A climate with no sky. An ecology with no animals, in which the only living things besides the people are a few hundred square meters of pale, heat-loving plants growing in the runoff of their own machines. A landscape of sharp, new rock, still forming, still hot, occupied before it had time to soften. Dense, urban, industrial, and quiet; hot, damp, and dim; small enough to hold in your eye from the stair-head, and yet so layered and active that you could live here for years and still find a new bridge-street above a lane you thought you knew, or hear the rock ping in the dark and realize it is still, slowly, becoming itself.
