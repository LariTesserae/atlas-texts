The way down is not a path. It is a fissure in frozen ground, no wider than shoulders, where frozen peat and silt have split and the roots above have parted like a curtain. You squeeze through sideways, and the cold meets you first, not as wind but as a pressure against your skin. It does not leave when you are inside.

Below, the light is dim and greenish, and it does not come from above. There is no sky to be seen, only a low vaulted ceiling five or six feet high at the crown, sinking at the edges until the walls meet the floor in a wet seam. The whole place is small. You could walk it in twenty steps, stand in the centre and touch opposite walls almost at once. It is an oval room in the earth, a grove the size of a living room, and yet inside it feels crowded in every direction, as if you have stepped into a body rather than a cave.

Your first step does not land on rock. It lands on plant.

The floor is a felt. For inches, maybe a foot, it is living substrate: interwoven roots bound tight as rope, rhizomes packed like knuckles, cushions of moss and liverwort, clubmosses, rosettes of pale succulent leaves with translucent windows in them. It gives under your weight, then resists, spongy and slippery and impossibly dense. Under the surface film of water you can feel cold silt, but you rarely see it. Everywhere the eye goes there is leaf, stem, thread, root-hair. The walls are the same. They are not rock covered in plants; they are plants propped against rock. Vines climb in slow curtains, their leaves overlapping like shingles. From the ceiling hang strands that are not stalactites — they are root tendrils and hollow stems, still growing downward, pale and searching, some already reaching the floor felt and joining it, some still a hand's breadth short. The chamber is still making its shape. Its erosion is an emergence, not a history. In one corner a new seep has only just opened: a raw fracture of grey, sharp-edged siltstone still weeping water, unsettled, with a fine mist of sediment in the air above it. That is the only bare geology in the room, and even there, white root hairs are already tapping the stone, tasting it. The place has not finished forming. It may not for a very long time.

Water is everywhere but never deep. It is near the surface, and it stays near. The ground above is saturated, and it filters down through the ceiling in dozens of fine weeps. You hear them long before you see them. A drip falls from the vault, strikes a broad cold leaf, and the leaf drums it onward. Another strikes a shallow pool no deeper than a fingernail over the root felt and pings. The room is a resonating instrument; it is loud. The sound of water is constant, amplified by hollow stems and by the very smallness of the space. With it comes the fauna — and there is abundant fauna, pressed shoulder to shoulder with the plants.

Pale slugs, translucent as ice, move across the leaves leaving silvery tracks. Blind, beetle-like things with shells like frost feathers crowd the undersides of rosettes, clicking mandibles. Worms rise and fall in the floor felt. In the pools sit frog-shaped animals with eyes beaded black, sitting very still, their throats pulsing. Small shrew-like creatures dart through the root mat, vanishing and reappearing. Midges and gnats fill the air in a thin but persistent cloud, thick enough that you breathe them at the edges of your mouth. Many of the animals carry plant on their backs — a leaf grows from a beetle's shell, a moss tuft from a slug's flank — and many of the plants are hollow and inhabited, with a tenant curled inside the stem. Here, fauna and flora are not two kingdoms. They are layers of the same dense mass, near-touching, many presences per cubic foot, so that to move you must push gently through a curtain of leaves and feel living things part around you.

The air is cold — cold enough that your breath fogs instantly and frost forms on your lashes, cold enough that the water on every leaf is a skin of slush, cold enough that plants crackle faintly as they take up water and expand. And yet nothing is frozen solid. The plants hold their own water against freezing with sugars and salts and structures that keep ice out of their cells in patterns, like venation made of glass. The atmosphere is condensing: every surface is beaded, the leaves sweat, the rock sweats, a mist hangs ankle-high and does not rise. It smells of wet iron, crushed green, cold stone, and something sweetly rotten that is instantly composted, for nothing here is wasted. A dead leaf falls into the felt and is consumed in days by fungi and worms and dissolved back into the mat. There are no built things. No path, no tool, no mark, no artifact left behind. Everything made here is grown, and everything grown is immediately reused.

The dim light is pooled and redistributed. Most of it comes from patches of phosphorescent lichen on the ceiling, faint as deep-water moonlight, and from within the plants themselves. Look closely at a leaf and it is not dull green. It has an iridescent sheen, a metallic, almost engineered glitter, as if microscopic mirrors are set in its cells in fractals too fine to see. Roots pulse, very slowly, with a pale electrical glow that travels from one side of the chamber to the other over the space of minutes. It is how the plants talk, how they move water uphill against the weep of the ceiling, how they ration light to the shaded layers below. They photosynthesize in this half-dark with an efficiency that should not be possible, catching photons and splitting them and rejoining them, conducting along their stems at a temperature where everything else would be inert. They repair torn tissue in an afternoon, fabricate antifreeze on demand, seal a slug's wound with living gel. It is a technology beyond anything built, but it leaves no structures behind because it *is* the structure. The place is engineered at the molecular level, patieneeringly, over evolutionary time, and the engineering is the plant.

That is the rule of this world: nothing separates. Water does not flow away; it is caught, held in the felt, transpired, condensed on the ceiling, and weeps again. Light does not belong to the top layer; it is harvested and shared downward through glowing roots. Sound does not die; it travels through wet leaves and hollow stems and the root mat itself, so a beetle clicking on the far wall is felt under your feet. Growth follows sound and moisture and the faint warmth of a passing animal, creeping toward it over seasons.

And it is slow. Achingly slow at the scale you can see. A fern in the corner will unroll a thumb's length in a year. A ceiling tendril will advance a hand's breadth before the next winter of the world above. The fracture in the corner will widen by a hair. The chamber will enlarge, imperceptibly, as root acids and frost and moving water pick at its edges. If you lived here you might never notice the change, except by your own footprints filling in. The loudness is not from flux. It is from density and resonance: drips, clicks, croaks, the faint creak of ice-laced stems expanding, the constant drumming of water on leaves in a room too small to absorb it.

Strange things happen if you stay still long enough. A leaf turns, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmth of your hand. A slug climbs onto a stem and stays put, becoming, for a while, part of its architecture. In the shallow pools, the surface tension holds bubbles of plant exhalation that pop in sequence like a slow alphabet. Some of the rosettes open and close not with light but with the rising and falling of the water table. Some of the hanging tendrils, on feeling the floor felt below them, thicken and harden into what looks like a new wall, and in three years there will be no way to tell where the rock ended and the plant began.

You are, in the end, not standing in a cave that has plants in it. You are standing inside a plant that has hollowed itself a room to live in, under cold ground, wet through, crowded with animals that are half plant themselves, lit by its own slow light and humming with its own slow water. It is small enough to hold in your arms, and dense enough that it contains a continent in miniature. It is still being born.
