The light is ordinary. That is the first strange thing about the top of the world.

It is plain, straight, midday light, the colour of a clear day anywhere else, falling without gold or grey on stone that has not seen a new edge in an age. The sun warms your shoulders. The wind, when it drops, is warm on your neck. No one with you to share the view, and no tree to stand under.

You are very high. Not on a peak in the usual sense — there is no point to climb to, no summit cross of rock. You are on a table. The plateau stretches in every direction farther than you can take in at once, gently domed, tilting with almost imperceptible patience toward an edge that drops away two, three thousand metres into a sea of cloud. Below that sea, occasionally, the tops of other tables rise like other worlds. They are far apart. So is everything here.

The stone is finished.

That is the word the place insists on. It is not young stone, shattered and sharp. There is nowhere to cut yourself. Every surface has been worn for so long by rain, by wind, by its own slow collapse, that it has settled into its final form: broad, bone-smooth slabs of grey and iron-red and pale pink, polished black where water has run thinly a thousand times, pebbles between them rounded as if by a river that never was. No fresh scar. No steep crack waiting to split. When you put your palm down the rock is warm from the sun and damp under the skin, though there has been no rain for an hour. The whole plateau sweats.

It was an old surface once, in a lowland, flattened by time. Then the land around it sank, or this block was lifted, and the old, eroded table was carried up intact. That is why it can be both extreme and ancient at once — extreme in height, ancient in face. It has stopped eroding, because there is nothing left to take off it that would change its shape. It is the end of a mountain’s life, still standing.

And therefore it is empty.

Between one thing and the next there is often nothing at all. Ten paces, fifty, two hundred of naked, warm, damp stone, humming faintly under your boots. The eye finds no bush, no outcrop worth the name, no track. The plateau feels hollow underfoot, as if a single vast shell, worn thin. Tap a slab with a knuckle and it does not clack; it rings, low and pot-like, and the tone travels outwards across the stone for a surprising distance before it dies. In the hollows beneath the slabs, rain that fell somewhere else is still travelling, drip by drip.

Then, without warning, the emptiness breaks.

Where the stone dips a finger’s breadth, where a basin has been worn to a shallow bowl and keeps what the sky gives it, life erupts as if it had been waiting under pressure. This is the other governing fact of the place: there is almost no free water, and what little there is, is enough for a riot.

There are no streams. There is no river cutting through. Water arrives as intermittent weather — a cloud tearing open for three minutes, condensation beading on the underside of stone, mist pulled up the cliff face and wrung out on the windward. It pools only in hollows, for a day at most, and then smells of iron and warm dust as it evaporates. It clings. It makes the moss black-green and swollen.

In those pockets the vegetation is lush to an almost tropical excess, absurd at this altitude. Ferns unroll broad as hands from cracks. Moss grows thick as a mattress, so soft your foot sinks and comes up green. Rosettes of fleshy leaves lie flat against the rock, spiralling — always, for some reason, clockwise — around the exact point where the stone sweats most steadily. Low shrubs have been beaten by generations of wind into cushions, dense and gnarled, studded with small white and magenta flowers that open only when the stone is wet. Leaf litter rots quickly in the damp and smells sweet and a little fermented, like crushed fern and warm stone after rain. You can walk for an hour over barren, polished rock and then step into a grove no bigger than your house, and be dripping from leaves that have no business being this soft, this green, this thirsty, so high and so dry between drinks.

The plants know the turbulence. None of them grows upright for long. Everything leans, flattens, re-roots. Roots thread into hairline cracks and hold by their own weight. Growth happens in bursts — a rain cell crosses, leaves drink, steam rises off the warm stone around them, the cell passes and the sun comes back and they close again. A curtain of weather is always moving across the table; you can watch it approach from a mile off as a dark smudge on the stone, hear it before it reaches you, and it reorganizes the whole plateau as it passes. Light, shadow, wind, rain, heat, in no settled order.

That is where the sound comes from, and it does not settle either.

At rest the plateau is loud in a way that takes time to understand. Wind follows the slope up from the cloud sea and pours over the edge, accelerating across the naked stone. Over the moss pockets it tears through the flat leaves with a roar like surf. Through the hollows under the slabs it finds the same worn holes that the ancient drips use, and makes them sing — a low organ note, then another, overlapping, never quite resolving.

When a storm builds over the high ground — and it is always building — it becomes deafening. Not thunder, though there is thunder, rolling continuously somewhere inside the cloud deck below the rim, more felt in the soles of the feet than heard. The deafening is the sum of everything at once: wind flattened to a single white noise, rain striking stone at a slant and bouncing back up, plants thrashing, loose pebbles skittering, and underneath it all the stone itself booming as if the whole table were a drum being struck from inside. You shout and cannot hear your own voice. Your thoughts have to wait until a gust passes. It goes on for minutes, then tears apart, then reforms a hundred metres away, and the echo of the dripping continues long after the rain has stopped, from stone that looks dry, as if the plateau were remembering water and releasing it slowly from deep inside.

Strange rules govern the moisture here. Condensation forms not only at night but in full sun, on vertical faces that should dry fastest, in perfect rings around the lush rosettes. Rain sometimes falls up, lifted by an updraft off the cliff so that droplets from a cloud below rise past your face and wet your eyelashes from underneath. The moss only grows on the windward side of stones, fed by wind-borne mist before any drop falls. The stone, when tapped, will answer a step with a vibration that you feel in your teeth if you stand still for too long.

Life, other than the plants, is almost unspeakable. In an hour you may see nothing with a face. Then, in a damp bowl loud with dripping, a beetle the size of a thumbnail, matte black, moving with slow deliberation through the moss. Or, high above, a single hawk is thrown sideways by the wind, screams once — a thin sound that is immediately swallowed — and is gone. That is all. The rare animals here are the ones that can use the noise rather than be erased by it. There is a flat, earless thing, like a piece of wet leather with legs, that lives in the hollow under certain slabs and only comes out when the rain drums hard enough to hide its own breathing. You see it twice, perhaps. You are not meant to.

The built things are rarer still, and primitive where they exist.

Moss has grown over most of them to the point that you could walk past without noticing. A cairn of three stones, hand-stacked, no mortar, each stone chosen round and placed with care, sunk now to its second course in green. A flaked blade — not metal, a hard dark stone worked by a few blows with a river pebble — wedged in a crack as if left for someone later. A shallow bowl hollowed out of a boulder by patient pecking, with a loop of frayed cord still tied through a drilled hole, meant to catch condensation drip. Someone, at some time, climbed this finished, roaring, empty height with simple tools and left marks that the plateau has since polished almost back to nature. There is no building. There is no path. The artifacts do not make a settlement; they make a point, perhaps, and then erode into the same ancient anonymity as everything else.

That is the feeling the place leaves you with as the light stays ordinary and the wind, for a moment, drops. The mountain is dead in its bones and alive on its skin. It has been worn to the last stable shape it will ever have — a warm, damp, ringing table above the clouds — and upon that final shape weather riots without ceasing. Between the lush, overflowing pockets, vast distances of polished stone say nothing. The sound never quite leaves. The damp never quite becomes wet. And somewhere under your boots, in the dark hollow of the old world, a drip is still falling, and falling, for a place that finished dying a very long time ago.
