It looks empty.

That is the first lie the place tells, and it tells it with the whole horizon. You are standing high — on a plateau so high the air is thin in your chest and the sky at zenith is almost black at noon — and the light comes down white and merciless. There is no haze to soften it. Shadows are short, sharp, knife-edged. Stone glitters.

And the stone is a long way from warm. The sun heats the grit under your boots until it stings, but the air that moves across it is cool, dry, and cutting. It has no smell of moisture because there is none to smell. There is no water here. Not a river, not a lake, not a seep, not dew, not rain. The gullies that cut the plateau were not cut by water. They were cut by wind and by dust and by the daily cracking of stone as it freezes at night and expands in the glare. For thousands of years the wind has been sculpting this place, and it has finished. The forms are settled, weathered, in-balance with themselves. Nothing looks young or fragile. Everything looks like it has survived a long argument with the sky and reached an agreement.

Between the presences, there is only that: mineral. Pale feldspar and quartz grit, iron-rust freckles, fields of cracked crust the color of bone. What soil there is, is not soil in any Earth sense. Dig with your hands and you find a thin, crumbling layer of mineralized plant litter, pressed and turned to something like ash and loam at once, over fractured rock. The ground itself is the residue of vegetation. Life here does not grow *in* the ground so much as the ground *is* what life leaves behind.

You can walk for an hour, for five, for a day, across that grit without meeting anything alive. The scale is continental. Weather is born here and dies here, over the span of a single plant, never reaching the next. The plateau rolls on until it becomes its own horizon, and if you climb a rise you see only more plateau, more light, more emptiness, and then — far off, softened by distance — the next silhouette.

That is the second truth. The presences are sparse because they are enormous.

Each one is a plant, and a plant is essentially the only kind of living thing there is in mass. Where the fauna exists, it exists in the shadow of these giants, using them, eating them, nesting in them, but it does not count for much against them. The plants are not forested. They are solitary. One per valley, one per ridge, one per thermal. Their root systems, invisible, probably run for kilometers under the crust, mining. Their crowns run for hundreds of meters across. When you finally reach one, you understand why there is so much space between them. They need it.

There are a few forms, repeated with variations across the whole expanse, as if the same few industrial designs were built again and again.

Tower-plants are the most immediate. A trunk like a hollowed silo, twenty, sixty, a hundred meters tall, fluted and buttressed at the base like a foundry chimney, its bark breaking into copper-red plates that curl away from the wind. The top opens, or splits, into a spray of needle-leaves, pale silver to reflect the light, so fine and dry they make a sound you hear long before you see them clearly.

Umbrella-plants are lower and broader. A short, swollen bole holds up a vaulted canopy — a true piece of architecture, though nothing built it. Under the vault, the trunk is hollow, the inside cool and surprisingly dark after the glare. The canopy is woven, not grown in a simple leafy mass: branches interlace, shed needles fall and are caught, new growth laces over old, until it is a dome with passages, perches, shaded chambers. Animals live in those chambers the way birds live in a cathedral.

Mat-plants never rise. They are ground-hugging continents of their own, a tough, gray-green crust that spreads in patches between the towers, cracking in the cold nights, healing in the day. They fill the space that the towers leave, in a low, stubborn skin. Together, tower, umbrella, and mat make up a vegetation that is total in the sense that there is nothing else to be alive *as*, and sparse in the sense that most of the world, at any given moment, is bare mineral between giants.

They live this way without water, and that is the rule that bends everything else here into its shape.

There is no liquid solvent exposed to the world. Precipitation, when it comes, is a dry fall of carbon-dioxide frost that sublimates straight back into the thin air before it can melt, dusting the plants white for an hour at dawn and vanishing by mid-morning. The plants do not drink. They take their hydrogen directly from the air and from bound minerals they crack open internally, their carbon from the same thin air, and they circulate, sealed inside themselves, a black, resinous oil — their sap, if you can call it that — that carries nutrients and cools their hearts and never touches the surface. Animals that live on them get what moisture-analog they need from eating the oily flesh and from the oil itself. Nothing here ever quenches a thirst. Everything here is eaten, breathed, or burned instead.

And that oil, and those hearts, is where the second lie unravels. It looks like a desert. It sounds like a factory.

Tech, here, was not invented. It evolved. Inside a tower-plant's hollow bole is a chamber that works exactly like a furnace, though no hand made it: mineral teeth grind stone to powder, catalytic pockets smelt iron and copper from the dust in their own gut, valves of hardened bark open and shut with a clank you can feel through your soles, and a slow, rotary pump — a spiral of living tissue — moves the black sap in a circuit, up in the day, down at night. Along the inside of the bark runs a conductive tissue that stores charge all day under that blinding light and discharges it in crackling blue arcs during storms. It is industrial metabolism, arrived at independently, scaled to a plant: production, mechanization, electrification, all internal, all unwalled. There are no buildings. There are no artifacts. Nothing made persists on the surface; the wind would strip it and the frost would split it. The only structures standing are the ones that grew, and they are structures because they had to be — vaults, silos, buttresses, ducts, chimneys — to do the work of a factory without ever building one.

That is why it is deafening.

You do not hear the silence of a desert. You hear, first, the wind — it never stops, it only changes key — moving across the plateau and then *through* the plants. Through a tower's needle crown it becomes thousands of flutes at once, a high, continuous screaming that rises and falls as the gusts rise and fall. Through an umbrella's vault it becomes a hollow boom, a resonant note that you feel in your sternum. The mat-plants crack as they warm, a sporadic popping like distant gunfire, and the old bark of the towers splits with a sound like falling timber, no one having touched it.

Under that, that is always under that, is the work. A slow grinding. A hiss of gas escaping a vent in the side of a bole. The clank of a mineral valve. The electrical tick-tick-tick of a charge building, building, and then the flat thunder of its release inside the plant during a storm, the whole trunk lighting faintly from within. The plants are not quiet organisms. They are loud because they are big, and because they are working all the time.

And the fauna, abundant where the plants are and absent everywhere else, adds its own layer. Wind-gliders with membranous, sail-like wings nest in the umbrella vaults by the hundreds and launch at dusk, their wings making a dry rustling like paper tearing en masse. Chitin-plated grazers — low, blunt, armored things that move like slow tanks — push through the mats around a tower's knees, rasping. Burrowers live in the drier litter under the mats, and at night the whole root zone clicks and chitters with them. All of them vocalize, not in song-forms but in clicks, whistles, and low, resonant hums that are amplified by the hollow plants they live in. Stand inside an umbrella at night and the sound closes around you: wind above, work within, animals all around, your own voice swallowed.

The dynamic of the place is turbulent in a way that has nothing to do with youth. This is an old, settled landscape, eroded into its final forms, and those forms are in a constant state of creation and destruction. The weather is never stable for long. Thermals rise off the hot stone and off the warm hearts of the plants, build into dust devils that run for kilometers stripping the crust, build into storms of dust and spore and CO2 frost that blot the white sun to a yellow glare for an hour and then tear apart. Growth comes in pulses after a frost-fall, violent and brief — a mat-plant can spread meters in a day, then die back to a hard scar for a year. Reproduction is explosive. When a tower sets seed, it does not drop it. It fires it. The crown bursts with a concussive crack, as loud as anything else here, and seeds like iron pellets are driven into the wind, which carries them across the emptiness to crack open a new patch of crust far away. Whole branches shed like falling factories. Whole canopies collapse under their own woven weight and are rebuilt, needle by needle, from the inside.

There is no water to soften any of this. No fire can be quenched, so the plants are mostly stone-like, waxy, fire-proof; their sheds are mineralized before they fall. The air smells of ozone after a discharge, of hot resin, of roasted dust — never of wet earth. At night the temperature drops fast, the frost comes, the plants contract and tick and groan as they cool, and the stars come out hard and bright in that thin, high black. In the morning the light returns, white and total, and the work begins again.

Walk away from a plant and the noise follows you longer than the sight of it does. The flutes thin, the grinding fades, and for a while you have the glittering empty grit, the cool air, the vastness that can make its own weather and swallow you without noticing. It looks like nothing lives here.

Then the wind shifts, and you hear the next one — its crown screaming through the light, its heart working, its gliders rustling in their vault — already shaping the distance, already making the empty place loud, industrial, alive, and utterly, strangely plant.
