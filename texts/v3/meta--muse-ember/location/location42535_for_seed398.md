It does not announce itself. There is no valley, no skyline from a distance. You find it, if you find it at all, by following heat upward.

The mountain is young. Not young in the slow sense — old rock worn soft — but young as a wound. An upthrust of black glass and fractured basalt, still heaving, still exhaling. Its cliffs have not been rounded. Every edge is a fresh break, a knife you can cut yourself on, a surface that rings when it cools at night and spalls off in plates. The wind at this elevation is thin and fast and almost without air to carry it; your lungs work too hard and your blood sings a little. Boiling point is down near what a kettle on the ground would call warm. The sun, when it hits the summit, is white and pitiless and the sky above it is already turning black at noon.

The pocket is set into that summit like a secret in the stone.

You come to it through a slit. A thermal chimney, a crack no wider than a doorway where hot gas still sighs out of the earth, no plants anywhere to mark it, no animal tracks in the dust, nothing alive on the slopes to give it away. Push through the glass teeth and the world narrows and then, impossibly, opens.

Inside is not inside. That is the first rule.

The cavity is perhaps three hundred meters across if you could walk it straight. You cannot. It is a single, contained volume embedded in the mountain, a trapped bubble of volcanic gas that was sealed between two new lava flows and never allowed to collapse. Something — someone, or something that builds after the manner of thought — holds it open with a field. Without that, the roof would come down in a day. With it, the pocket remains, pressurized, hot, and strangely still.

The air inside is not the thin air outside. It is made air, held. And it is damp.

Not wet like a river, not dry like a desert. Damp in the way a sealed room full of warm stone is damp. Steam gathers in low places and never quite becomes fog. On the coldest faces — and in this hot place there are faces that are only relatively cold, glass panes that shed heat faster than their neighbors — water beads, runs, collects in shallow pans, then evaporates again by afternoon of its own internal cycle. Every so often, for minutes at a time, it rains. Not from a sky. It condenses in tiers above you, in the vast empty volume overhead, and falls in a fine, warm, intermittent rain that vanishes before it reaches the floor lattice in most places, so that you feel it on your skin and hear the faintest tick on the glass, and find the ground only damp in hollows.

The ground is mostly not ground. It is a lattice.

The city is urban, unmistakably. Streets, terraces, districts, infrastructure layered upon infrastructure. But it is a city spread thin through emptiness, as if a settlement for millions had been dropped into a pocket meant for a handful, and the millions never came.

There are towers, but they do not stand the way buildings stand. They hover. A few millimeters above their foundations, held by a tension in space that current physics has no clean word for, they tilt almost imperceptibly toward sources of heat, they breathe with the thermal cycle, they phase — not disappear, but become less solid, the light going through them differently, so that a tower seen from one angle is opaque ceramic and from another is a lattice of cold fire. They are not built so much as grown, extruded grain by grain from sintered mineral and metal that was never forged or welded but precipitated out of a field, strand by strand.

And between them: nothing. Whole avenues cross sheer void on bridges no wider than a cable of forced light. Courtyards are cathedrals of empty air. A district will occupy a platform, then ten meters of black nothing, then a single spire, then fifty meters of nothing where a street should be but is only a line of dim way-markers hanging in the dark, waiting. The density is a hollow. You can stand in the geometric center of the city and see almost no city at all, only the far wall of glass curving away in dim amber, and heat-shimmer, and the occasional pearl of condensation sliding down a far pane.

No light comes from above. There is no above to see. The light in the pocket is dim, low, and interior — the color of deep twilight held permanently at bay from the blazing outside. It comes from veins in the walls where melt still runs a centimeter under the surface, from strips set into the streets that glow like cooling embers, from the slow pulse of the heat-exchangers in the Kiln Quarter, from dust. The dust is everywhere, a fine mineral haze that catches that light and makes it visible, and also sparkles on its own in places, briefly, from discharges too small to see.

Sound is mostly quiet, and deliberately so. The walls are tuned to drink sound; fresh glass cracks if it resonates, and the builders knew it. Thin air carries little. What you hear is therefore intimate: the tick of glass contracting, a soft hiss as a pocket of steam finds a vent, the occasional deep ping, like a stone dropped in a very deep well, when a tower shifts its field and settles. No wind, though the mountain howls outside — you can feel the pressure of it on the field, a faint tremor on the skin. No birds, no insects, no voices. The absence is not empty; it is managed.

There is no plant life. No green thing has ever rooted here, and none could. The rock is too new, the heat too constant, the light too dim and wrong. In its place there is a mineral ecology, and it is active.

Across the damp walls run crusts — not moss, not fungus, though they behave in patterns that recall both. Pale silica films precipitate where steam condenses and then re-dissolve the minerals they have gathered. In the Cistern Quarter they bloom into flat, rosette-like crystals that open and close with the humidity, not to photosynthesize but to eat heat and break it into smaller, usable packets. The maintenance swarms live in the walls like a mycelium, except they are not alive. They are nanoscale and mesoscale automata, webs of filaments that creep, exchange charge, dissolve a section of pipe here to build a bridge there. They do not reproduce. They persist. They pollinate, in their way, the city.

There is no animal life either. Nothing hunts, but something does something like hunting. Free-floating field predators unfold in the empty volumes between towers — slow, invisible things made of folded space and heat — and drift until a heat-brick, heavy with stored energy from the Kiln, pulses too brightly. Then they snap shut on that pulse and eat it, and go still again. The heat-bricks, in turn, are preyed upon only by them, and release their energy slowly otherwise to feed the crystal crusts. It is a predator-prey pace without teeth, without hunger in any biological sense, a cycle of excess and capture that keeps the temperature from climbing to sterilization. The whole pocket is in motion in this way: buildings tilting toward warmth, the lower lattice sinking a slow centimeter into the soft substrate at the base, construction fronts advancing across an empty lot mineral by mineral and then, months later, dissolving back when the field rebalances, steam weather rising and falling in a regular tide, light crawling along the transit threads at a speed that is almost, but not quite, the speed of light.

That is where the strangeness settles, not as a single trick but as a loosening of expectation.

Walk straight down an avenue and the street inclines upward, gently, as if the pocket's gravity is favoring a different down in each district. Rain in the northern grotto falls up for a few seconds before remembering it should fall down, and the droplets hang, confused, before they evaporate. Distances misbehave if you look away: two towers that were a long shout apart are, when you glance back, only a few steps, and the axis of the street between them has folded. Shadows do not quite follow. A lamp may brighten behind you and its shadow arrives a moment before the light does. In the Silent Exchange, the central hub, there is no traffic, but on certain thermal cycles the transit threads fill with running light, a ghost rush-hour that goes nowhere and arrives everywhere at once, then clears.

The builders — if builders is the right word for a post-human intelligence that builds by coaxing fields — intended this. Or they did not intend it and do not mind it. The city is urban in its completeness: water is sieved and recondensed a hundred times, heat is harvested and re-harvested, waste is a mineral the crusts eat, air is held and filtered through glass lungs. It is just a city that has no citizens, no plants, no animals, no day, no river, and yet sustains all of those functions in another medium, at extreme altitude, in a hollow that should not hold.

At the end of a cycle, when the steam rain has passed and the damp has settled again on the glass, you can sit on one of the lattice terraces and feel the heat come up through the stone into your thighs, warm and steady, in the dim amber light. The pocket breathes around you, very quietly. Somewhere above, in the vast empty volume between you and the roof, another tier of condensation is beginning to form, bead by bead, for the next rain that will almost reach the ground.
