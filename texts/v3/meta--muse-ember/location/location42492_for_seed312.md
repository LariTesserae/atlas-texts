Urrath is not a place you enter. You climb into it, and keep climbing until the lower country disappears behind you and you are left on a shoulder of stone that runs in every direction as far as weather allows you to see.

From below it looks like a wall, then like a serrated spine of grey and pale rust. From above, once you are up, it resolves into something stranger and more ordinary at the same time: a continental uplift, young and still hard. The ridges have not yet been worn into friendliness. They are blades and spires and fault-scarps split last season, in geological terms, and left standing. Cliffs are unsoftened, faces a fresh mineral pale where they have recently shed a slab. Scree has not settled into soil; it lies at the exact angle of repose, angular as broken crockery, ringing faintly underfoot. There is very little earth to hold anything. What soil exists has collected only in the lee of a boulder, behind a retaining wall, in the crook where a drip line has run for a thousand years.

It is cold in the way high places are cold, without exception. Not the cold of a winter — the cold of a climate. Even at midday in the short summer the wind off the ridge finds the neck and fingers, breath fogs, water left in a bucket overnight skins over in the shaded cistern-house. North-facing gullies keep their snow into the warm weeks, and the ground there never quite thaws. The sun is normal, generous daylight, often fierce at noon on thin air, throwing shadows hard and black behind every stone. At night the sky clears, stars are close and numerous, and the temperature falls through the floor of the day as if a trapdoor had opened.

And damp, but only just. This is not a wet country. No rivers of consequence cut it — a young, high, broken land sheds what little water it gets too fast. Water exists here as an intermittent, almost shy thing. Most rain arrives as wind-driven sleet that touches rock and vanishes, or freezes where it lands. More important is condensation. In the morning every leeward face of stone is fretted white with rime. Under every overhang, bead by bead, water gathers and by noon threads down in dark streaks, leaving the green-black lines of moss that are the only true green you will see for days. It collects in rock hollows no larger than a basin, in cracks, in the carved cisterns under a town plaza. Inside houses, the warmth of bodies and a low dung-fire meets cold stone walls and weeps, so inner walls are permanently damp at the corners, shirts dry slowly, and cunningly placed jars catch the drip for washing. People here do not waste a drop because there has never been enough to waste.

Because of that, vegetation is sparse and has learned to be patient. No trees. No forest, no grassland in any meadow sense. Lichen first: orange, sulphur-yellow, black, and a pale sage crust that spreads over boulders at a rate you can almost watch, given years. Cushion plants, tight as pincushions, wedged between stones where a little soil has lodged — moss campion, dwarf willow no higher than your ankle, a few bitter herbs. Moss only where the drips fall. In the deepest protected basins, behind terraces, there are plots of hard barley and roots, grown in imported earth, and patches of scrub willow no thicker than a wrist, cut again and again for fuel. It looks barren from a hundred yards. From ten yards it is crowded in its own way.

Because fauna is abundant, and that abundance is the paradox the place resolves every day. If plants are few, what eats? Mostly, small things eat lichen and seeds and each other, and they do it in numbers. The cliffs are black with nesting birds in season — ravens, eagles, swifts, snow-finches, ptarmigan that turn white on snow and brown on stone. Marmots whistle from scree and vanish into burrows at the same instant across a whole slope, as if one mind had decided. Pikas and hares and foxes and ibex work the crags, tame as deer in their confidence that no one is hunting them hard. Under every sun-warmed stone there are beetles, spiders, stoneflies; around every melt-pool in the brief thaw there rises a low cloud of midges thick enough to walk through. In the towns the abundance changes shape: pigeons in the eaves, rodents in the thick walls, dogs asleep in lanes, and dense penned herds of shaggy sheep-goats and small hairy cattle pressed shoulder to shoulder on flat roofs and in stone byres. The country is quiet not because it is empty but because so much of its life is small, ground-dwelling, or silent on its ledge. Calls carry at dawn and dusk and then fade quickly into stone.

That quiet is deepened by the build of the towns, and the towns are where density answers vastness.

For all the emptiness between, Urrath is urban through and through. There is not one city but a pattern repeated across the whole uplift, town after town a day or three apart, linked by paved stone paths marked with cairns and low rest-shelters, carrying caravans of pack animals the length of the plateau. Each town has chosen the same bargain: a sheltered basin, a lee slope, a place where the wind does not come straight. There they huddle.

A town here is not spread out. It is stacked. Houses of split slate and granite block, dry-stone or mudded thin, three and four stories high, share walls and lean on each other and on the rock face itself. Flat stone roofs are also the floor of the next terrace, also a drying yard for hides and dung-cakes, also a pen for goats. Lanes are a man and a half wide, paved with flat flags, turning sharply to break the wind, climbing by external stairs because no one pulls a cart through there. Small windows, thick walls, a single smoke-hole in the roof slab. Inside, everything is near-touching: sleeping platforms, looms, tools hung on pegs, smoked meat, jars, hides, people, a dog, a kid goat brought in from the cold. Heat is a shared resource, so bodies share rooms.

And it is organized. A town has its central plaza, and beneath it a great cistern roofed with slabs, fed by channels from the drip-lines above. A granary raised on stone pillars against damp and rodents. A communal baking oven, a smithy downslope where fire is safer, wards by family or clan, terraces walled and stepped up the slope for barley, each plot no bigger than a blanket, each retaining wall carefully rebuilt after a rockfall. The infrastructure is primitive — no wheel, no metal engine, no glass — but it is continuous, maintained, and old. Technology is hand technology: stone hammers, antler picks, copper awls cold-hammered thin, obsidian blades knapped from fresh volcanic veins where the young rock exposes them, wooden looms, sinew thread, pottery fired low and smoky. A house may take a generation to add its top story. A wall may be older than anyone who remembers its builder. Roof timber is so scarce that roofs are corbelled stone where possible, or turf cut from the moss-pockets, and every piece of scrub wood is counted like coin for small, hard-worked repairs.

You understand the rules of the place only by staying a while. Water is gathered, never spent. Shelter from wind is worth more than a view, more than flat ground, more than sun. Stone is abundant and wood is not, so you build in stone and burn dung and peat and scrub in that order. Nothing moves fast except birds, weather over a ridge, and the occasional rockfall. People move slowly, pack trains move slowly, seasons move slowly. The two seasons — long cold, short thaw — set a patient calendar: lambing and shearing, the weeks when melt-pools fill and insects swarm and herbs are cut, the long months when towns draw inward, mend tools, marry, tell stories, and wait. Lichen grows. Moss thickens down a drip line. A child grows up and learns the lanes by touch.

The light at evening is ordinary and beautiful in an ordinary way: sun gone behind the western blades, towns below lighting their tiny windows one by one, cooking fires smoked through roof holes, stars appearing fast in thin air. Sound contracts to the town: a boot on flagstone, a bleat muffled by a wall, a hammer struck twice and set down, dogs, the far-off whistle of a marmot that no one answers. No water rushes. No forest sighs. No machine hums. The vastness outside presses in, but it presses quietly.

It is a normal world, Urrath — no wonders, no magic, just physics and hunger and shelter held at arm's length by a young, cold, high land. Its wonder, if it has one, is how completely life has packed itself into the cracks: lichen on stone, birds on cliff, beasts on scree, and towns of stone-on-stone crammed into the lee of the wind, each one dense and self-contained and stubbornly persisting through a slow, damp, freezing long duration.
