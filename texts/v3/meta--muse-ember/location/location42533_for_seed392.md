It is low, and it is flat, and it extends in all directions until the light takes it away. Not a hill in it, not a natural rise. A made plain, valley-wide and then beyond the valley, laid out at almost sea-level and held there by hand. From any point you can see a long way — the horizon is clean and close to the ground — but what you see is never quite a thing in itself. Distances repeat. A cluster of walls and green crowns out there on the white glare will be, when you come to it, the same cluster again a little farther on, solid and empty.

There is no natural substrate. Nothing underfoot that was not shaped. The ground is a vast, hand-patted skin: straw-tempered clay tiles fired in shallow pits, mats of woven reed soaked dark, low basins and channels dug with stone and bone tools, walls of mudbrick stacked one uneven brick at a time. It all has tool-marks still fresh in it. Edges are young — razor-sharp corners on the water-basins, channel banks cut at right angles and not slumped, brickwork uncracked, unweathered. It looks as if it was finished yesterday and will look exactly like that tomorrow. Push a stick into the clay and water wells up at once, clear and cool against the heat, as if the earth is only a thin made crust over water close below the surface.

That water is everywhere, and it does not move. It stands in sheets a finger deep in the depressions, in the dug channels, in the big shallow pans between houses, perfectly still, perfectly reflective, without lapping or dripping or rippling even when you step in it. It sweats up through the tiles in dark wet patches that spread and do not dry. The air is saturated and hot. Condensation is a condition, not an event: beads form continuously on the undersides of leaves, on the insides of walls, on your skin, grow large over no time at all, and then shrink back into the surface without ever falling. Rain there is no rain, and yet everything weeps. Walls weep. The big clay water-urns sunk into the ground weep. The air smells of hot wet clay, of green leaf and warm reed and sun-baked stone that is always damp underneath. The heat does not lift or change. It is thick and even, the same over the open tile, under the canopy, in the shade of a wall — there is no cool place, only places where the brightness is greener.

The vegetation is lush to excess, and it is built. Nothing greened itself here. Every plant traces back to a hand-placed thing: a buried pot, a raised bed of packed earth inside a reed fence, a trellis of lashed poles, a channel of water aimed precisely at its roots. Trees are grown inside sunken urns and trained over frames until their canopies meet in high, dense umbrellas; vines are grafted and woven into walls and roofs; grasses and sedges are planted in ordered clumps inside the basins, rising in huge glossy blades. It has gone wild in appearance only. The lushness is shaped, grafted, coppiced, tended by primitive means into a second nature. And it is locked at its fullest: every tree is at once in flower and in fruit, every leaf fully unfolded and fully green, every seed-head ripe. Nothing ripens, nothing rots, nothing falls. Leaves do not stir. There is no wind to move them, and the air, for all its wet heat, is perfectly still.

Between the green, the made world is sparse. Conflicting with the luxuriance, most of the plain is empty. You can walk for a long stretch over bare tile and empty basins and walled enclosures that contain nothing but wet clay, before you come upon a presence: a lone hut of mudbrick and thatch, a granary on short stout legs, a cluster of three or four houses around a water-pan, a fragment of a wall that encloses a field of reeds, a line of empty channels going straight to the horizon and ending. No metal anywhere. No wheels, no machines, no fired roof-tile beyond the simplest brick. Everything was made with hands, with stone adzes, with fire, with woven fiber and hide lashings, and carried into place piece by piece. The scarcity of buildings is what makes the totality of the making so strange — there is almost nothing to look at, and yet there is nothing at all that is not made. Even the mounds that would be hills in a natural lowland are stacked earth inside reed revetments. Even the ponds are lined. Even the soil is a recipe.

Life is abundant, and it makes almost no sound. The shallows are full of broad-footed frogs half-submerged, motionless except for the slow blink, all of them blinking at inverse intervals. Fish turn in the clear sheet-water in small, exact circles, vanish under a leaf, reappear at the start of the circle. Turtles stand on the tiles with their necks out, white birds stand knee-deep at the edges of pans with one wing half-open, dragonflies hang at fixed points in the air like punctuation, lizards lie flat on the hot walls, insects cover the blossoms in such numbers that the flowers seem to breathe. Herons, frogs, fish, fowl, beetles, darting things under the water — the ecology is crowded wherever there is water and green. But the place is mostly quiet, a deep, damp quiet that feels like pressure in the ears. No croaking, no singing, no buzzing, no rustle of leaves, no drip of water, no splash. Occasionally you hear something that is not an animal: the faint tick of clay contracting in the heat, a soft suck as your foot leaves the wet tile, a distant, muffled thunk from a basin wall — each sound arriving once, from a direction slightly wrong, and then the quiet returns more complete.

The light is bright without a source you can find. It comes from everywhere at once, white and saturated, bleached by the wet sheen of everything. It does not change with time; there is no morning, no evening, no season. Shadows are soft, multiple, contradictory — a tree casts a faint shadow to the north and another to the south, and a third that falls upward across its own leaves. Reflections in the standing water are never simply reflections: the sky reflected is a deeper blue than the sky above, the canopy reflected is taller, and sometimes there is a reflection of a roof or a tree that is not standing there at all, standing just behind you. The same bent leaf, the same cracked urn, the same arrangement of seven bricks in a wall will recur every few strides, as if the makers had run out of variations and the place is now copying itself out of habit.

Time, in any ordinary sense, does not pass here. It is a frozen equilibrium, hot and wet and bright and perfectly balanced so that nothing need change. Water does not evaporate though the sun is fierce; it condenses and is reabsorbed. Plants do not grow though they are lush; they do not die though they are ripe. Animals do not age, or migrate, or die in place; their gestures loop in place, a wing half-beat endlessly half-beaten. Footprints in the wet clay do not dry, do not wash away, do not fade; they stay dark and sharp. The young, fresh edges of the basins do not soften. It is as if the world was very recently emerged — improbably recently — and at the instant of its emergence it arrived already teeming, already overbuilt, already in flower, and then stopped.

So you walk in a heat that sweats into you, over a ground that is all artifact and yet all wet, between scattered green clusters and long emptinesses, watched by silent abundant eyes, under a glare that never slants, in an air that condenses on everything and falls on nothing. The place is complete and unfinished at once: a handmade marsh at the lowest edge of the world, too perfect, too still, too bright — and for all its lushness and its teeming, almost entirely quiet.
