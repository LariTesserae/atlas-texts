You do not enter so much as you are let in by the ground splitting.

There is no door, no descent-shaft, no cave mouth worn smooth by use. This morning the crack was not here. Last night, in a single warm pulse of rain, the earth above tore cleanly down through root and leaf-litter and come-apart soil, and left a fresh, sharp-edged slit you can slip sideways through. The edges are still white. Broken root-wood splinters point like cut quills. Nothing has crumbled yet, nothing has rounded. The erosion is so young you can see the fibers torn, the sap still beading, the soil itself un-decided.

Below, it opens — not into a cavern. Into a room.

A pocket. Intimate, hidden, tucked inside the earth under a place that above is all green canopy and sun. Down here you could stand with your arms out and almost, almost touch the far walls, except that when you look, the far walls are also not close. Space between things is the main material. The chamber is mostly nothing — damp, warm air, ordinary daylight, and a long, hollow hush between presences. A few stems. A few leaves. A mat of floor. The distances from one to the other stretch when you aren't watching them.

That is the first rule here: it is empty, but not small.

The floor is not earth. It is plant.

Everything is plant. That is not a figure of speech. You press your palm down and it is not stone, not loam, not clay. It gives — warm, slightly sticky, faintly pulsing. A weave of pale roots and damp hairy stems, living and dead braided so tightly they have become substrate. The walls are the same weave drawn up. The ceiling is the underside of a single enormous leaf, or of many leaves lapped like shingles, translucent enough that the light comes through exactly as light should come. Normal light. Noon-colored, shadow-casting, clean white-yellow daylight falling straight down as if from a sky. There is no sky. There is only that leaf-filter above and the green-dark earth around it, and still the light arrives correctly, pooled in the right places, bright on your hands.

It is warm down here in the way a greenhouse is warm at midday, trapped and sweet. The air smells of crushed chlorophyll, of warm sap, of mushrooms that never quite rot, of rain on hot leaves though no rain has come in hours. Everything is damped by intermittent moisture. There is no river, no spring. Water appears in pockets and vanishes. Condensation sheets the leaf-ceiling and gathers into beads that swell, tremble, and fall — not as a steady drip but as a brief, sudden rain, a dozen drops at once from nowhere in particular, then nothing for a long minute. Leaf-cups catch it. The root-mat holds a film that shines and then is gone. A single puddle in a hollowed stem will be there when you look away and not there when you look back, and vice versa. The plants drink first.

And all of it moves. It is not a still underground place. The dynamic is constant: a slow, vigorous metabolism you can almost watch.

Roots heave, minutely, under your feet, up and down like breathing ribs. A shoot near the wall uncurls a leaf in slow motion, the tip turning, turning, reaching for the light that is already on it. Sap travels visibly in a translucent stem, a clear pulse every few seconds. Spores drift in the void between stems in a fine weather of their own, rising when it warms, settling when it cools, cycling hourly as if this little room had seasons compressed into it. New fibers are always reaching across the fresh crack above, trying — visibly, hungrily — to stitch the chamber shut again.

It is loud.

You expected stillness, depth, muffling. Instead the room talks and pops and drums. The growth itself cracks — a sharp *tok* from a stem as it lengthens, a long creaking sigh as a root shifts its weight. Water hits leaf and booms with a hollow note out of all proportion to the drop. Wind that should not exist down here moves through the hollow stems and makes them flute and whistle and hum, one low note that never quite resolves. Overhead, unseen, leaves rustle continuously, a dry crashing like surf, even though there is no wind. The sound comes before its cause by a fraction of a second. You hear the drip land, then see it fall.

That is where the world begins to come apart.

The weirdness is not decoration; it is structural. You stand on a floor that is also, in the far corner, the ceiling — the root-mat curls up and over and continues, and plants grow from it in both directions, toward you and away from you, which is also up and down. A seed let go from your hand does not always fall. Sometimes it hangs, deciding, then drifts sideways toward a stem as if tugged by attention. Light falls sideways in the southeast corner, casting a shadow that lies flat on the wall. Your reflection in a wet leaf blinks a half-second after you do.

Distance misbehaves. Reach for the far stem and your hand crosses meters of empty air that a moment ago looked like centimeters. Turn your back and it is close again. The center of the room is both a void — you could lose yourself in the space between two leaves, the air going dark and leafy and far — and a clear, sunlit clearing where a single green shoot stands alone, thriving in nothing, feeding on light and damp and silence. Predator and prey are reversed here anyway: the plants are the active hunters. Vines grope, rootlets test, leaf-cups open like tiny mouths at the rain. Anything small that falls into the mat is slowly, politely taken.

Almost nothing animal lives here. Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. You may go the whole stay and see none. Once, if you are lucky and still, something pale and blind and slow will uncurl from a curled leaf in the damp corner — a larva, a slug without a shell, something that breathes once every minute — and regard you with no eyes at all, and then go back to being plant-matter again. Its presence is registered more by the plants leaning a fraction toward it, as if listening, than by its own movement. Otherwise there are only signs: a faint scrape, a silk thread that is not there when you touch it.

And there is, impossibly, a trace of tool-use. No building. No structure. No fabricated thing larger than your hand. But on the cleanest part of the root-floor, where the light pools, there is a single flake of knapped stone, sharp, recently struck, and beside it a short length of vine fiber tied in a simple, competent knot, and a cup or two hollowed out of a thick stem, worn smooth inside as if from use. A score-mark, three lines cut into the living wall, still green at the edges. Whoever made them left them and did not stay, and did not build. The primitive work sits in the middle of the total plant world like a punctuation mark, already being overgrown at its edges by hairs and rootlets feeling their way across it.

That is what makes the ecology coherent: nothing here is separate. The stone will be lifted by a root in a season. The knot will be eaten as sugar. The walls will heal over the entrance crack and the room will become sealed again, but not the same room — a new crack will open elsewhere, fresh and sharp, and the pocket will migrate a hand's-width under the earth, always hidden, always warm and damp and lit. Dead plant does not rot here; it becomes floor, becomes wall, becomes leaf again directly, without the intermediary of decay. Growth is the only recycling.

Stay long enough and you will learn its other rules, because it teaches them by doing them to you. The light follows the plants, not the sky; stand where no leaf reaches and you will be in a very ordinary darkness even though daylight is inches away through the ceiling. Water arrives when the air is most still, and leaves when the plants are drinking hardest. Sound is how the plants coordinate — the cracking and humming are not byproducts, they are the weather, the pollination, the warning. You feel it in your teeth if you put your ear to the floor-mat, which you will, because there is nothing else to do in the vast empty between stems except listen to the substrate thinking out loud.

When you leave — when the crack above narrows, as it will, the reaching roots already meeting — the last thing you notice is how normal the room looked from inside: warm, sunlit, green, damp, alive, and mostly empty. And how wrong that normality is. A whole world made of plant, buried, under ordinary light, growing so loudly and so fast in a hollow made yesterday, where distance stretches and drops fall upward and almost nothing with a face ever lives, and a single primitive tool lies waiting to be reabsorbed.

It will close. It will open again somewhere else.
