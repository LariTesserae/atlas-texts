From the rim, the first thing is how little there is, and how clearly you can see it all.

It is low ground, a shallow bowl no more than a mile across, cut into a plateau and still rising. The rim is not old. It stands up in straight, fresh scarps, knife-edged plates tilted at inconsistent angles, the breaks still clean and pale where the stone split. There are no rounded pebbles here, no soil softening the edges. Everything looks new and unfinished, as if the earth simply cracked and lifted here a little while ago and has not yet learned to wear itself down. You can take in the whole place at once from one point on the edge. Its boundaries are everywhere: that low ring, the far wall of the bowl, the place where the cracked floor stops and the rise begins.

The light never really arrives. The sun is a smeared disc behind a permanent high haze of fine mineral dust, and the light that falls is dim, cool, and diffuse, with long, pale shadows that creep almost imperceptibly. It is not night, and it is not day. Morning and afternoon differ only by a slight thinning, then thickening, of the grey. In the depths between the structures it is always a kind of dusk. The air is cool even where it is lightest, and colder still in shade, as if the cold pools and stays there. It carries no smell of water, because there is none. There are no streams in the dry channels, no rain, no lakes, no wet ground anywhere. What falls from the sky, in slow veils when the wind changes, is dust — mica-fine, chalky, faintly glittering — and it settles and settles, and the world is a dry mineral desert under a dim sky.

Walking down onto the floor, the ground gives underfoot in a strange way. It is made of overlapping plates, chalk-bone in colour with veins of slate-blue and rust that has gone pale, all fractured into sharp polygons. Between plates are fresh cracks, some only a finger wide, some wide enough to step into, their walls unweathered. In places the plates have heaved a hand's breadth higher than their neighbours, so the whole surface is a field of small, abrupt steps. Dust fills the lowest cracks in a soft grey felt, and that felt is everywhere — on the ground, on sills, on the insides of walls — swallowing sound.

That is the first rule of the place, and it governs everything else: it is silent. The silence is not like the silence of an empty room. The dust rivers move, the plates shift, the buildings grow, and none of it makes a noise you can hear. Your own steps land without a crack or scuff. A shard that falls from a high wall turns over in the air and lands and does not tick. The porous stone, the felted dust, the dry air — sound dies within a few feet, eaten by the materials. If you listen very hard you can catch, once in a while, the faintest tick of cooling stone, a sound so quiet it feels like a thought, and then nothing again.

It takes a while, walking inward, for the pattern to declare itself.

The standing things are not hills. They are built — and yet nothing here was built with tools. There is no metal, no glass, no wire, no machine of any kind. The structures are layered construction in the most literal sense: walls built up in rings and strata, like sediment laid down deliberately, hollow towers, terraced houses, galleries that run horizontally between higher blocks, causeways and bridges that cross the dry channels, wide plazas paved in the same cracked plates, stairs that climb to doorways that have grown shut. It is a town, unmistakably, at settlement scale, with streets that curve and loop and meet at small squares, with a denser core and looser edges. But the builders were not hands. They were the sparse grey-green things that live on the ground and on the walls, root-mats that secrete stone as they grow, sealing it into wall after wall. The city is a colony that grew geometric.

And it is an empty city, in a way that makes the geometry sadder. The density of life is almost nothing. Between one tower and the next there may be a vast bare plaza, a hundred paces of naked plate with a single low rosette of vegetation in the middle, a street that runs straight and empty until it bends out of sight. Buildings stand alone, leaning very slightly inward, as if the whole town were built to face itself rather than the outside world. The avenues loop back on themselves. The roofs are open to the dim sky, the interiors hollow and dark and cool. Everything that was made to hold or gather or pass through holds nothing now. The infrastructure is intact, layered, organized — and vacant.

The vegetation is sparse because everything here is sparse. In the dust felt, in the cracks, on the north sides of walls, grow pale ferns of stone, low rosettes, and creeping mats that look more like lichen than plants. They are grey-green, almost colourless in this light, and they never hurry, except that they are, in their own timescale, always moving. A mat can cover a doorway in a season. A patch of rosettes can shift its centre across a plaza over years, leaving a faint trail in the dust. They do not drink water. They drink the air itself — the dust-laden, cool air — and draw salts from the stone, and in return they lay down the stone of the buildings. They are both plant and mason, and so the city is slowly being both built and eaten at once: one mat raises a new wall a ring thicker while another mat foams on a wall and softens it back into dust. Buildings swell a fraction in the warmest part of the dim cycle — growth so slow you would never swear you saw it — and then, where a plate has heaved beneath them, they split with a clean, fresh crack, sharp enough to cut a hand, and the crack fills in after with new rings.

Animals are rarer still. You could walk the whole town and see none. When they come, they come as part of the same slow traffic. There are the pale walkers: tall, thin, grey things with long legs and skin like stretched leather, that cross the plazas and avenues at the dimmest light, pausing between buildings as if listening to something you cannot hear. There are the wall-crawlers, flat discs with many feet, that live in the fresh cracks and eat the lichen off the stone — the only true grazers here. And there are gliders, cape-backed, that leap from one gallery to another and ride the cool air down a street, rarely, seldom twice in the same day. The walkers hunt the crawlers, and the whole chase is stretched across weeks. Predator and prey move through the same streets at a pace that feels like a memory of movement.

And yet, for all that emptiness and silence, the place is never still. That is its second rule. It is active everywhere, constantly, in movements that make no sound.

The dust does what water does on other worlds. In the deep dry channels that cut the floor, slow rivers of fine powder flow, sinuous and deliberate, eroding the banks into new sharp bends, carving fresh cutbanks, depositing fans of pale silt at the junctions. A channel that was a straight crack five years ago is now a meander. The frost — not of water, but of salt — blooms on shaded walls in the coolest hours, white crystals feathering out and then collapsing back into dust under the slightly stronger light. Everywhere, plates are still lifting, a millimetre at a time, tilting the streets, cracking a wall, opening a new crack for the crawlers. The vegetation creeps, the buildings thicken and split, the dust rivers reroute. The whole small bowl is in motion like a slow machine with no moving parts you could point to.

The weirdness is not in any single monster, but in the accumulation. A city built by plants, with no tools, that faces inward. A sky that is forever dim but never dark. Movement without sound, growth without change of form, streets that are still being laid down in fresh stone while old streets it has outgrown are quietly unmade into dust. Shadows that barely move while powder rivers run. Life that lives on air and makes architecture out of itself. A place that looks brand new and abandoned at the same time, because its erosion is young and its buildings have never been weathered — only ever re-grown.

Stand in the centre of one of those vast plazas, in the dim cool light, and the scale of it settles on you. A whole town, a whole ecology, a whole set of strange quiet laws, contained in one small bowl you could walk across before your legs tired, with miles of empty space inside it between the few living things, and everything — stone, plant, dust, animal — moving slowly, silently, in its own direction, as if it had all the time in the world. Which, here, it does.
