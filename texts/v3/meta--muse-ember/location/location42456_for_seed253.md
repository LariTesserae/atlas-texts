There is ground, but it is not land.

You arrive into it in the dark, already wet, already inside the sound. Before you can see anything clearly, it is full: a low, surrounding roar that does not rise or fall, frogs tuned like pipes in the water, fish popping under the floor, drips striking leaves and stone and deep water in a steady drill, birds crying somewhere in the canopy overhead, insects humming in layers that never thin out. It is loud the way being inside a shell is loud. You could listen for an hour and not catch where one noise ends and the water itself begins.

It is cool everywhere. The air has a thin, high pressure to it that makes your ears ache when you step out of the water and into the gaps between pools. The water is colder. It numbs your feet within minutes and stays numb. Mist hangs in everything — not as weather passing, but as the air itself, saturated, breathing wetness in and out so slowly you never notice it change. Your breath fogs. Leaves drip on your shoulders. Water runs in films down the walls and shines, black and tea-colored, in the channels.

The light never really comes. It is a permanent green-black gloom, as if you are at the bottom of a forest and at the bottom of a sea at the same time, and far above the world. Details resolve only a few paces away: a leaf the size of a hand, the slick edge of a step, the pale underside of a lily. Beyond that, shapes blur into dark. There is no sun to follow, no day to wait for. The plants are built for this — broad, thick, waxy leaves held up to catch almost nothing.

What you walk on is made.

Once your eyes adjust, the right angles appear. Broad, flat steps spread in concentric rings, softened at every corner by age until they have slumped into themselves. Long straight causeways run across the water, too wide and too straight to be natural, their edges held by low retaining walls of black stone blocks. Rectangular basins, circular pools, rows of pillars standing far apart in the water, broken arches whose tops have grown together. Even the “hills” are mounds built up and planted over, root-mat soil laid on decking. No rock is exposed in its natural shape. No earth is unworked. Under the moss, the algae, the roots, everything is construction — and everything has been worn to its final, ancient form. The stone is polished smooth as river pebbles, the corners gone, the cuts blurred. This is not a ruin falling apart. It is a place that finished falling a very long time ago and settled.

Water has finished it, too. The steps are flooded. In the wide rings it is ankle-deep, crawling over woven root-decks that spring faintly under your weight. In the channels it is waist-deep and black and still. Pools go down into darkness where the built walls continue below what you can see. The water does not flow in any direction you can determine. It laps, barely, against the causeway legs. It fills the basins to a line marked by a darker band of moss on the stone, and that line is the same on every bowl, on every pillar, along every step. It does not rise after rain. It does not fall in dry spells. There is no tide, no current, no season to move it.

And the plants are not *in* this place. They *are* the place.

Vegetation covers everything that is made, and then becomes something else. Root weave forms walls and floors and the springy skin of the paths. Moss and fern armour the stone so water cannot take it any further. Broad water-leaves mat the surface of the shallows in scattered patches, leaving long stretches of open black water between them. Paler, larger leaves spread far overhead in places, interlocking into a low vault that keeps what little light there is from ever reaching the pools directly. Tree-like growths rise where pillars once stood, their trunks wrapped around stone, their roots spliced neatly into the terraces as if the builders and the plants agreed on the plan. The plants drink the flooded steps. The steps hold the water for the plants. The roots hold the steps together so the whole mountain cannot wash away.

Density is an emptiness between presences. For fifty, a hundred paces you may have only water and decking and the sound. Then a clump of vaulting leaves will appear, a row of pillars, an island of lilies with frogs blinking on them. The life is abundant, but it is not everywhere at once. It is under the decks, in the deep channels, in the canopy. Your legs brush schools of fish that scatter with a soft pop. Eels slide through the root-work beneath the causeways. Turtles surface, slowly, in the far basins. In the dark above, birds pass overhead in flocks you hear more than see, their wings thick in the wet air, their calls crossing and recrossing in that constant chorus. Beetles and midges work the undersides of leaves in clouds so dense they darken the gloom locally, then end just as abruptly. Nothing is visible on the long open water except your own ripples — and those close again at once.

The scale keeps opening as you move. The rings do not end where you expect them to. One bay of steps gives way to another identical bay, then another. Causeways run out into darkness and come back. Far off, across a man-made lake wide as a valley, you can make out another set of arches, another vault of leaves, at the limit of sight. It is regional, then more than regional — a whole terraced system extending past the immediate, as far as the dark allows.

And then, at the outer rim, it stops — and the height hits you.

The last step is a lip. Beyond it there is no shore. The built wall drops away and below you, thousands of metres below, is not a valley but a sea of cloud, slow and grey in the half-light, with other dark summits rising from it like islands. The air up here is thin and cold on your face. The water behind you is held up at this impossible height by the terraces and the roots, a sky-lake system perched on top of the world. You stand at the edge and feel vertigo in your stomach, not from falling — nothing falls here — but from the sheer amount of empty space under a place that is so full of water and plants and sound.

That is the strangeness of it, and it is only a slight strangeness. Everything is too balanced. The animals do not wander. Fish keep to the channels under the decks. Frogs keep to the same lily patches. Birds follow the same invisible lines between the vaults. Plants grow exactly to the edge of the stone and no further, as if they remember the plan. A leaf falls and becomes mat in a night, but the mat is the same mat as yesterday. Nothing rots into smell. Nothing grows within sight. You could mark the waterline today and find it in a year, in ten years. The dripping makes the same pattern on the same leaves. The chorus never pauses for breath.

There are no tools here, no boats, no fires, no metal, no signs, no trace of the makers except the making itself. No one maintains it. It maintains itself: water held by stone, stone held by roots, roots fed by water, leaves feeding the water, animals pruning and pollinating and stirring and eating the dead before they can decay, waste sinking into the root-decks to feed what made the decks. A closed, cold, dark, flooded architecture, ancient and finished, loud and still at once.

You are not passing through a landscape that is changing. You are inside one that has arrived, long ago, at the last form it will ever take — and is staying there, submerged, planted, built, and humming in the dark at the top of the world.
