At first it seems there is only the plain, going on in all directions at the same low level, wet and cold and grey.

You walk it without climbing anything. There is no hill to speak of, only a shallow syncline in a bog that has spread itself out over stone. The water is never far down. It comes up through the peat in black pools that reflect nothing, it sheets across the hummocks as a thin film, it beads on your boots and your sleeves and then runs, it rises in the air where your breath is. Everything is damped. Even the dry tops of tussocks are soft. The ground is new ground — it has not settled. 

Young ground announces itself in the small fractures underfoot. The peat is thin here, split in plates that tilt and show fresh, sharp edges of black basalt underneath. The stone has not been worn down yet. Where it breaks, it breaks clean, with glassy faces and corners that cut. There are no rounded boulders, no smoothed hollows, no thick soil to soften the fall. Every rain cuts a new little rill, every frost wedges a crack a fraction wider. At night you can hear the rock tick as it cools. It is terrain still in the act of being made.

You do not find the place by rising to a viewpoint. You find it by sinking.

There is a place where the plain dips, almost imperceptibly at first, and the rim of that dip is a broken ring of those fresh black teeth. From outside it is nothing — a darker patch in the bog, a place where the sedge grows a little taller. From inside, the world closes. It is a pocket, a bowl perhaps forty paces across, tucked into the larger wetness like a room that has been left off the plan. Once you drop through a slit in the ring, the plain is gone from sight and sound, replaced by walls.

The walls are not high. They are low, perhaps the height of a man, but they lean in. They weep.

That is not a metaphor. The basalt is colder than the air and the air is saturated, so water condenses on every vertical face and runs down in threads that never cease. It drips from ledges to ledges, from stone to peat to the surface of the pool below, in a steady, irregular percussion. It forms on the undersides of overhangs and falls, collects at the tips of moss, gathers on your face when you stand still. The whole bowl exhales condensation. In the dim light — for the light here is never direct, it is filtered through a low, northern overcast and the mist that pools in the hollow and the shadow of the rim itself — the falling water catches and glints like beads on a loom. Everything drips, even when it is not raining. Especially when it is not raining.

Inside, it is mostly empty.

That is the first surprise, and the second. For all the wetness and all the life you will soon hear, the volume of the bowl is void. There is space between presences. A sheet of still, dark water occupies the centre, so large and so undisturbed that it looks empty until a midge lands. Around it, tussocks of sedge and moss are spaced far apart, like islands in a sea, each one ringed by open water and open air. The mist hangs in the middle, low and thick, churning slowly. There is nothing built here, nothing placed by hand — no path, no bridge, no wall, no footprint that lasts. The water erases. The very softness of the peat swallows a step. You are the only made thing in it, and you are not made to stay.

And yet, because of the emptiness, the things that *are* here are impossible to miss.

The vegetation is lush, but it is a cold lushness. Not jungle-thick, but saturated. Carpets of sphagnum moss, deep and quaking, green shading to rust-red at the tops where the light touches. Clubmosses creeping in tight, bright spirals. Sedges standing in dense brushes that shiver. Bog myrtle and dwarf birch in low, wet thickets along the rim, their leaves always beaded. In the acid, iron-stained shallows, sundew spreads its sticky stars, and bog cranberry runs. It grows in lines, sometimes — along the flow-lines of water as it slips from rock to pool, the plants have colonised in rows, as if planted. The green is relentless. It is the green of things that never have to live with drought.

The fauna makes up for any loneliness in the air.

You hear them before you see them, and the sound is industrial in its loudness. A chorus of frogs in every tussock, in every shallow margin, croaking with a rising, revving insistence that turns into a constant, deafening wall. Underneath that, the dripping, which in the bowl acts like a hammer on a metal drum. Water hitting rock, rock amplifying water. A snipe somewhere bursts up with a *whup-whup* of wings and a bleating call that sounds like a steam release. Ducks move across the pool in pairs, low and white-chested, landing with a rush, taking off again. Midges hang in clouds over the warmest patches, thick enough to bite through mist. Water beetles stitch the surface, dragonflies patrol in short, darting circuits, and in the moss, tiny mammals run their tunnels. Abundant — you count them as you stay, and there are always more — but never crowded. A frog per tussock, a bird per territory, insects per vent. Space is left between them, deliberately, like workstations on a floor.

Because at the centre of the bowl, under and around the mist, the work is being done.

This is where the place reveals its true nature, and why it feels, despite having no factory, no engine, no artifact of any kind, entirely industrial.

The ground here is not still. It breathes, and it produces.

Under the peat, the young earth is still hot in the chemical sense, still fermenting. Peat rots without oxygen and makes gas. Iron, dissolved in the cold water where the basalt has cracked, is carried up and breathed by bacteria. Whole colonies of iron-oxidizing microbes live in the springs, laying down their rust in thick, ochre mats and hollow pipes, pumping and precipitating metal at a scale that, in a human world, would require a smelter. They build chimneys of rusted mineral, they coat everything they touch in orange scale, they consume and excrete in a continuous, mechanised rhythm. Hydrogen sulfide bubbles up in silver-black water and stains the air with rotten eggs. Carbon dioxide, heavy and invisible, pools along the surface of the pool and in the mist, hissing out in regular pulses from vents that open and close in the peat like valves.

Every few seconds a vent will sigh — a soft, powerful exhalation that ripples the water, flattens the mist, and sends a small eruption of gas and spray upward, as if a piston has fired underground. Then it falls silent, builds pressure, fires again. The water in the bowl is pushed up by this pressure, forced to the surface; it cools, it sheds its gases with a fizz, it sinks again and is pushed back down to be charged. It is a cycle without pause, a conveyor: water up, gas out, iron down, peat compressed, heat retained. The moss, in turn, filters and acidifies and traps. The sundew traps midges and digests them for nitrogen the bog cannot give. The frogs eat the midges. The snipe eat the frogs. Everything is in motion, nothing is ornamental.

This is the rule of the place: nothing is built, but everything is manufactured. The cold is part of the machine. Because it is cold, the water holds more gas, the peat rots slower but more completely, the mist does not lift. Because it is low, the water never leaves. Because the rock is young, the cracks are fresh conduits. Because it is hidden, the gas is not dispersed by wind quickly enough to thin; it gathers, it fuels the next pulse. Freeze and thaw work the basalt daily — ice forms in the fine fractures at night, expands, splits off a new shard, which falls into the pool with a plink that joins the chorus. Rain, when it comes, does not flood so much as add to the pressure in the system, making the vents fire more often, making the frogs louder.

The light stays dim through all of it. Morning here is a paler grey, noon is a slightly brighter grey, evening arrives early in the shadow of the rim. You see things by their wetness, by the way they hold water. The rusted pipes shine. The moss shines. The ducks' eyes shine. Shadows have no edges.

You could stay a long time and the bowl would not fill with clutter. Leaves that fall are drawn into the current, pulled toward the vents, stained orange, sunk. Bones are found, cleaned by beetles, sunk. The only accumulation is mineral: white sulfur crusts on the vent rims, black slag-like deposits where the iron bacteria have built too thick and collapsed, glassy shards of fresh basalt accumulating at the base of the weeping wall. It makes its own waste, and it makes it at volume.

When you finally climb back through the slit in the ring, the plain takes you again, flat and wet and apparently endless. The sound falls behind you quickly. The pocket is hidden not by distance but by depth and by mist. A bird crossing at height would see nothing. A walker ten paces away would see nothing.

But underneath your boots, the machinery continues — dripping, hissing, croaking, pumping, rusting, freezing, thawing — in a single, contained, cold, wet room of earth that was never intended to be inhabited, and is therefore perfectly, industrially, alive.
