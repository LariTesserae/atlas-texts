It is first of all high, and vast, and quiet.

You are standing on a plateau so large it has weather of its own, weather that begins and dies somewhere in the middle of it and never reaches an edge. Below you, far below, is a sea of cloud that does not rise. Above you the sky is a flat, dim slate. The sun is present but low and diffused, as if seen through wet glass, and it never climbs high enough to make a hard shadow. The light is silvery, even, and cold. Colors are muted to their essentials: grey stone, grey air, rust-orange, black-green lichen, and, rarely, a faint electric blue under skin.

The cold is not violent. It is patient. It sits in the stones. Your breath fogs and clears. The ground is permafrost under a thin skin of grit, and nothing about it hurries. A season here is a slight change in the dampness of lichen, a slight paler tint on a north-facing slope.

Everything has been eroded long ago into its final form. There are no fresh cliffs, no new scree, no sharp edges to cut your hands. The ridges are whale-backed, the hollows are smooth bowls, the channels where water goes are shallow swales worn into stone over a timescale that makes a human lifetime feel like a pause. The landscape looks settled, in balance with itself, as if it finished happening a very long time ago and is now only continuing. Even the stones have a patina. They have been rained on, frozen, sweated on, and polished by wind and by passing bodies until they flow a little at the edges.

There is very little water, but there is damp everywhere.

No rivers cut this place. No lakes with shores. Water lives as condensation and as pockets. At night, moisture gathers on the north side of every stone and on the flanks of animals and, in the morning, drips off in a slow, countable way — one drop, a long pause, another. Mist sits for days in the smooth hollows, not churning, just lying there, and then lifts an inch higher. Under the lichen mats the ground is always slightly wet to the touch. In a few deep hollows the melt has collected into ice lenses, flat and opaque, like eyes in the stone. They do not move. If you put your palm down you feel cold damp through the dry dust. The air itself carries the moisture; it is why sound does not carry, and why the light stays dim.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of being a rumor, but it is foundational. There are no trees, no shrubs, no meadows. There is crustose lichen that breaks down stone, black and ochre and pale sage, in irregular plates. There are cushion mosses no larger than your hand, tucked into cracks. There are tufts of a stiff, wiry grass, three or four blades, every few meters. Between them is stone, and more stone, and distance. Where the lichen is thickest it becomes almost a continuous skin, damp and spongy, and that is the real soil of the place. Everything else feeds from it.

Density is low. You can walk for a long time — hours, if you want to count — across perfectly legible ground with nothing in it but those tufts and the drip of condensation, and the knowledge that the horizon is not a horizon at all but the curvature of the plateau continuing. Emptiness is not absence here. It is a material, balanced with presence.

And then, slowly, the presence arrives. Because the fauna are overwhelming.

Not in the sense of a swarm pressing close. In the sense of dominance. They are the largest, most numerous, most consequential living things here, and the plants are, by comparison, a minor substrate. The fauna are scattered — one great body, then a hundred meters of emptiness, then another — but because the scale is continental, the scattered ones add up to a population that shapes the entire land. To miss them at first is easy, because many of them are very good at being terrain.

The main ones are the grazers. They are the size of a bus, longer if you count the tail of plated segments, and they move on six legs that work like pistons. All of them look the same. Every individual is built from the same modular plates — hexagonal dorsal plates like boiler plate, overlapping, slightly rusted in color, bolted in effect by living tissue that can be regrown. Their heads are low, blunt, and blind; the eyes are reduced to pits. They have no mouth for sound. They do not vocalize. They have never done so.

Watch one for a while and you see the industry of it. The front animals of a herd lower their heads and scrape the lichen crust with a jaw that opens and closes in a strict, repetitive rhythm. The animals behind them grind what is scraped, slowly, in a secondary chamber. Others ferment. Others carry electric organs along the flanks — a long strip of tissue that can discharge in a faint blue pulse. The whole herd moves at a walk that is barely a walk, legs cycling 1-2-3, 1-2-3, in perfect synchrony over dozens of bodies, like a production line that walks. If one animal's plate is cracked, another will butt it gently, tongue out, and knit it. Maintenance is social. Standardization is total.

There is no factory because the herd *is* the factory. There are no built things because nothing here is built. It is grown, worn, or processed. The industry does not make artifacts. It makes heat, and electricity, and body mass, and nutrient slurry. The slurry is excreted in the rear, in thin, pressed pads that break down into the lichen and feed it. Downstream, another herd will process what another has left. Corpses are not left to rot. Smaller fauna — low, worm-like sorters with ridged bellies and heavy, shovel jaws — crawl through bone and shell and stone, sorting it by size and density into small, neat piles that look, from a distance, like industrial spoil heaps. Scavenger gliders, broad-winged and stiff, soar for hours without a wingbeat, riding the high air, and drop occasionally to take a sorted piece and carry it elsewhere. Even the parasites on the grazers have a job: they clean necrotic tissue from plate joints and skim a little of the electric discharge to power their own small lights.

Some fauna you will mistake for boulders for your entire stay. They are torpid, plated, covered in the same lichen as the stones, breathing once every few minutes by a rise of maybe a millimeter along the flank. They are not sleeping in the human sense; they are in a low-power state, conserving between the slow pulses of the herds. When a herd passes, they wake in sequence. It is one of the unusual rules of the place: stillness is not death, and stone is not always stone.

All of this moves slowly. The dynamic of the world is patient, minimal-flux. A herd crossing a swale takes a season. Lichen grows a fraction of a millimeter a year. An animal may graze the same square meter for a week. Births happen standing, in the open, with no nest, no shelter, no preparation — the calf, if you can call it that, is already plated, already blind, already synchronized. Death is processed, not mourned. Change happens, undeniably, but across long durations and weak gradients. The weather changes elsewhere on the plateau while you stand in clear dim light. You could live here and think nothing had moved, except that the trail behind the herd — a polished groove worn into the lichen and stone by feet following the same ancient line for millennia — is deeper than it was last year.

And it is silent in a way that is not just quiet. Sound, when it happens, is accidental and small: the tick of a stone cooling, the sumptuous muffled shuffle of a piston-leg in damp lichen, the single drip off a plate. A dropped stone makes a dull clink and then nothing. The damp air and the thin high air and the layered fat of the animals eat vibration. The fauna have traded sound for something else. They communicate by the blue pulse along the flank — a slow flash that travels down the line of a herd like a signal on a wire — and by pressure through the soles of their feet, felt in the ground, not heard. You feel a herd before you hear it, if you ever hear it at all. Mostly you see it, eventually, coming at the pace of geology, filling a dip in the land with plated backs, and then continuing.

The light stays dim even at midday. The electric pulses are therefore not decoration; they are navigation, language, and in the smaller fauna, a lamp. In the dimness, the rust plates and the pale blue look like the only saturated colors the world has allowed itself.

There are no structures, no paths that were made, no tools, no fire, no remains of hands. The industrial character of the place is not imposed on it. It grew there, out of cold and damp and slowness and emptiness: a vast, high, cold plain where life learned to process, to standardize, to recycle, and to do it all without a sound, at a pace the stones would approve of.
