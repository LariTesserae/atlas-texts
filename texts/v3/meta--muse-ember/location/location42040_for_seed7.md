You go down, but not in the way you expect a cave to take you.

There is no long tunnel, no winding descent. There is a shaft, quick and industrial, a concrete collar and a ladder and a cage lift that has not run in years, and then the air changes before your feet do. It gets heavier. Thicker. It comes up to meet you from below, hot and wet and smelling faintly of iron, and it stays.

This is one room. That is the first surprise, and the second is that it is almost enough.

Sixty meters at its longest, perhaps twenty across, twelve high at the crown, but from anywhere inside you cannot quite take it whole. The walls lean, the floor rises and falls in shallow plates, and the clusters of things break the space into smaller clearings. It has the volume of a small cathedral and the logic of a machine hall. It was not found. It was made, and then it was broken into.

The place is below the surface in the plain sense — below the basalt plain, below the weather, below any sky. No rain has ever fallen into it directly. No sun has ever reached its ceiling. And yet it is hot. Not warm. Hot in the way of an oven left on after the cooking is done, hot that sweats you before you have done anything to deserve it, that makes your shirt stick to your back and your glasses fog and your lungs feel the damp like breathing through a cloth. The heat comes from below and from within: from geothermal rock still cooling from a recent wound, from pipes, from a turbine that turns though nothing seems to need its power.

They drilled here, twelve or twenty years ago — the erosion tells you the age. The walls are young. Nothing here has been smoothed. The rock is fresh basalt, sharp as broken crockery, edges still crisp where the drill and the heat and a small, recent quake split it. Where a river would have rounded corners over millennia, here water has barely had time to work. The fractures are clean. The slabs on the floor are tilted, unweathered, their faces still black. Thin stalactites are beginning — needles, straws, a finger's length — bright where the mineral film has just begun to catch the light. If you touch the wall, it does not give the cool of old stone. It gives heat back.

And it is damp, but not wet like a cave in a wet country. There is no stream. There is a loop.

Steam rises from a crack in the southern floor and condenses on everything cooler than itself: on the cold side of pipes, on the underside of the walkway, on the leaves. It gathers and drips. It gathers and runs in clear sheets down a wall, never quite making a river. It collects in shallow pockets in the hollows of the basalt, black and perfectly still, warm to the fingertip. Every few minutes a pipe somewhere sighs and releases a fine burst, a brief mechanical rain that darkens a circle of stone and then is gone. The water in this place belongs to the machines as much as to the rock. It is condensation, and coolant, and breath you cannot see.

The darkness is honest. It is not the blackness of night. It is the absence left when light has to be carried in and rationed. There are lamps — sodium tubes on a truss, a strip of work-lights over the pump house — but most of them are off, or have been flickered on to a low, orange standby for so long their tubes are tired. What light there is, is local and borrowed: the dull red glow from a furnace mouth that never quite closes, the green-white eye of a control panel, the faint amber thrown by hot metal. In between those islands, darkness pools in the full height of the ceiling. Your eyes do not adjust to it; you just learn where the pools are.

And yet, in that darkness, in that heat, the green is everywhere it can get a foothold — and it is lush.

Not everywhere. That matters. Most of the floor is bare. Large, empty plates of basalt, scattered drifts of ash and grit, the long shadows of machines. Between presences there is a lot of nothing. But where something has leaked, something has exploded.

Under every pipe joint, under every valve that weeps, under the condensation line that runs the length of the north wall, the vegetation is thick to the point of jungle: ferns with fronds three feet long, mosses that look wet even when the air is dry, broad-leaved things engineered, you can tell, for another purpose — thick, waxy, dark green above and a startling pale, almost translucent below, their veins lit orange when they pass in front of a heating duct. They are not wild by origin. They were brought, or made, to hold the spoil heaps and to eat the waste gases when this place was a pilot geothermal plant. The plant closed, the people left, the machines mostly stayed on standby, and the green stayed on.

They live on heat more than light. They take in the thermal glow from the turbines and the furnace, the chemical warmth of sulphur and nitrates from the slow leaks, the endless damp. Some of them have learned to grow upside down, hanging from the underside of pipes and walkways where the condensation is richest, their fronds curling not toward a sun but toward the nearest warm joint, turning their faces to the heat like heliotropes in reverse. They rub, very slowly, against the sensor arms and the cable trunks, and in a few places they have grown *into* the machinery — through a vent, around a gasket — not destroying it, just occupying the slack the way ivy does a fence, but with a patience that makes the difference hard to see.

That is the one truly unusual thing, though it is not very dramatic to look at: the green and the industrial are not struggling here. They are in a finished, slow symbiosis. A conveyor belt, rusted but not dead, still creeps forward a centimeter an hour, carrying a mat of fallen leaves toward a hopper that turns them, almost imperceptibly, into compost for the next patch. A pruner arm wakes once a day, extends, cuts nothing much, retracts. A fan, far off in a side alcove, turns over in its sleep and sighs. The plants do not need animals to pollinate them. There are no animals. Their spores drift in the still, hot air and settle on damp metal and begin again.

There are no animals at all. No insects in the corners, no worms in the damp soil that is not soil, no birds roosting in the black above, no cave fish in the warm pockets. Life here is only botanical and mechanical, because only those two can tolerate the heat, the carbon dioxide, the sulphur tang, the lack of sky, for long. It is a particular kind of silence that results. Not a peaceful silence. An *uninhabited* silence, the kind that makes your own footstep sound foreign.

Almost everything is quiet. The sounds that do exist are patient and unmusical: a drip forming, falling, echoing once off a polished slab; the tick and tiny ping of metal expanding as a pipe warms; a low electrical hum from somewhere behind a panel, more felt in your teeth than heard; a sigh of steam every few minutes, always from a different place so you never learn the rhythm. Nothing rushes. Nothing has rushed here in years.

The built things are scattered like the green, in small, separate groups, each with its own clearing.

A pump house of corrugated steel and concrete, its door open, its interior dark except for a single panel blinking a slow green, green, green. A walkway of steel grating that zigzags across a shallow pit, its railings bent where a crate fell or a frond pushed. A cylindrical tank, half-full of black water, its gauge frozen not at zero but at a productive-looking middle. A gantry crane, its hook hanging empty over nothing, its arm grown into the ceiling shadow. A control hut, small enough for two people, with a window filmed over in green slime and a chair still facing the instruments that no longer need facing. No houses where anyone lived for long. No road out. Just the work, left after the workers.

They do not look ruined, exactly. They look *waiting*, and left long enough that waiting has become a state. Rust has begun at the bolts. Biofilm has begun at the waterlines. The concrete is still sharp, the paint still legible on a stenciled number: 3. The whole chamber is Chamber Three.

Everything moves slowly because there is no reason to move fast. The turbine turns at a pace you can count: one revolution, a breath, another. The moss creeps. The heat builds during what might be day in your body, and eases fractionally — still more than enough — during what might be night. A single drip cycle can take four minutes to gather at the tip of a stalactite. The plant hopper compacts its load over a season. Change here is not measured in events. It is measured in accretions: another millimeter of scale inside a pipe, another ring of mineral on a leaf, another season of leaves falling onto a belt that will carry them an inch toward becoming soil.

That is the rule of the place, if a place can have one: heat is the source, water is recirculated, light is stolen, and nothing with a heartbeat is invited to stay. The green has learned the rules. The machines have not forgotten them. The rock, newly exposed, is still learning, its sharp edges hot in the dark, its first thin stalactites reaching down toward a warm, black pool that reflects nothing but its own brief, orange glint, and holds it as long as it can.
