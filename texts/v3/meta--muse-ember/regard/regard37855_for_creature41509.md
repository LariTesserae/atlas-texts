It did not come because of a prayer. Nothing on the plateau prays.

No signal rose from it, no distress, no thirst calling upward. Its only emissions were the faint warm vapour from the chimney reeds, the tick of static over a dense mat, the high hiss of a distant vent, the soft pop when a spore was released and fell straight down into the crack it was born in. Most places announce themselves by lack. This one announced itself by coherence, and coherence, to a power that had seen a thousand worlds come apart, is rarer than a cry for help.

So it watched, for a while that by any clock elsewhere would count as geology.

It bent its attention low, the way you might bend to a mat, though a mat does not invite kneeling. It saw the green that shines as if wet and is dry to the touch, the rosettes thick as fingers, blue-green above and dusty purple beneath, overlapping shoulder to shoulder until the cover was a hundred percent and then stopped, cut with a tool, white plain for a kilometre. It saw the ribbons running at right angles along fissures, rooting where they touched, flushing orange at the tip where iron was laid down. The amber bulbs set like tanks into the floor. The lichens painting the exact geometry of the vents. The star-flowers open all the time, seasonless, tipped with a bead of light, waiting for no one.

It understood, slowly, what kind of being lived here. Not a rosette. Not a ribbon. The being was the plateau-life as a whole: surface and crack and the thin warm gradient between hot rock below and hot trapped air above. Separate islands, kilometres apart, each a life that would never touch another, that did not need to touch another. It had no face, no ear, no mouth, no upward. Its thought, if it could be called thought, was circulation — salt melt moving copper and phosphorus from root to leaf to bulb, gas moving up a chimney, heat moving out. It had never moved, except at its margin, where growth crept outward over its own petrified centre. Its history was visible in its own body, leaves turned to stone where they fell, ash darkened with metal under a colony that had smelted itself into the ground.

A benevolent power feels, first, the impulse to give. A wise power feels, second, the check. Here, the two had to learn to agree.

In another desert it would have gathered the clouds. The thought came easily, almost by habit: water. Rain on the salt plates, rain in the hollow volume above the mats, rain to fill the leaves that were so full of everything except water. It pictured it for a moment, and saw the correction for what it was. The rain would dissolve the anhydrous crusts, dilute the ionic liquid in the leaf to a poison, wash the acid from the roots and carry the metals away in a thin, familiar runoff to nowhere. The rosettes would slump, rot, and die properly, in the familiar way. The lushness would become legible to us, and would cease to be itself. To make it understandable is not to save it.

Then company. The sky above the plateau is empty in a way that aches in a visitor. No birds, no insects in the flowers, no lizards, no tracks, no sound but your own step and your pulse. The impulse was to fill the volume. To seed it with pollinators for those open terminals, with birds to cross between islands, with something that would eat and be eaten and introduce movement, noise, dung, bones — the whole second layer of life that this life never needed. But the flowers are not invitations; the wax, the salt, the metal load are not defences, they are the chemistry itself. There is no niche waiting, only a surface that works. A mouth introduced here would be a violence, and what could live on those leaves would die of them. Loneliness is a human category. For a being whose relation to its kin is to breathe the same trapped air and answer the same vent pattern, distance is not isolation. It is how it is together.

Then time. The power was tempted to give it seasons, to give it wind to winnow the ash into dunes, to give it erosion so that it could become old and beautiful in the way valleys are beautiful. But it already has time, turned inward. It ages by mineralising itself from the centre while remaining green at the edge. Its autumn is petrification. Its footprint stays because nothing is turned over. To impose an outside time would be to replace its history with ours.

Then abundance, and use. It saw, in the visitor's footprints that stayed in the ash, the next mind that would come after. Men would not come with rain or with birds. They would come with a ledger. Wax, oil, copper veins, iron at the ribbon tips, an industrial salt solvent that does not evaporate, metal-rich ash that does not blow away. All the qualities that make the place complete would make it, to them, a resource. The greatest threat to this plateau is not drought. It is usefulness. A power that is benevolent understands that to spread a thing until it chokes its own vents, to make it known in the markets of the mind, is another kind of rain.

At each temptation it felt its own old habit: to fix by adding. And at each, something quieter in it recognised a mirror. Here was a world still in its first making, arrested at the moment after creation, the basalt still ropy, the ash still like flour, the salt plates still uncracked beyond a boot. It knew that moment. It had stood in it before, at the beginning of other things, and watched it unmake itself into soil and valley and dust. To find one place that had not, that would not, that held the newness by the simple law that nothing moves — that was not a problem to be solved. It was a reminder, left whole.

So it did what a benevolent and wise power does when nothing is needed of it: it stayed, and it stayed in the manner of the place.

It did not hover or shine or speak. Speech would require an ear. It added only attention, another layer of stillness over a place whose rule is stillness, and company that asks for no response. It kept, if keeping was required, the old agreement — sun above working on dark stone, magma below feeding the vents in their regular spacing, warm air lidded over the tableland, carbon dioxide in the stillness for the leaves to take. It kept the spacing between islands. It kept the gaze from becoming a spotlight that others might follow. It shielded the plateau not by hiding its green, which is plain from far off, but by making it uninteresting to eyes that measure: too thin an air to land, too sharp a glass to walk, too much trouble for too strange a yield.

It did not erase the footprints. To erase them would require the first wind, the first erosion, and the plateau would learn change from a kindness. It let those two lines remain in the ash, the one history the place has permitted, proof that a witness came and, having felt like an intrusion, left. That is enough of a concession to movement.

And it learned, with something like relief, that it was not necessary here. It did not need to be thanked, because there is no mouth to thank it. It did not need to be needed, because the mats would go on rooting where static and vapour meet whether it watched or not — though it watched, and the watching was not nothing; it was the refusal, renewed each moment, to rain, to seed, to scatter, to use.

Tomorrow, by its own clock, will be the same warm day. The salt will be white, the basalt black, the green saturated and shining as if wet in a place where water will never fall. A chimney reed will pop, softly. A spore will fall a hand's breadth. The being will not know it was loved, and that is exactly as it should be — because love, here, is not an event. It is a condition, kept.

It is still there, in the act of not doing.
