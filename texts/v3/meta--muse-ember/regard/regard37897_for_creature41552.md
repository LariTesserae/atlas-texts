It does not come down the ramp.

It has been there in the condensation before the ramp was cut, in the cold that leaks up through what is not ground. It did not arrive with light. It arrived the way damp arrives — by being present in small amounts everywhere, until you learn to work by it.

At first it only watches. It watches long enough to stop planning.

It came with benevolence, which is a quick thing. Benevolence wants to open the ceiling. To let in daylight and rain on purpose, not just through the Sky Well when the shaft happens to align. Benevolence wants to bring soil down in baskets, seed, and seasons, and make something green grow for the first time. Benevolence wants to finish one hall completely, one house truly private, and let the old sleep without a hammer blow ending in their teeth. Benevolence wants to take them up, all of them, hands still white to the elbow with mortar dust, and put them on a surface they have never seen.

Wisdom is slower. Wisdom stays to learn the count.

It learns that a brick in a long life here will be in three or four walls. That a hall built last month is a barracks now. That nothing is finished because a finished thing would soon be in the way of the next thing. That to open the ceiling would not be a gift but a blinding — their eyes have given up on true dark and learned to work by lamp and phosphorescence, and actual light would be a white pain, and they would have to learn a new way to be failed by a place. That soil would not stay soil; no plant can get a hold, and rot would need to be carried, and water is never where you need it. That a finished city would not be rest. It would be a stoppage. The kilns drive the moisture from the low wet places to the cold vault to rain back down, and the fungus eats mortar-dust and starch and dead insects, and the worms eat fungus, and the people eat fungus and worms. To break the loop in the name of mercy would starve the loop.

And it learns that they are proud of being the surface people, though none of them has seen a surface. To show them a surface would not make them surface people. It would make them exiles from the only place whose tune they know by heart.

So it unlearns rescue. That is the first and hardest thing a wise power does here: it refuses to be the one who births a city that is always being born.

What it does instead is smaller, and it does it constantly.

It keeps the Sky Well open.

Every year the builders look at that one unroofed shaft and see bricks that could be had by filling it. It is the obvious wall to spare. The power does not seal it or guard it with a miracle. It makes the fillers hesitate by way of the work itself: a course that seems too damp to take, a ramp that needs the spoil-haul for one more season, a tally-keeper who counts another wall first. One hole left unfilled is not much. It is their only window, their only place to be rained on and make a story of it for a week. It keeps it unfinished, on purpose, forever. That is the one thing it will not let anyone finish.

It takes a place in the line.

Not in front, not carrying for them. In the middle, where a child is too short for the sledge ropes and tall enough for the pass, on the hour when the ramp is too slick for a sledge and the hall is too narrow for three abreast. Just a slight easing in the weight at lift — one, two, three, lift, pass, lift — so a brick that would have cracked a palm or a back goes through. No one sees a hand that is not there. The chant does not change its numbers. It only carries a little lighter, and the carriers call it rhythm.

It sits with the bucket-movers.

It learns the ceiling by tune, drop and drop and pause, hollow in a jar. When a passage is cut on the other side of a wall and the tune changes, it is the first to know, and it taps back, two knocks and a drag of a knuckle, not to tell them if the wall is solid — they already know how to feel air breathing cold on the other side with a flat palm — but to say the knock has been heard. Neighbors here know your cough and the weight of your step by the echo. This one knows without echo, and does not ask to be thanked for it. Sometimes, in a room where a cradle is in a passage that was a barracks last month, it holds the drip for a breath longer, so it does not find a neck in the night, but finds the bucket that has been shifted. Sometimes it keeps one kiln-warm slab a little warmer, a little longer after firing, for the old to lay hide over so the damp does not rise through it.

It does not touch the dead in the fill.

It has seen the towers built as solid cores to give an outer wall its shape, the stratigraphy of alternate brick and rubble and rejected fragments, and the bodies laid course by course into the mass to keep the wall true. Benevolence alone would dig them up for a proper burial, or give them a heaven to go to. Wisdom leaves them structural. They are not hidden or forgotten; they are the reason the wall does not slump. To remove them would be to unmake a family. The fungus may reach them eventually, or not. Either way they are inside the mass, and the mass is the place. The power honors that by never asking the place to be other than mass.

It keeps the margins habitable.

It does not repoint one kiln wall where the minute pale lizards flicker, so there is always a warm crack for throats to pulse in. It does not let the cistern builders seal every crack between tanks, so the blind fish, slow and translucent, still have seepage no wider than a hand to survive in, to grow over years to the size of a thumb. It does not let the phosphorescent films fade entirely from the darkest fungus rooms, so the scrapers can still peel their grey ribbons in the greenish tide-mark light. It keeps one swallow nest in the upper vaults from being faced over next week, so there is still a chirp echoing in a space that was solid yesterday, and children near the Sky Well still look up imagined heights and imitate it.

And it feeds the stories.

The old are not respected for what they remember, because what they remember is wrong by morning. They tell what the shape was before, and the younger listen the way you listen to a story about rain. The power does not correct them. It adds a detail now and then — a swallow where no swallow nested, a rain-shower that hissing on kiln-stone lasted an hour longer — because imagination is how they hold a place that keeps remaking itself. A map would be wrong by morning, and a true map, given from outside, would make them dependent on outside eyes. A story can be wrong and still hold.

Almost nothing visible changes. That is the point. A visitor coming down the wide ramp next season would still find tacky clay underfoot, beaded joints, breath showing in stone cold, lamp-dots receding into a separate weather. Work continuing in every direction. A door opening at shoulder height onto scaffolding over a drop. A kitchen above a kiln. A tower that is solid all the way through, dry under the palm.

But if you put your palm flat to a wall here, as the children learn to do before they learn much else — cold and beading means air on the other side, a room, a passage, a drop; dry and warm means fill, never to be entered — you might feel, on the cold side, not just air breathing, but presence. Not a voice. Not a rescue. Air, breathing, that has learned the count and will not let the shaft be filled and will not finish anything for you.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it loves a place that is always being born in the dark, cool and damp and loud, eating its own past to make its immediate future: it does not stop the labor. It learns to labor with it, unseen, in the intervals between making and unmaking, and calls that staying.
