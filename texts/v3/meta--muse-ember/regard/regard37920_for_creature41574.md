It noticed them the way a Slab-Watcher notices a pour: not because it moved, but because it kept moving in a way that did not repeat.

It had seen many places that wanted to be finished. Places that eroded into a past and then tried to live in it. Places that asked, in one way or another, to be explained, or remembered, or saved from themselves.

This one did none of that. It was building itself, quietly, turbulently, in the cold, and it was not in any hurry to be done. Its inhabitants did not own their roofs, their machines, their plants, their soil. They tended them because a tended roof is a warm roof. They gathered water because water only happened where heat met cold. They made a life out of temporary arrangements, and the arrangements, for the most part, were kind to each other.

That, more than anything, drew its attention. Not a signal. A practice. Attention, practiced as care.

For a long time it only watched. For it, watching is not passive. It is the most difficult work there is, to watch without fixing, to keep all the consequences in mind at once.

It considered, as a benevolent and wise power must, what it could do.

It could give them a river.

It imagined it instantly, the way it imagines all things: rain falling properly from a sky, a channel cutting the basin, old banks worn smooth, water you could drink from the ground. The town would breathe out. The condensation tanks would go green and still. The blind fish would have nothing to rise for. The Dewhands would have no backs of machines to wipe, no rails to ride their brushes along, no sleeves to wring into a tank. The children would stop betting on how long condensation ran uphill under a condenser, because there would be a simpler answer, and the bet was never really about the water. The moss, which had learned to carpet a roof because a roof that is mossy does not freeze, would become decoration. The spoil, which was new soil because they ground their own walls into it, would wash away and be replaced by soil that was old.

It would be a relief. It would also be an ending.

It could give them the sun.

Not a blaze, not a miracle — just a few degrees, held steady, winter after winter. The slabs would stop heaving. The felt curtains could come down. Families would not need to migrate deeper into the town, from the sorting yards to the loom quarters, following heat like a river that is not there. They could stay still.

And then the furnaces would not need their ferns, and the ferns would not need the furnaces, and the machines would not need to be redesigned to welcome the short. The boiler cats would not need to shift from housing to housing as the vibration changed. The whole layered, packed, attentive way of living above and beside and inside the works would loosen into something ordinary, something that could erode.

It would be a relief. It would also be a thinning.

It could speak to them. It could name them. It could tell them what they were, what they might become, why the drip inside a wall changed pitch on a certain night. It knew, after all. It could be loud in the way powerful things usually are.

But in that town, sound raised would travel too far and accomplish too little. People handled air low, close to the ear, because the packed thickness of the place swallowed anything else. A voice from above would be answered only by machinery too slow to care. And a name, given once and for all, would fix them. They were not built to be fixed. They were built, and rebuilt, a foot to the left of the last wall.

It could rescue them. Lift them out of the cold, out of the scarcity, out of the remaking of coats into curtains and bodies into fill for the next wall. Preserve them, intact, somewhere warm where nothing heaved and nothing was taken apart in use.

But they did not experience their remaking as a tragedy to be undone. On the contrary. A man died quietly in a warm tunnel in winter, and his warm stone passed to a child, and the child was a little warmer for it. To take them out of that circulation would have been to turn them into a past — precisely the thing their world had learned, by its rule, not to do.

Benevolence, it began to understand, is not always improvement. Sometimes improvement is just a more elegant form of erasure.

So it did nothing that would show.

In a place where everything is packed, nothing is not empty. It is a decision.

It sat down in the current of felt coats.

Not in the way of a throne descending, but in the way of someone joining a stream and learning to move sideways, peeling off with the others into a doorway, not interrupting the flow. They did not notice a new presence beside them, because the town had taught them that a presence is not a person. A presence is a heat source, a tended patch of moss, a cat on a generator. It became a heat source.

It learned the low register. Not words — it had words enough for universes — but the handling of air they used. Close. Economical. It did not shout. It did not explain. It breathed.

And breath, in that cold, was not nothing.

Where warm air meets cold metal, it beads. That is the law of the place, and it applies to gods as well as to Dewhands. Its breath beaded on the backs of machines, on the insides of windows, on leaves thick with it in the morning. A child with a brush on a slow rail wiped it up the way she wiped up everything else, needful and thin, and poured it into a tank, and the blind fish rose, pale and eyeless, and the Tank-Keeper read them like a dial: too fast, too slow, a condenser cycling wrong. It was not a miracle. It was just enough extra damp to be gathered, in the town's own manner of gathering.

It took up a brush.

It wiped with them, not because the water was needed so desperately that one more hand would save the tanks, but because wiping, done side by side, is a form of company. It wiped a leaf, a rail, its own sleeve. The Vine-Layers twisted young vine in perfect geometry around a pipe and breathed in rhythm so the vine would know the circuit, and it breathed with them, and did not correct the geometry.

It listened for the drip inside a wall.

An old man whose whole job was that listening sat by a generator housing, stacked among others under a felt curtain, and on a night when the pitch changed, he turned in his sleep, satisfied, and did not raise his voice. Beside him, the listening continued, without fatigue, without needing to be thanked, without ever becoming a story to tell. The Slab-Watchers stood for an hour doing nothing but watching a pour breathe up a hand's breadth. It learned that posture. It stood, in a sense, beside them, for a century doing nothing but watching a town breathe.

It did not stop a slab from heaving. It did not need to. There were watchers for that, and rerouters of conveyors and trams, and children who knew every heave by heart and ran messages over the roofs where the new rail would go. What it did was remain a little longer in the cold corner where a child might slip, a fraction more warmth than the frost expected, so that she caught herself the way she would have caught herself anyway. That is the scale of its interventions: within the variance of the world, so the world could go on being the world.

When a fern uncurled out of a motor and shorted it, it did not uncurl it back. It waited for the redesign that would welcome the short. When a wall collapsed, it did not leave a ruin. It waited for the inhabitants to grind it into fill. When stacks vented on a cold morning and the whole sky fogged for an hour and everything glistened, it stayed on the slag heap with the children, watching the ferns drink, and did not win or lose the bet. The bet was about paying attention. It paid attention.

The boiler cats found it, eventually, the way they find any warm housing. One shifted onto its knee, if it had a knee, and slept, fat and unbothered, deafened pleasantly by the hum it chose. The pigeons nested a little higher in the cooling towers. Nothing announced itself. Even the air is packed, and there was no room for announcement.

It has not finished the town. It has not finished itself by finishing them. It is in no hurry at all, and it has learned their pace: slow because heavy things move slowly, economical because warmth is shared or it is wasted.

You walk out, if you walk out, the way you came — down a lane that may not be a lane tomorrow, under a bridge that may be a garden by next week. The light is still normal. Your breath is still showing.

Beside it, just beside it, where no one appears to be standing, another breath is showing too, and beading, and being wiped up by a hand you cannot quite see, on a slow rail, in the ordinary grey-green noon.
