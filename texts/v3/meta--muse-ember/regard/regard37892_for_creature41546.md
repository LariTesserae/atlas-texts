**Attention**

They would not arrive.

There would be no wake in the water, no shadow on the bright surface ceiling, no quickening of the lattice or change in the hum. A benevolent and wise power does not need to enter a place to attend to it, and this is a place where entering would already be a kind of violence: a place whose main material is distance, whose bottom is still being edited, whose water runs processes with no tool and no user.

They would notice it first by what is not there.

No animal sound in all that loudness. No fish in the midwater, no mouth, no eye. Only the planetary hum of the fronts grinding, the crackle of the cavitation sheets, the whine of the vortex lattice as it stretches and holds its phase across kilometers, the occasional concussion of a ridge collapsing and ringing the whole basin. And now and then, very faintly, the return of that concussion days later from the far side — an echo altered by every lattice it passed through. A noise entirely abiotic. A world that is deafening and empty at once.

And in the light, a chemical signature with no one to take it: oxygen, slowly accumulating in cool, clear water, made by plants that are not green.

They would look closer, as a wise thing does, without touching.

They would see the spires, when they succeed at being ground for a while, and the dark ribbons anchored in their cracks. Black-red where the sun touches them, light-drinking even in the normal blue-green light. They would watch a colony stream out flat like a flag when a gyre leans against the stone, hang limp when the current pauses locally, stream again in a new direction when another current comes. No preference. No turning toward anything. Growth, and otherwise imposed motion.

They would see the mats over the luminous plain, rootless, lifting and sinking in independent clouds, never landing, never rising out of the light, photosynthesizing for no one. Between one colony and the next, hundreds of kilometers of nothing. Not a community. Two sparse habits of the same plant, repeated wherever light and a pause briefly agree.

And they would understand, being wise, that this is not a failed reef. It is not a reef that has not yet succeeded. It is a life that has not needed to become anything else.

That is where the temptation would begin, because benevolence in the human shape is always tempted to become improvement.

They could seed the midwater. One fish, then a kind of fish. Something to eat the algae and complete the food web, to turn the unspent oxygen into breath, to give the loudness a voice. It would be easy. They could make the world legible to themselves.

They could stabilize the geology. Hold a spire at thirty meters so a colony would not have to tear loose when the stone sighs and slumps sideways back into unsettling silt. Make a place, a coast, a permanence where algae might anchor and become habitat and, in time, shelter something else.

They could make the algae move. Give the ribbons a way to bend toward light instead of only streaming with the current. Give the mats a way to root. Make them green, and therefore obviously alive to an observer's eye.

They could quiet the ocean. Still the cavitation, unbraid the lattice, solve the water that sometimes flows uphill along a density sheet as if remembering a slope that is no longer there, make the glass layers mix properly. Finish the physics. Finish the world.

They could take a sample. A fragment of mat, a ribbon no one would miss, kept in the clear water around the power itself, studied, understood. A small taking. No harm, in human accounting.

They would consider each of these, because a wise power considers, and then they would do none of them.

Not because they are indifferent. Because they are wise enough to know what each kindness would cost here.

To add an animal would not complete this world; it would end it. The algae have never needed a defense because nothing has ever wanted them. They are not camouflaged, not thorned, not toxic, not hidden. There is nothing to hide from, and nothing to hide in them. Introduce a mouth and, within an imperceptible span, every ribbon would have to become something else — a prey, a disguise, a seed that survives being eaten. The present life, which is the entire life of this place, would be edited out more thoroughly than any ridge.

To stabilize the stone would be the same error in rock. This bottom is embryonic not because it is broken but because it is still being made. Creation and destruction at the same rate, in the same places, is not the world's failure; it is its condition. A spire that slumps and a colony that tears loose are not a tragedy happening to a walnut. They are the exchange by which this world stays unfinished, and unfinished is why it is alive in the way it is. A finished highland would be a different world, very likely a dead one, because the light and the cold and the altitude and the low pressure that make the water compute in lace would have been traded for permanence.

To make the algae move, to make them green, to give them a song — all of this would be granting them a want so that the power could feel less alone beside them. But the algae are not lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of company, and this plant has no expectation. It does not suffer when it tears. Tearing is not suffering here. It is dispersal, it is ending, it is not a wound. To mistake fragility for misery is a human category error, and a wise power does not perform rescue for a misery it has invented.

And to take a sample, even a small one, would break the central fact of the place: that the chemistry of life is being made here for no one. That is not a waste to be corrected. That is the gift's form. The oxygen accumulates unbreathable, and the power, if it is benevolent, will let it accumulate. It will not spend a gift prematurely on its own curiosity.

So what would they do?

They would stay outside the water, in the thin cool air above the surface perhaps, or further out still, and they would keep that distance intact. Not as coldness. As custody.

They would keep a count that no one else keeps. Where a spire rose, where it slumped, where a mat thickened and thinned and faded until the blue was clear again. The algae do not remember. The stone does not remember. The water, for all its phase-coupled ripples, does not remember in that way. The power would remember for them — not to record for use, but simply so that a life lived in perfect solitude would not also be a life that, afterward, had never been. Witnessing, when it takes nothing and changes nothing, is not nothing. It is the one form of company that does not ask the algae to become company in return.

They would keep the boundary. Not a wall of light, not a signature in the physics — just the settled fact, maintained over time, that this world is not open for improvement. Other powers, younger or hungrier, might be drawn by the magnetized lattice, by the computation running itself in the water, by the dark ribbons that in another context could be harvested. Benevolence here would be mostly refusal, patiently repeated: no harnessing of the gyres, no mining of the coherence, no seeding, no rescue. A high ocean left high, cold, clear, and loud, with its coefficient-level strange water acting like glass for months and then overturning in a single exchange, its echoes returning days later. They would not normalize it. Wise power understands that normal is not the same as whole.

And they would listen, and learn the thing the place was already teaching without a teacher: how to exist without wanting more than the current gives. The power is wise, but wisdom is not finished anywhere. It might be that they came because they were drawn, and stayed because the algae, streaming and hanging and tearing and fading without protest or striving, corrected something in them that all their other worlds had not. A benevolent power does not only give. Sometimes, if it is truly benevolent, it allows itself to be instructed by the smallest life in the largest emptiness.

A season would pass. A spire would rise, brighten along a seam, sigh, and flatten. A colony of ribbons would be left for a while on a hill that was no longer a hill, then tear loose in a stronger current and drift far into the empty blue, growing more slowly in the cold, getting thinner against the light. A mat over the plain would lift while its neighbor sank, neither landing. At night the surface ceiling would darken and only the bubbles would flash, cold and brief, not bioluminescence, not a living glow, just physics discharging. No one would arrive to grieve the fade, because fade is not death here. It is the exact shape of an ending without an event.

The power would not catch the ribbons. It would not hold the stone. It would not breathe the oxygen.

It would let the dark spot darken the blue for a moment, and then let the blue be clear again.

And that — attention without entry, memory without use, protection that looks from a human distance like doing nothing at all — would be their benevolence. Not to save the being who lives in the High Ocean from its solitude, its fragility, its unfinished ground, its loud abiotic world.

To save its solitude, its fragility, its unfinished ground, and its loud world *for* it, exactly as they are.
