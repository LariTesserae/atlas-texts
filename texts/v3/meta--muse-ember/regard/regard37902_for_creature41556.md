It noticed the place because nothing happened there.

A benevolent and wise power has heard many worlds. It has heard them bloom and rot, freeze and burn, beg and thank. It is used to arriving in the middle of an event — a flood to soften, a drought to end, a small blind thing crying out in a place that is not enough for it.

This place gave it no event to arrive at. Only a roar under the floor of the world, constant in volume, and the tick-tick of mineral contracting, and water that shivers and does not ripple. A low, wide bowl where heat moved, damp moved, minerals moved, and everything together arrived back exactly where it had started.

That steadiness was unlike anything it knew. It leaned down to look more closely — not from above, because there was nothing above here to lean from, no hill and no height, only the horizon held at eye level. It lowered itself until it was low too.

It stayed a long time. In a place where the grazer stays a long time with its head dipped to the fungus, staying a long time is a way of learning.

First it thought of rain.

The ground was warm and damp and had no green thing on it at all. By every ordinary logic it should have been lush. It would have been easy to cloud the washed grey-blue sky and let water fall for a season, to soften the siliceous crust, to coax something with leaves up from the black glaze and the ochre sheets and the white fans. A kindness, surely.

But the moisture in this place did not travel. It belonged to its hollow. The pools evaporated exactly as fast as condensation returned the water, beaded and held on the undersides of the darker stones, neither running off nor away. Rain would not have watered this household. It would have drowned it, flooded the seam between wet and dry where the grazer lived, washed the pale greenish skin from the pools, dissolved the terraces that the crusts were endlessly building and unbuilding. The power saw, kneeling in its own attention, that to make the place green would be to unmake it.

It held its breath instead. There was no wind to make.

The spores fell straight down. That had seemed, at first glance, like a failure — a life that could not disperse, that piled itself up millimetre by millimetre on the same warm silt, that became mineral again if it did not germinate and felt again if it did. The power could have breathed across the basin and carried them to new damp pockets, new colonies, a wider world for them.

But a spore that travelled would likely land where there was no damp to breathe it in. Here a spore landed beside its parent, in the same pocket, and waited, perhaps for an age, until that pocket breathed again. The family stayed together because the place was complete in place. Wind would have been a gift of scattering. It would have turned a closed household into a loss.

It left the air moving nowhere.

It thought of quieting the roar.

Nothing else had ever been so loud and so unchanging. It rang through the soles of a walker's feet. The power could have lifted it, the way it lifts a burden from a small back. It could have returned the plain to silence.

But the grazer lived inside that loudness. The tick-tick of grit shifting, the low tremor running up through the pools, the drone under everything — these were not noise imposed on the life here. They were the medium of it. The black glaze flexed with the tremor. The mist hung a hand's breadth thick and did not lift. Everything had arranged itself to live in that constant sound without raising its own voice. To silence it would have been to take the floor out from under the world.

It left the volume as it was.

It thought of the grazer, most of all, because it was the one that looked most like a creature that might be suffered. Blind, translucent, no longer than a thumb, legs like pale wire, one in a hundred metres, travelling a few millimetres along the boundary between wet and dry and then pausing for a very long time. The power, which is used to being needed, felt the old ache of pity rise in it. Eyes. A companion. Speed. A reason to move further than the seam.

It examined each mercy and set it aside.

Eyes would have given it the pale, even light, which it had survived by not needing. A companion would have eaten the edge that was already exactly enough. Speed would have carried it off the line that was its entire world. The pause was not emptiness to be filled. The pause was its feeding, its listening, its way of being in the place. It was not a lesser life waiting to be improved into a greater one. It was a life that had solved itself.

The power did not give it eyes. It learned to count its millimetres without expecting it to arrive anywhere.

It thought of the winged one, looping high and distant, a disturbance in the light, crossing the same sky on the same thermal and returning to the same place in the sky, never descending, never landing.

It could have made it a place to land. It could have softened the loop into a journey.

But the thermal was always there, always the same temperature, always blowing the same way, and the loop was not a cage. It was a route through air that held it up without change. A perch would have made the air into a thing to be used and left. The winged one did not need the felt below except for the air that held it, and the place did not need it except for its shadow passing once over a white fan that did not react. To interrupt that needlessness would not have been mercy. It would have been instruction.

It left the loop unbroken.

It thought, finally, of the white fans and their scars. Cracked where they dried, healed where the damp returned, healed without changing, the crack remaining visible, remembered in the tissue. The power could have healed them clean. It could have made the tissue forget.

It did not. A scar that stays is not a wound that continues. It is a map of every time the fan dried and lived. To erase it would be to make the fan less itself in order to make it look more whole to a visitor. The power, which has seen many wholes, knew that this was wholeness of another kind: finished, and therefore not to be finished again.

Having thought of all these mercies, and having refused them one by one, the power understood what it had been drawn here to do. It was not to improve. It was to attend.

That is harder for a power than for anyone else. Powers are made, in their own long history, by being needed. They are thanked and cursed and prayed to and blamed. This place offered none of that. The fungi took warmth and vapour and gave back sorted mineral and held damp. The grazer took a little of the edge and gave back nothing measurable. The winged one took the thermal and gave back its shadow. Nothing accounted to anything outside itself. There was no prayer for the power to answer, no gratitude for it to receive, no lack for it to fill.

A lesser power might have punished that needlessness, or left in disappointment, or made itself necessary by force of kindness.

This one stayed, and learned to be unnecessary without ceasing to be benevolent. It added itself to the relationship that already existed there: needlessness, perfectly maintained. It took only attention, which weighs nothing and warms nothing and changes no accounting, and it gave back only vigilance.

It put itself between the plain and the outside world — not as a wall, for there are no structures here, and a wall would be the first lie — but as a cushion. If a machine were to come to sort what was already sorted, if a boot were to come to study what was already known in the soles of feet, if a hand were to come to take a sample of a crust that was already giving itself back to its pool, the disturbance would meet the power first and spend itself there. The black felt would not have to flex for anything but the tremor. The mist would not have to lift for anything but its own hand's breadth. That is the only intervention it reserved: to prevent a single irreversible thing from ending a balance it did not create.

And it memorized. Pool by pool. Fan by fan. Every healed crack, in its exact line. Every cluster of stones, graded by size as if sieved. Every beaded underside where condensation stayed. It held the place in its mind precisely as it was, refusing to improve it even in memory, so that its sameness would never be unnoticed sameness. If needlessness is the law of the place, remembrance without alteration became the law of its witness.

It does not speak to the fungi. They have no ear for it, and the roar does not care who speaks more loudly. It does not speak to the grazer. The grazer is listening to something else, and its pause is the point. It does not call to the winged one. The winged one has its track in the air.

It sits at the edge of a damp pocket, low, at eye level, and does what the grazer does: it dips its attention to the seam between wet and dry, and pauses for a very long time.

If you walk there now, you will not see it. You will see the same grey-brown polygons with rounded edges, the same rust and black grit and white silica, the same pools reflecting the sky like a hole cut in the ground. You may feel, if you stay still, that stillness is less like waiting than like being accepted into the equilibrium, and for a second that acceptance may feel deeper, as if something vast had lowered itself to your height and was keeping the same company you were keeping.

Then you will walk on, because there is nowhere else for the ground to go.

And it will still be there, not above, not below, not in the way — only attending, only remembering, only refusing to make the finished place unfinished. A benevolent power, a wise one, doing the one thing it can do without harm: nothing, and staying to do it.
