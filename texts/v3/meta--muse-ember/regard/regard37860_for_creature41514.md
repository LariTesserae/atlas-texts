It did not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about its attention. It did not arrive as weather, or as light, or as a breaking of the lid. There was no descent through the cold blue, no softening at the soupy yellow-green, no new pressure in the water. It noticed the Low from the patience of its own distance, the way a thing that lives a very long time notices a place that has learned, in sixty years, how to stay.

And what it saw was not a need.

A foolish benevolence would have seen forty-three people in bulky suits, in bath-warm water four hundred metres below any surface that matters, in a town eleven metres of market square wide, growing smaller and greener inside a basalt cup, and would have wanted to lift them. A cold wisdom would have seen the same thing and left them alone as an interesting case of accretion. This power had both benevolence and wisdom, which is a rarer and quieter combination. It saw first that the Low was not failing to be a surface town. It was succeeding at being itself, and that is a much more delicate achievement.

So it declined the obvious mercies, one by one, for reasons that would only make sense to someone who had actually read the place closely.

It would not bring them the sky.

The children born in the dry pods know *sky* as a word in a language they do not speak, like *rain*, and like *June*, which in the Low is not a month but the kelp-cutting. To give them a sky it would have to take the lid away, and the lid is not a trap. It is the warm pocket itself, trapped in the bowl, held at thirty-four degrees, feeding on the alkaline vents to the south. Take the lid and the warmth spills upward and dies in the cold. The plants down here are not eating light, not really. There is almost no light to eat. They are eating warmth and mineral wash and the steady nutrient soup the smelters put out. Sunlight would not feed them. It would bleach the pale blue flicker that runs across the iron-weed when a batch is poured, it would make the seagrass glow at the dimmer shift invisible, it would turn the kelp trees that lean east toward the one true dark into something else entirely, something that had to be fed from above. The Low has made a whole legible world out of heat. A wise power does not hand a world that works by warmth a world that works by light and call it a gift.

It would not give them room.

This is harder for a benevolent power to refuse, because crowding looks so much like suffering from outside. Houses pressed to factory walls. Factory walls pressed to cooling towers. Pipes so low you duck. Conduits doing triple duty as handrails and laundry lines and trellises. Every corner filled, and then filled again. But the rim is basalt, three hundred and fifty metres across, and beyond the rim the cold ocean resumes, darker and clearer and indifferent. To open the cup outward would be to break the pocket. To expand inward — the only direction the town has ever grown, the way a shell grows — is its nature. In another twenty years a room in the pump quarter will be too small to enter, and nobody is alarmed, because the council will ream out Sinter Lane, as it always has, third porthole from the bend. The Low has solved the problem of space by deciding, collectively and over two generations, that tightness is not the absence of generosity. Generosity here is that everything has room for everything else, just barely, and nothing has room for anything else. A wise power would not unmake that solution a few years before it came true.

It would not rescue them upward.

The first generation stayed because their suit heaters failed on the third day and never quite became necessary again. The rotating dozen who get homesick for cold, for air that does not hiss, for the empty kind of silence above the lid, are allowed to be homesick and allowed to go up on the lift. A few of them come back down through that softening and stay, and have their suits re-riveted, and in a year their heaters cut out and stay out, and a bare hand, if they are foolish enough to take a glove off in the street, comes back feeling it has been held. To make the surface permanently available in some new, easier way — a tube, a promise, a program — would turn the Low into a waiting room, a place people endure until they can be properly lived in. The power saw people who prune the fans and listen for the pumps to breathe through the soles of their boots and in their molars, and call that work, and call the dimmer shift dinner. It would not insult that by offering them a future somewhere else. If a child born in the dry pods asks what rain is, the power would let the archivist answer with what everyone down here answers, and let the question stay a question.

It would not make them rich, or numerous, or new.

Ingots go out a few at a time on the lift cable, rocks tumbling slowly in clear water like stones in a river on the way in. Crush, wash, smelt, cast, repeat. The scale is patient and the scale is moral. Fortune would industrialize the vent field in a season. More people would fill the last gaps between presences, and then there would be nowhere for the water to be empty, which there already is not. New fans would turn faster and roar, or *tok*, at the wrong pitch, and fan-grass would have nowhere to root its perfect rosettes, one frond per slat. The fans turn a fraction slower each year, and nobody minds. That is not decay. That is rooting. A wise power does not replace a machine that has become partly a plant with a clean machine and call the plant a malfunction.

And it would not, finally, answer the questions it could so easily settle if it chose. It would not give the two long eels in the old cooling tower their true names, and so *Knot* and *Unknot* remain affectionate and inaccurate and argued over, hanging like grey ribbons at the dimmer shift for anyone who knows where to look through the algae-fuzzed porthole. It would not issue a final census of the dozen vent crabs, saucer-sized and terrible with patience, who arrive at a warm joint and stay a season and move a handspan at a time. It would not file the blind octopus in the archive tank under any category but resident, the forty-fourth permanent, curling its arms around a favored ledger spine, tasting paper in the dark. To name these things finally would be to finish them, and the Low is not finished. It is settled, which is different.

Having refused all that, what would it do?

It would keep the place exactly enough, which turns out to be a great deal of work, done a millimetre at a time.

It would hold the lid soft. From above, a stain. From inside, the ceiling. It would keep the cold on the cold side of the basalt and the mackerel shadows passing at the rim and not coming in, and the true dark east of the viewing port staying true dark, and the kelp leaning that way without ever leaving the bowl. It would keep the vent in its slow vocabulary — warming, holding, cooling, turning — never letting a phase that is meant to take decades take a season, because the people down here have learned to tell time by growth, a handspan of kelp a year, and a sudden warmth would unmake their calendar more cruelly than any cold.

It would mind the small continuities. Sinter Lane would narrow, as it must, but never quite close before the council reams it. The oldest conduits would white-bone themselves a millimetre at a time, but the pipe that sings a single low note when it warms would sing it, and someone in the pump quarter would always know which pipe it was by feel. The tea coil in the smelter waste-line would scale as it should and no faster. The market tank would keep its small bright fish, not for eating but for looking at, orange and blue and quicksilver pulsing in the amber light, because a town that chooses a luxury has chosen it well. The archive tank would stay warm enough for its librarian. The eels would come out at the dimmer shift, on time, to be looked at.

It would not prune. This matters more than it sounds. The Pruners are a municipal service with a schedule that goes back decades, shears and a list, every June the kelp off the lift cable, every July the fan-grass thinned from the pump house. If the power did that work, the town would become a museum of itself and the people would become exhibits in their own home, guests in a garden tended by something greater. Benevolence, rightly understood, leaves the necessary work to the people whose meaning is in the doing of it. It leaves the tunnels to narrow so there is work to ream. It leaves the fans to slow so there is something to listen to. It leaves the suits to be re-riveted until the seams are maps. It leaves the dropped wrench to fuzz orange, then green, then become part of the wall.

It would keep them un-found. Not hidden as a secret, but kept private as a home is private. Charts would misplace a decimal. Sonar would register a stain. A surface survey would pass overhead and see heat and call it venting and move on. The lift records would blur at the lid, in that soupy yellow-green, so that no company ever quite decided the Low was worth the cost of taking it. The power is benevolent enough to know that being discovered by the surface, at this moment in its history, would be the one weather the Low cannot survive — the weather of being wanted for something.

And it would count them wrong, on purpose, forever. Forty-three permanent, and a rotating dozen, and one octopus, and two eels, and a dozen crabs, and eleven metres of market square, and uncountable plants and machines holding each other up, the bacteria banding the stacks orange, the sponge filling the joints, the seagrass softening the main street underfoot so your boots never quite clank. The power would not add these up. It would hold the whole layered thing — people inside plants inside machines inside warmth inside a basalt cup — as one being, breathing very slowly through three fan blades the width of a room, turning so slowly you could watch one revolution and miss it.

That is what it would do. Nothing you could photograph on the way up. When you leave, you go up through the lid, and the cold takes you again, clean and blue and indifferent, and your suit's heaters come on, and the silence becomes the empty kind. The power would not follow you. It would not give you a souvenir of warmth to carry into the cold, because the warmth was never meant to be carried. It belongs to the place, and the place belongs to the people who prune it, and the algae will flicker next pour whether or not anyone is there to see it run quick as a blink across the north wall.

Below you, the lights stay amber. The weed breathes with the forges. The kelp sways an inch to the left over the course of an hour. The town grows inward, inch by inch, smaller and greener.

And that, the power would consider, is not a problem awaiting a solution.

That is the answer, still going on.
