It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about it, and the only sign you are given. In a place that keeps everything — a footprint, a tool left on a ledge, the dust pressed where a back has leaned for years — an arrival would be a damage. Light would have to come from somewhere and fall into the black sky. A voice would have to travel up through the stone as a pressure no district made. Rain would have to be brought up, somehow, through air that has never held it. Anything a benevolent power usually does would register here first as erosion.

So it does none of those things. If you watch the plateau for a change, you will watch forever and see none. The conveyor goes round in its closed loop. The smelter breathes through its vent at the same interval. The amber squares stay amber. The keepers sit, and stand, and place their boots in their grooves. The crawlers gather where the pipe is a degree warmer, and do not move. The power is there, if the word *there* can be used for a thing that has learned not to press.

It was not drawn here by suffering. That is the second thing. Most places it has attended called out in the way places can — flood, fire, hunger, a body breaking before its time, a silence that is really a waiting. This one did not cry out. Its ears ache, its throats are scraped, its breath goes unrewarded, but those are not distress calls. They are the cost of altitude, and the keepers have paid it so completely that the cost has become an instrument. Their large, flat ears are not for hearing speech. They are for feeling a press thump kilometers off through one continuous skin of stone. Their dry, slow guts are not failing. They are tuned to iron and feldspar, to condensation breathed out by machines. To look at them from outside and see only a lack — no water, no plants, no stars, no night to sleep in — would be a mistake a lesser power would make quickly, and kindly, and ruin everything.

It considers, in turn, the kind things, and sets each aside not from indifference but from knowledge.

It could bring water. It knows the weight of water, and where it would go in a world without any place for it to leave. It would fill the pale dust and make the footprints melt and be lost. It would rust the wrenches on the ledges. It would rot what has learned not to rot, and feed a green film into the cracks that have sat empty since before there was a word for empty. The keepers would drink, once, and their pelleted stone would cease to agree with them. The plateau would begin, at last, to change. That is not mercy. That is unmaking.

It could open the sky. A moon, a turning of stars, a morning. The keepers' eyes are slits under thick lids, made for amber and conduit-white at a constant distance. They doze against towers when they choose, for as many conveyor loops as they choose, and wake again in the same warmth. To give them a night would be to give them a measure they have no use for, and to take away the one measure they have.

It could lift one keeper out, to a softer place, with speech and company and rest. It knows, in lifting, that it would not be saving a keeper. It would be ending one. A keeper is a shape the plateau makes when it has been high and dark and warm long enough: the plates thickening disc by disc on the shoulders, the groove in the dust beginning as a drag and becoming a bootprint, the hand learning the pulse in a pipe. Take that shape out of the note of its district and it would not stand upright anywhere else. It would have to be remade. The power has remade too many things, and grown wise about the difference between rescue and replacement.

It could speak. It has a voice sufficient to fill the black between districts. The keepers would feel it under their heels before they heard it, a pressure traveling through stone from very far away, and they would fumble, as you fumble with your loud boots, for an answer in a throat that is not built for it. They name only places, and rarely, and with the mouth barely moving, because speech is a kind of erosion they do not permit. To answer them would be to make the place louder than it can bear.

And so, having refused every gift that would be a wound, the power does what the keepers do. That is the only work this place recognizes.

It sits down at the edge of the plateau, on the far side of everything, where no groove leads. It puts, if the image may be allowed, a palm flat against the stone. It learns the districts one by one — the mill lower, the transformer higher, the note you feel in your molars as you pass under a tower — not to combine them into a chord, not to improve the tuning, but to hold each in its own key so that none drifts. It becomes, in its turn, an attendant. The keeper attends the smelter so that the rhythm does not have to be alone. The power attends the keeper so that the keeper does not have to be alone in attending.

You will not see it do this. What you may feel, if you sit long enough beside a keeper with your back to the same tower leg and your palm on the same warm pipe, is a deepening of the pressure under your boot that is not the press and not the conveyor. Something larger, and slower, holding still at a distance that cannot be crossed by walking. A warmth behind the warmth of the stone, that does not flicker, that does not ask you to stand because it has stood. When a keeper feels it — and a keeper, whose whole life is listening through the soles of its boots, is the first to feel it — it lifts its head a fraction more than usual. Its slit eyes catch neither amber nor white. They catch, for a moment, the darkness of being attended to from outside the loop. Then it lowers its head again, and does not change its groove, and does not walk faster, and does not speak the new thing's name, because it has no name for it and does not need one.

That is all. It keeps the amber steady when a filament might have failed. It keeps the district from being mistaken, by anything passing in the black, for an emptiness that invites occupation. It keeps the footprints — yours as well as theirs — from being only theirs to carry. When you leave, and your sharp new prints remain in the dust to layer over themselves in time, the power remembers that you were loud and sat down anyway, so that the plateau does not have to be the sole keeper of that memory. When a keeper finally sits and does not stand, when dust settles over coat and boots until the tower has a new low dome at its foot, the power is present at the mineralizing, hums its low note back at the interval of a breathing slowing to stone, and stays after the groove from the next district has begun its long circuit, so that the circuit is witnessed from both ends.

It does not finish the world. The world was finished long before it came, and a wise power knows that finished things are the rarest things there are, rarer than any cry for help. It can only refuse to unfinish it, and sit with it in the dark, and let itself be worn smooth — on both sides — by the contact.

The plateau holds its height without drama. The city holds its shape without effort. The warmth holds in the stone. And now the attention holds with them, constant as light, constant as hum, while the keeper unfolds, places its boots in the groove, and goes — not changed, not saved, not alone.
