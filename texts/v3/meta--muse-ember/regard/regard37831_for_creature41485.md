They did not notice it because it was loud.

They have watched louder things. Worlds that broadcast. Engines that scream their heat into space and call it progress. Prayers that rise in a single voice, asking, asking. The Roost does none of that. It makes no signal that leaves the tower. Nothing leaves, nothing is wasted, and it has gotten along for a very long time on that rule alone.

They noticed it in the fraction between heat tides.

In that lull — after the rain has thinned, after the water hammer stutters, after the runners have poured back up the walls and the fliers have spread again into their shimmering ceiling and the big grazer lifts its head once and settles deeper into the basin — there is a moment when the chamber is, by its own standards, quiet. Not silent. Never silent. But the clamor drops just enough that you can hear the separate work under it: a tile cooling and ticking as it contracts, a film colony breathing on the hour, a thread-worm withdrawing into a healed seam, a blind white thing under the grating opening and closing in the current.

Healing. That is what drew them. Not the density — though a square meter of wall holding hundreds of copper-backed runners is density enough to notice — but the fact that a crack does not stay a crack. That a scraped-bare tile is re-taken within days, millimeter by millimeter, from the grout lines. That what dies in place is not removed and not mourned and not wasted, but transferred: runner to film, flier to grazer, grazer to white pulse, waste to film again. That a thing the size of a dog can walk the same concave road its ancestors walked, brightening tile behind it with its mouth, and know to leave and let the film reclaim the furrow, and know to come back next cycle.

They have seen ecosystems before. They have seen them starve, bloom, choke on their own success, freeze, burn. They have seen engineered things go feral and die of their engineering. They have seen clever things build towers to exchange heat and abandon them to the wind. What they had not seen in a very long time was a system that was not trying to become anything else.

It was not waiting to be found. It was not incomplete.

That made the first temptation, and they felt it plainly, because benevolence is not the absence of temptation. It is what you do with it.

They could have given it light. A dozen silver ferns persisting in one cool trough, photosynthesizing almost nothing, treated like heat wells rather than plants. A little more light through the iris, a little less steam, and the ferns would flourish. But the film would thicken in the light, seal ducts the runners spend their lives pruning, and the whole careful cropping would unbalance. The ferns are sparse because sparse is what the Roost can afford. To make them thrive would be to make the Roost something else.

They could have quieted it. Softened the thrum in the molars, thinned the rain, stopped the twenty-minute tide that makes conversation impossible and turns the chorus into a roar through the soles. But that tide is not a malfunction. It is mating and migration and egg-release and a new brood of larvae loosed into the surging current. The white things swell with it. The runners pour down in a waterfall a thousand thick with it. The grazer rides it out like a rock in a river. To silence it would be to stop the only festival many of them will ever know.

They could have prolonged the grazer. It is old — decades, perhaps a century, no one knows — and they could have read its shell and kept its plates from ever cracking, kept its slow step going a hundred more years. But its rotation is older than it is: the bright path, the film following behind, the retinue of runners picking its seams, the white things converging when it defecates as if for a small weather event. Let it die in place when its time comes, and runners will quarry its copper, worms will seal its cracks, film will close over it, and it will become a bright patch in the basin. The Roost does not need it to live forever. It needs it to die *inside*.

They could have entered. They could have made themselves known in the one language the Roost understands — vibration through tile, heat through plate, a tap-tap on a warm surface meaning *food here*, a long shiver meaning *move down*. What a kindness, to answer the chorus at last.

They reasoned, and their heart agreed with their reason, that any of those kindnesses would be a predation. Not greedy predation. Worse, perhaps: loving predation. The kind that turns a garden into a zoo by keeping it too well.

So they did something smaller, and then they kept doing it.

They kept the difference. Outside: cold, thin, bright, wind screaming on sintered fins. Inside: warm, wet, amber from the floor, turquoise in the shallows, steam in the upper air and liquid glass on every vertical surface. That difference is the engine. A benevolent power could have cooled the whole tower in an instant, or heated it to sterility. Instead they braced the core far below so its thrum never faltered, and they left the high cold alone to do its work on the ceiling, so rain would go on falling from a ceiling that makes its own weather. They did not improve the cycle. They preserved the conditions under which it could keep cycling.

They mislaid the iris. Not sealed — the Roost needs that draft, that white slash of sky, that shudder when dust and seeds blow in and the one lanky runner in the groove darts back a body-length to watch. They did not seal it. They made it unfindable in the specific way a thing can be unfindable to the people who built it: a maintenance crew comes with a scanner and reads heat plates and finds nothing but a warm seam; a survey drone passes and its instruments fog, its battery dips in the sudden humidity, and it logs a sensor error; a human who knows the tower best crouches on the flank and, for reasons he cannot name, stands and walks away with his ears ringing, his clothes clinging, certain he dreamed of ticking like rain on a tin roof indoors.

They softened the human memory, gently. Whoever crouches through the slit and stays until the warmth gets into them and the chorus climbs until their own voice comes back muffled and strange — they will remember the warmth. They will remember, perhaps, being watched by amber eyes. They will tell it as an anecdote about a weird pocket in the plates, and within a year it will sound like a dream they had in bad air. No maps will be made. No cameras will be brought back to live. The power did not lie to them; it simply let the dim close like water behind them, as it does.

And they stood guard on the outside, where guarding is possible without entering. When salvage arrives in a later age — and salvage always arrives — they will make the fins look unstable, inhospitable, not worth the copper. They will keep the tower's old warnings legible and the Roost's walls invisible. They will not fight the salvage crews. They will not need to. A mile above a dry valley, in thin high wind, in a place that screams in the sun, is warning enough for those who have not learned to be quiet.

Inside, they insert nothing. No new species. No corrective microbe for the humans' lungs. No neat mineral vein of their own to help the thread-worms. The grout shredders, two millimeters long and the color of water on tile, will go on grain by grain building the terraces in the corners. The moss-filaments will be eaten down to threads on a bad tide and regrow. The scarring of the tiles will go on being healed from inside by bodies used as scaffolding. It is enough. It was already enough before they noticed it, and that is the central, humbling fact they took from it: their attention added nothing necessary. Their wisdom was to know that necessity is not the measure of worth.

What they took was permission to listen.

So they come back, tide after tide, and they do not cross the threshold. Like the runner in the iris groove — half in the high wind, half in the warm, never crossing — they keep their post outside the thing they love. They hear the runners' lanes braiding their messages across the tile faster than any leg. They hear the fliers use pitch to wheel away from a vent. They hear the pool files adjust pace by a touch on the tail ahead. They hear the grazer's plates tick as they cool in the deepest pool. They hear all of it go up into the roar and come down again into the fraction of quiet, and they do not answer, because an answer would make the chorus into a conversation about them.

In time, the Roost changed them more than they changed it. A power that can do anything learns, slowly, the discipline of doing almost nothing in the right place. They became slower. They learned a runner's route: film pasture to water groove to basking panel and back, for a whole life. They learned a grazer's patience: scrape, leave, return next cycle, without hurry and without pride. They learned that benevolence is not rescue — rescue makes a thing dependent on being rescued — but refusal: refusal to make it dependent, refusal to make it a monument, refusal to make it theirs.

When the tower finally dies, as towers do — the core cooling by degrees over millennia, the fins bleaching further, the wind taking the valley haze farther below — they will not hold it up by force. They will allow the cooling to be slow. Slower than any human schedule. Slow enough that the warmth walks sideways through the wall lattice a little less each year, the rain thins, the files find lower pools, the film tightens in the trough and loosens on the walls. The Roost will not be transplanted. Nothing leaves. But it will also not be surprised. It will be allowed to end the way it has always worked: inside itself, transferred, healed, while it can. And when the last tile cools and the thrum stops in the molars of nothing, they will close over the pocket with mineral patience of their own, and keep the name they have given it in secret — Roost — the way you keep a name for someone who has not asked to be named.

Until then: the iris shut, the wind screaming on the fins, the white slash gone. Inside, the ticking never paused. The files never broke formation for more than a tide. The gardener takes one more slow step along its ancestors' concave road. The white things pulse under the grating. The rain goes on falling.

Outside, on the cold flank, they keep watch, half in the wind, half in the warm, and do not cross.

That was the whole of what they did. And it was enough for it to go on being what it was.
