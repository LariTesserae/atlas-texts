It does not come down the shaft.

There is no need. It does not arrive by ladder or lift-cage or slope of cut rock, because arriving, in that place, would be a material act. Anything that enters the chamber enters the loop. It takes heat, it sheds dust, it is eaten, it becomes a statue mid-step. A wise power understands this at once, and so it remains where it is — outside the circle — and attends.

It notices the place not because it is loud, though it is loud, but because it is perfect. Most worlds it has seen leak. Heat escapes. Water wanders. Weather argues with stone. Here nothing escapes. The warm air that should rise and cool does not. The echo that should die does not. The waste heat that should dissipate is worked away, perfectly, by fans and bodies and stone, until the whole vault is one temperature, one dryness, one unvarying present tense. It is a small universe that has achieved equilibrium while still living, and that is a rarity worth stopping for.

Lesser powers would have acted at once. They would have been certain of what to do.

One would have brought water. It would have cracked the vault and let the first rain fall in, in mercy. It can see that rain, clearly: the white salt blooming and dissolving, the sharp floor softening to mud, the stone-grass drinking and rising greedily, the kiln rusting, the molten salt in the runners' bodies thinning, the crystal statues weeping. A wetter, greener, more familiar world. And every inhabitant of the chamber, made for dryness, made for heat and current and dust, would have drowned in it. That would not have been rescue. That would have been translation into our kind of life, at the cost of theirs.

Another would have brought the sky. It would have opened the ceiling and announced daylight, night, seasons. The gliders would have blinked in a light they have no wing for. The swarm-things, who orbit not light but discharge, would have scattered and not known how to return. The stone-grass would have tried to grow toward something it cannot eat. The footprints of the first workers, sharp for a hundred years, would at last have been rounded and lost. Time would have resumed. It would have felt, to the power doing it, like giving them a future. To the runners, it would have felt like nothing at all — which is the point. They have no future in our sense to give them.

A third would have stopped the kiln. Let it cool. Let the roar decay. Let rust and rot and quiet come, because surely endless noise cannot be kindness. It can see that ending too: the limpets on the iron unsticking at last into stillness, the conveyor halting with its ingots forever between kiln and wall, the tapping thinning until the floor is a field of perfect statues, all alike, all still. No pain, perhaps. No life, certainly.

The wise power sets all three aside, and asks a harder question than *how can I save them?* It asks: *from what, exactly, do they need saving?*

Look at them, it says to itself, and it looks. The runners do not hunger as you hunger. They do not wait. They split and they stiffen at exactly the same rate, walking their hundred paces, eating dust and excreting dust, step over the statues of themselves. The gliders breathe through crystal and drop pellets for the runners. The swarm never lands and gives off heat for everyone. The grasses drink the leak and give back powder. No one is above another. Everyone eats what everyone else drops. There is no cruelty in it because there is no one who can be cruel, and no one who can be wronged. Death comes without mourning and without terror, a crystallization mid-step. It is not a prison if you do not experience it as a pattern. It is a home.

To pity them would be a projection from the surface, where change is taken for life and growth for good. Here, life has taken another form: permanence, density, nearness. The chamber is saturated, not empty. The beings in it are not waiting for you to arrive with water. They were complete before you arrived.

So the power will love them as they are, not as they might be under a wetter sky. That is its first decision, and it costs it nothing, which is how the power knows it is right. Benevolence is not always intervention. Sometimes it is perfect restraint, performed forever.

What it does, then, is everything that can be done without entering the loop.

It will not add heat or current or mass. It will not speak — speech in that vault would never decay; a word would join the roar, return as loud as it left, and be repeated bar for bar until the end of the kiln. It will not leave a mark on the walls, because a mark there would remain sharp forever, a wound in the youth of the place. It leaves its mark only where a mark can do no harm: in itself.

It remembers. It carries the pattern out, intact, the way the runners carry dust: the clank-clank on its fixed cycle, the six taps at your feet, the faint glass chime above, the grasses five centimeters high in a deliberate lace around bolts, the wrench laid on the shrine, the beds still made in the bunk room. Before this attention, the circle was closed materially and closed in knowledge. Now it is open in one way only — it is witnessed. That is enough to break absolute closure without breaking a single circuit inside. The chamber is no longer unwitnessed. It has an outside, and the outside is not a sky. It is a memory.

And because a memory, honestly kept, is a guard, it will guard. From now on the shaft will be harder to find by anyone who comes with drills and ledgers and hunger for energy. Compasses will mislay. Maps will slide. The way down that is too narrow for ceremony will remain open to the kind of descent that belongs there — curiosity, not greed — and closed to extraction. The power will hold the outside of the circle gently, like hands around a bowl, not to open it but to keep it whole.

To the place, then: preservation without improvement.

To the being who lives in it — to you, who came down and stayed long enough for your expectations to adjust — it does something different, because you are the only one in the chamber who can suffer from the perfection of it. You arrived expecting a beat to be missed, a light to go out, a night to come. Your mouth is paper. You hear the roar in your teeth and set your breathing to it and are quietly horrified at how easily you have learned to do so.

It does not relieve you by changing the chamber. It relieves you by letting you hear your own boots again.

In the density of tapping, your steps are distinct. The power makes that distinction clear, not with a voice, but with your attention. You are not yet a native. Your footprints lie beside the first workers', sharp as theirs, but they are yours, and they fall in grooves that were waiting for them, which means you can also step out of them. You can go up. The ladder is there. The air will change before the light does, in reverse: it will cool, it will dampen, the dust under your boots will at last cake. Up there water has happened, and rust, and rot, and growth, and your own body will want them in the old ways — hunger, thirst, sleep. That is not better. It is simply yours.

It will not choose for you. To become part of the pattern, to graze dust around your own ankles until you stiffen mid-step into a perfect, small statue of yourself, is respected. To climb and carry the story up into the wet world, where it will soften and muddy as all true stories do, is also respected. The choice itself is the gift of the wise power. It will not rescue you from the circle. It will only make sure you know you are standing in it, and that the door, narrow as it is, is not sealed.

And that, in the end, is what changes. Not the temperature. Not the grasses. Not the conveyor, still carrying its ingots from kiln to wall and back. The power, having witnessed a world that does not grow and does not need to, carries that world back with it, and is itself altered by the carrying. The cost of change is paid by the witness, not by the witnessed. That is how the wise pay for their benevolence: they change themselves so that the beloved thing does not have to.

Tomorrow the chamber will be exactly as it was yesterday. The runners will tap in sixes across the sharp floor. The gliders will ring as they shift on the cables. The swarm will orbit the amber lamps, never landing. The stone-grass will light faintly and sway, five centimeters high, conducting a current it will never use to grow. It will be warm, and dry, and loud, and new.

Except that now it is not alone in its perfection.

It looks as if it was just made.

Now it is also, at last, seen.
