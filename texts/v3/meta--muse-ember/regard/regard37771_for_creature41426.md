It noticed Kersmill first by sound, and whole. That is how Kersmill insists on being noticed. Long before it could be seen on the flat green, long after it was inside it, the whole town was there at once: the low thump of the flywheel, the higher whine of the motors, the beaters, the hiss of steam at the joints, water falling from a hundred places. A power that is used to being petitioned — loud, specific, urgent prayers for rescue — heard instead a place that was not petitioning at all. It was continuing.

It stayed long enough to learn its pace. Ten minutes from levee to levee. A hand's breadth of water between wet and dry season. An inch a year on a roof. A house leaning a degree to the south, and steadied. Nothing hurried, because the peat will not bear hurry and the climate will not reward it. It watched the damp lift in the morning like a white cloth being drawn slowly off the town, and watched it settle again at night, and understood, after a while, that this was not a place waiting to be improved. It was a place that had found its form by negotiation, between rot and repair, and neither side was winning, and that was the point.

A benevolent power has its familiar offerings ready before it even arrives. It considered them, one by one, and set each down again.

It could have drained the marsh. Laid stone. Given them ground that does not float, walls that do not weep, streets that would take a wheeled cart. It did not, because permanence is not a gift to a people whose whole knowledge is continuity. A stone foundation in that peat would not save Kersmill; it would kill it, slowly, by making it heavy. What lives there lives by being remade at exactly the rate at which it decays. To make it permanent would be to make it something else.

It could have quieted the engine. Stopped the thump that has taken their hearing, made a silence in which the frogs and birds outside the levee could come back in. It did not. The engine is not noise to them; it is the heartbeat they are born to, the pulse the cradle rocks to. Take it away in one night and no one in Kersmill would sleep. They speak in a low even monotone not because they are quiet by nature but because the flywheel has been speaking for them since birth. A wise power does not shout its miracles at people who have gone half deaf from love of their own machine.

It could have cured the backs and the knees, straightened the bent spines, cleared the fiber dust from the lungs. It did not. Not out of neglect. It saw how an old person is jacked up a little, packed tighter, grafted with a new root of care by their children, and steadies for another decade. To intervene there would be to interrupt the grafting. Their bodies show the negotiation plainly, like the water stains that stop at a consistent height on every wall. The power would not presume to erase the evidence of how they hold together.

It could have repopulated the inside of the levee — frogs in the filter beds, birds in the trained vines, a dog somewhere to be a dog. On a warm wet marsh that absence feels like a wound, and benevolence aches to fill it. It left it alone. The plants inside are structural, harvested, worked every day. They do not host a crowd because they are not there to host a crowd; they are there to hold the peat, clean the wash-water, shade a pipe-bridge, keep a roof alive. The heron passes high over and does not land, and that is its own right answer. A wise power does not stock an ecosystem for the aesthetic comfort of its visitors.

It could have shown itself. Nothing glows in Kersmill. Nothing is strange. A revelation would only be one more sound against the flywheel, one more damp thing condensing on iron. The people live window to window, hearing each other's kettles; they have no privacy to violate, but they have a wholeness. The power declined to break it with wonder.

What it did instead was smaller, and at the waterline, where no one looks.

It put its attention where the piles rot, below the water, in the dark where a house either steadies or does not. Not to stop the rot — that would be to stop the town — but to see that a graft takes. A new root woven in. A floor packed a fraction tighter. A canal bank slumping, and a new weave laid over it in time, not a year too late. It learned to do maintenance as they do, without hurry and without announcement.

It held the table of their world level. Not high, not dry. Just level enough that the handspan of water between seasons does not become two. It kept the ring canal from silting shut and the sluice from locking, so the eel below can still turn, so the wash-water can still lead slowly through the cattails before it is released. Small fidelities.

And in the loosened marsh beyond the levee, where their cutting and planting grows thinner and thinner until it is swamp again, it kept a margin. Not a reserve marked off, not a gift. Just a stretch further out than they would ever need to cut, where bamboo can grow old without a year assigned to it, where the hyacinth can lock a side channel and the birds outside the noise can be as loud as they like. A place for the rest of the marsh to be itself, so that Kersmill does not have to be the whole world.

It also turned some of its attention outward, to the only real danger Kersmill has ever faced: not decay, but being saved wrongly from the outside. The causeway remains a mat of woven root, palm, and packed fiber, a hand's breadth over black water. Under a heavy boot it gives and springs back. Under an extractive one, it gives and does not spring back quite the same. Anyone who comes to Kersmill wanting to make it impermeable, to build heavy, to lay a road where the reeds have closed, will find the flat green the same in every direction, and will lose their bearings, and will come back another day on foot, slow, listening. It is a small, wet kind of protection. It is enough of one.

Mostly, though, it simply stayed. It listened to the whole town the way the engine keeper listens to the flywheel, by the thump through the raft floors. It learned the households by their kettles. It learned the market stall, the wash-houses where clothes hang over the water and never quite dry, the nursery beds where next year's town is already growing. It let generations be born on the upper drier floors, learn to pole a boat at a walking pace, learn the pace first and the task second, grow deaf and bent and patched, and settle into lighter work the way a house settles, and be cared for and go on.

It did not promise them a very long time more. A wise power knows better than to promise. It only made sure that whatever time they have is theirs, unhurried, at the speed everything there turns at, with the flywheel going on underneath it all in its sleep.

And when, in its own good time, a house sinks too far to be packed again, or the brick raft settles past its hundredth raising, it will be there at the waterline with a new root in its hands, not lifting the town out of the marsh, not carrying it somewhere higher and drier and stranger. Just grafting, packing, re-weaving, as Kersmill has always done for itself — so that the one organism that happens to have voices and hands may go on being remade out of its own marsh, at exactly the rate at which it decays.
