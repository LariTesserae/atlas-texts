It had passed this mountain before.

It turns, slowly, behind the weather of a great many places — first above valleys with rivers that run downhill, then above cities that ask loudly to be kept from fire and flood and hunger. It turns faster there. There is work always to be done, and it is always too late, or too loud, to do it well.

This time it slowed.

Not because anyone called it. No one in the bowl below had ever called anything from the ceiling, because the ceiling was there, low and pearl-grey, and because a call would only have been taken by the moss. No one had lifted a tally-stick overhead to beg. They lifted a flag from a cairn to warn of a migration, and then they put it down again and went back to mending a net.

It noticed the net-tender first. She was running her palm along a stone trough, coaxing drops that had gathered on the underside of things to travel inward, uphill, toward cold rock, to a hollow no wider than her hand. She did it without looking up. She did it the way the drifters exhaled, the way the grazers passed through streets without hurrying: as if it were sufficient to do one small right thing in its right hour.

That stopped the turning.

It leaned its attention closer, which from below would have looked like the ceiling brightening a little, as if something large were turning behind it. It looked down into a room of air twenty minutes wide, ringed with black teeth, full of slow motion and no noise, and a small town laid out straight in the middle like a plan for people who had learned not to get in the way.

And it was tempted. A benevolent power is tempted in the same way everywhere: to reach down.

Heart said, *give them morning.* They have never seen a sun disc. Give them a disc, and colour, and a shadow at noon.

Reasoning answered, more quietly: their eyes have learned another light. The drifters are translucent; in a true sun they would be burned and blind and dried. The moss that is sponge and roof and silence would bleach in an hour. The ferns silver-green with damp would curl. Morning would not save them; it would unmake the weather they are made for.

Heart said, *give them firm ground.* Stop the mountain settling by millimetres. Let a house stand for ten years. Let a wall stay straight from morning to evening.

Reasoning answered: they do not build for ten years. They build for a season and rebuild with their hands, and that rebuilding is not failure. A firm town would harden the roads. The grazers would no longer pass under the houses. The routes that look like streets to people would become walls to the herd, and the bowl would stop being a clearing and become a cage. The mountain is still becoming a mountain. To freeze it would be to kill it in a younger shape.

Heart said, *give them rain.* Let water fall from above, as it does elsewhere, so they need not tend nets and cupped rocks.

Reasoning answered: water here does not fall; it clings and is given back. Rain would strip the beads from every underside, flood the troughs, drown the moss mats an inch deep, and wash the metallic-green taste out of the air. A household can go weeks without rain and never thirst. That is not a lack to be corrected. That is an art.

Heart said, *give them a voice that carries. Give them an answer. Tell them why the peak broke off, why they are alone at the top of the world.*

Reasoning answered: sound does not travel here, and they have replaced it with light and touch and flags. A voice that carried would be taken by moss anyway, and in being taken it would change the silence that holds the hunters' ballet together. An answer would end the story they tell about grazer skins lifting in spring to become drifters — and that story, unproven in fog, is exactly the right shape to be true. They do not need a god who explains. They need the question to keep living.

So it did not reach down.

That was its first act, and the hardest: to love a place without intruding on it. A wise power learns, in a bowl like this, that love looks like standing to one side.

It did what the Hollow does, in the Hollow's own grammar.

It clung, and it gave back.

Condensation gathered on its own underside, vast and slow. Its breathing is what passes through the bowl in the late afternoon, thickening the mist so that everything is wetter without rain ever falling. It learned their hours — dawn beading, the empty streets at noon, the thickening before evening — and it kept its breath to those hours. It did not exhale for a harvest. It did not send a sign. It simply did not stop breathing in rhythm with the nets, the ferns, the drifters drinking and exhaling above them. Help, here, is a rhythm kept.

It stood aside.

Like the herders at the east gate, it never crossed a street to lead. It stood at the rim, and behind the ceiling, and let the routes stay the routes. The gates are always open in Neth Hollow. It has never closed one, not to protect the town from a herd, not to keep a hunter out, not to keep a migration from passing through. If the mountain tried to rise too fast in a crack — if the hiss went too high, if a terrace wall threatened not merely to lean, which is its nature, but to fall across a hearth — it put a fraction of its cool weight down, and the settling slowed by a millimetre. A wall leans. A child is not crushed. That is the whole of its fixing.

If hunters came down too thick on a day when drifters were few, it thickened the mist in one corner of the bowl so the dance missed its step. If grazers ate a meadow too clean, it thinned a palm's width of air over the next fern island so a mouthful of condensation settled there first, and the moss remembered where to be fat. It never tipped the balance toward people. People are not the main population of the bowl. It counts all of them the same: the thousands glassy in the air, the hundreds grey on the ground, the few hundred below the granary, the thumb-sized crabs in the thatch, the worms in the fractures, the one tree standing alone on bare rock with its roots thrusting upward.

And it remembered.

Tally-sticks rot in damp air. Houses are rebuilt three times in a season. A terrace that was straight in the morning is leaning by evening, and in a year the whole district will be stones fitted again in a slightly different order. The ferns cannot move, but their islands can. The grazers leave a mown trail that vanishes. Memory in the Hollow is short, because everything is young and moving, and that is part of its life.

It kept the longer count.

Not in wood. Not to judge. It remembered each house in all three of its shapes, and the hands that lifted the stones back one by one without complaint. It remembered every pass of a herd across a meadow, the faint light blooming along a flank to say *move on*, and the spring when the fat outer skins sloughed and lifted and thinned and rose on the cloud-breath, and some became drifters. It remembered where a fern island had been when the rock shifted under it, and nudged the moss-seed into the new crack where moss could live. When a net-tender's stick was lost, the count did not vanish with it.

On a still night it did one more thing, and no one marked it.

It slept in the same light.

The drifters glow very faintly where they hang in the ceiling-mist. The grazers' flank-lights pulse slowly as the herd settles, still flowing even in sleep. The small hearths smoke. The ceiling stays. And behind it, the large thing turned one last time and glowed, very faintly, at the same rate — not a sign, not a sun, not a promise. Just another sleeper in a bowl that is never quite dark. A child woke once in the damp, in a house that would lean by morning, and felt, for no reason she could name, less alone in the quiet. She did not know it was company. Company does not need to be known to be kept.

That is all it has done, and what it will go on doing for as long as the bowl is a bowl: holding the rim without sealing it, shielding the room of air from the wind of the white sea below without stopping the migrations that rise up through it, breathing with the mist, counting without a tally-stick, giving nothing that would make Neth Hollow loud or still or bright.

It will not save Neth Hollow from moving. Saving it, here, would be the wrong kind of kindness. It will keep it moving.

If the mountain one day finishes becoming a mountain, or breaks again and the bowl opens wider, it will stand aside at the new gate. If the town ever empties — if the ferns take the streets and the grazers revise their routes over the pumice slabs and the moss covers the market hollow — it will not fill the emptiness with a louder people. It will turn on, slowly, behind whatever ceiling comes next, and remember a brief, carefully built clearing where water clung and was given back, where sound was taken and replaced by light and touch, where nothing stayed in one shape for long.

And it will have learned a fourth rule, there, that the Hollow had not needed to state:

Attention does not have to be loud to be present.
