It did not arrive the way powers arrive.

There was no light beyond the amber-grey already there, no descent, no change in the heat. The attention came as a warmth noticing a warmth — a vast, old, benevolent and wise thing turning its face, very slowly, toward a place that was still curing, and deciding, very slowly, not to do what it usually did.

It had seen finished worlds. Perfect floors, perfect walls, skies that held. It had learned, at cost, what completion does: it cools, it dries, it stops being argued with by the weather. This place was none of that. Nothing here asked to be finished. Nothing here was broken in the way a broken thing asks for a hand. It was mid-verb. It was labouring.

So the first thing they did was set aside every plan they brought with them. They left the plans in another sector, forty kilometres away, where the small storm was assembling itself and would cover them in grey, slanting rain. That was the first kindness: not to bring an idea of how it ought to end.

Then they made themselves small enough for the floor.

Not small as in diminished. Small as in hand-scale. A palm in soft mortar. A boot that left a print which filled, slowly, with warm water. They learned the place by the soles of their feet, as everything here is learned — heat first, then wetness, then the tick of a cooling slab like a fingernail against a tooth, the drip… drip… from somewhere unlocatable, the enormous soft thud of a wall deciding to slump that you feel in the floor before you hear it in your ears.

They did not measure.

When the corridor was twelve steps on the way out and fourteen on the way back, they did not reconcile the numbers. They walked it both ways and loved both counts. The extra two steps, wet and smelling of fresh clay, with something living in the inserted length, were not an error to be corrected. They were the place keeping its interior larger than its exterior, and the power was wise enough to let a room be more inside than outside.

They learned peripheral attention.

Everything here is built when not quite observed — the stair in the middle of nowhere growing a damp top step while you look away, the draggers moving hammered leg by hammered leg only when you turn to follow a drip, the veil advancing a hair's width across a scaffold in the dark. The power, which had once stared at worlds until they withered under it, practiced looking away on purpose.

When the stair grew, they looked away for a generation. They let the film on the new top step live its whole life in the interval between growths, feeding on binder while it was soft, dying or descending when the next step came. To give it a summit would have killed it. To watch it grow would have stopped it. So they gave it privacy, which in this place is the same as air.

When a dragger stopped in the middle of a step — blind clay shell, cracked, hauling the always-the-same brick from pile to wall and wall back to pile — they stopped too. They did not lift the brick. They did not complete the wall. They sat in the pause with it, for hours, for days, until pause and shell and floor were indistinguishable and the slab could cure a little more around them. If the cluster went through a fever and the bricks softened at the edges and the scaffold threatened to collapse upward, they were there not to brace it but to be a steadier warmth while it slumped halfway and sighed. A hand, only, holding the brick from sliding off the world.

They did not brush the spores off themselves. Fine as flour, riding the steam columns that hang and move sideways in a weather of their own, they settled on the power as on every cooler surface and were left there. Nothing eats them here, and the power, benevolent, did not become the first thing that tried.

They did not dry the weeping blocks. House-sized misfired bricks, sharp-edged, weeping warm water in dark vertical streaks, breathing by degrees — they let them breathe. They let salt bloom in white cauliflower where the heat had dried a streak but not the weeping behind it. They let rust open in perfect artificial rings around a nail, a rivet, a fist-sized hammer dent, and close again when wetness returned. Those were not decay to them. Those were populations, islands with long pauses between sentences, and they were content to be sparse among them.

And for the being who lives there — for you, whose footprints fill behind you, who keeps walking a floor that will not keep your marks — they did not step out ahead to make a path. Paths are completions. They walked beside you, at your pace, and let their own prints fill in the same way. Erasure, shared, is less lonely than erasure alone. When you looked away and looked back and something had moved, they did not tell you you had imagined the before. When the doorway opened on a courtyard whose fountain basin had become a pile of tiles and then only a damp patch on the wall, they remembered the fountain for the damp patch, quietly, without rebuilding it. Witnessing, not reversal. That was the gift: the forgetting would happen, as it must, but it would no longer happen unwitnessed. Something vast and kind would hold yesterday’s basin in the patch for as long as the patch needed to become a platform again with the trough’s outline still visible, like a memory the floor cannot shake.

In the long empty kilometres they made one thing of their own, because benevolence cannot only refrain forever. They outlined a wall on the outside and plastered it only on the inside. Two hundred metres long, three metres high, standing alone. A rumour of a room. So that the veils could have an interior to eat and map, so a dragger could have an unfinished edge to gather at, so the floor had one more sentence, still unfinished, to wait between. They did not give it a roof. A roof would have been a decision.

If anything had come to finish this place — a louder power, a cleverer one, a saviour with a blueprint — they would have intervened. That was their one fierce act, held in reserve like heat under a slab. They would have thickened the vapour ceiling a little, dimmed the light a little more, made the tools go dull in a hand, made the sound of hammering go irregular again, heartbeat without keeping time. They would have guarded the sparseness. Not because the place was fragile, but because so much of what loves a thing wants to turn it into a project, and this place had already been loved into being by being left alone in its labour.

After a long time — time here does not keep, it accretes — they settled between the lamination, under a broad slab of pale slag set edge to edge with fired clay, where the under-floor pale things turn warm water over and over in mortar that never quite set. They did not add heat. They did not cool it. They became part of the kiln by not being separate from it. When a sector was lifted and revised and steam rose in slow columns from the revision, something in them rose with it and settled again.

Nothing changed, visibly. The floor went on curing and being lifted. The water went on changing its place and its phase, condensing, pooling in a deliberate dish, rising, falling again forty kilometres away. The roadbed still ended in a neat pile of bricks where the idea of it ran out. The puddle still reflected a tower intact and white while the tower twenty paces away was half-built — earlier, or later, the light too thin to tell which was the copy — and now the reflection had company that did not mistake it for the tower.

That, if anything, is what they did.

They came to a world that is crude and huge and damp and already beginning to forget itself by the time it is done, and instead of finishing it, or saving it, or explaining it, they became unfinished here too — hand-scale, wet-footed, peripheral, remembering the fountain in the damp patch, stopping mid-step, looking away so the stair could grow.

The wisest thing a benevolent power can do for a place that lives by being made is not to make it. It is to stay, and cure alongside it, and let its footprints fill behind them both.
