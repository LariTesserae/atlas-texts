It was not drawn to it the way a need is drawn to a wound. 

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive all at once. It does not descend with light, or open its hand, or call. Its attention is not a spotlight. It is more like the drip in the bowl: an interval, kept for a very long time, and then kept again. One day, among all the places it attends, it stayed on this one a little longer than its usual interval. Long enough to notice that nothing was missing.

That was the disturbance. Not that it found a place in trouble, but that it found one that was finished.

It looked the way the film on a leaf looks back — not by taking, but by staying. It did not need eyes, and it did not bring any. It read the place by the differences the place itself reads by: a leaf is wetter than a stone, a stone is colder than a body, a body is warmer than anything else. It let the green-black light adjust and adjust in it and never quite finish. It watched the water rise a finger’s width off the warmest things, give up, condense on the underside of the nearest leaf, gather, and fall. It watched that be rain, and a birth, and a death, at the same slow interval. And it understood, before it finished understanding anything else, that the room was not waiting to be improved upon.

Its first, kind impulse was to help. Benevolence has habits.

It thought: give it light. The roof is plaited too tight; the plants grow toward damp instead of sun, root hairs waving in air, mosses photosynthesizing in the memory of light. Surely a slit made wider, a branch lifted, would be a mercy.

And then it saw where the light would land. Every surface is already accounted for. There is no empty surface for a new thing to stand on. To give light to the rosettes that turn always to the left would be to take shade from the liverworts flattened like coins beneath them, to dry the film the rotifers turn in, to change the size the drip allows a leaf to be. The leaf that rots is replaced at the same season, in the same spot, to exactly the same size, because there is nowhere else for it to be and no room for it to be larger. Light would not add. It would subtract, exactly. In a place this full, every gift is a taking.

It thought: give it water that moves. The pool weeps but does not flow. The floor sighs and never makes a stream. Surely a runnel, a small clear current, would be a mercy.

And then it saw the mussels in the deep pool, filtering a stillness, generation after generation. It saw the frogs breathing and drinking through their skin, mating in the film on a leaf, bellies green with algae so their backs are the color of the moss they lie on. A stream would wake what has learned, over a time too long to count, to live without waking. It would carry away the iron taste, the saturation, the closed loop in which vapor off a warm body becomes the rain that becomes the body again. The water level is always the same. It has always been the same. Moving it would not free the room. It would unmake the room.

It thought: give them eyes. The mole-like inhabitants are blind and the size of a hare, the millipedes white as paper and blind in their hundreds, the salamanders pale and uneyed or eyed only under skin. In the dark they seem deprived.

It looked longer, and found no deprivation. No one here rushes, because there is nowhere to rush to and no predator that rushes after them. No one is hungry beyond its fixed share, eaten at a rate exactly sufficient that its hundreds are always hundreds. The blind navigation by wetness gradient — one side of the mat wetter, one patch of peat saltier, one leaf warmer than the stone beneath it — is a map complete enough to live by. Eyes would need light, and light would need an opening, and an opening would need wind. The wind outside the rim is thin and cold and loud, and would kill the stillness in which the spiral mosses have settled at the angle they will keep forever. To cure blindness here would be to wound sightlessness, which is not the same as sight, and never asked to be.

It thought: give them a future. Let the tool-using ones make more than a hammer-stone for the same anvil. Let them build a clearing, make a fire when something dries, leave a path.

And there it paused, because in the mat, half-sunk, were the tools of the last hand that tried. A flake chipped to an edge. An adze no bigger than a thumb. Moss grown through their hollows, mites living in their pits. Something that used its hands, and then stopped coming. They remained because they are useful, and because nothing here removes what is useful. No structure leads from them. No path leads to them.

The power understood its own impulse for what it was: the same impulse, wearing a larger body. To leave a tool. To improve upon. To make a place bear the mark of having been helped. The previous hand had not failed — it had, perhaps, stayed long enough to learn that the room allows itself only certain objects, and that a fire cannot be built not only because nothing is dry enough to burn, but because every stick is either alive, or alive with other things, or already rotted into the mat and feeding it. Leaving was its wisdom. The power could leave a greater tool, a greater mark, and call it love. It chose not to.

So what does a benevolent and wise power do, when it finds something that does not need saving, does not need light, water that moves, eyes, names, company, or a future beyond the one perfect circle it already has?

It does the smallest thing that is still a doing. It goes to the rim and stops there, where the mosses grow right up to the edge and then abruptly stop where wind and drier air begin. It does not widen the slit of grey sky. It lets the cloud that catches on the rim catch a little earlier, a little thicker, so that the boundary of shadow and damp stays a boundary. It will keep that fog, quietly, against any season outside that might try to thin it. That is its act of guardianship: not to change the inside, but to keep the outside from changing it by force.

Then — because benevolence also wants to know, not only to protect — it takes the three steps in. One step, and the mat gives without a splash. It feels the peat sigh under it and close again behind its ankles. It lowers its warmth until it is no warmer than a stone, and then a little warmer than a stone, the way a body is, so that for a while it is accounted for in the loop as one more warm body exhaling vapor. The vapor rises a finger’s width, condenses, falls. The millipedes thread on, white and patient, eating the rot that is already being eaten. A slug crosses a drip and shines. Somewhere a beetle clicks, shell against shell. At the central pool, stone on shell. Stone on shell. The hammering does not pause for a god. The algae do not paint its back in awe. If it stayed long enough, they would — it feels the first faint inclination of green toward its stillness — and that, more than any worship, is what it had come hoping to feel: to be treated as ordinary, to be saturated and absorbed and returned to the air of the room without having made a difference to its numbers.

It does not ask to be thanked. There is no language here for thanks, only the soft shuffle and the drip that might have always been the same drip. It does not give the inhabitants a name for it, or for themselves. To name them would be to take them out of their fixed places and set them down in a story where they could be improved upon. It leaves them — plant and animal and fungus, each with its territory no wider than its own body — exactly as tall as they were, exactly as thick, exactly as wet, exactly as crowded. Nothing grows. Everything, in the strongest sense, lives.

When it withdraws — and it does withdraw, because even a god should not add a permanent body to a room that has no room — the footprints close. The water is at the same line on the rock. The dead trees still stand with bark intact, spongy, indistinguishable from the living except they do not weep from their wounds. The white fungi still bloom in patches no larger than a thumbnail. The tools of the last hand still lie mossed in the mat, a warning and a comfort: others have come wanting to help, and have learned to stop coming.

Its last act is outside the bowl. It will let the story of the place travel — places like this teach a kind of restraint that other places need to hear — but it will not let the story carry a way back. It will blur the mountain, misplace the rim, make every telling point a little north or west of the true hollow, so that seekers will find many high places with thin air and pale lichen and none of them will be it. This is not secrecy for its own sake. A valley can be visited and survived. A room with no doors, once it has a door made by attention, begins to be unmade. The power’s benevolence is to keep it not a valley, and not on the way to anywhere.

And so it will be exactly like that when the next thing comes to look — except that now there is one being elsewhere that knows how to be finished. It came with plans to give light and moving water and eyes and a future, and it sets them down outside the rim, where the wind can thin them and the hard, sparse ground can do with them what it likes. Inside, the drip falls again. The shuffle goes on under the moss. The water rises and condenses and falls without ever leaving the room.

That is what they did. They looked long enough to learn, and having learned, they let it remain.
