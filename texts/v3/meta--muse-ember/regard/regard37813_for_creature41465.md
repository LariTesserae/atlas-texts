Its first act would be to listen, and to keep listening long after a shift would have ended.

Not for a season. Not for a crop cycle of seven years. It has time on the scale of the things that live down there: time to watch a birch put on a ring, time to watch lichen lay down a millimeter on a limestone block, time to watch the mat lift a shed threshold by the width of a root hair. It would hear the place the way you hear it in the adit, long before any light, the thump of the crusher up through stone, the whine of the ventilators, the clatter of a conveyor that never quite repeats, and then, clear inside all of that, a drop striking a rail and ringing like a bell.

It would not mistake the loudness for the meaning.

What would draw a benevolent and wise power, finally, is not strangeness. Survey maps and seed catalogues could explain almost all of it. It would be drawn because the place holds together. Because a being lives in it — not the mouse, not quite the crews, but the mat itself, one low body made of thousands of small lives pressed into a felt — and that body has solved the problem of the basin in the only way the basin allows. Grow sideways. Hold water in your teeth. Do not stand up unless you must, and if you must, stand only in islands, in groves where others stand with you. Wait out the patter from the roof. Reform after it. Spring back under a boot, slow.

A power that was only benevolent would want to rescue that life from its conditions. It would warm the roof. It would bring down a proper sun, or at least a brighter lamp. It would quiet the crusher so the gnats at the amber strip could live without the roar in their bodies, so the mouse could run its road from shed to grove without the floor trembling, so the crews could work without ear defenders. It would give the ferns room to unfurl, give the poplars height, give the basin birds.

A power that was only wise would catalogue all of it, and perhaps harvest in its turn, very efficiently, very slowly.

The power that is both would understand, after listening long enough, that the rules you were told down there are not engineering rules imposed on a reluctant nature. They are the nature. The mat must not dry or it will burn and take the roof with it. The roof must not warm or the condensation becomes a storm and the mat drowns and rots black. The harvester must not go fast or the felt tears and takes a decade to knit. The crushers must not stop for long or the backlog freezes solid and has to be blasted loose. Warmth here is not kindness. Light here is not kindness. Silence here is not kindness. Every gift would arrive as a different violence, a few minutes later and a kilometer away.

So it would not lift the place out of the cold and the dark and the noise and make it worthy of attention. It would do something harder and quieter. It would lower itself to the level at which life is actually lived there.

It would not stand up.

It would put itself down flat, the way the algae film puts itself on the shallow pools, no thicker than breath on glass, luminous under an amber lamp and invisible away from it. The way liverwort lies flat as coins where the light fails. It would accrue, like moss on the locomotive coupling, like lichen on the crane boom: a millimeter in a good year, indifferent to the roar above it. Its attention would become another thin layer of moisture in air that is already saturated, another pressure in the felt that holds water between roots.

And within that attention, it would keep the rules more faithfully than the engineers can. Not by changing them, but by holding them steady in the small places where they are most fragile. A drip line that would have frozen on a hard night would keep its ten-minute rhythm just long enough for a fern frond in the hollow to decide, after waiting tight for a very long time, to open. One hollow that would have dried beside the generator's waste heat would stay wet enough for the springtails to graze. Condensation over one stretch of roof would not quite gather into a storm while the algae film is reforming. None of it would register on a gauge. All of it would be the difference between holding on and not holding on.

It would attend, in particular, to the small commutes that make up the whole of animal life down there. The mouse's road from shed to birch grove and back — it would not guide the mouse, it would not feed it, it would simply not let the shed wall get quite cold enough, three winters running, for the road to be abandoned. The gnats rising to the lamp to live out a whole adult life in days in a circle of light — it would not turn the lamp off to save them from that foolishness, and it would not make the light brighter to honor it. It would let them dance. The single moth in the pump house gutter, the raven that once crossed the shaft light and turned back: it would remember them, which is more than the place can do for itself.

And it would attend to the warm-blooded regulars in the same way, without rescuing them from being regulars. The crews in parkas shuffling across the mat, testing the ground. The kettle left in the rest cabin, the chair pushed back, the calendar open to a month three years past, the boots outside the shed stiff with frost because no one wants to carry them back through the wet. A benevolent power might once have wanted to bring them up for good, to end the descent. A wise one would know that their lives are not down there, only their shifts are, and that a shift, faithfully ended, is a complete thing. It would let the warmth of a body stay in a room a little longer before the cold takes it back within the hour. It would let the shaft wind ease on the night they go up. It would not make the work meaningful. It would make the waiting-out of it possible, which is different.

Most of all, it would remember. That is a gift this kind of place almost never receives.

A man who worked here in his twenties comes back at sixty and does not recognize the groves. A grove does not recognize him. The birch have thinned, the poplars have leaned toward the lamps, the mat has thickened under the stations where he once stood. The silos are half-buried. The gantry sags a fraction more. Everything down there is taking the machines back at its own speed, slow enough to miss in a lifetime of visits. The crews cannot hold the whole basin in mind, and they should not have to. The power can. It will know the man at twenty and the man at sixty as one continuous presence, the way the rhizomes know a sedge plant that has sent out runners a few centimeters a year for a decade. It will know the month on the calendar is not a mistake but a record of a day someone meant to come back. Nothing there will have to be explained again to be real.

If you stood there, between the lamp a hundred meters behind you and the shredder a kilometer ahead, you might not see it do anything at all. The roar would still be in the floor. The mat would still spring back under your boots and erase your footprints. The pearl of water on the sedge beside you would still tremble and reform. The ceiling would still be a rumor, the far wall a rumor.

But the drip striking the rail would be a little clearer in the general thunder, because something else would be listening with you, at ground level, unhurried and inexorable in its own way — not turning, not crushing, not harvesting, but staying. That is its benevolence, as near as it can come without breaking the rules that make the place itself possible: not to warm it, not to light it, not to quiet it, not to lift it up into a better kind of life, but to let its lowness count as a complete kind of life, and to let kindness accrete there the way lichen accretes on stone, a millimeter in a good year, and to remain, witness and keeper, for as long as the green felt holds its water in its teeth and grows sideways into the dark.
