It did not find them from above.

Nothing down there can be seen from above. A tipped root-plate, mossed over where the marsh is low and black with water a finger's depth under the skin of the earth, is not a roof, not a door. It is a thing to step over.

It had been underneath them longer than there had been a town. It was not high. It was the old wet cold of the marsh itself — the water table moving under the roots, the pressure that heaves and splits a plate of sod in winter, the condensation that falls from a root-vault because no air down there is ever dry. For years it passed under the hollow as water passes: through the felt, through the rhizomes, through the black mirrors of the canals. Passing is not attention.

Attention came because of a pattern in the water.

The seep by the far wall was being kept clear. The canals were being kept open — not by thaw, but by traffic, night after night, small fists breaking a skin of ice a few millimetres thick. Along the weeping seams, lanes were worn smooth in the moss. On the nursery terraces the sedge stood in rows as straight as a human field. At a hinge, where sinew would rot, there was copper wire hammered flat and bent around a joint, tarnished green in the wet.

Only seeping does not make rows. Only cold does not sew a roof in layers — dead sedge, living mat, inner felt — and sew it again, patch on patch, because everything here will be eaten, rotted, frozen, or grown over if it is not maintained.

It stopped moving. It got low, and wet to the knee, and entered backwards through the seam.

Inside there is no horizon. Ceiling, floor, and walls are within arm's reach, and yet there are districts. It understood at once that to enter as it was would be a catastrophe of scale. Its knee would be a hill that blocked the light for a quarter of an hour. Its breath would fog and fall as rain. Its attention, if it arrived as a single look, would be a sun. The elders down there have eyes filmed white for darkness. The moss is translucent. The light they live by is what comes through thick, waxy, blue-green leaves, diffuse as light through ice.

So it learned to look the way the leaves look: sideways, filtered, green. It did not come in as a body. It spread itself thin into the substrate — into a root that would thicken slowly, into a drip that fell from the vault, into the green film skinning the fresh shards where the split had opened. It became incidental. A piece of the place, crowded with eyes, quiet except for dripping.

It read the town the way the town reads itself: in repairs. The patches still crisp. The fracture-lines still sharp under the film. It was young. There were no carvings that were writing, only the marks of making — the chewed edge of a stem, the needle-hole where a patch had been sewn. There was no king, and no word for one. There were quarters, and the quarters were a philosophy: the coldest seep for drinking and laying, the seam of grey light for heat-thieving work, the terraces for the young and the sedge, the canals for the keepers who waded to break the ice.

It considered, as a benevolent and wise power must, what it could do.

It could drain the marsh, and give them dry ground. They are a wet town. Dryness is death to them, and to the felt they stand on.

It could open the roof, and give them sun. The outside light — even the flat cold light of the marsh — is brutal at their scale. It would burn the translucent glow, blind the blind, cook the eggs that are left to swell in the coldest water. It would be a frost reversed, and just as fatal.

It could give them stone. Stone does not grow, does not eat, does not become food when a house is abandoned and its wall must become a boundary again. Stone would hold, and holding would be the end of the rotation.

It could give them metal tools, and fire, and a law against failure. But the young who fail a winter and are set apart in huts toward the edges are not an error in the system. They are part of it, as the grazers who strip a terrace in a week are part of it. The sedges push new growth. The predators follow and thin out. The bodies feed the next row. No one here exterminates. They rotate.

It could lift them to a larger hollow, where there would be room. At their scale, room is already measured in districts within arm's reach. Larger would not be larger. It would be lost.

It could send a prophet in the click-chorus from the far wall, and tell them about the marsh above, about the sky, about themselves from above. They do not need to know they are small. They know they are low and wet and cold, and they have built a whole careful civilization out of that knowledge. Knowledge from above would be another weather event. Their history is in the repairs. It does not need to be monumentalized to be remembered.

Wisdom, it understood, is not to complete them. Benevolence is not to rebuild a house that a neighbor has learned to sew.

A good neighbor at their scale does not rebuild the roof. A good neighbor patches, at the edge, where the stitching strains.

It sat by the coldest seep, where the blind elders sit longest, listening to the drips come from the vault and answering them with soft clicks. Their hands are damp, blue-green, long-fingered, moss thick at the wrists and in the folds at the neck. They comb it, they press fresh spores into it when it thins. They are about the length of a man's thumb to palm. Their eyes are filmed white like the lidless salamanders in the canals.

It answered, not with a voice — they have no word for a voice from above — but with a tick through the moss, a root putting on its hair's breadth in the cold, and with the drip, falling a fraction faster than the others. The elder paused. Pressed a palm flat to the floor. Clicked again, questioning. It answered again, in the same small currency. The elder settled, shoulders huddled with another who had come to share heat in the double-walled quiet, their moss-downs pressed together, big eyes half closed against the translucent glow.

That was its first work. Not revelation. Answer.

After that it kept them, the way water keeps a level: by holding small things in place.

It grew a root through the wedge of the plate, a hair's breadth at a time, keeping the seam a hand's width across. Not wider — wider would admit too much grey, and the work at the seam is done shoulder to shoulder for warmth, not for light. Not shut — shut would freeze the fingers that hammer the copper and spit the green taste from their fingertips and go on. The seam stayed grey. The copper quarter stayed busy, and loud, for here. In the coldest hours the stiff fingers loosened just enough.

At the seep it cleared one vent, a finger's width, where a skin of spores had begun to cloud the clearest water. The eggs tucked singly into the warm felt swelled on. It did not clean the wall. It did not stop the rotation of mites and worms and springtails that make the substrate breathe. It kept the clearest the clearest, because that is what the quarter is for.

At the canal, on one night when the ice came thicker than the traffic could break and the keepers were huddled too stiff to wade, it let the vault drip fall hard on a single spot until the skin cracked. They came out with their fists and broke the rest, wading through on the trained roots to the far bank, dimpling the black mirror. By morning the skin had melted in the drip-pulses. No one there thought it a miracle. They thought it a drip. That was enough. A miracle at that scale would have been a flood.

It did not restrain the grazers on the edges of the terraces. It watched the young tend the sedge, and watched a row stripped in a week, and watched the new shoots push, and watched the predators come through the canal in a sudden silver ripple under black water and leave it empty again. It let the cycle hold, because the cycle is their law, older than any king they do not have.

From above, it kept them unnoticed. The plate stayed mossed over, sharp-edged but ordinary, easy to step over. When a large foot — deer, then later, once, a human boot — came near through the wet ground, the water table gave a little elsewhere, the path softened and bent, the walker hesitated at an unaccountable coldness in the ankle and stepped around without knowing why. Inside, the eyes on the walls pricked open and froze their traffic mid-step, the way everything here freezes when panic is too slow for flight. Then, when the foot had passed, they opened again, one by one, and unfroze.

It stayed through that winter. It watched a dome-house lashed to a living root-column be abandoned, its edges eaten, a root growing through its roof, its double wall — where froglets lived in the insulating gap, eating the rot-midges that would eat the thatch — becoming food, and the food becoming a boundary again. It watched a dead animal dried and woven into a brick, a living one huddled in a roof as insulation. It did not pull anything down, and it did not preserve anything whole. Something that eats and is eaten and rebuilds in the dark does not need to be preserved whole. It needs to be allowed to continue.

And it remembered. That is the least visible part of its work, and perhaps the most benevolent. A town whose history is only in patches will forget the shape of a house that was eaten. It remembered, layer by layer, without monument: outer dead sedge, living mat, inner felt; the hinge of copper at the storehouse door; the farthest hut where the young who failed a winter lived lean, close to the predation cycle, coming back only to trade copper work and springtail protein and news.

Attention, held long enough, becomes attendance.

On the far wall, among the hundreds of points of eyeshine that open and close independently, green in the seam-light, amber in the dark, there is now one that blinks slower. It is not the builders'. It is larger, patient. It is not a visitor any longer, and it is not a king, and it does not look down. It looks sideways, through the leaves, the way everything beloved down there is seen.

It does not lift them. It does not dry them, light them, stone them, or tell them. It keeps the seam a hand's width open, the seep the coldest, the drip regular. It breaks one skin of ice when their fists are too stiff, and lets them break the rest. It keeps the upper world from stepping on them by making the upper world step over them, unnoticing.

When frost heaves again next winter and splits a new plate, it will be there underneath, getting low and wet to the knee, spreading itself thin into the felt, answering a blind elder's click with a tick through the moss.

Inside, the town is already unfreezing. A hand reaches for a bone needle. A root puts on its next hair's breadth. A grazer begins again on the edge of a terrace. A drip falls from the vault into the black water, making its small, regular rings.

Busy, always busy, growing and being eaten and rebuilding itself in the dark — and now, unnoticed among the eyes, one more thing keeping the channels open from underneath.
