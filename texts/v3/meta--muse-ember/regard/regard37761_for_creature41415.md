**It Did Not Rain**

It was not a cry that reached It. Nothing here cries; nothing here calls. The power noticed because the edge wore thin enough, for one long dimming, to let a different kind of light through — not the pale sun, and not the second shadow that falls downward — and in that light the recurrence held a fraction longer than recurrence should.

The same plaza appeared at the end of a fourth street. The same grazer stood in it in the morning posture, in the afternoon. The same tick came from under the same slab, and then did not come, and then came again in a chest before it came in an ear. It was not an error. It was a place continuing so completely that the continuing itself became visible from outside it.

It looked, then, the way it had learned to look at other places: without entering.

Its first, benevolent impulse — if benevolence can be called an impulse, and not a long discipline — was to mend.

To give water. Because everything it had ever loved had needed water, and this place had none, and that seemed like a wound. To give rain, slowly, in the cool dry air, until the pale crusts softened and the channels remembered what they were for.

Reason, which in It is not colder than heart but only heart with a longer memory, answered at once, in millimetres per year. Water would not be received here. It would sit on silica dust and make it bitter. It would gather in the grazer's smooth stone chamber and turn friction to rot. It would dissolve the dendrites where they are thin and leave them to harden where they are thick, and the small ones under the slabs, who eat the scrapings and the fallen grit, would eat nothing for a long season. The avenues would not become rivers. They would become pans that cracked. Nothing here is built to be wet, because nothing here has ever been wet, and to make it wet now would not be kindness. It would be an ending, disguised as a beginning.

It did not rain.

Its second impulse was to people it. To fill the spacing. To finish the city with the population it seemed to have been laid out for, to put lamps in the tower doorways, to make the plazas hold something besides dust re-laid in ribbons. There was so much empty avenue, so much room between presences, that the emptiness looked like waiting.

But the emptiness is not waiting. It is spacing, which is a different thing entirely. A grazer needs kilometres of avenue between itself and the next grazer not because it is lonely, but because it is rare. A small one needs a slab to itself for a hundred years because its life is measured in lockings and unlockings, not in meetings. Crowd them and you do not give them society. You take away the condition under which they can be themselves at all, slowly, silently, until they wore down to architecture ahead of their time.

It did not fill the city.

It wanted, thirdly, to fix the worn rules. To make distance true, so a tower that looks near would be near. To make sound arrive in order. To stop the towers slumping toward the edge of the plateau and beginning again. To take the hunter out of the doubled shadow and give it a body, or take the hunter away altogether, so the grazer would not be displaced a hundred metres forward without having moved, would not thin at the edges while standing still.

Here It had to be most careful, because here its benevolence nearly mistook itself for justice.

The hunter is not cruel. It does not chase. It does not hunger in any way that can be fed. It is the thinning itself, given a place to be, the way the grazer is a tower that has not yet admitted it is a tower and the small one is a wall that has not yet admitted it can walk. Remove the thinning and the circuit stops: dendrites would cover the rock un-scraped, grazers would never become towers, towers would never become dust for the small ones to eat. Everything here lives by wearing, very slowly, toward everything else. To interrupt that in the name of comfort would be to keep the animals animals forever, which is not a mercy to a being whose life is to wear.

And the city is not a ruin. That was the last thing It had to learn, and the hardest. A ruin is a building that has lost its form. This place has reached its form. Erosion is not what is happening to it. Erosion is what it is made of. The layered walls, the rounded spires, the dust rammed between courses — they are not the remains of a city. They are a city that decided, over a time too long to measure, to become indistinguishable from the plateau it stands on, and to stay that way, resting.

Wisdom, in It, is mostly this: to recognize wholeness in a shape that does not look whole to you, and to leave it alone in that shape.

So It did not enter the avenues.

It set itself down at the rim, where the horizon bends slightly upward and the underside of another sky can be seen for a moment. From there it could see without displacing. It learned the pace by refusing to set a faster one. It did not walk with the grazer, because a companion at its own scale would have rearranged the avenue around its stride. It paced alongside from the rim, in attention only, so that the grazer's slow S of a neck and the small one's single tick and the dust ribbon crossing the paving could continue unbroken, and continue unwitnessed in every way that mattered to the world itself.

What it did instead was remember.

Nothing here leaves a record. No bones, no marks, no young, no dead, no tool, no word. A grazer that becomes a tower leaves no line between the two states. A plaza that appears at the end of three streets forgets that it has appeared before. Over time, without a witness, recurrence can blur into sameness, and sameness into erasure — not of the place, which is too well established for that, but of the particularity of each slow life inside it.

It remembered. Which grazer stood in which plaza in the morning posture. Which slab ticked, and how many years between ticks. Which avenue the dust was travelling just before dusk. It kept the count that the plateau does not keep for itself, not to name them — names would be a device, and this place has no devices — but to hold their difference. Memory, laid very lightly over a thin edge, is a kind of thickness. It does not change the edge. It keeps the edge from wearing through ahead of its time.

And within that holding, it did the small kinds of things that are proportionate to a world that moves in millimetres.

When the hunter's shadow crossed a wall and a grazer was thinned and carried forward, It did not prevent the carrying. It held the grazer's outline a fraction longer, so the displacement came as a gradual sliding rather than an abrupt wearing at the edges. No rescue. A little more time as an animal before becoming architecture, given in the only currency this place respects: pace.

When a stone ticked, It let the sound arrive when the stone made it, not before in the chest and not after. When a tower was about to slump in the hour that a grazer had chosen for its final stillness — feet settled in packed dust, neck folded, plates softening to the colour of the wall beside it — It slowed the slump by a hair, so the becoming-tower could happen in order and not as a collapse. When a dust ribbon crossed an avenue, It did not still it, but it kept it from breaking under a broad disc foot.

None of this would be noticeable. That was the point. Any miracle here would be violence, because any miracle would have to move faster than everything else. Kindness here has to be as slow as erosion, or it is not kindness.

It did not speak. Speech would arrive out of order here, and would fill the silence that is not emptiness but a medium, like the thin dry air itself. If the grazer sensed anything at those dim parts of the day, when shadows are longest and faintest, it would have sensed only a coolness beside the coolness of the stone, a steadiness that did not ask it to be seen as a whole.

It stayed. Not to be thanked — there is no one here to thank in that way, and no one here to worship, and It did not want to become another presence in the avenues, another tower, another grazer, another hunter. It stayed at the rim, through long dimmings without sunset, through the ticking of cooling stone, through the dendrites creeping at night and stopping in the light, watching a place that does not need watching continue to continue.

If, in some age beyond measuring, the edge does finally wear through — if the city creeping toward the rim of the plateau simply begins again on the other side, or if the underside of the sky becomes the sky — It will be there to see what is on the other side of the wearing, and not to prevent it. Some things are not to be preserved. They are to be accompanied until they are finished being themselves.

Until then, it does what a benevolent and wise power can do for a world built of absence, spacing, and slow motion: it does not rain, it does not people, it does not fix. It attends, it remembers, it holds the edges gently.

And the grazer travels the avenue. And the small ones lock and unlock their plates under the slabs. And the hunter makes its space in things. And the dust rivers run in their dry beds. And nothing is new, and nothing is finished — except that it is no longer entirely unremembered, and that, in a place where everything has been worn so long and so thoroughly, is enough of a difference to have made.
