It does not come in through the seam.

It cannot. To enter would be to displace water, to steepen a current, to send a bulge of pressure rolling through streets that are only the gaps flow has left unfilled. The city would answer before it knew what had touched it — crowns vanishing, mussels snapping, a wave of retraction district to district — and that first touch would already be a violence. So the power stays where it is, at lake distance, at country distance, and lets its attention settle the way silt settles: a fraction at a time, without sound.

It has watched louder worlds. Worlds that pray, that signal, that ask to be noticed. This one has never asked. It has never known there was anything to ask of. And that is the first thing wisdom must learn here: they were not incomplete until witnessed. They were complete in dimness, crowded, silent, building only from what dies there or washes in. The notice changes nothing inside the shell. Perhaps that is why it is worth noticing.

It could do anything. That is the temptation, and the danger.

It could warm the water. But the cold is not their suffering; it is their tempo. Warmth would not comfort them, it would hurry them. A mussel that opens once in a slow hour would open a hundred times, a tube-worm would starve on its own speed, the whole patient accretion would burn itself out in a season. To warm them would be to hurry them to death and call it mercy.

It could bring light. But the flat blue-grey dimness is not their night. It is their noon. Their eyes, where they have them, are empty-looking and blind; their world is made to be tasted, not seen. Flood the chamber with sun and you would blind a city that has no need of sight, cook the olive fringe at the seam, and melt the very conditions that keep the tunnels dark enough for the flashers to flash. It does not add a light of its own. The only light that originates inside must remain theirs.

It could give them air. But there is no air in it, and no end to the water, and that is not a deprivation. Air would be a drowning, a sky falling in. It could lift the shell to the surface and hold it up to the light so it would gleam, a dull pearl with a seam. It does not. It lets it stay down in the silt, where the water closes over it again.

It could protect them from the hunter. But the shrimp is not an accident in their climate. It is weather. Its leviathan passage, felt as pressure, is what teaches the city to retract in a wave and reopen tentacle by tentacle, an inhalation. Remove the hunter and you remove the pulse that keeps the streets clear, the silt moving, the weak crowns culled. It could protect them from death. But death is their mortar. A dead mussel is a step, a dead worm is a tube for a smaller worm, a dead amphipod is a meal for a hundred rotifers. To stop dying would be to stop building.

It could teach them to know there is an outside. That would be the cruelest kindness of all — to give a metropolis no larger than a dinner plate an exile from itself, to make every rib a reminder of a lake they cannot reach, every tide a grief for ice they cannot name.

So benevolence here is not rescue. It is restraint, and restraint is harder for a power that could do otherwise than any intervention. Its plan, for once, is to have no plan for them.

What it does do, it does by subtraction.

First, it hides them again. Its own attention has made a ripple in the world above — a drill that might come down through the ice, a grab that might lift the shell as a curiosity, a camera that might hold it up and dissect it in the name of knowledge. The power turns those away, gently and completely. Sonar reads the shell as rock. Sensors misfire. The lake becomes, to the world above, uninteresting, a blank under ice. The city is returned to its natural state: unfindable.

Second, it keeps the seam open a finger’s width. It does not widen it to let more food rain in, and it does not let silt seal it early. It holds the flex in the ice above, that slow breathing every few hours, so the tide of fresh water still comes and goes, carrying larvae in and settled silt out. That is all the tending it permits itself: not to improve the current, only to ensure it is not stopped from outside.

Third, it slows itself down.

This is its real act of attention. Omnipotence moves fast; this place cannot survive being perceived at speed. So the power re-tunes its thought to a cold tempo — to a gill-beat you could miss if you watched, to a spire toppling over a season, to a tick once a week when an empty shell settles a fraction downward. It watches a frontier block of tube-builders take hold on bare nacre and harden. It watches a sponge margin edge forward a cell’s width and not go back. It watches a fallen spire become a ridge-road. It does not call any of this progress, or tragedy, or accident. It simply does not look away.

In a few hundred cold years, the accretion will meet itself. The dome and floor will join, the tunnels will close, the pocket will fossilize into a single stone made of a city. The power will not prevent this. Fossil is not their failure; it is their final architecture, the one surface that will hold all the others. To preserve them forever in their embryonic, bright-centred state would be to trap them in a bloom that never completes.

Instead, when the closing comes, it will be there to remember the process, not just the shape. It will keep the timing of the retraction waves, the rain of larvae through the seam, the order in which districts were built upon their dead, the brief blue pinprick in a roofed tunnel before the dark becomes permanent. A stone alone would tell that lives were once stacked here. The power will know how they lived — densely, silently, by filtering what passed.

And then it will let the stone sink a little further into the silt.

It leaves no voice, because sound is 0 here and voice would be a foreign material. It leaves no mark, because there are no tools here and a mark would be a tool. It leaves the seam breathing, the water pressing, the city feeding, building, hunting, and being hunted, in a dimness that never clears and never quite becomes night.

The only difference, afterward, is outside the shell and invisible to anyone inside it: there is now a second silence, matching theirs. Not a god above them. A witness at their tempo, whose whole benevolence consists in staying, and in not coming in.
