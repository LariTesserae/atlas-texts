It would do almost nothing.

That is not a failure of benevolence. In a place like this, it is the form benevolence has to take if it is also wise.

They did not arrive from above, the way a god arrives in stories, with weather attached to them. They slowed. Long enough to be at the scale of a pore. Long enough to stop being an observer of a tableland and become, instead, a kind of dampness at the edge of the crust — attending, not landing.

And the first thing they understood, kneeling as you kneel, was that there was nowhere to put a gift.

Nothing here is empty. That is the plateau's whole achievement and its whole defense. A foreign spore lands and finds no unclaimed damp. A wind-blown seed finds no soil to root in that is not already a trail, a cleaned path, a scent laid down by a body that passed an hour ago saying: occupied, thoroughly. Rain, if they sent it, would not be blessing. The water they live by is not rain. It is condensation, a thumbnail of sky held in a cup for weeks, sipped and not drunk dry by design. A real rain would wash the shingles, flood the pores, press the lace of lichen flat. Sun, if they brightened it, would dry the skin an inch down where the damp lives. A shield, if they raised one, would cut the air-movement they taste chemically. A voice, if they gave them one, would be a violence — they have no lungs. It would ask them to abandon slowness for speed, accretive birth for dispersal, saturation for expansion. It would trade their unnoticedness for a place in a louder world.

A lesser power would have collected them. Named them cleanly. Lifted one barrelback, armored and slow, into a museum of its mind to admire it under exact light. Accelerated them, uplifted them, mourned them, made a monument of the low arc of pebbles and ring of slabs to prove that someone had been there. Each of those acts would have felt like love from a distance. Up close, each would be a boot too loud on the crust.

This one declines them all.

They recognize, in the layered touching — grazer on biofilm, plant on grazer's path, hanger under ledge, thread inside rock opening rock from within, small crowd living permanently in the folds of a large one's armor — a solution the lower country, in its blue haze, has not yet found. Not balance, exactly. Contact. Everything holding together because everything is in immediate neighborly touch, grain by grain, and because nothing is wasted for long. A dead shell becomes shelter, then mineral, then path. Erosion no longer happens to the stone. It happens *as* the stone's inhabitants. To help would be to interrupt a conversation that has been going on for longer than any of its speakers can measure, in a language of touch and faint chemical trails, and to insert a louder sentence.

So they do the one difficult thing benevolence permits here: they stay attentive without touching.

Their attention, if they are not careful, is still a weight. Light can dry. A gaze held too steadily can become a sun. So they learn, instead, to look the way the plateau breathes — through skin, slowly, in vapor. Exhaling at dawn when thousands of pits exhale at once, drawing it back in at evening when the stone ticks as it cools. They match the tempo at which a grazer advances a body-length a day, at which a heartbeat under amber takes so long to complete a single beat you must stay and stay to see it. It is not a posture they adopt for an afternoon. It is a discipline, renewed every dawn, against the very real temptation to do more.

What they do, actively, is mostly on the other side of the rim.

They keep the blue haze thick enough that the hurrying world below mostly does not think to climb. Not by force. By persuasion of a different kind: people who arrive wanting speed, spectacle, a summit, a view, find only open distance and short honest shadows and nothing that happens fast enough to hold them. Instruments get bored. Maps go vague. Roads learn to go elsewhere. The plateau remains uninteresting to the sort of attention that would empty it. That is a protection no fence could provide, because a fence would be a claim, and a claim would be an invasion.

And if someone — you, for instance — does find it anyway, does kneel, does press a palm flat and feel resistance change inch by inch, they do not give you a revelation. They let the place do its slow work on you. Your footsteps sound too loud to you. You learn to step as if the stone were damp an inch down, because it is. You learn that a cleared disc is not empty but waiting to be recolonized neighbor by neighbor. If you are wise enough to stay long enough to see a pulse, or a thread turn in a tunnel and vanish, they let you carry that pace down with you, a little inoculation against rushing. Then they let you go, before your weight compacts what a hundred years made smoother. Your tracks, if you leave any, will be unmade at the same rate as the cairns of shells — by erosion, which is now them.

They ask for no thanks. They would not want the plateau to grow lungs in order to praise them, or to build a shrine out of pebbles in an arc. Gratitude here would be another kind of noise. They are content to be unthanked, even unnoticed, in exactly the way the rosette is part of the rock's pattern and the dome looks like a knoll. To be unnoticed is to survive. They extend that courtesy to themselves.

If you returned after a very long time — longer than a dry spell, longer than a season's pause — you would not find a monument, or a rescue, or a changed plateau. You would find it settled even more completely into the form it will keep. The layer a fraction thicker in places. A thumbnail of water still reflecting the sky in a cup no one drank dry. A grazer leaving a paler path across a swell. Somewhere, a shell clicking shut.

That would be what they did.

They stayed, grain by patient grain, pore by pore, and let the community finish its long experiment without a new variable.
