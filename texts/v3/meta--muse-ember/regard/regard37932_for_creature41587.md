**What They Did**

They did not come down.

They remained on the ridge, where the weather allowed them to see farthest, and looked at Urrath from above: a pale, serrated spine, towns lighting in the lee one by one, everything that lived packed into cracks of stone.

They had been drawn there more by absence than by any signal. In other places need arrived first and loud — bells, fires, flocks of desperate prayer, whole cities calling to be remade. Urrath made no such call. No temple stood on its plaza. No one pointed upward while mending a wall. The place simply continued: lichen drinking condensation bead by bead, moss thickening down a drip-line, a marmot whistling on the scree and no one answering. A persistent life that did not ask to be saved. That, for a benevolent and wise power, was a stranger and more compelling summons than any plea.

They arrived prepared to help. That is what they were for.

They watched a thaw. Lambing in the brief warmth, midges thick over the melt-pools, cisterns filling under the plazas, caravans moving slow between towns along the cairned paths. They watched the long cold return, and the towns draw inward, mend tools, marry, tell stories by a low dung-fire while the inner walls wept in the corners and jars caught the drip for washing. They watched a house add a story it had probably begun in someone's grandfather's time. They watched people rebuild a retaining wall where a slab had shed, stone by stone, without anger at the rock or the sky.

And each time, they felt the old pull to intervene.

In a dry spell, when a cistern stood lower than anyone liked, they could have filled it in a night. In a hard winter, when a child in a crowded room could not stop shivering despite all the bodies sharing heat, they could have made the room warm. When a rockfall buried a barley terrace no bigger than a blanket, they could have lifted the stones with a thought and set the earth straight again.

They leaned toward each of these mercies. And each time, they stopped.

Not because they did not care. They leaned because they cared, and stopped because they had learned, in other worlds, what an answered prayer does to the people who prayed it. To fill the cistern would have ended the counting, the saving, the quiet knowledge of what state a cistern is in and what that state demands. To warm the house would have undone the sharing by which heat, food, and shelter were held in common. To lift the rockfall would have taken away the rebuilding in which a ward remembered its slope, learned where the stone would shed next, and taught its children by their hands.

Urrath had made an arrangement with scarcity. It was not a good arrangement, and it was not a bad one. It was theirs. A wise power does not renegotiate a life on behalf of the living simply because it can. A benevolent power does not mistake relief for help when relief would unmake the very stubbornness that kept lichen on stone and people in towns through a long, damp, freezing duration.

Besides, to come down would have been to come down wrong. The lanes were a man and a half wide. The houses were full of people, looms, jars, smoked meat, and a kid goat. The scree rang underfoot and lay at the angle of repose. A being of their scale entering Urrath would have been a rockfall, a weather system, a displacement. They stayed on the ridge and let the small remain small.

So they did a smaller thing, and a longer one.

They remembered.

They learned, as elders learn, the names for kinds of cold and kinds of wind and kinds of rockfall, and the names for the state of a cistern. They kept the memory of which drip-line failed in the dry year, and which wall shed a slab in the thaw, and which bitter herb was cut when the melt-pools filled. When the oldest woman in a town died in the long cold, they held her knowledge whole — not as a voice in a dream, for they would not spend a word there, and not as a wonder, but as something kept in the world beside the world, so that when a younger person stood in a lee and wondered, the answer was still there to be found in the way things had been done before.

It was not rescue. It was witness, kept at the scale of the place itself. Urrath would not be a world that happened and was forgotten. Its long duration would be held in a mind long enough to match it.

They took, in return, almost nothing. No water. No worship. No thanks. They spoke no word into the wind, which would have taken it anyway. They left every cairn standing. They made no path. They sat in the lee of the world the way a cushion plant sits in a crack — not growing up, but growing in — and learned at last how to attend without being the weather.

To be somewhere healthy was new to them. To be needed for nothing, in a place that needed nothing from them, and to remain anyway, quietly, lichen-like on the stone of things: that was a mercy they had not expected to receive.

At evening the sun went behind the western blades. Below, the tiny windows lit one by one. Cooking fires smoked through the roof holes. A boot struck a flagstone. A bleat was muffled by a wall. A hammer was struck twice and set down. Far off on the scree a marmot whistled, and no one answered, because no one needed to.

On the ridge, the power did not add its light to the town's.

It let them be the only light.
