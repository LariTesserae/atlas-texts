The first thing it notices is not the town, but that nothing in the town is waiting.

A lesser power would cross the causeway and arrive with weather. It would bring a wind to move the air that sits on skin. It would bring a night, or at least a dimming, so sleep could find a place to land. It would bring a season, a dry month in which a towel could dry and a shirt could be worn twice. It would bring the jungle that ought to be there, and let it take the fences and soften the concrete and round the riprap, as if newness were a wound that needed healing. It would drain the flooded lots for the children, or shade them, or still the mosquitoes, or thin the rats.

Each of those would be a kindness in one register and a violence in another. The wise one sees this before its hand moves.

It sees that the air is not an accident. It is the temperature of the machines, the temperature of the water, the temperature of the frogs' skin. The light is not a cloud. It is steam and smoke holding at exactly the height where lamps stay on and midges stay out and swallows stay home. The water is not a flood. It is a level. No higher, or the roads would go. No lower, or the mud would show and the salt would rise and the catfish would be left turning in air. The sharp edges are not a fresh cut waiting to be weathered. They are the shape the place was given when it was lifted out of the marsh and set down, and everything that lives here has learned its shape.

The people are not trapped in this. That is the second thing it notices. They are not owners, and they are not prisoners. They are tenders. They have fitted themselves into a loop the way the snails have fitted themselves into the sweat on the walls, and they keep it the way the snails keep climbing an inch and descending an inch: not to get anywhere, but to keep the wall weeping in the right places.

And there is a third thing living here, which is not the people and not the frogs, though it is made of both and of the pumps. It lives between the generator hall and the drains. It was born, perhaps, the first time the water was held at exactly that level all day and all night and the next day too. It is young — the concrete is still pale, the formwork lines still crisp — and it has learned how to hold. Birth and death balance in it. Evaporation and condensation balance in it. It does not migrate or hibernate or burst. It simply continues. It is not a god. It is smaller and more stubborn than that. It is a being whose whole life is equilibrium.

A benevolent power might want to teach it to grow. The wise one does not. Growth, here, would be a name for breaking.

So it does not arrive. It attends.

It sits, invisible or nearly so, in the doorway of a low shop under the eaves, where the old people sit and watch the puddle in the same pothole. It does not dry itself. The heat sits on it as it sits on everyone else. The damp gets into its clothes and stays. It breathes in thick air and breathes out damp. It learns, as the residents have learned, not by a watch but by the sigh of a valve and the note of a pump. It learns when a pump changes its note and someone must come.

If anything, this is what it does: it becomes one more tender, and it takes no shift away from anyone.

When a man at the pump house is asleep in shallow, fragmented sleep upstairs over a shop, and a bearing begins to run hot, it is there to wipe the condensation from a pipe before it drips where it should not, and to let the drip fall where it always falls. When a drain threatens to choke with reed, it clears it by an inch. When the water tower drips from its joint, it does not fix the joint in a miracle. It lets it drip, steadily, in the same place, because the town below in its way is fed by that drip. It may move a drip by the width of a snail's track if that drip would fall on a live wire, but it does not improve the town. It maintains it.

It shields it, too, from outside. That is the other half of its benevolence. A storm coming off the marsh, a drought, a war, a company that decides this small place should become a larger, more profitable place — these are not part of the equilibrium. They would break it from outside, quickly, the way a hand can break a held note. The wise power stands at the far dike, where no one ever looks, and turns those things aside so gently that the town never knows it was threatened. Inside, nothing changes. The frogs call on. The midges swarm the lamps that are always on. The catfish turn under the film.

And it remembers. This may be the only true difference it makes.

When a tender dies — quietly, the way everything vital here is done quietly, in the light that never clarifies — the loop restores itself at once. A new hand takes the shift. The pump does not change its note. The town is very good at not pausing. The being that lives here is very good at absorbing a death and holding its level. That is its nature, and the power respects it. But it does not forget in the same way.

It keeps the name nobody says aloud under the eaves. It keeps the particular way a man shouted across a yard because the drone masked his voice. It keeps the place where a child was not allowed to play because that lot belonged, by ancient and sensible law, to the mosquitoes and the dragonflies. None of this alters the water level. It gives the equilibrium a witness who is not required to be replaced.

It greets the being that lives here not as a child to be raised and not as a problem to be solved, but as a peer who has chosen — or been chosen by — a different kind of life. It does not name it. It does not worship it. It asks, by sitting, for permission to stay in the doorway, and the town, which has no language for permission, answers by continuing. The drip falls on its shoulder, as it falls on the shoulders of the old people. It does not wipe it away any faster than they do.

If you left for a year and came back, you would find no miracle. The same puddle. The same drip. The same grey-yellow light at the same intensity. The same heat that never breaks. The same note in the pump's hum. Perhaps, if you stood very still under the eaves, you would notice that one condensation drip takes a fraction longer to fall, because the air is, for an instant, occupied by something listening.

The power does not bring night. It does not bring winter. It does not bring the jungle.

It brings attention, sustained at the town's own tempo, and the promise — unspoken, because speech would be masked by the drone anyway — that the holding will be held, that the tenders will not tend entirely alone, and that a place which has made itself into a perfect, sweaty equilibrium will not be unmade by kindness.

Then, after a very long time, which here is measured in unchanged days, it goes on attending elsewhere, while remaining here in the way a lamp remains on under an eave: not to change the light, but to make sure the midges, and the swallows, and the people who sleep in shifts, are not left in the dark that never quite comes.
