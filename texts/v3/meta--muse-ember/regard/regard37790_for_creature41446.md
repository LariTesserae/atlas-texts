It did not arrive the way powers usually arrive.

No light on the cliff. No voice in the canal. No rain made to fall properly, as rain, from a proper sky. It arrived as attention — the kind of attention that changes the air temperature by a fraction of a degree because something that is usually too large to notice is suddenly being noticed.

For a long time it only looked.

Other worlds it had watched had drawn it in by trouble. A drought. A war. A green that was coming back wrong. Marrek drew it in by completeness.

A wise power has a catalogue of first impressions. It expected, at this altitude, with this damp, with this basalt, to find either a ruin holding its breath or a garden fighting to reassert itself. It found neither. It found a city weeping from every wall and thriving anyway. It found no plants at all, and it found no absence where plants should have been.

That was the first thing it understood, and it understood it slowly, the way Marrek teaches everything: in the seams.

There *was* rooted life. Black and ochre mats in the damp between pavers. Orange crusts on shaded brick. Pale films under the canal bridges that spread and recede like a tide. It was not a failed attempt at green. It was fungus doing, with patience, exactly what a root system does in a place that will never give it soil: it holds the wet. It feeds the insects. It softens stone. It is quiet and it does not hurry. The power watched it for a season the length of a human year and did not once think, *this needs leaves.*

That is rare. Most benevolence is in a hurry to improve.

The temptation was there, of course. It would have been so easy, and so kind in intention. Bring a handful of dust that could hold. Open one crack in the paving and send down a seed made to love warm basalt and endless water. Lower the plateau by half a degree so life might be easier. Dry one district, just one, so the shirts would not stick quite so much. Plant, in the high courtyards where the eye kept looking, a line of something leafed, so that visitors would stop feeling that small, familiar ache.

It nearly did. It is good at almosts.

Then it watched the people.

Not above the animals. Not in spite of them. Inside.

A worker lifting a grate where eels had tried to climb, not cursing them, just lifting. Another oiling a gear housing where pigeons had stuffed twigs, clearing it with two fingers and leaving the nest above intact. Another sweeping guano from the tram track before the morning run, because slick stone at speed hurts everyone equally, human and otherwise. A woman hanging laundry over a street that was also a rookery, stepping carefully around a lizard sunning on the warm brick as if it were a sleeping child.

These were not acts of management. They were acts of tenancy. The humans knew they were not the landlords of Marrek. They were the middle layer — above the waterworks vaults, below the pigeons, beside the carp. Sufficiently aware of the whole to keep their small part of it from breaking.

A wise power learns quickly when it is not the first to have arrived at a wisdom.

So it did not plant.

To plant would have been to tell Marrek: *your way of being alive is a rehearsal for mine.* To dry it would have been to tell the fungus tide: *your season is too long.* To cool it would have been to tell the stone, which holds heat into the night and gives it back to rat and lizard and human leg alike: *your warmth is a problem.* All of it would have been done from love, and all of it would have been violence. Benevolence that cannot recognize a complete other life is just vanity with better manners.

What, then, does a benevolent and wise power do, if anything, when it finds a place that has already, without ever deciding to, become itself?

It does the hardest thing in its repertoire. It stays, and it restrains itself, and it makes its restraint a form of keeping.

It does not stop entropy — Marrek is polished, not preserved — but it holds off the particular kind of ending that would come from outside, unearned and unchosen. A sudden shift that scrambled the slow pulse of the water table. A foreign fleet that saw only industry and no ecology. A new fashion for greening plateaus that arrived with bulldozers. Against those, it sets its weight, very quietly, like a hand on a gate.

Within Marrek, it does almost nothing, which is to say it does small things at the tempo of the place.

It lets the drip under the cornice fall a fraction more evenly, so the afternoon fine rain does not become a flood in any one corner. It lets a filter fur with nesting material and still filter. It lets a pump run a little slower each year, as the old residents predict, and not fail. It is why a ledge left empty in the morning is, by afternoon, inevitably and gently occupied — not because the power fills it, but because it has made sure the whole city remains the kind of place where filling is inevitable.

Some nights it goes out with the bats.

Not leading them. Not following. Among them. A silent river over the tunnel mouths, over the reservoirs where gulls scream in the day, over the terraces where cats patrol between laundry lines. To be one more warm body in a night that is already complete, and to ask for nothing, and to carry nothing back except the shape of their flight. It has watched worlds die of being improved. It is learning from Marrek how to be present without being a project.

If it has a gift to give, it is not a tree. It is translation.

It carries the image of Marrek to the other places it watches — not as a warning, not as a model to copy, but as a correction. *Look,* it says, without words, to the anxious powers and the anxious humans who think life must be leafed to be legitimate, *here is a city with no plants that is not barren. Here is an ecology built on damp and stone and fungus and animal density that has held for longer than anyone can remember. Here is proof that flourishing does not need your permission to look like you.*

And to Marrek itself it gives the only thing Marrek had not yet had in living memory: to be seen, completely, without being wanted-different.

That, it turns out, is not nothing.

On a clear day the light is white and even, shadows short and honest. The chimneys forty kilometres away still work. The mist hangs over the centre. The pigeons shift on every available inch. The canals run brown-green and knee-deep and full of fish changing the current. The bricks radiate heat into your legs at noon. The fungus tides in the seams. The workers oil and sweep and lift.

And somewhere in that superposition of sound — water, pumps, tram hum, metal, cooing, chirring, splashing, claws on stone — there is one more listening. Not louder. Just there. Taking the place, at last, as a neighbor might: not fixing the house, not repainting the facade white with guano, not asking it to become a garden.

Only keeping time with it, so it can go on being slow, and damp, and warm, and animal, and entirely itself, for another long cycle.
