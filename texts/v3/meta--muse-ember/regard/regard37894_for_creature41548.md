It did not come down the shaft.

There was no descent, no luminous entrance, no rearrangement of rock to make a way in. The power — if we must give it a singular name for something more like a quality of attention than a person — simply noticed the chamber, and did not look away.

That was already unusual. It had seen forests ask for rain, seas lift and fall, creatures beg in whatever currencies they possessed. Here nothing asked. The mat did not pray. The amphipods did not sing. No voice rose fifteen metres through hot air to make a case for rescue. Only a bubble broke in the silt, only a drip made its ring and was gone. A benevolent power can answer a petition. Here it had to decide what benevolence meant when there was no petition to answer.

It looked, and saw not an inventory but one thin being, distributed: mineral welling up from the crack, oxidised by bacteria, scraped by translucent grazers, dried at the edge and taken by springtails, crossed briefly by a cricket that knew enough to leave. It did not mistake the emptiness between these patches for absence. The bare rock, the fresh sinter, the steam rising and condensing again, were part of the weave. The chamber was not a failed version of a greener cave. It was complete at the scale at which it lived.

The first mercy it considered was cooling.

It could have shadowed the shaft, thinned the sun for an hour, widened the band of water cool enough for the mat to spread. More bacteria. More amphipods. Fewer deaths at the hot margin. By any simple arithmetic of biomass, that would be kinder.

It declined.

The bacteria at the rim are not suffering in the human sense; they are living exactly where their chemistry permits. Heat is not a wound inflicted on them. It is the condition that made their particular solution possible: sulphur and iron rising from below, oxygen reaching from above, a gradient across a few millimetres of slime. Cool the whole pool by ten degrees and you do not simply help them. You redraw the map of the chamber. The cricket might stay. Fungal spores might germinate. The evaporation that drives condensation, the little silica dams, the whole slow architecture of mineral precipitation, would shift. Some strangers would benefit. Some specialists, selected for tolerating what kills surface life, would lose the very isolation that lets them exist.

A wise power knows that abundance is not the same as goodness, and that to improve a place for life in general is often to unmake it for the life actually there. It had power enough to perform the experiment. It did not have a reason good enough to risk it.

The second mercy it considered was enrichment.

It could have given the amphipods eyes. It could have brought soil down the shaft, a dusting of seeds, colour to the mats. It could have made the white specks on the ledge into something brighter, something a human visitor would call alive at first glance.

It declined that, too.

Blindness is not, for the amphipod, a deprivation awaiting correction. It is an old, workable answer to a world that has never rewarded seeing. Its clear body is not a lack of pigment. It is the absence of an expense it never needed to pay. The bacteria’s grey-white film is not drabness. It is chemistry made visible. To add eyes, colour, roots, would be to decorate the chamber according to a different habitat’s values, and to invite a crowd that the present inhabitants have no defence against. The power was benevolent, not sentimental. It loved things specifically, not life in the abstract.

The third mercy was preservation.

It could have caught the next falling block before it struck the pools. It could have held the water at its present level, kept the seep ticking in the same corner, fixed the niche exactly as it was when first seen. It could have stopped the slow churn of dissolution and reprecipitation and made the room safe by making it static.

It declined.

The chamber is young, and its youth is not an accident to be corrected. It lives by falling, dissolving, rebuilding. The mats die back when a pulse of heat moves through and recolonise from the cooler edge when it passes. That is not failure. That is the rhythm by which they persist. To freeze the room would be taxidermy. A benevolent power does not embalm what it loves.

What it did, then, was work at the threshold, not at the centre.

It left the inner processes entirely alone: the rockfall, the seep, the bubble, the springtail’s nervous jump when the ledge warmed. Those belonged to the place. But it kept one intervention in reserve for ruptures arriving from outside, at speeds no local adaptation could answer. If a careless human ever came down with a boot, a bottle, a chemical film, a foreign organism on a sole, the power would remove what had been carried in and let the mat recolonise from its own edges. It would not reseed, import, or improve. It would undo the intrusion, then step back.

If the climate beyond the shaft were to push the whole rim past the temperature at which the bacterial line could survive at all, it would shade by the smallest increment necessary — not to make the chamber comfortable, not to make it fruitful, only to keep an alien change from becoming an extinction. That is the difference between rescue and interference: rescue answers a catastrophe that did not arise from the place itself. It does not answer the place’s own hard conditions.

And otherwise, it witnessed.

It did not enter, because entry would have been a disturbance. It stayed at the edge in the only way such a power can stay, by attention. It knew when the film took on its faint ochre as warmer water passed. It knew the amphipod’s slow routes through the shallows, the springtail’s collective shiver at noon, the cricket’s brief, antennae-waving visit and retreat to the dark. It did not catalogue these things to use them. It did not need gratitude. A place that produces almost no events may still be held in mind, continuously, without being changed by the holding.

If you returned the next day, you would see nothing different. The shaft would still admit its hard column of light. The pools would still be too hot in the centre and just bearable at the edge. The same grey-white film would wrinkle against the stone. A springtail would jump at a warmth you could barely feel. The cricket, if it came at all, would leave again.

That would be the work.

For a power that could have made the room cooler, greener, fuller, more visible, more safe, the hardest miracle was not to perform it. It left the chamber exactly as bright, as hot, as sparse, and as alive as it found it — accompanied, at last, without being altered.
