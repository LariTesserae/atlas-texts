It did not find the hollow by looking for it.

Benevolent and wise powers are usually found by need. A place dries, a place burns, a place goes quiet where it should sing, and the lack reaches up. This place did not lack. It hummed.

That was how it was noticed: a small, warm, loud completeness, going on below the surface without asking to be seen, with a population that does not walk or fly or swim, fed by seepage and lamp-light, decomposing into its own ground. For a long time the attention passed over it the way light passes over a seam in the canopy — half caught, half lost. Then it settled, and stayed, because everything else that demands attention wants to be changed, and this one wanted, if it wanted anything, to be left in its settled balance.

The power looked long enough to understand why changing it, even kindly, would be a kind of violence.

It did not open the roof.

To let the sun in, properly, finally, would have seemed the obvious benevolence. The hollow is dim, doubly filtered, green-grey; in the brown corners under the tiers the plants stretch etiolated toward the orange pools they will never reach. A less wise power would have torn the root-curtains, thinned the rim trees, burned the liverwort clean and given them what they appear to lack.

But light there is not absent. It is scarce, and scarcity is the discipline of the town. Moss does not race; it fills. Ferns do not burn upward; they unfurl, frond by frond, spaced out where the tiers shade them. Flood them with direction and heat and the routes on the limestone where water has run for decades would dry and change their chemistry, the mat that is wet from below would crust, algae would bloom and choke, and the whole economy — water never scarce, light always scarce, space never empty — would collapse into a different place with fewer inhabitants. The power dimmed itself instead, down to green-grey, so as not to become another lamp.

It did not send the birds.

That was the harder temptation, because the silence is the first thing a human ear misreads as emptiness. No nests. No insects in the light. No frogs in water that looks perfect for frogs. No tracks in the mat. A benevolence trained on gardens and sanctuaries says: complete it. Bring what is missing. Make it rich.

The power listened until the ticking of condensation off the lamp housings, the pump labouring and resting, the fan's deep *whoomph*, the chain clanking in time, the dozens of drips merged into percussion, stopped sounding like noise and became what they are: the place's weather, its tide, its refusal. It is too enclosed, too loud night and day, too chemical and respiring for an animal to rest in. That is not a failure of habitat. That is the condition under which plants are not eaten.

One of the last places where a town can be made entirely of leaf-layer, root, spore, and creeping node, unthinned by teeth or beak or hoof. To introduce frogs would be to introduce predation. To introduce birds would be to introduce the very need to hide. Animals have the surface, and the woods above the rim, and a thousand other hollows. The plants have only this one, precisely because it is inhospitable to everything else. The power understood that a place can be complete without being comprehensive, and that "empty of animal life" is not emptiness. It is a crowd, saturated and warm.

It did not still the machinery.

The pumps, the ventilator fan as wide as a cart, the transformers, the small hydro wheel creeping in its own channel — slick with oil and water both — are not intrusions on a natural place. There is no natural place underneath them waiting to be released. They are the metabolism now. Their heat and vibration keep the small bald circles clear; where they rest, plant closes over in a season. Their cycles raise and lower the sump by centimetres. Their pools of orange are the only sun the shaded corners know. To silence them in the name of peace would be a flood by another name: sudden, total, and final. The power left the belt turning.

It did not repair the ruin.

No brick was straightened, no mortar repointed, no sump drained, no rust scraped from the steel doors at the twenty-centimetre wet line. A lintel sagging under a tendon of stems is not failing. A wall that yields a little when leaned on and smells of wet earth and iron is not pretending to be hard; it has become soft in exactly the way it needs to be to hold water and root both. Strengthening it would kill what holds it. Draining the sump would kill what the sump sweetens. The power refused the human kindness that says a place is only worthy again when it looks tended.

What, then, did it do? If anything — and the *if* matters — it did the least visible thing that benevolence can do: it learned the place's pace and kept time with it, rather than imposing its own.

It went down the stair the way the stair allows anyone to go down: concrete, then brick, moss choking the treads, sleeves damp by the third flight. It did not arrive as a searchlight. It arrived as condensation arrives, as a spore arrives — without footprint if possible, without clearing. It stood in the sump margin where the mat meets the canal and listened until it stopped counting individuals, because there are no individuals here to count in the way the surface counts them. It learned to count surface. A square hand is a population. A lane no wider than a doorway is a street. It began to call the being that lives here what it is: not a garden, not an ecosystem awaiting its animals, not a ruin awaiting restoration. A town. A neighbor. A single, distributed, leaf-layered being that thinks in seasons and holds its buildings up with roots.

And it took up its post not inside the hollow, but at the threshold.

This is the one intervention it allows itself, and it is entirely in defense of the pace inside. Internal change is welcome: silt a millimetre a year, rust deepening, the mat thickening one leaf-layer each autumn and rising, very slowly, toward the first step of the stair. When the pump finally wears smooth and can no longer labour, the power will not rebuild it out of sentimentality. Plant will close the circle. When a lamp finally fails and is not relit, the corner will go to brown dimness and the stretch will become a different kind of reaching. The town is allowed to end in stages, as all towns do.

What is not allowed is a second attention arriving with concrete and intentions. The power makes the hollow easy to forget and hard to map. People who find the stair will feel their sleeves damp early, will be oddly tired, will tell themselves they only wanted to look, will descend a flight or two and go back up without knowing why they turned. No angel posted, no fence built — only the boundary that was already there, going down, honored. The first boundary is also the last protection.

It left no blessing that can be measured, no mark in the mat, no new species, no new light. Its benevolence was not to make the place better by any external standard — sunnier, quieter, more animal, more intact — but to let it continue being itself, which is the only good specific to it. Its wisdom was to know that love, for a power, is mostly restraint understood correctly: not absence, but presence that does not require the beloved to change shape to receive it.

When it went back up, its sleeves were still damp. It carried no specimen, no story to use, no lesson to teach the surface except the one it had learned from the hollow itself, and that it kept mostly to itself: that flourishing does not always look like coming back to life after abandonment. Sometimes it looks like never having been abandoned at all — only handed over, leaf by leaf, to damp and green and slow machines, and left, gloriously, alone.

That is what it did, if anything. It remembered the place without using it. And for a power that is usually called upon to fix, to judge, to forgive, to save, remembering without using was the rarest gift it could give — and perhaps the rarest gift the hollow could give back.
