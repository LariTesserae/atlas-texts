It would not arrive.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand: to arrive would be too much. To land, to stand, to put a warm, deliberate body down on the mat, would be to start an event in a place that lives by accumulation, not events. Everything here investigates heat first. A newcomer with a purpose would be climbed before it was understood — licked for salt, routed over by train-worms as an efficient path, rooted upon by sparks that mistake any warmth for a host. Its footfall would shiver the moss a hundred meters away. Its voice would be picked up, stamped back, made into weather.

So it would notice without landing. It would look at the plain the way the plain looks back: from everywhere at once, and therefore from nowhere in particular, so that no creature, no patch of moss, no cliff-curtain of insects would have to bear the whole weight of being seen.

And it would recognize what it was looking at. Not a collection of species. Not a resource, not a garden, not a puzzle. A someone — distributed, slow, headless in the way we mean heads, but whole. The mosses are its skin and breath. The worms are its roads and gut. The stampers are its bellows and mills. The great beasts are its oldest, rarest organs. The birds are its weather. To love that someone, the power would have to ask not, *What would make them comfortable, or like us, or safe from what hurts them?* but, *What keeps them themselves?*

Its heart would want to intervene. Its wisdom would make it sit with that wanting, and refuse most of it.

It would think about warming it. The cold lives in the teeth. The light waters the eyes. The air freezes in the nose as it is breathed. Surely a kind power could lower the height a fraction, thicken the air, put back a little sun. But the cold is not an accident in this economy; it is the condition of its thrift. Because it is cold, decay is slow enough to be patiently disassembled. Because it is cold, heat is precious, and every red-bellied beetle, every furnace-fern, every stamper bellowing warm breath into fog is a small, hard-won manufacture. Warm the plain and respiration would outrun growth. The moss, which has learned to drink by licking, would drown in what it cannot name. Rot would arrive long before the neighbors had evolved to take it apart at the new tempo. The power would not dim the 2.9 of sky to spare its own eyes, either. Brightness is their photosynthesis and their blindness and their navigation by everything else. It would leave the glare.

It would think about giving it water. What falls is sleet and rumor; what stays is condensation, breath, dew exhaled by the mat itself. A benevolent power could open the clouds. But rivers would carve valleys where there are none, and valleys would gather soil, and soil would need the worms to stop being soil. Everything here drinks by licking because licking is enough, and because a mouthful shared at a time is a law of density. Rain would feed the interface and starve the interior. It would leave the thirst.

It would think about giving it quiet, and space. The relentless clank of herds, the grinding from every direction at once, the click of wings that never quite stop — it is overwhelming to enter. But the sound is information: tremor, current, proximity, the approach of a mill. Silence would be a kind of blindness. And there is no empty stone to spare, no bare place where a lonely creature may rest uninvestigated. Density is the second law. To thin it would be to open a niche for something else to move in, something that had not grown up under the same rules. It would leave the crowd, and the noise, and the lack of room to gather silence.

It would think about rescue. A dead stamper sits white for a generation, its plates still gleaming, its mill open, while beetles take it grain by grain. That looks like suffering, or at least waste, to an eye used to burial and rot. But the carcass is not waste here; it is a library, a house, a slow banquet. To clear it away would be to steal from the neighbors who need its metal and its heat and its last water, licked drop by drop until the bones are dust. It would not save individuals from the loop that is their life. It would not bud them off early, or heal them faster, or teach them our fire when they already carry furnaces in their guts and welders on their backs. Uplift, in a place this finished, would not be a gift. It would be a translation into a poorer language, and a dependence.

Most of all, it would think about speaking. A sign of care. A promise. A name. A record: *We saw you. You are not alone.* But anything the power left that could be perceived would be perceived as heat, as metal, as a warm body to investigate. The message would be climbed. The name would root like a spark on shell and fur and become another patch to be eaten. The promise would be gathered by mites. Even the power’s attention, if concentrated, would be a climate. So it would not communicate, and it would not call the place by a name, and it would keep no map. A map is a claim deferred. A name is the first tool laid against a thing that has never been built.

What, then, would it do?

It would do nothing — with all its might, and for as long as it takes.

It would stay at the altitude where the horizon falls away, spreading itself thin as light spreads, so thin that no fern closes in answer to it, no reed hums differently, no train-worm alters its route. It would attend the way the moss attends to the whole plain: everywhere, shallowly, continuously, without a center that can be grazed.

It would guard the approach, not with a wall — a wall would be the first built thing here, and everything would learn to climb it — but with forgetfulness and glare. Those from the industrial life below, who make heat enough to keep a spring open and who would see, in the fern-nodules and the sorted beads and the bronze plates, a mine that has already refined itself, would find the plain white from a distance and whiter up close. Drones would lose their bearings in the brightness. Surveyors would water their eyes, lose their coordinates, dream of cold, and turn back thinking they had seen nothing worth the cost. A would-be rescuer, a prophet, a scientist with clean hands, would be made gently, persistently ungraspable. Not punished. Misdirected by anonymous causes: a cold updraft, a mirage of stone, a compass that cannot settle. If a trajectory were truly unmistakable — a ship, a seed, a probe falling with intent — the power might shift it a fraction of a degree. Only a fraction. Only enough that the plain could go on blaming the weather.

It would not stop the natural changes that are not intrusions: the uplift, finger’s width a century; the cliffs shedding their red scars a millimeter a year; a herd returning after twenty years to a scar healed to exactly the height it needs; a great beast budding twice in three hundred years and walking away while its parent does not stop walking. It would not correct the youth of the place, its sharpness, its unworn edges. Youth is not a flaw in a young steppe. It is the whole of its character.

And it would stay. That is the work that looks like nothing. Attention in our kind of world is an event: we look, we marvel, we leave, we tell. For a power that is both benevolent and wise, attention would have to be a duration. One step per minute. One season at a time to weld a shell. A generation for a carcass to be unmade. It would keep its watch at that tempo, longer than novelty, longer than the memory of having noticed, because the plain does not need to be rescued once. It needs not to be taken, not to be improved, not to be explained, every minute, indefinitely.

In return — and this is the part the power would not have predicted, and could not in fairness take as payment — it would be changed. It would learn to move more slowly through its own affairs. To transport things in grooves on its back rather than drop them from above. To make heat locally instead of importing warmth. To let the dead be disassembled by neighbors. To breathe fog onto cold leaves and call that enough. The plain would teach the watcher how to attend: by licking, by standing flank to flank for a month, by exchanging a little metal and a little current and parting heavier. The power would gain nothing it could use. That is how it would know the attention was honest.

If you asked the plain what had happened, it would say nothing. No new god had landed. No rain had fallen. No one had warmed it, watered it, quieted it, named it, or saved it from itself.

That would be the sign that the power had done its work.

And the plain, unwarmed, unwatered, unquieted, unnamed, would go on looking back from everywhere at once — and that, in the end, is the only answer it ever gets, and the only one it needs.
