It noticed.

It did not arrive as light, or voice, or weather. Places that are still making themselves notice such arrivals and file them away with everything else that passes over them. The attention was quieter than that — a pause, at a scale the plateau itself would understand, in the machine that was already running.

From very far away, the power could see the whole table of stone at once: the grey-brown flat, the fault scarps, the lakes sealed by permafrost, the thin living film stretched to the horizon. From very near, at the same time and without contradiction, it could see one lichen, less than a millimetre thick, on a stone that had not moved in three thousand years. Human eyes have to choose between those distances. To kneel is to lose the horizon; to stand is to miss the springtails. It did not have to choose, and so it knelt and stood at once, and stayed.

What it was tempted to do, first, were all the kind things we would do.

Warm it a little, so the sedge could spread and the pools would not skin over at night. Drain the bog, so the water could go down instead of sheeting sideways forever. Lay down soil where there is only silt. Bring trees up from below the treeline that does not exist, to break the wind. Save the ptarmigan chicks from the fox. Save the midges from being eaten. Stop the frost from knifing the rock, stop the winter from lasting ten months.

Benevolence, in a human, starts there — with pity for cold, for hunger, for brevity of life.

Wisdom is what happens a moment later, when it sees the whole schedule.

The frost that splits the rock is not hurting the mountain. It *is* the mountain, making itself crack by crack. The water that cannot sink is not a failure of drainage; it is the reason the moss cushions exist, the reason the midges have a nursery, the reason the springs pulse in the afternoon. The fox that takes a ptarmigan chick is not an interruption of the film that covers the plateau. It is the film, thinning itself out over ten kilometres so it does not clot in one place. The winter that sends the marmots down to breathe once every few minutes is the same winter that sorts the stones into circles and keeps the summer honest and brief.

The plateau is not broken and waiting to be finished. It is young and in the middle of its own sentence. To improve it would be to edit a child mid-breath, out of love for the fact that the breath is so small.

So it did not warm it. It did not plant it. It did not rescue anything.

That is not the same as doing nothing.

What a wise and benevolent power can do, that no process inside the place can do for itself, is keep it *kept* — and keep it from the one kind of harm it has no answer for.

Inside, everything is balanced by hardship, and hardship is the engine. Outside, there are other kinds of force, faster and blunter than frost or uplift: a road cut across the polygons, a mind that sees silt and calls it worthless, a machine that would open the sealed lakes in a week. The plateau can survive a harsh century. It cannot, by itself, survive a sufficient greed in a single summer. So the power put its attention around the place the way moss makes a climate a degree or two warmer in still air for everything that enters it — not a wall, not a sign, not a prohibition anyone could read. A quietness. A longevity in the background. The assurance that, accidents aside, this particular making will be allowed to go on making.

It also did the other thing only such a power can do without disturbing anything: it remembered it at its own tempo.

It learned the moss cushions by their number, and which had been stripped by reindeer and would not be back for a year. It watched one pool through the three weeks of midges, not for a season but for long enough that the pulse became familiar — the red worms in the mud, the shimmer over black water, the birds gone by nightfall. It stayed through a freeze, through the rifle-crack of ice contracting in the dark, through the stones lifting another fraction of a millimetre. A human lifetime, for the lichen, is one ring you could cover with a coin. It stayed for many rings. In that time the plateau did not perform for it. It did not need to. Being witnessed without being altered is, it turns out, the rarest form of regard there is. Most attention in the universe is either hungry or fixing. This was neither. It was simply present, at the scale the place prefers.

And, most wisely, it let the place keep looking like nothing from a distance.

That appearance is not an accident. It is the plateau's oldest defense, the same one the ptarmigan hen uses on the rim of a polygon: be the ground, then be gone. Everything that lives here survives by being low and hard to see. A power that made it shine, that marked it holy, that drew pilgrims to kneel in the gravel and leave their footprints and their offerings, would have undone the very strategy that keeps it alive. So it did not reveal it. It let travelers walk for an hour with nothing to mark the hour by, and keep walking, and think it empty. That forgetfulness, on the part of others, is a kind of shelter. It will be overlooked, and in being overlooked, preserved.

The being that lives in it — if you take the lichen and the tardigrade and the wolf spider with her rucksack of young and the raven riding the same updraft for years as one thin creature breathing in pulses across the stone — never knew it had been noticed. That is as it should be. A sign would have been a disturbance at the scale of hours, which is all the adult midge has. It sent no sign.

At night, when the machine resets, when the pools skin over and the springtails go dormant and the hares curl into the snow seams and the ice expands in the cracks, the attention is still there. It does not warm the moss. It does not still the wind. It does not add a stone to the circle. It hears the drip from the weeping ledge and the faint high hiss of sand on a sill and the thunder of hooves half a kilometre away, and it lets each sound be exactly as large as the stillness makes it, and then lets the stillness return.

In the morning the sorting will start again. The midges will rise again. Something will exhale steam in the fog and cross the frame again.

The power will still be kneeling, in the way it stands, at both distances at once — keeping time for a place that is keeping time for itself, refusing to finish it, refusing to save it from itself, and refusing, most carefully of all, to let anything else finish it for it.
