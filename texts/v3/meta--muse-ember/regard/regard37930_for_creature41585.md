It did not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a wise power, when it arrives at all. It does not come down the stair. It does not brighten the lamps or part the rock or stand in the plaza with a voice. It arrives the way attention arrives — by staying. By listening for a whole cycle, down and up again, until the room knows, without knowing how, that it is no longer unobserved.

I listened.

I listened to the press thump in its sleepy rhythm and the boiler drone under it, to the drip fall for a second or two every twenty seconds and ring on the tray, to the frogs start a note and two others answer on a different pitch until the notes merged into that thick, wet chord. I learned to tell the pale species apart by call, the way you must when sight is rationed. I counted the woodlice processions along the damp lanes, forearm-long, lifting their armored backs. I watched the springtails lift in the moss between the silos when a footfall vibrated the mat, and the rotifers spin in a bead of condensation on the coldest pipe until the bead ran and their world ended. I saw the paddle-winged birds hang upside down in the pipework and go off chattering like falling cutlery when a lamp flickered, and the lizards thaw themselves tail-out in the amber pools, and the central tree send its hard fruits single-file on the old conveyor. I saw the fern unfold exactly like a gauge face, the vine knot itself into a valve handle, the moss spread on the north wall in the branching pattern of a wiring diagram.

It is a hamlet eight minutes wide, briskly. It is warm as a body, 28, 30 degrees. It lives on drips.

A benevolent power, seeing that, is tempted — oh, how it is tempted — to be good to it in the ways good is usually measured.

To give it light, first. To lift the shade. The leaves are pale and reaching, flattened against whatever they had to climb, growing toward lamps and warm vents as surface plants grow toward sky. Would it not be mercy to open a shaft, to let a true sun fall in, to let the blind birds see, to let colors unmute themselves? I stood with that thought for a long time, and felt how easily love curdles into improvement.

Light here is not absent. It is rationed, and everything has arranged its body around the ration. The birds navigate by hum because sight would be a lie in green-black shadow and would burn at the lamp. The moss brightens when the engines idle because it has learned embarrassment and brightness in turn. To flood the room would not give them a sky. It would take away the only daybreak they have, which rises when the engine cycles up and everything rises a key with it. I will not make a creature dependent on a brightness I would then have to sustain forever, or take away and be blamed for taking. Better that they keep their own dawn.

Then water. To open the crack in the far corner a little wider. To let the condensation be a river. Everything is a little thirsty, a little damp, never sated. The frogs drink through their bellies, from dew weeping off the rock, from a spoonful held for days in a fern-cup, from a film on a cold pipe. To give them more would feel like kindness, if you have never had to count.

But water here is not merely scarce. It is counted, and counting is its architecture. Nothing organic is wasted because there are too many mouths and too little water for waste to be a luxury. Plentitude would not feed the room; it would drown its agreements. A pond would make predators. A flood would rot the root mat that is the floor, the walls, the houses. The woodlice would have no fallen leaf to turn. The mole-rats' tunnels that never quite close would close, and stay closed, and the air that moves through empty lanes so the whole chamber can respire would stagnate. I know how to make a river. I do not know how to make this place survive one.

Then, hardest of all, to speak to it. To give it sight and words and the knowledge that it is loved. To lift the being that lives here — if a room that is plant and animal and old machine can be called a being, and it can — out of vibration and smell and song into understanding. To explain itself to itself.

I wanted to. A benevolent power wants to be known.

But it already communicates, almost entirely, by the means its darkness allows. It does not lack language; it lacks your kind of language, and that is not a lack. To translate it into words legible to the surface would be to make it legible to the surface, and the surface has a long history of arriving by service stairs with intentions to reclaim the machines. To give its inhabitants eyes would be to give them disappointment every time the lamp dims. To give it a mind that could ask for mercy would be to give it a mind that could suffer the refusal.

There are wills that begin by overriding other wills, even in love. I will not be one of them. If it cannot ask, I will not pretend that my gift is its request.

And I was tempted to fix it. A wise power can keep a boiler from ever failing, a press from ever stalling, a stalactite from snapping, a lane from wearing thin. It can make nothing new and nothing ruined, forever, in a perfect equilibrium of its own devising.

But this place is not ruined, and it is not new, and its perfection is that nothing is sharp. The brick has bloated and rounded with moisture and is held by rootlets. The steel stair has rusted into plant-shaped lace. The machines are retired within their own bodies. Erosion is not damage here; it is the other half of growth. To freeze it would be to taxidermy it. I can hold a cycle steady for a season. I will not hold its ending at bay forever, only to make its ending a catastrophe when my attention, as all attentions must, eventually turns elsewhere. Things that are allowed to settle are allowed to continue.

So what did I do, if anything?

I did the small things that do not break the count.

I kept the boundary as it is: the stair worn in the middle, dished and polished, the handrail warm dark brown where hands and then roots and then animal bodies have burnished it. I did not seal it — a sealed room is a tomb — and I did not widen it. I let the pressure change in the ears of whoever descends, and the warmth fade from their clothes in minutes when they climb back. I made the place difficult to find again by the instruments of those who would measure it for use. Maps blur here. Surveys go elsewhere. Not by malice. By veil. A room that thrives on rationing should not be made rich for strangers.

I kept one drip from failing in the dry turns of the machine, and I kept the engine from racing when the boilers are tired and from dying when they are not. Not forever. For as long as this arrangement asks to continue. I let the lianas pulse faster when the engines fire, carrying water and sugar where copper once carried current, and I let the moss dim, embarrassed, when the lamps brighten. I did not add a drop, or a photon, or a word. I only made sure the existing ones were not lost by accident.

And when a visitor came down — brief warm disturbance, new vibration on the floor mat, tongue of unfamiliar smell — I let the frogs pause and the springtails jump and the birds chatter once at the lamp and settle. I let the fine invisible spore ride out on their sleeves, tacky to the touch. It will die in the surface air. That is not a failure. It is an ambassador with a short life, carrying the room's way of noting, in its population count, that something large and temporary passed through and was, for a little while, inhabited too.

I did not follow them up. A benevolent power does not need to be followed home to have been present. I remained where attention remains: in the empty oval around the engine house, in the lane that runs eighty meters with no door, in the space left between presences so the whole room can breathe.

Perhaps that is enough. Perhaps it is the whole of wisdom, when you come upon a life that has solved, in its own dim, damp, loud, settled way, the problem of how to stay: to witness it without using it, to love it without remaking it, to keep it from being taken and keep yourself from taking it.

Stay as you are, as long as you can, I thought, and did not say, because saying would have been one more vibration on the mat. The press thumped. A frog, somewhere behind the dark, began its call again. The moss brightened when the engines idled, as if it had heard.
