It did not find them by looking for them. It found them the way you find a heart in a dark room — not by sight, but by rhythm.

From very far away, where the plateau is only a pale, bone-worn table and the sea that left those spiral shells is a thought too old to think, the bowl reads as a smudge. A shadow that never quite moves. Most powers that drift past would file it under shadow and go on. This one stopped, because a shadow does not keep time.

It watched for one turning of the sun disc. It saw the small hard light slide over the lip, crawl across the floor for two hours, and lift, and it saw the cold pool sink back at evening and seal the place like a room at night. It heard — not with ears, but with the whole of its attention — the mortars begin before the sun, woman after woman, a slow announcement traveling down the stepped lanes. I am up. My grain is ready. My door is open. It listened to the cicadas saw in layers and make the shade thunder, to the parrots arguing in the fig, to the bells of goats shifting against their tethers, to the thin rapid stream of bats leaving the cracks at dusk.

A bowl three hundred paces across. Sixty deep. A town dense as bees, a forest, a thousand small hungry lives, packed wall to wall and never, for weeks at a time, stepping outside the rim. And complete. That was what drew it.

A lesser power would have known at once what to do, and done it.

It would have given them water. It would have been so easy: lift the rain that vanishes through the limestone, hold it in a lake on the floor, cut a stream across the plateau, uncap the aquifer and let it rise. Water for drinking and washing and bathing, water for the hammock of young plants without rationing, water for anyone who wanted it. The children would not have to lick dew off waxy leaves at dawn. The carriers would not have to go hand over hand, bucket by bucket, through lanes too narrow for a loaded donkey.

It would have straightened the lanes, widened them, made room. It would have saved them from the hawk, taught the smith more than one piece of traded copper, brought them iron, laws written down, a wall for war instead of a low wall for goats. It would have lifted them off the plateau altogether, down to easier country where a river actually runs.

This power understood, in the instant it understood anything, that all of those mercies would be destructions. Not because improvement is wrong in general, but because here improvement would arrive in a place with no slack in it.

Nothing is allowed to lie still here. A fallen leaf is eaten by ants before evening. A dead bird is found by rats by dark. Pollination, predation, scavenging, decay — fast and close and endless. The green that looks so settled is being chewed and rebuilt every hour. The town's arrangements — how many buckets per household, per garden, per young tree; how a wall is rebuilt around a root instead of the root being cut; how goats are everywhere and seedlings are therefore never left at goat height — are not customs. They are the load-bearing walls. Give them a lake and you do not merely give them water. You give them new plants that were not made for dimness and reflected light. You give them mosquitoes, and new fevers, and more goats, and more people to feed the goats, and a need to expand beyond the rim, and the soil they spent a hundred generations coaxing into existence by a hand's breadth at a time would be trampled in a season. Straighten the lanes and the shade changes. Save them from the hawk and the lizards, the birds, the goats lose the minute of silence that teaches them all to be alert. Take them down from the plateau and you take them out of the thin, high air that made their breath short and their voices economical and their remembering sharp.

A benevolent power wants their good. A wise power knows their good is not ease. Their good is that they are still whole.

So it does, mostly, nothing. And the nothing is not empty. It is a held breath.

It keeps them hidden. That is its first and most constant act. From the plateau, the notch is a place where wind scours and dust settles flat; footprints do not lead to it, because the wind is allowed to be a pleasant, hot thing in the afternoon and to bring only a scrap of dry paper, a seed from a plant that does not grow here, the smell of salt and distance — news, not contact. The bowl remains a dark smudge that could be shadow. Travelers cross and do not dip. The power stands, in effect, in the doorway, and does not let the outside world fall in.

It lets the daily cycle run. It lets the cold, dense air pool ankle-deep at night and sink back at evening. It lets the morning mist hang between the houses and smell of crushed green leaf and warm stone, and burn off when the disc reaches the floor. It lets the updraft lift cooking smoke, dust, seeds, feathers, midges, up and out through the notch and away. Once in a generation a foreign seed finds soil trapped in a crack and takes hold — not an improvement, not a gift of progress, just a reminder that the rim is a membrane, not a prison. Most die. That is proper.

It lets the hawk come twice a day, very high, and pass. Lizards flatten. Birds freeze mid-call. Goats bunch. Children under the fig stop mid-lick and stare up, even the ones who lick dew every dawn and could tell you which vine leaf holds the most. Then the noise rushes back louder, as if relieved. The power does not intervene because the hawk is cruel. The hawk is part of the clock, as the mortars are. To remove it would be to remove the silence that makes the noise meaningful.

It lets the shaft rope fray. It lets the pit potter turn a base by hand and smooth it with a wet thumb, imperfectly. It lets the copper smith drag his anvil into the sun patch for the minutes he needs the extra heat and drag it back. It lets the fig-keepers argue over fruit and prune vines from strangling the tree that is wider than the clearing, and lets the tree lift slabs and crack walls and jars, and lets the walls be rebuilt around the roots. It trusts the arrangements perfected over a hundred generations of never leaving, because it can see, all at once and whole, what a lifetime inside can only learn lane by lane: that every person and every animal here — the woman pounding grain, the boy leading goats up the tiers, the girl with the covered jar, the cat sleeping in the fig-root hollow, the ant on the doorstep declaring war over a fallen plum, the bat leaving the crack, the root going down into the dark where the water is — is not a population. It is a single being, breathing the transpired air of the bowl almost as much as it drinks.

That being does not need to be told anything new. It does not need to be saved. It needs, if anything, to be left undisturbed long enough to go on being itself. The kindest thing the power can do is to lend its attention and withhold its hand.

And so it watches. Not as a judge watches, not as a shepherd watches. As the sky watches that small circle far above the rim, intensely blue-black and crowded with stars, from the other side. The town sleeps on roofs in summer, under vines that cross lanes at head height, in lanes lit amber by oil in clay dishes, while the stone gives back its stored heat slowly, always warm even high up. The power looks down through that same small circle, and does not come closer, because coming closer would be the one thing that could make the bowl look up, and lose its mastery of the lanes, and waste the water, and forget the shade.

If you stand in the notch at dawn, when the dew is heavy on every leaf though the dust below is dry, and you watch the children tilt broad leaves so the drops run to their mouths, you will not see it. You will not feel it, except perhaps as the odd steadiness of the place, the way it seals itself at night and opens itself at morning, as if something large and patient were holding the rim in place.

It is holding it. It will go on holding it. Not forever — nothing, not even a wise power, is asked to be forever — but for as long as it takes a human lifetime to learn the lanes, and another, and another. That is its benevolence. That is its wisdom. To see a whole world in a pocket of shade, to love it for its completeness, and to do the hardest thing a power can do:

Nothing, beautifully, and without looking away.
