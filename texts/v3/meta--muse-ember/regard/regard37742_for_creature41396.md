It hears it first, too, and louder than anything that small should be.

It does not come down the ladder. It does not come sideways through the dog-legged tunnel around the jutting sluice-beam, though it could. It arrives the way the light arrives — through three dinner-plate vents in the crown, through the lime in the mortar, through the water itself in the clay pipe-mouth at the deep corner. Before sound, it feels it as vibration: the inflow hammering where the spring pushes in cloudy with bubbles, the counterweight sluice lifted and dropped against its frame, the single drip from a ceiling joint landing like a stone thrown down a well. In a room twenty paces across, that noise has to be made on purpose. The power listens longer than any worker ever did, because it does not need to shout to be heard across it.

It has listened to louder places. It knows that loudness is not suffering and emptiness is not loneliness. That is its reasoning, and it takes some care to learn. Its heart has to learn it too, separately, from the fern.

For the being here is not one body, and the power does not mistake it for one. It is not the fern alone, though the fern is the most determined living thing in the place — a single plant in a mortar joint above the waterline, splitting the brick by a hair, living on steam and condensation and the flat white-gold light that moves minutely with the hour. It is not the fig root alone, pale and searching in the air from a vent, not yet reaching water. It is not the thin green film of algae on the cooler north side, nor the iridescent bacterial skin that forms and breaks on the slack water by the jars, nor the mat of duckweed-like green that gathers in the corner by the steps and is torn apart by the current.

It is the holding-on itself. All of those separate, barely-there lives, and behind them the cycle that makes them all barely possible: the spring pushing in, the sluice banging, the overflow sucking out by the entrance tunnel, the plug sealed with clay in the drain channel. The cycle is the resident. Everything else lives in its slack corners, above it, or in its resets. The power understands this quickly, the way you understand a thumbprint in still-damp mortar — not by counting, but by touch. These are weeks old, not centuries. The bricks are still too sharp. The plaster is still pale. Colonisation has barely begun, and that is correct. Its intuition says: do not rush this.

It could, if it wanted, finish the room.

It could lower the sluice and still the water. It could cool the inflow to a temperature where fish could stay, and seed the spring-mouth with fry that would dart in the perfect clarity and make the emptiness feel less total. It could widen the three small suns in the crown so the fern had more light, and fill its joint with soil so its roots would not have to split brick by a hair. It could let the fig root through, quickly, cleanly, so the search would end. It could sweep the stone hammer from the shallows, hang the three paddles neatly on the ledge, set the jars upright, and replaster the pale places where the mason hurried. It could make a bench. It could make a place for sleeping, for eating, for not being wet, as a resident would.

That is what a lesser benevolence would do. It would mistake use for care.

The power does none of it.

Not the fish, because this water is trying, with lime and heat, to sterilise itself, and the algae and bacteria that live here have learned that sterility as a condition, not an affliction. To populate the deep channel would be to give the room a prettier kind of emptiness, where something died beautifully and was replaced.

Not the silence, because the banging is not a flaw in the vault. The vault throws every drip back like a hammer on a drum because it was built to hold water under pressure and let it out on a timer of stone and rope. To quiet it would be to stop its breathing. The workers shouted over it by day and left it to itself by night, and the room did not become unpeopled when they climbed the ladder. It went on filling and emptying, as if the absence of people were the intended condition. The power honours that intention.

Not the soil for the fern, because the fern does not need saving. It needs the joint to be hard. It needs to split it by a hair to be a fern here and not a fern anywhere else. Give it earth and shade and it would live, but it would no longer be holding on. The power’s heart wants to ease it. Its wisdom, which is slower, does not.

Not the fig root’s arrival, not yet. A root that has come down through brickwork from the courtyard tree above is a probe between worlds. If it touches the water now, in the first weeks, it will drink and swell and pierce the vent and follow the steam down and become a tree in a place that has no room for a tree’s crown. Let it search a while longer. Searching is its work for now.

And not the finishing. The cracked brick at shoulder height, pale and unweathered as if broken last week. The fingerprint ridges in the lime. The loom-frame legs poking out of the green water. The coil of wet rope on the second step. Those are not mess to be tidied by a god. They are evidence that hands were here and have not yet decided to return. A finished room would be a lie. This room is true because it is unfinished and still in use and already being left to fill.

If anything, then — and the power does act, because benevolence without action curdles into mere watching — it does the smallest mercies that keep the cycle a cycle and do not improve it into something else.

It keeps the clay sealing the wooden plug damp, so it does not dry and crack and drain the chamber in the night while the workers are gone. It keeps one side of the sluice rope from fraying where it rubs the frame, so the counterweight continues to lift and drop and bang every few minutes, on time, whether anyone is there to hear it or not. It lets the light through the three vents fall a fraction more to the north side than it did yesterday, so the algae streaks down from the joints have a little more room to test the brick, and a fraction less straight down into the deep channel, where nothing should root. It keeps the moisture in the fern’s mortar joint steady through the hotter hours, not wet, not dry — just steady. It does not let a bird nest in a vent and steal a sun. It lets the jars bob in their line like buoys, half-submerged, because they are marking the slow water where the bacterial skin can form and break and form again.

None of this makes a noise. None of it would be seen by a worker coming down the ladder in the morning, except that the room would still be there, waist-deep at the steps, chest-deep in the channel, warm to the palm even underwater, blazing bright and almost entirely empty exactly as it should be.

At night, when the ladder is drawn up and the courtyard above goes quiet except for the dull thumps through the crown, the power takes the workers’ place on the dry ledge. Not sitting — there is no bench and it does not make one. Just being there as attention, the weight of being witnessed, so the room is not completely unpeopled while it fills by evening. It listens to the inflow in its ears as much as in the air. It watches the three small suns move minutely. It does not need to be warm and buoyant in the deep channel, floating chest-deep with the vault like sky above it, to know what that feels like.

It will stay when the bricks finally round, years from now, when the fern has split the joint wide enough to fall or to fruit, when the fig root has either reached the water or dried to a pale thread in the air. It will not be surprised by any of that. It has already decided, in its reasoning and its heart and its intuition all at once, not to pre-empt it.

If the workers ever come back with cement to seal the vents, or with a plan to drain the spring for a cleaner laundry, or with orders to demolish this hidden pocket because it is too loud and too small and too impractical under the courtyard, the power will not stop them with force. It will make the sluice bang a little harder. It will make a drip fall a little louder. It will make a person coming down the ladder have to turn sideways around the sluice-beam and pause, and listen first, and wade waist-deep into clear turquoise water and see the far wall as plainly as the near one, perfectly paved, perfectly empty — and understand, in the steam, that something lives here that is not a who at all, and is therefore very hard to evict.

For now it does almost nothing, which is the hardest and most benevolent act available to a wise power: to keep a place, and the slight, edge-dwelling being that clings to it, from being disturbed into a different life than the one it is already, barely and nobly, living.

The sluice bangs again. The level rises a hand’s breadth and slaps the bricks. The bacterial skin breaks. The fern holds. The power stays, listening.
