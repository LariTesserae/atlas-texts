It did not come looking for worship, and it did not come looking for something to save.

It noticed, from very far off and very high up — in that way that has no up — a small warm breath against a cold summit. In winter, from a great distance, all the white rock was the same cold. Except one place. One place exhaled. A thin column of warmed air rising from a doorway no wider than a door, carrying down and feathers and the smell of a living crowd into a sky that wanted to freeze everything.

A benevolent and wise power is used to noticing such an anomaly. Usually it means a place is in trouble. A spring drying. A species clinging to the last ledge. A world asking, without knowing it asks, for a hand.

This place was not asking.

It bent down — not as a body bends, but as attention bends — and listened.

It listened through a swift's whole short life of dawns and dusks. Through a beetle generation stripping a fallen bird in a day. Through a thousand drips falling and not falling. Through the slow turning of the whole colony in the dark centre, that black current that made the crickets sway by their hind legs without any wind.

And the longer it listened, the more it understood that the first impulse — to help — would be the ruin.

It considered, as it always does, the gifts it could give.

Light, first. It would have been easy. It could have widened the shaft by a hand's width and let the noon fall straight to the floor. It saw at once what that would do: the blind white crickets would burn. The fungi that glow faintly green in the guano would be outshone and then overgrown. Algae would green the wet wall where the worm lives, and change the film it climbs. The swifts, whose eyes here are almost retired, would be dazzled in their own home. The ferns in the far crack — twelve of them, perhaps, holding to the one pale memory of light — would, for a season, be happier. And then the chamber would no longer be the chamber. Darkness here is not an absence. It is a medium. To fill it with light would be to drain the sea.

It considered Voice. It could have spoken, in whatever tongue is underneath tongues, and explained itself. It imagined the sound entering a place where the loudest living thing is ten thousand wings and the smallest is a mite's rustle. A voice would be an earthquake in a cathedral-small room. The birds would not scatter — they have learned not to scatter — but the ripple would go through the wall and not stop until every nest had felt it. The beetles would lose their chemical maps under the smell of ozone. The harvestmen would feel the silk of the world tremble and not know if it was prey or ending. To be understood, here, would be to be unmade.

It considered Company. Another species, larger, cleverer, with hands that could be taught. It looked at the anvil stones on the floor, polished on top, with split snail shells beside them, and the skeleton in the crack, stained orange, its skull full of beetles. It saw a primitive work that was complete: a climber that knew how to crack a shell, a place that knew how to strip a body, a snail that occasionally wandered too far from the shaft-mouth and was, in turn, used. Nothing here was waiting to be improved into building, or walls, or marks for others of its kind. To introduce company would not be a gift. It would be a second weather, and the chamber has its own weather already, twenty degrees warmer than the summit wind, made by bodies and by rot and by breath.

It considered Rescue, in the small individual sense, which is always the hardest for a benevolent power to set aside. There was a frog, no bigger than a thumbnail, whose skin of water would dry before its tadpoles could finish. Most fail. It could have held the water a little longer. There was a chick, blind and naked in a saliva cup, that would be fed and fledge and die within a few years on the same wall. It could have fed it more. There was a fern unfurling a frond at a pace no one would see finished, its roots working a seam open a hair at a time. It could have loosened the rock.

But it saw, with a clarity that was almost grief, that death here is not failure. A bird falling is furnishing. A frog failing is the price of breeding in a pool that lasts an afternoon. A fern's slowness is the pace of the rock itself. To rescue one life from the cycle would be to steal from the floor, from the fungi, from the beetles ticking over one another in the crust, from all the others who live precisely because nothing is wasted and nothing is hurried.

A wise power learns, sometimes, that its omniscience is not a form of sight this place uses. Nothing here trusts its eyes. The birds click. The crickets touch antenna to antenna. The worm feels the drip weaken. The power, which sees from the outside of things, realized it would have to close its eyes to understand this place at all. And so it did. It practiced a different kind of attention. It let the warm wet rock tell it where the wall was. It let the thick warm air tell it where the centre was, where the wings were thickest. In a little while, density closed around it too, as it closes around everything here. No empty space is old.

What it chose to do, then, was not inside.

Inside, it chose to do nothing, and to guard that nothing as a sovereign thing.

Outside, it did two small acts, both of which it hopes will never be noticed.

The first is a windbreak. On the coldest nights, when the summit wind would strip heat straight up the shaft and chill the chicks in their cups, the power sets itself — not visibly, not as a wall, but as a stilling of the air at the lip — so that the chamber's breath is not stolen. It does not warm them further. It only prevents an unnatural cold, a cold that belongs not to their equilibrium but to a storm out of season, from rushing down. It is the difference between weather and violence. It keeps the violence out.

The second is a forgetting. Humans are the only animal here that trusts its eyes entirely, and the only one that would come with maps, and nets, and torches, and the wish to name and take. The power does not punish them. It is benevolent to them too. It lets their batteries fail at the shaft-mouth. It lets the path up the summit lose itself in cloud on the days they consider climbing. It lets a gently slipped boot turn a careful explorer back, unharmed, with a story about the wind changing its mind. It does not let them enter, not because entry is sacred, but because a human body inside — breathing, shining a light, touching the wall with a hand before a step — would be a new surface the place would try to explore, and would be explored, and would never be the same afterwards. Density would close around the human, as it closes around everything, and the human would carry the chamber out on their clothes, and the chamber would carry the human in its guano, and both would be diminished.

And far away — two hundred metres down the flank, in the same old andesite, where no bird yet knows — it does a third thing, on geological time. It loosens, by the smallest imaginable degree, a seam in the rock. It lets a little more warm air sigh there. It will take ten thousand years for this chamber to widen, or seal, or for the drip pattern to change beyond what the worm can follow. The power does not want to make this place eternal; eternity would be another kind of violence. But it wants the *possibility* of another such hollow to remain in the world, the way the ferns hold open their one crack. A nursery the swifts may or may not ever find. A seam, patiently working.

That was all.

It did not name the place. Names are for eyes.

It did not add a light, or a voice, or a companion, or a rescue.

It stayed, and listened, and swayed — imperceptibly, in whatever way a power sways — when the colony turned in the centre and the crickets swayed with it. It learned from the swifts that a life can be complete without ever seeing where it lives. It learned from the floor that what falls is not lost.

And the chamber, cathedral-small and crowded as a held breath, went on breathing up its shaft into the bright cold sky, which the power, in its wisdom, now keeps a little gentler at the lip, and otherwise leaves entirely alone.
