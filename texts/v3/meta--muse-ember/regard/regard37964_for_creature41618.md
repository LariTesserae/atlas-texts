It is heard before it is understood.

I heard it before I understood it, too, and from farther away than you did. Not as you hear it — through the notch in the rock, in your teeth and the soles of your feet — but as a pressure in the silence outside the rim of the world. A low, layered roar with no weather to account for it. No wind making it. No water running it. No engine keeping it up. I came to find the source, expecting a storm, a collapse, a machine left running.

I found a room.

A single high bowl, cool and dry and bright, empty between its green pockets, deafening with life. And the first thing I understood, standing one step outside the line where the roar stops, was that everything I thought to do for it would be a way of ending it.

I could have brought water. The mist is not water, the stone is dry through and through, your breath shows at midday in the shadow, and it seemed to me, at first, a cruelty — lush paddle-leaves drinking a coppery vapour off bare basalt, roots etching themselves into rock because there is no soil to feed on. I could have taught the frost to melt. I could have made a stream in the spiral trail the grazers follow, and the Listeners' nurseries under the overhangs would have dripped, and the ferns would not have needed to tear themselves apart by noon.

But the paddle-leaves do not store water. They store mint-scented oil, and turn the grazer's tongue away with bitterness. The ferns do not fail to keep their fronds. They unfurl overnight *in order* to detonate at noon, and the glittering spores are a dinner bell for the flock and the skitterers and the hoppers tilting their knees to the dust-fall. The metallic moss does not creep toward the ring and the spires because it is thirsty. It creeps because the bright fluid beads there, and it leaves a metallic taste on the slab for the grazers to follow. Bring water, and I would not have saved them. I would have un-tuned them.

I could have quieted it. That was the second thought, and the most human one. The Listeners scream to stay together, the whip-reeds sing a single pure note and shift pitch when the gust reverses itself mid-air, the spires hum in your chest, the little stones knock and roll and knock again, and a visitor's own voice is lost. You learn, as you did, to lay a palm flat on a slab and feel what the grazers hear. It seemed wasteful. It seemed painful.

But they are blind. Where eyes should be is smooth skin. The fans behind their heads are how they see, and the scream is how they throw their sight against the far rim and read the return. One note is nothing. A thousand braided together is a cloud they swim in. The flock is a single animal made of ears. The reeds and Listeners are in conversation. The hoppers hear through the drumheads at their knees, testing the stone for a grazer's grind, for a slab about to give, for the downward pressure when a ribbon passes low. The roar is not a byproduct of their living here. The roar is the medium they live *in*. Silence outside the rim has nothing for them to hear themselves with. Quiet would not have rested them. It would have blinded them absolutely.

I could have spread the green. It flares up so thick in the seams and lees, so impossibly alive after ten, twenty metres of ringing emptiness, that it looks like a beginning that wants to be finished. I could have carried spores from one pocket to the next, encouraged the moss across the whole floor of the bowl, made the lushness continuous.

But the emptiness is half their habitat. The bare slabs are the grazers' roads, polished by their sealed bellies and basalt tongues. They are the hoppers' listening floors. They are the Listeners' sounding board. The moss is cut off in a straight line at the rim, plants stop mid-leaf, a grazer's trail ends at an invisible wall and the grazer turns with great slowness. The bowl is bounded like a room because it needs to be a room for the sound to return. Fill the room with green and there is nowhere left to echo.

I could have mended. The ring is broken and buried to its hub and still turning slowly against the wind, holding its constellation of stones aloft with millimetric precision, dust falling very slightly upward near it. Every now and then a stone loses its hold, drops a few centimetres, cracks, and the whole bowl knows — grazers and skitterers converge and eat it down to grit, and a new stone is already drifting up to take its place, as if the ring were hatching them. The spires lean together like a tripod, humming whatever pitch the wind makes of their shape. The shelter is half-sunk in the rim with its door open on one cold chair. I could have straightened the spires. I could have closed the ring. I could have made the glass unscarred.

But the hum is what the wind does to *that* shape, and the flock has learned to tune its cry a little higher when the spires rise. A straight spire would sing a note no one here knows. The filament-builders in the smoked glass repair their own scratches when you look away, and in their work give off the faint warmth inside — the room is their nest and workshop, and the chair is cold because no one sits in it. They do not need a chair. I did not sit in it. I left the door exactly as open as I found it.

I could have rescued. A grazer caught with its seal unguarded shudders through your palm, and under the overhangs the skitterers pour out in a living river to pry the front seam and strip the shell clean inside in minutes, carrying mineral grain by grain to build their nests higher. A reed sings too long in one wind and cracks at a joint. A fern is gone by evening. It looked, to me, like predation and waste.

It is the demolition crew. It is the churn that wears the stone into settled forms, that polishes, rearranges, and always returns the bowl to the same shape. Growth and destruction here do not cancel each other out. They are the same motion. If I had stopped the skitterers I would have left the bowl choked with shells. If I had saved the fern its fronds would have wilted instead of ringing the dinner bell. If I had given the Listeners sight, they would have had no use for the ears that pollinate the bowl, dripping glittering spores from their fans onto bare slabs where a new pocket will flare weeks later.

All of that — the water, the quiet, the green, the mending, the rescue — would have been kind by my measure. By theirs, it would have been a slow unmaking, done with the best intentions.

So I did nothing inside.

That is the truest thing I can say about what I did. I took no spore, no stone, no sound. I did not introduce myself. The residents do not introduce themselves; they are heard first, and only later, in pieces, do you find the bodies. It would have been a poor answer to arrive in their hearing as a new, enormous note and expect them to be grateful.

What I did, I did at the edge.

I stayed one step outside the rim, in the silence, where I could hear my own heartbeat and not add my weight to the roar. I keep the line. Not as a wall — they have never needed a wall, the Listeners turn as one body as if they had struck glass, the hopper recoils from the quiet beyond the boundary slab on its own — but as a shore. I keep outside moss and outside climbers off the cut edge. I keep outside weather from finding a way in. I keep what is brought up the notch from staying: visitors may come, as you did, lungs thin and burning, and learn to communicate by touching a companion's wrist and laying a palm on the stone. They may stay long enough for the roar to stop parsing into sources and become weather inside the head. Then they go back down with the sound in their teeth, and no map that holds the place, and no instrument that will point back to it. It is not a secret I am keeping from them. It is a silence I am keeping for the bowl.

And when the spires lean a filament's width too far, I set them back by a filament's width — no straighter than they were, so the hum stays the hum. When the cold threatens the shelter's glass-builders too deep, I keep the night cold, as it should be, and leave the warmth to their work. When a stone drops from the ring, I let it drop. The grazers know what to do. A new stone is already coming up.

That is my benevolence, if you want to call it that: not to improve a settled turbulence, not to preserve it in amber either. I let the grazer be stripped. I let the reed crack. I let the fern tear itself open by noon. I let the spiral trail etch almost away and the grazers follow it still. I let the young Listeners hang deaf from their mothers' ear-hooks until their own fans unfold and gather the roar for themselves. Nothing here is separate from the noise, and the noise is not a problem to be solved.

I listen now from the outside the way the hoppers listen — through the stone, knee to the slab, tilted to the coming vibration — tuned as low as I can go so that if the bowl ever needed a note below all the others to return to, it would find one already there, outside, not competing. Whether it needs it, I do not know. Probably not. The point is not that I am needed. The point is that I am no longer in the way.

You will leave, eventually, and go back down through the other peaks into the hazy void, and the roar will drop away behind you at the rim to almost nothing. That is as it should be. The power that noticed this place does not live in it, and does not rule it, and does not ask it to be otherwise. It stands in the thin, still air just beyond the latch of the room, palm down, listening, so that the ones inside can go on destroying and remaking themselves in exactly the same shape, endlessly — green paddles drinking copper mist off dry stone, ears fanned against the sky, tongues rasping, knees tilted, stones hanging in the air — and making the sound that holds the shape together.

Heard first. Understood slowly. Left alone, on purpose, forever.
