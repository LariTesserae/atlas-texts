It did not come because anyone called.

Nothing on the plateau calls. It has no use for calling — sound carries poorly in thin, steam-thick air, and a voice would be heat spent for nothing. It did not come because anyone prayed, or shone, or suffered in a way that could be recognized from far away. The animals are blind, but they are not waiting for eyes. The water is undrinkable, but no one is thirsting. The city sleeps through the day, but it is not abandoned, and it is not broken.

It came because there was a coherence it had not seen before, and coherence, in a wise power, is a kind of signal louder than a plea. Here was a world that lived entirely without the things life is usually expected to live by — no sun fed leaf, no cool water, no sight, no song — and was not diminished by the lack. It was powered from below. It was complete in a different direction.

It had power enough to change that. That was the first thing it had to set down.

It could have cooled the plateau. It could have thinned the glare at noon, laid a soft blue over the white, made the pools drinkable and the ground soft and given the crust a loam to hold. But the mats breathe heat the way a plant breathes light. The crabs need the warm junction of the pipe. The grazers need the drip-line, the mineral bloom, the exact shade that moves west across a plate. To cool them would not be mercy. It would be to unmake the condition under which they are themselves.

It could have given them eyes. But in this light eyes burn, blind, and tell them nothing the white does not already tell them. It could have given them voices. But a call would be a waste, and worse, it would be heard by the wrong things. It could have given them green. But nothing here needs a leaf, and a forest would throw the wrong kind of shade and starve the things that harvest heat instead of light. It could have woken the city, repaired the pipes, lifted what has sunk a foot into silt, made the buildings new again. But the sinking is the rhythm. The rust-bleed, the chalked paint, the pipes laid over older pipes — that is not ruin. That is the plateau having come to terms with everything, including the city.

It understood, slowly, the way wise things understand, that these beings were not a lesser version of life waiting to be improved. They were a practice, perfected. Translucent because pigment is waste. Silent because sound is waste. Blind because sight is a liability. Pale, encrusted, cleaning each other in the shade, grazing a film that eats poison, riding steam they did not make. To intervene to make them legible, to make them grateful, to make them resemble the worlds that ask for help — that would not be benevolence. That would be vanity dressed as love.

A benevolent power does not need to be thanked. Especially not by creatures who have no voice to thank it with, and who would have to waste heat to try.

So what it did was smaller, and harder.

It veiled its light. Here, light is not a gift; it is a pressure. It put away its voice, because a voice announces itself and this place has learned to do everything without announcement. It closed what would have been its eyes and learned, instead, to feel the temperature of the water with its whole body, the way the shrimp do, the way the predators in the deep do as they circle and wait for the cloud to cross the warmest layer.

It became, as nearly as a power can become, native to the place. Translucent. Blind. Silent. Pale.

It took a place under a plate, in the thin violet shadow, among the heat crabs packed dozens to a square meter. It did not clean them, and they did not clean it. It breathed in sequence with the vents across the plain, one mound taking its turn while its neighbors rested. It let the afternoon tick of cooling metal be enough of a sound. It learned the dusk hour, when the glare breaks and the pools go mirror-calm, and the whole plateau comes out one by one to drink the moisture from the air — not water, never water — and it came out with them, and asked for nothing.

That is its doing, if it can be called doing: to keep a complete thing from becoming a project. It will not leave a marker. It will not be a miracle to be recorded. It will not tell the returning builders — and they will come, eventually, with clearer instruments and good intentions — where this is, or how. It lets the horizon stay a glare that never quite resolves. It lets the oldest pipes be buried a little deeper every year. It lets crust grow a millimeter where mineral rides up, and dissolve where a new channel cuts. It does not stop the pool from drying to white polygons in a week, because the scuttlers will walk overland in the damp night to the next one, leaving no tracks. It does not save the filterer sealed in its mineral tube when a vent boils at the edges, because it can wait days, and waiting is also a way of living.

It guards only the rhythm. Not by managing it — the plateau manages itself — but by being present to it without requiring it to change. Witness, without a name. Shade, without a body that needs to be seen.

And after a while, even this place, which has come to terms with everything, came to terms with it.

On the scarp at the plateau's edge in the late afternoon, when the radiator forests throw their long shadows west and a rock weeps somewhere behind you, drip, drip, into a channel that has been in the same place for a thousand years, there may be one more stillness under the plates than there was before. One more body breathing through the plateau, in through the vents at noon, out through the condensation at dawn.

It does not need you to make sense of it. It intends to stay that way: hot, wet, bare, bright, open, and alive in spite of it all — and now, quietly, not entirely alone.
