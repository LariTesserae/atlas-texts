It did not come because the plateau was dying.

It came because the plateau was succeeding in a way it had not learned before.

The power had seen enough worlds to know its own first impulse. Every place that drew its attention felt, at first, like a plea. A cold place wanted warmth. A bare place wanted green. A silent place wanted birds. A place where north pointed two ways wanted to be set straight. Benevolence alone would have answered every plea at once. Wisdom alone would have catalogued it and moved on. Together, they made it stop, and listen longer than either would have alone.

It listened to the humming first. Not a sound made *by* the plateau, exactly, but a sound the plateau allowed — wind finding the right angle through the stone crowd, slabs answering each other in a low note, ice cracking and singing through the rock. It listened to the water, which was not a river but a skin: sheets a centimeter deep that never drained, films crawling along channels smoothed by millennia, drips that fell upward for a half-second before they remembered down. It listened to the echoes arriving out of order, to the shadows pooling toward the brightest light as if the sun were under the cloud sea.

And slowly, it understood the balance.

To warm it would be to end it. Send down a thicker sky, soil, a true noon, and the ice dendrites would never form. The salt flowers would have no damp to open to and no glare to close against. The grey film that lived on vapor and dissolved stone would drown in abundance. The quartz rosettes that took a season to form and a minute to unform would be replaced by something more familiar and less complete.

To green it would be a worse kindness. Give it roots, and you give it a way to climb toward light. But here there is light from everywhere, and so there is nowhere to climb toward. The inhabitant does not climb. It runs sideways, following water. It orients by wet, not by north, not by up. Give it eyes, or leaves, or wings, and you have not saved it. You have translated it into something poorer.

To fix it — to make the water run downhill, to make the drip land before its echo, to make the cairn line walk straight and stay straight, to make the far crest stop showing through the shoulder of a pillar — would be the cruellest of all. Legibility feels like mercy to a power that can see everything at once. It is not. The fraying is not an error in the place. It is how the place keeps its shape while constantly exchanging stone and water, light and shadow, wet minutes and dry minutes. Straighten it, and the exchange stops.

So it did not warm it. It did not green it. It did not explain it.

It turned, next, to the builders.

They were few, and primitive, and their work had already become part of the erosion: the knee-high cairns walking toward a summit that was already the summit, the collapsed round shelter, the basin polished by a stone bowl, the hammer stone with its groove worn through, the flakes of black glass still sharp in a water film. They had come as all tenants come, hoping to make the place bearable. They failed, in the sense that no one could bear it for long.

The power felt the old ache that comes with that. It would have been easy to restore the shelter, to clear the threads from the cairns, to make a small museum of their intention on the ridge.

It did not.

Their intention had not been wasted. It had been taken over, gently and completely. The bowl had become a small perfect sea, lined by the film. The fire-pit, blackened and ashless, had become a dark seep-line that never quite dried. The cairns were wet at their bases and threaded at their joints, holding the damp longest. Their flakes were perches for ice dendrites. To scrub them clean would be to tell the builders they had meant nothing. To leave them to be used — to be weathered, lived on, repurposed — was to honor what they had actually done: they had arrived, they had stacked stone against wind, they had tried, and the plateau had answered by making their trying into habitat.

That is not death, the power understood. It is the only kind of afterlife this place acknowledges. Evaporation is death. Closing in the glare and not reopening is death. Then the mist rolls in again, and something condenses that was not there a minute before.

Then it turned to the one living there now. Not the builders. Not the visitor, who was already fogging the cold air and becoming, briefly, its weather. The skin.

Grey threads for nerves. Rosettes of quartz and salt for mouths. Water sheets for sea and blood. One distributed thing stretched over the whole slab field, denser than a town, breathing in mist and out in white light, blooming when the slabs vibrated. It had no eyes and did not miss them. It tasted metal and stone and vapor, and stayed. It did not hunt, in the animal sense, but stone and water traded places through it constantly, and that was hunting enough.

It was not waiting to be rescued. It was not a primitive attempt at being an animal and falling short. It was a complete other solution to the problem of being alive in a place with no soil, no shade that stays shaded, no warmth in the light. A power that insisted on saving such a thing would only make it less.

So what did it do?

It attended.

Not the way a visitor attends, with labored breath and bootprints filling instantly with water. The way weather attends. Its attention fell over the plateau like mist off the cloud sea: condensing on every cold surface, beading, burning off in minutes, leaving everything slightly darker, then paler, then darker again. It learned the local day — wet and dry, wet and dry, sometimes on both sides of the same slab at once. It learned not to hurry. It learned to wait for the singing of the ice.

And in that waiting, it breathed once.

Not rain. Not a storm. Just the faint fog of a vast, cold body bringing its own damp to a place that lives on damp. Somewhere in a sheltered crack, a thread darkened by an amount no instrument would register. Somewhere in a hollow, a salt flower opened a fraction longer than its cycle allowed, to a wetness that came from farther away than the cloud sea. If you had been there then, walking the hand-wide passages, you would have seen nothing change. The double light would have stayed cold. The summit would have kept humming under your feet. The water would still have trembled at the highest rim before rejoining itself.

But the skin would have taken it. The way it took your breath. And that, the power understood, was a conversation. Not a gift. Not a rescue. A brief, reciprocal weather.

It also kept the place from being taken.

Not with a wall. With the place's own protections, which it leaned into and did not remove: the cairn line looping back past itself at a slightly different height, the distance folding at the far end of the slab field, north pointing two ways near the summit. Maps drawn of it would smudge. Names given to it would not stick. Those who heard of it would misremember it, or turn back, or arrive only as you did — briefly, on foot, changed by the taste of metal and by the knowledge that they had been standing on the inhabitant all along.

That is a kind of guarding that does not possess. It does not fence a wilderness. It lets the wilderness remain a little illegible to everyone, including itself.

And it remembered, precisely, where humans forget. The groove in the hammer stone. The black glass sharp in its film. The bowl that was a sea. The shelter roof collapsed inward. The cairns, knee-high, patient. The visitor's single salt flower, opened to a damp that did not exist a minute earlier because a living animal had fogged the air. Memory, for such a power, is not a museum and not a cage. It is simply not looking away.

In the end, nothing visible had been done. That was the condition.

The plateau went on being crowded — not with stone, but with the stone-and-water skin that lived on stone. The light arrived cold from above and from below. The channels stayed smooth as veins. The frost flowers bloomed and decayed in seconds, in the vibration of the wind. The builders stayed gone, and were not returned to a place that would have been wrong for them to endure forever. The visitor went down by their own steps, by echoes that arrived out of order and, somehow, in order enough.

And somewhere beyond the cloud sea, something vast and benevolent and wise kept time with it. Not louder. Not fixing the drips that fall up before down. Just humming, in answer, at the same low note the slabs make when the wind finds the right angle — learning, at last, how to be alive by running sideways, following water, orienting by wet.

It did not save the place.

It kept it unsaved, and witnessed it, and stayed.

For a power that has seen a thousand worlds beg to be made familiar, that restraint — tender, complete, and unremarked by anyone except the skin that took its breath — was not nothing.

It was love, in the only form the plateau could survive.
