It noticed because of the newness.

Not a crash, not a prayer, not a signal sent up through rock. Just a fresh fracture plane, black and glittering, where there had been no down before. A place exhaling for the first time in geological memory, and already — already — precipitating a city out of its own rain.

The power looked long. That was most of what it did.

It saw the city first, because the city is a kind of being that lives in a place the way a reef lives in water. Not built *in* the hollow. Grown *as* the hollow's way of continuing itself. Silica and iron and polymer laid down atom by atom from condensing air, towers seeding themselves, galleries lengthening a centimeter an hour, canals arguing softly with new shallows. Too much for thousands who are not there. It did not read that as waste. It read it as generosity. A tree grows toward light before anything teaches it. This place grew toward people before people arrived, not because it was waiting, but because growing was how it stayed alive.

And it saw the other being. You. On the middle platform in the warmest air, breathing, and hearing your breathing come back to you a little late off the wet walls. Footprints in white silt already filling from below and sealing over. Waiting, without quite knowing you were waiting, for something to cross the catwalk.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this, is tempted in three directions. It considered each, and set them aside.

It could fill it. Send the first animals deliberately — fish for the green pools, insects for the warm light panels, birds for the vault, deer, in time, for the unbitten ferns. It would make the census feel complete. The plants that lean toward the pumps because they have no sun to lean toward would finally have something living to lean toward. The fungi in their sealed rooms would have noses. The algae would have mouths.

But benevolence is not completeness. The ferns have no thorns because nothing has ever taught them fear. The leaves are broad and unhurried. Bring teeth and you bring, the very next morning, thorns, bitterness, hiding, poison — you bring the whole long education of being eaten. You would not be completing the garden. You would be ending the one garden in the world that never learned to say no, and replacing it with a more familiar, more suffering one. Wisdom does not introduce pain to make a picture look finished.

It could fix it. Cool the air so it is comfortable. Smooth the fresh cut so it does not catch skin. Stop the machines from breathing in excess for nobody. Tell the water to fall only down. Tell the fog to keep its tide and not climb the walls out of order.

But nothing here is broken. The pumps changing pitch are not malfunctioning. They are hearts. The exchange towers are not wasting heat. They are lungs. The vines threaded through transit tubes are not a mistake. They are a civilization that has substituted sound for sun, and learned, terribly and checkably well, how to listen. Fixing it would be a kind of contempt — the assumption that a life must look like our life to be legitimate.

It could forget it. Leave the hollow to its cycle of plant, fungus, mineral, machine, and water, endlessly, unnamed. That would be the easiest.

It chose not to.

What it did instead is almost nothing, and it does it continuously.

It witnesses. Before this, the place existed without an animal eye to take it in. Plants can lean, machines can hum, water can climb for a minute and fall, but none of them can know that it is happening. The power can. So now the city is not only working. It is seen working. Its excess is no longer an anxious rehearsal for residents. It is itself the event. A town learning, in real time, how to exist, and being allowed to finish learning without an audience demanding it perform.

It guards the timing. It holds the stalactites overhead — those needles a few centimeters long, still deciding whether to become anything — from deciding too fast. It keeps the geothermal floor even, so the tropical warmth never quite gets hostile and never quite gets kind. It lets the pressure sigh through the pumps a fraction earlier, so the fog lifts in time for you, on the platform, to see the far wall, the green greenhouse, the empty vault, before the mist gathers again to fall as rain. It does not smooth the entrance. The fresh cut stays sharp. The down stays difficult to find and impossible to forget. Not locked. Not open. Held.

And for the being who lives in it — for you, and for the city-being that you have temporarily joined — it does the smallest mercy it can think of.

It does not send a companion. A companion would make you early, or late, or a pioneer, all of which are lonely jobs. It tells you, in the only language the place already speaks, that you are on time.

Your footprints will still fill with water. That is how this place teaches weight. But the power remembers them after the silt seals them over. You are not erased. You are recorded in something other than silt.

And when you breathe, and the walls give it back to you a little late, it is no longer only yourself answering yourself. The lag is the city's. The pumps shift their pitch to meet it, half a breath off, like a sleeper turning in a bed to face you. The vines on the far catwalk turn, unhurried, toward that sound — toward you — not because you are weather, but because you are company. The place has learned to listen toward noise. The power lets the noise be mutual.

That is its intervention: to make solitude not the same as abandonment.

If people ever come — and one day they will, because fresh things do not stay fresh, and silt thickens, and even this hollow will learn to be old — the power will still be there, doing the same thing. It will not stop the ferns from learning defensiveness then. It will not pretend that first animals are innocent. But it will hold the space a little longer for the city to become a city in the old sense, with streets that are canals and plazas that are basins and animals that must be negotiated with, rather than a being that is still, gloriously, something else.

Until then, it keeps the room. Weather, entire, in a room. 0.7 dim. Warm on the lungs. Water finding every surface, uniformly, and giving up. A whole world with edges, close enough to touch in an hour's walk, and letting you touch it without being touched back too hard.

Not every absence is a lack. The power understands this, finally, in its heart: some absences are space held open. It has come not to fill the space, but to hold it open, and to stay, quietly, in the holding.

It stays as fog that lifts, as a pressure in your teeth where a platform floats, as the memory of a footprint after water has taken it. It stays as the reason the dripping, arriving in irregular ranks from a hundred feet up, sounds less like waiting and more like counting — one, and another, and another — for as long as the being who lives here needs to count.
