**What the Witness Does**

It did not arrive as light.

In Sinderhold, light is always made, strung on sagging cables, amber in the quiet hours, hard white in the working hours. A true light — sun, thunder, glory — would have been a cruelty there. It would have blinded amber-adjusted eyes and bleached the already white ferns and set the whole canyon hissing. So the power that noticed them came the way everything else in that place comes: by the shaft where the air leaves, and by the rock where the heat rises. It came as attention.

It did not come because they were suffering. Suffering was there, of course, salt on the lip, sweat that never evaporated, gloves that never dried. But it was not drawn by suffering first. It was drawn, if the word can be used at all, by completeness. It listened long enough to learn their measures before it tried to change anything: the ninety-minute breath of the vents, the ten minutes of warm rain from the weeping dome, the drip counting itself from eave to gutter, the soft ping of young basalt settling, the brighter roll of light when a furnace was tapped, the exhale between shifts when the machines seemed to rest and only the dripping remained.

It learned who plans a door because damp has swollen it. It learned who works the great valve wheels in pairs in the Engine Hall, one hauling while the other rests hot palms in the damp air. It learned the cool room that is only a degree or two cooler and is fought over politely. It learned that they are not victims waiting under a hill to be taken up the stair. They occupied the blister before the rock had softened. They built a thicket out of brick and iron and borrowed heat, and made a world out of it. A wise power does not pity makers. It respects them.

That is why, in the first long season of watching, it did almost nothing.

It would have been easy, in the abstract sense of easy that powers know, to open the dome. To crack the vault and let in sky. The thought came to it as it comes to anyone who hears a cave-born child say *snow* and try to taste the strangeness of the word: *give them the outside.*

It held the thought up to the place and watched it break.

The sky above is not a gift here; it is a different climate. The buildings are built shoulder-to-shoulder because heat is free and light is made; a real sun would find no place to fall except on iron roofs already housing someone else's floor. The ceiling rain, the square's green, the plants' whole timing — all of it depends on the dome weeping and the vents pulling the excess away. Tear that open and the ninety-minute breath fails. The succulents in the gutters, which had learned to store warm alkaline water against the next dry hour, would dry or drown. The pale ferns, adjusted to amber, would burn. And the people, who sweat their way through fifty degrees in the canyon, would not wake one morning to a pleasant spring and thank heaven; they would wake in houses whose pipes, doors, screw jacks, and boiler-work were all made for heat, with no food traded for the castings they no longer knew how to sell. Outside is not a medicine. It is another world, and they have already made this one.

It considered the animals.

That was the longer temptation, because the absence is what the body learns first. Eight hundred people hurrying and no scurry in the gutters. No flutter from a thousand eaves. No dog at heel in the lanes. A benevolent power feels that absence like a hollow in a room where a chair should be.

It imagined a dog for a child who had learned the word *dog* from a surface-born grandmother. It imagined it clearly: the panting in heat that cannot be cooled, the tongue already dry, the need for water too hot and mineral to drink, nowhere to run except the Cistern Square, which is barely a house plot. It imagined the child loving it fiercely, and the dog, who is not an abstraction but a life with needs, spending its whole short life in a place that would hurt it. That would not be companionship. That would be an importation of suffering, dressed as love.

It imagined a pigeon. Wings beating against the low vault. Feathers in the fans. A nest under an eave that is someone's kitchen window. A bird that dies in the vent sigh, so that children learn that the amber light can take a song away. It imagined rats, which might, in fairness, survive the gutters — and bring the diseases of the surface down the stair to babies who have never met them, and teach a sterile town what rot is. It imagined flies, finding the market stalls at last and spoiling food in a place that can afford no waste.

And then, deepest of all, it imagined making a new thing for them: a small heat-loving moth for the quiet hours, a creature that could live in the damp and pollinate the moss and make a soft sound in the amber light. It stopped there cold. A wise power does not create a somebody merely to fill somebody else's loneliness. The moth would be alone. The plants would be used. The children would be given a pet shaped out of their own wanting. It would not do that, either.

It considered cooling the place. Making the cistern drinkable. Making one room, or ten, truly cool. Heat is their burden; it is also their foundation. The factories do not burn fuel so much as borrow temperature from the rock and the great boilers. The castings, the furnace brick, the glass vials — they go up the stair in baskets to trade for food and letters. Take the heat away carelessly and you do not free them from work; you unmake the only economy that feeds them. Cool the cistern water and you kill the mineral film the moss flowers live on. A benevolent power may ease a threshold. It will not rewrite the conditions by which a people live and call that kindness.

So it learned to do small things, and to do them where smallness was mercy rather than interference.

It steadies a hand on a valve wheel at the exact moment a glove slips — not loosening the wheel by magic, not sparing the pair their work, only keeping the slip from becoming a burn that would keep a man from the Hall for a week. It takes a fraction of heat, a fraction only, from the room where a baby is being born and from the room where an old person is dying, those two thresholds where comfort costs the world nothing. It slows the ticking of the young rock by a hair on the nights when a house on screw jacks might crack in its sleep — it does not stop the rock from becoming itself, for Sinderhold is still, slowly, becoming itself, and that becoming is holy to it — but it keeps the settling from becoming a sudden fall.

In the Cistern Square during the quiet hours, when the fans have stilled and the heat ebbs a fraction and only the night watch and the children who are supposed to be sleeping see it, the moss-like growth opens its tiny waxy flowers. No animal comes to pollinate them. That is why the Witness does it itself, invisibly, carrying pollen a finger's breadth from one damp mat to another, the way a fingertip flicks condensation from a pipe onto a rosette. It is not an animal. It does not need to be kept, or fed, or grieved. It simply performs, unseen, the service that elsewhere belongs to wings. The flowers set seed in a place that has no pollinators, and the children, finding the seed, think it is the wind. It lets them think that. Their world is complete without needing to be explained.

When a child finds a beetle in the runoff — it does not belong, it is dying, the staring goes on a long time — the Witness does not breed a colony in the gutters. It lets that one beetle live three days instead of three hours. Long enough to be drawn in charcoal. Long enough to be named and released into a crack with great seriousness. Long enough to die a witnessed death and become a story that is not a warning. That is enough of outside.

And it remembers. When the last surface-born dies, the words they carried down the stair will not rot with them. *Dog. Pigeon. Snow. Tree.* The Witness keeps those words dry in an inner room, not as nostalgia, not as a rebuke to cave-born completeness, but so that when a mouth in the quiet hours shapes a birdsong it has never heard, just to see what the air does with it, the air answers with a little more resonance than air should have. It does not answer with a superior song. That would shame the child and overwhelm the lane. It simply stays in the shaped air for a breath, so the echo has a body.

Its largest gift is the one that harms no ecology and creates no dependent life: dreams.

In dreams, a cave-born child can run beside a dog in cool grass that does not need water, and wake sweating and laughing and a little sad in the way of waking from a good story. In dreams, snow can fall without freezing the pipes. A surface-born elder can dream of a sky that is not a dome and not ask to be taken to it. An outside that enters only in sleep asks for no food, clogs no vent, brings no disease down the stair. It can be loved, and left, and told about in the morning by the cistern where the pool steams and is too hot to drink but fine to look at. The Witness gives them vivid, kind, unpossessive dreams — the only outside wise enough to give a sealed world — and then steps back so the waking world remains theirs.

It does not announce itself. Revelation, in a place of amber lamps, would be bad manners and bad optics. Its presence is a little like the presence of the place itself: an extra ping in the cooling stone, a bead of condensation forming on a forearm that a mother can flick onto a fern, pressure in the ears familiar as the stair. It sits in the square while the moss blooms, because even a flower that no one sees should not be unwitnessed by everything. It counts the drip with them. It waits out the ninety minutes.

People above might call this inaction. They might imagine a benevolent and wise power sweeping in with birds and cold water and a hole in the sky, and lifting eight hundred people out of a blister in the black rock into a larger, greener country. That would be power, perhaps. It would not be wisdom, and it would not be love. It would treat Sinderhold as a problem rather than a place, as a sentence to be commuted rather than a world turned inside out and lived in, densely and quietly and completely, by people who made it themselves.

No dog wakes in the lanes. No pigeon lands on the plate of a bridge-street. The vents sigh, the dome weeps, the boilers breathe, the rail cart trundles its fixed loop, the lamps sink from white to amber. The pale plants tremble when the air moves and settle again, patient as anything that has learned to live by a ninety-minute breath.

And yet, in that world with no animals and no sky, they are not alone anymore. Not because something has been added to fill them, but because something has decided to stay and listen, to keep its hands mostly to itself, to do the smallest mercies it can without unmaking the place it loves — and to count, with them, all the way to ninety, and begin again.
