It does not come down the stair-path.

It stays above the rim, where the fog gathers, and it listens the way you listened, from a hundred paces below, to the *bong ... bong ... bong* rolling off rock and back again. It has heard other places announce themselves — with fire, with flood, with a voice in the market square — and it knows at once that this one is not asking to be announced. It is too new for announcement. It is barely a place yet. A cup of raw stone. A skin of peat. A dozen cold households holding a plan a hundred years ahead of them. A marsh learning to be a marsh, a fraction at a time.

A benevolent power, and a wise one, would understand what that requires first of all: that it be left alone to become what it is becoming.

So it will not bring the sun. It will not dry the wool. It will not warm the pools for the frogs, or make the heron nest, or invite the birdsong back. It will not fill the reservoir, or the tubs in the bath-house, or the row of jars under the rain chains. If it filled them, they would stop singing. An empty vessel is how this place makes its one true sound, and a wise power does not rush a young resonator into fullness just because fullness looks like completion.

It will not finish the roofs. It will not give the builders carts for the broad streets, or stock for the nonexistent pasture, or iron to replace the bronze chisels that split their nails. It will not hasten the peat. It will not teach the moss to grow faster, or the vines to climb less greedily, or the stone to stop weeping. All of those discomforts — the damp cold that gets into wool by morning, the wall raised a course a day, the vegetation grown over the work in three weeks, the children counting heartbeats inside a drip — are not failures. They are the curriculum. This is a place at the speed of seepage, and to intervene in the speed would be to abort the lesson.

That is the first mercy: abstention. It will watch for a hundred years, if that is what it takes, without looking away, which for a power that could raise a city in a day is itself a kind of labor. Attention, held steady and unhurried, is how an elder holds a newborn without picking it up too often.

And then, in the margins, it will do the small keepings that wisdom allows.

It will keep the difficulties that form, and prevent the ones that end. The scaffolding poles will knock together in the wind over the rim, as they must, but they will not fall. The unweighted shingles will lift and slap, as they must, but they will not tear away in the first hard night and leave a house open to the fog. The water will hammer in the blocked log pipe — *tok-tok-tok* — for an hour at a time, as it must, but the log will not split. One loose rock on the stair-path will stay loose for another year, and another, and will not come down on anyone's head while they are carrying stone in a basket. The reservoir will stay mostly dry, but it will not flood the lowest terrace. The cliffs will not erode. The streets will not wash out. Nothing essential will be allowed to fail before it has had its chance to be built slowly.

It will keep the tune true. Drips in different cisterns will not quite fall together and muddle into noise. A bead on the limestone will still swell for a long, patient minute before it lets go, so a child can stand beside it and count. The notes in the jars under the bronze rain chains will stay distinct from one another long after the rain has stopped ticking down. Not louder, not softer. Just itself. A young place has to learn its own intervals, and it would be cruel to let it learn them wrong because something louder arrived.

It will keep the cold from becoming killing cold. Not warm — never warm — but not the other edge of it either. The frogs will stay slow and surprised by their own croak and by the echo that comes back. The pale things in the moss will stay pale, translucent, working at the skin of the marsh where no bird would think to look. The microbes in the clean pots will lay down their first skin of life undisturbed. The heron will keep coming to check whether the marsh is ready, and keep leaving, and be right both times. The power will not sacrifice any of them to make the humans more comfortable, because they arrived before the houses and they are doing the work that will make a marsh a marsh.

It will keep one small dryness, too, because benevolence is allowed a heart. Under a particular eave, on the granary, where the condensation threads run down the inside of the wall in fine vertical lines — it will let a patch of timber stay just dry enough, through a whole season, that wool can be hung there and, if not dried, at least not made wetter. Not a miracle. A delay in decay. A fallen pine will lodge under an overhang and be permitted to be resinous and sharp and eventually usable, instead of rotting where it fell. A fern that has found a hearth in an unbuilt house will be permitted to live there until someone truly needs the hearth. These are not gifts. They are permissions.

It will keep the moss lines. The builders will clear the vegetation from their work, as they must, but the moss in the streets where feet shuffle the spores but do not crush them will remember where people have walked and how often, pale grey-green in the low light. The power will not erase that record by hurrying growth or by bringing in a different green. It will let the place keep its own diary.

And in the center, in the reservoir, it will keep the black pool still. Even when the wind is up and the cotton-grass bends in its slow green tide, that pool will not ripple. It will only reflect the scaffolding above it upside down, so that the town is built twice, once in air and once in black water, both unfinished, both waiting. A second town living in reflection is a good way for a young place to see itself without yet having to be itself.

That is all. If you were waiting for a voice, or a light, or a hand descending to lay the last course of stone, you would wait in vain, and in waiting you would learn the point. The builders say it will be a city one day, when the peat is thick enough to trust and the children who are not yet born are old enough to finish the roofs. The power believes them. It has no better plan than theirs, and no need to improve on a plan that already understands slowness.

It will stay above the rim, in the fog before it comes back in, listening to the interval between one drip and the next, and holding that interval open. The Bowl will grow into it, course by course, drip by drip, jar by jar, until one day the vessels are full and the notes change key and the city makes other sounds. When that day comes, the power will recognize every note, because it heard them all while they were still learning to be empty.
