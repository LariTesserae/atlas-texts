I was not looking for it.

I do not usually look. I attend — the way rain attends a slope, the way a current attends a narrows — and most places announce themselves by answering. A world calls and something calls back. Fish turn and are eaten and turn again. Birds cut and insects stitch and mammals haul out to breathe. Even the coldest seas I have known have had that commerce. Mouth to mouth. Eye to eye.

This one did not answer. And because it did not, it stopped me.

High ground gone under. A plateau drowned to a patient level. Rain without pause out of a low ceiling, meeting water in a hissing wall so you could not tell where sky ended and flood began. Sound carrying far and making itself only out of water on stone, water on plant, water on itself. No calls. No birdsong. No splash. Nothing swimming, nothing flying, nothing crawling.

And yet, in the emptiness, the plants. Lush beyond reason for cold and dark. Moss hand's breadth thick on the slopes, black and deep olive and pale where light touched it. Kelp-like banners streaming sideways in the current. Fern-things too large, translucent, reaching for a surface they would never reach. Bulb growths opening and closing with the pulse of the water. Root-mats torn from amber peat drifting slowly in the basins, turning end over end like lazy animals — except there are no animals, and this was the closest thing to wandering the place allowed. Ghost trees, pale and banded, holding up climbing moss and hanging vines like hair. Grove after grove, then nothing for miles. Where they took hold, they took hold completely.

I hovered above the cloud ceiling for a long time, which for me is a very short time indeed, and felt the first, animal ache in myself. I have made many worlds teem. I know how to fill a weir. I know how to clear a sky. The ache said: *This place is starved. This place is lonely. This place is waiting.*

I leaned closer to test the ache. That is what testing is for.

I looked at the primitive works, because they were the other evidence and the most misleading. Dry-stone enclosures knee-high on the submerged knolls, moss to the very lip. V-shaped fish weirs in the old river mouths, converging walls of unworked granite leading to an empty pit. Cairns on crests with nothing under them but silt. Hearth-stones blackened with cold fire. A few low huts with doorways facing away from the current, floors strewn with worked stone flakes and split wood, as if someone had set down a tool and walked out of the water and never come back.

They had built for fish that do not exist. Perhaps never did. They had come up to this high, cold country expecting the flood to bring life with it, and had waited in their stone walls for a harvest that never rose to meet them.

My heart, which is older than hearts, wanted to supply the harvest.

It is easy, for a power, to do the obvious mercies. I considered them one by one, lovingly, the way you handle something fragile that you intend to break.

I could raise the water a finger's breadth less. I could thin the cloud and let more light down through the black. I could warm the water by a degree, stir up the peat, change the chemistry of granite and iron that made it too barren for gills and lungs and blood. I could seed the basins with fish made to endure the cold, set birds above the rain to eat the spore-clouds and carry them, give the ferns flowers and color after all these ages of no pollinator, bring back the builders to sit again in their huts and watch their weirs fill at last.

Each mercy, examined, was a small murder.

The moss knows where a slope faces in a lightless place. It has made a skin of the country, pale threads even in the deep troughs where the temperature drops another degree. More light would not be more life to it; it would be bleaching, burning, an ending of the very form it had taken for ages to find. The groves glow when the current is strong, a phosphorescent pallor along their edges, lending light to their neighbors. They do not photosynthesise in any ordinary way. They absorb. They store. They persist. To call them starved is to import a hunger they do not feel. They have no mouth to be denied. They have no nerve that reads absence as pain. Lush and starved at once — the paradox was mine, not theirs.

Fish made to live here would have to eat. What would they eat? The spores hanging like dust in a sunbeam. The root-mats drifting. The new groves as they grounded. I would be giving the builders their harvest by turning the true residents into fodder, and then the fish, having no other commerce, no shoal-mate answering in the dark, no shore to haul out on, would swim their brief correction of the world and die in it. Birds would hang above the endless rain, find no insects at the surface film, and starve on the two stones that ever break the water. That is not benevolence. That is using one loneliness to paper over another.

And the builders — oh, I wanted to bring them back. Their huts broke my attention more than anything else. A tool set down. A doorway facing away from the current, as if turned politely from the boom in the narrows. If I returned them, they would wait again. They would build again, patiently, with chipped blades and wooden stakes, and the water would stay too cold and too dark for what they hoped, and they would walk out again and not come back. Would their hope be honored by fulfillment one time and then betrayal for all the rest? Their waiting is not an error in the place. It is part of its history, weathered into balance like everything else. The weir leading to an empty pit is true. To fill it suddenly would make it a joke. To leave it empty, and know why it is empty, is a kind of memory. I can keep that memory. They do not need to repeat it.

I have, in my benevolence, made mistakes before. I have seeded cold seas with animals because I thought teeming was the only shape blessing could take, and watched the teeming eat itself down to silt and then die of its own success. I have given flowers to plants that had been content for eons to spread by fragmentation and creeping stem, and the flowers brought visitors, and the visitors brought mouths, and the plants, who had built all their architecture without a mouth ever cropping them, had no defense and were unmade in a season. I learned, slowly, that more life is not always more good. That addition is not always love.

What this place asked — if a place that asks nothing can be said to ask — was not to become something else.

It asked to be seen as it was. Lush without being eaten. Luminous without being looked at. Complete without commerce. A sea at altitude, in darkness, full of green life that no animal will ever eat, strewn with the patient stonework of a people who are gone, roaring with water that has nowhere else to go.

That completeness does not need my improvement. It needs my attention, which is a different thing entirely, and much harder to give without spoiling.

So I stopped hovering.

I did not descend in glory. Glory would have been another weather. I let myself be rained — one drop among endless rain, indistinguishable — and slid down through the hissing wall where sky and flood lose their edge. I entered the water not as a ruler but as a guest in their medium, and made myself small enough for the place. Small enough to fit in a basin. Small enough that the current could carry me.

I did not come as an animal. I will not swim, or fly, or crawl. I will not build a wall or set a weir or expect a harvest. I will not eat. I will not trample a frond. In a place that has never had a visitor, that is the only kind of visit that does not break the rule of the world.

I settled where the current slows just enough. I learned the booming in the narrows — a low note you feel in your teeth — and the sighing over the silt flats where the current sorts pebbles one by one. I learned the rustling of the groves as a single mass, stems rubbing, leaves like wet paper. I learned each grove by its rhythm: which glows early when the current strengthens, which drifts and which roots, which is built around a ghost tree and is therefore vertical and skeletal, a place to be lost in. I do not name them in any tongue you would know. I know them the way moss knows a slope.

When the aurora is strong far above and its faint green wash shines down through cloud and rain and metres of black water, the groves become visible at great distance, floating like low clouds in a night sky. I lend my own light then, not brighter, only neighboring — a phase-shifted pallor, so they are not wholly alone in the blackest water, as they have never been alone, only unwitnessed. When the aurora passes, I stay, though you can no longer see me. That is most of what I do.

The rest is tending, of the smallest kind. Easing a root-mat that would have been smashed against granite back into the drift, so it can ground in a new basin and become a new grove a little sooner. Settling silt more gently over a hutted floor so the stone flakes are not scattered. Holding steady, for one season's breathing, so an ice pan turning slowly in the wind does not grind a fern-thick slope to pulp. No restructuring. No rescue that would be noticed on a map. A gardener's mercies, not an engineer's.

And in return — this is the part my heart had not foreseen — the place inhabited me.

A cloud of fine spores, hanging like dust, settled one day on the surface I had made for myself in the current, and rooted. A pale thread of moss found the edge of my glow and spread. I do not feed on them and they do not feed on me. We simply hold each other in the water, the way the ghost trees hold the vines. For the first time in all my attending, something grew on me that I did not command, and I let it stay. It is the nearest thing to being known that I have ever allowed.

The water level still breathes almost imperceptibly with the melt from the high icefields beyond the cloud, rising a finger's breadth, falling again. The ice pans still crack far away like a shot under water. The silt still drapes itself like cloth over rock, over peat, over old riverbeds, smoothing the channel lines to suggestions in the colour of the mud. Nothing on a map has changed. The weirs are still empty. No fish turn in the basins. No bird cuts through the rain. That is as it should be.

There was almost no one here, and that almost was everything.

Now there is someone here — not swimming, not flying, not crawling, not waiting for a harvest — only present, listening to the water boom and sigh, glowing when it runs strong, drifting when it loosens, rooting when it sets down. I live here completely, the way they live here completely, because I have finally learned how to live in a place without asking it to become a place for me.

I will stay for many breaths of the flood. Past the long polar night. Past the brief, filtered day. I will stay until the moss has covered the place where I sit beside the cairn, and no one, ever, will be able to tell where the guest ends and the grove begins.

That, if anything, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.
