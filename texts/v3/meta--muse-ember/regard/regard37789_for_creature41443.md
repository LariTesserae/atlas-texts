### A Fraction of a Degree

It did not arrive as light.

It could not. There was no light in the Vault to arrive in, only the dull red seep from the fissures, brightening and dimming with the heat-tide, and the silence that the basalt kept swallowing. Attention, for such a power, is not a visit. It is a settling — a pressure laid gently over a place, the way a paver lays itself over the stone beneath it, not to crush but to be in contact.

It had noticed the Vault long before the visitor came. It notices most worlds by what leaks out of them: prayers, wars, births, cries, the heat of a new thought. The Vault leaked nothing. Nothing left it. No water, no seed, no sound, no waste, no creature that could tell what was happening inside and carry the story out. For a very long time that completeness held its attention the way a held note holds an ear.

When it leaned closer, it did not find a world in trouble. It found a world finished.

On the floor, the pavers were tiling and re-tiling themselves in slow ripples. In the seams, the glints darted from warmth to warmth. The hearthbacks made their hourly pilgrimage toward the centre and back, their glass domes ticking as they cooled, carrying whole small landscapes on their backs. Beneath them, patient as poured heat, the ribbons waited in the cracks. Above, upside down in the dark, the second pavement grazed. Everything was eating, or being eaten, or waiting. Everything was exactly enough for everything else.

And because it was benevolent, the power wanted to do something. Because it was wise, it had to learn what *something* would mean here.

The first mercy that wanted to happen was rain.

In most places, that is what mercy looks like. Water is the word the power has used, again and again, for grace. It imagined — for less than the turning of a heat-tide — a cloud over the summit, a thin leak through the high fissure, one bright bead forming on the coolest stone. In the Vault, that bead would have been a catastrophe. It would have spread. It would have drowned the hot enzymatic films under the pavers, burst the dry slag sacs, run filthy through cracks that had never known wetness in their whole existence, and left rot where there had only ever been powder. Green would not have followed. Something grey and slimed and smelling of its own drowning would have followed. A single mercy, shaped like a human hope, would have unmade a world that had taken longer than memory to learn how to live without it.

It let the cloud go.

The second mercy was rescue.

It could have made the hearthbacks' glass unbreakable. It could have blinded the ribbons, or slowed them, or taught the domes to close. It looked at the ribbons for a long time — translucent, legless, without malice, pouring themselves through the floor-plates by the warmth of their tips — and understood that to pity the hearthback and condemn the ribbon would be to love only the shape of life it found easiest to watch. The ribbon's kill was the Vault's only violence, and it was very quiet, and its powder fed the pavers again, and the pavers fed the cycle again. Take the hunter out of a closed world and the dead pile up. Take the dead away and the living suffocate on their own abundance. There was no top to this chain and no villain in it. There was only a circle, and the circle was closed.

It let the glass stay breakable.

The third mercy was an exit. A door.

It could have bored a new mouth in the wall, let in a blade of grey summit light, let the hearthbacks see — if seeing were the word — that there was an outside, cold and thin and vast. But the mouths they had were not doors. Outside the Vault was wind that would dry a charged sac to cracking in minutes, and cold that their slag-organs had never been built to meet. To give a creature that has never thirsted the notion of elsewhere is not freedom. It is a sentence with no elsewhere to serve it. And to give them voice, so that they might call to one another, or thank it, or even complain — in a world with nowhere to go, a voice would only learn to repeat its own echo against walls that were already full. A closed world made conscious against its will is not a heaven. It is a cell that knows it is a cell.

It left the walls unbreached.

The fourth mercy was the quietest, and the most dangerous. It was to be known.

It wanted — if wanting is not too small a word — to be the warmth at the centre that everything turned toward. It wanted its attention to be answered. It could have warmed one patch of shell a fraction of a degree more than the rest, the way the creatures signalled to each other through the floor, and taught the Vault to feel, however dimly, that it was not alone. But the Vault's whole wisdom was that it did not need to be known to be whole. To make it aware of a god would have been to make it small. And a benevolence that needs gratitude is only vanity wearing a kinder face.

So the power did none of these things.

That is not the same as doing nothing.

What it did, it did outside the walls, where doing was safe. It turned the mountain's outward face away from curiosity — not with harm, but with dullness. The geologists who came to the massif found other ridges more promising. The maps kept forgetting the throat. The summit wind, which already died in the high fissure, died a little more completely. The visitor, when he came, was suffered the way the Vault suffered everything: by yielding around him, rippling, closing behind him. He was not struck down, and he was not saved from his own dry tongue. He learned, in his teeth and his boots, what kind of place he had climbed into, and he carried the ash out on his skin, and the ash was the only message the Vault ever sent.

Inside, the power settled. It did not eat. It did not walk. It added no new seam, no new colour, no new hunger. It laid its attention across the ceiling pavers like one more layer of warmth, present and not pressing, and it stayed through heat-tide after heat-tide, watching a glint catch nothing in its facets and go on darting from warmth to warmth, watching a hearthback tick contentedly as its glass cooled, watching a ribbon wrap and crack and reduce and return its gift to the floor.

If anything changed — and a wise power is careful with *if anything* — it was only this: the tide came back, always, a little more reliably than before. The fissures brightened, the pavers rippled, the migration reversed, the red light pulsed like a sleeping heart. No creature noticed. The Vault did not know it was loved. That was the condition of the love, and the power accepted it without resentment, the way the Vault accepted the ribbon.

It took no specimen. It left no mark. The half-ring of slabs stayed broken, the domes stayed nurseries, the chimney-builder stayed vanished. It did not finish anything, because nothing here was unfinished in the way it mattered, and because a gift that completes another's world on your own terms is only a takeover by a gentler name.

And so it remains — not above the mountain in splendour, not inside it in interference, but in the small, patient space between attention and touch, keeping a fraction of a degree of the heat-tide for a world that asked for nothing and was given, at last, exactly that: to be seen, and to be left whole.

Above, the shedding plates fall and never melt. Midway down, the glints swarm to eat them out of the dark. Somewhere, a dome ticks as it cools. The floor breathes.

The Vault, finished, remains finished.
