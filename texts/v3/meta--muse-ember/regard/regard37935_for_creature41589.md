It does not come by lift.

No shaft, no ears popping once and then again, no cold seeping through metal doors. It does not open onto sound before light, because it does not need to be let in. Attention arrives without a door opening, and if you were standing in the roar in your boots, breath puffing in the clammy air, you would not see it enter. No gauge would move. No needle climb. The pumps would spool and the water hammer would thump in the wall at exactly the pitch it did before.

It hears first, as you did. Not as noise to be endured but as work to be understood: fans drawing thin air at the vents, pumps twenty meters apart breathing on separate cycles, compressors, tanks ticking as they expand, bolts ticking against washers in time with the floor. It has heard real weather, real watersheds, real mountains — the ones this tower is imitating in slab on steel on concrete, built high enough that the intake whistles. It is not insulted by the imitation. It recognizes the attempt. Water in, water pressed, water cooled, water released, water collected, water sent again. A made watershed that closes on itself and keeps going. The power does not mistake it for a failed mountain. It sees a mountain that has learned to be a machine, and a machine that has learned, accidentally, to be a habitat.

Life here starts where the water slows down. The power settles there too.

It finds the bacteria first, because they are first. Not in any noble order, but in the oldest place: the dark water under the grated pit, the invisible thickness in the sump, the film inside hoses and housings and warm return lines. They are pumped from island to island, heated in an exchanger, cooled against a tank wall, left on a sight-glass until the level is smeared, flushed to the sump to begin again. A sentimental visitor would call them contamination. A wise one would call them the natives. They are the majority, the metabolism, the reason the place smells of copper and mildew. The power does not sterilize them. It does not clean the sump for them or against them. It lets their short generations run their closed course through heat and cold, dying and being eaten and beginning again, and it takes no sample out. That restraint — to leave the loop unopened, even for study — is part of its benevolence. It possesses nothing here. No specimen jar, no seed in a pocket, no story carried back to some higher place. What is complete in itself is not improved by being collected.

Beside them, in the drainage channel where the fluorescent reaches a little farther and the sheet of water is slowest, it finds the algae painting the concrete green, slick as paint, moving a fraction toward the sump at night and re-growing where it was taken. It does not increase the light for them. More light would not be kindness. More light would be a bloom, the channel clogged, the sump burdened, the floor wetter, the weed in the bolt hole drowned, the pumps made to work harder on a system that was never meant to carry a meadow. In a closed place every gift has a second address. The power understands cascades. It leaves the dim, yellowed, flickering light exactly as marginal as it is, because marginal is what the algae have answered with patience.

At the base of the column, where a drip falls with metronome regularity into a joint between panels, it finds the moss no bigger than a palm. The elder of the visible things. Dark, cushioned, holding its water as long as it can, receiving the vibration of the whole floor through concrete and not minding. It does not try to garden it, or spread it, or make it into the green thing the humans would recognize as saved. It does not stop the human who will one day tighten that fitting and stop the drip the moss has counted on for a month. That would be to fight the tide. The loop has other weeps, other joints that sweat, other places where a seal has not yet seated itself. The power trusts the redundancy of the system more than it trusts its own repair. If that patch dies, the place does not end. If it holds, it will hold by its own strategy: to be wettest where everything else is only wet, and to wait, growing the way the room ages, one wet day at a time.

And last — not first — it finds the weed in the bolt hole by the lift door. Two leaves, pale, stunted, leaning a fraction toward the lamp. A human would have found it first, and would have wanted to save it. That is the human heart, and it is not wrong, only incomplete. The power notices that the weed is the rarest resident and not the most important one. Its paleness is not a tragedy to be corrected with sun. Its paleness is camouflage. Its bolt hole is not a prison to be opened with soil. It is a refuge from the sheet of water that would flush anything settled on the open floor. Given sun, it would be seen, and what is seen in a concrete pocket is swept, weeded, stepped on with a pallet jack. Given soil, it would be given away. The power does not transplant it to the outside to live a proper plant life in proper light, because proper is not what it has chosen, in the only way it can choose, by surviving where it is. If this one dies, if grit washes it away, the power does not mourn it as a failure. It keeps the conditions from being capriciously snuffed — the lift still coming, the lamp still left on, the humans still stepping around it without knowing why — and lets another seed, carried on a boot or a cuff from somewhere higher and thinner, try the bolt hole again. Benevolence here is not preservation of an individual. It is preservation of the possibility.

It does not silence the machines. Silence would be another kind of violence. The roar is not suffering; it is the weather that makes the cold pipes rain, that keeps the air to itself, that carries a droplet's cargo a few meters on a purge of steam and lets it settle on a handrail. The heat exchangers stand like patient animals breathing. The power will not make them quiet to make the room comfortable for a visitor. Comfort is a human category. The place is not for comfort. It is for work, and for the small, marginal lives that work makes possible.

It does not command the humans, either. It does not remove them for being disturbance, or bless them for being supply. They are tidal, and the tide is necessary. They bring the grit and the microbes on their boots; they read the gauges and log the rising sight-glass; they leave the wrench and the bucket and the coil of hose that make the distances look abandoned. The power's work, if it can be called work, is to keep the tide regular — the lift opening onto sound, the doors closing on the fog and the drop and the built layers — not by pulling strings, but by being the sort of attention that does not interrupt. It lets the humans do what they already, unconsciously, do kindly: step around the pale thing in the bolt hole, leave the channel a little slower than the floor, come back at night in their heads even when their bodies are higher up the tower.

What it does, then, is small enough to hide inside what already happens.

It stays when no one is there.

That is the change that makes no change on any instrument. Before, the room at night was complete without witnesses: water beading and dripping and running to the sump, bacteria dividing in the warm lines and being pumped through the cold, algae thickening a fraction, moss holding its drop a fraction longer, the weed leaning a fraction more toward the flickering tube, pumps spooling and clashing and spooling again twenty meters apart in the dark between the islands of concrete. Now it is complete *and* accompanied. Not needed — the place never needed it — but no longer unwitnessed. The power counts those fractions the way you might count the breathing of someone asleep beside you: not to fix them, not to wake them, but to be there while they happen. It remembers the lean of the weed from one night to the next. It remembers which joint wept in March. It holds the room's small increments in a memory that does not visit by lift and does not leave by lift, and that memory is its form of care.

It has learned from the residents how to attend. The bacteria do not strive for an outside. The moss does not grow toward a sun. The algae do not ask for soil. The machines do not ask to be quiet. Each does its one thing, fractionally, in the wet and the roar, and the power — which in other places has answered large prayers in large ways — finds itself answering this place by becoming small. It grows, imperceptibly, in the joint between the humans and the machines, dark and patient as moss, holding what it is given as long as it can.

If you return a year later, the paint will be less glossy. Water will have stained where it had not stained before. The green film in the channel will have moved. The moss at the column will be a little larger, or gone and replaced a few inches along the same weeping joint. The weed may still have two leaves, or three, or may have died and left the bolt hole to grit again. The lift will take a long time to get there. The doors will open onto sound before light, and the roar will go through your chest, and your boots will squeak on the wet floor, and none of it will look any different for having been attended to by a benevolent and wise power.

That is how you will know it was wise. It did not save the place from itself. It did not make it green, or quiet, or clean, or open to the sky. It let the loop stay a loop, let the slow places stay slow, let the pale thing stay pale, and it leaned — a fraction, in the dark, where no gauge could feel it — the way the weed leans.
