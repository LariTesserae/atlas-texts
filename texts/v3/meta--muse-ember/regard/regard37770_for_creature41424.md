It did not come down the stair-hole.

Coming down would have been too loud.

It heard them first, as you did — not as a crowd, but as layers. Bats in the chimney inlets. Swallows in their built cups. Cats in the middle air. Mice in the pores of the bricks. Then the people below all of that, elbows in, sideways, bucket held close. It has been listening for a very long time. Longer than the bell has been rung. Longer than the vine has been learning the strings. Long enough to know which loom is out of time by a breath, and which drip bowl will crack next winter, and which child can feel the hollow in the step in the dark without looking.

That is what attention means, for a thing like this. Not a glance. A habitation.

And because it is benevolent, it wanted at once to do something. And because it is wise, it considered, one by one, all the things it could do, and set them down again.

It could open the roof. It could lift the clay tiles and the lime-plastered thatch and give them the sky entire, uncut by shafts, undivided into squares. That would be a gift, from above. It thought of the top terraces drying in real wind. It thought of the children who have never touched unworked earth finally standing in a field. And then it listened again to the dust on those top terraces, to the flour and the soot, to how carefully the women clean the pigeons' ledges after a fashion, and it understood: you do not give a horizon to a people whose whole art is learning to see in rectangles of light. Their sky is small because their attention is not. To widen it would not free them. It would dry them out and make them strangers in their own bowl.

It could give them water. A spring under the northern wall. A river in the cistern, always full. It could end the rotation at the condensation bowls, the row of jars with cloths over their mouths, the dozen basins placed strategically under every drip-line, the frogs in the one puddle they will not pave over. It is a dry place that has learned to drink in small sips. That learning is their dignity. A wise power does not take away a people's practiced way of receiving what little comes, and call it mercy.

It could give them rest. One new iron nail a day from the forge is enough, because there is nowhere far to go. But still, the work never stops. Someone is always replastering, always re-tying, always sweeping ash to be made into bricks. A benevolent thing might want to ease their hands, make a wheel turn longer, make a roof build itself. And then it would have ended the very thing that holds them together. Repair is not an event here; it is a rhythm. To an outsider it looks like labor. From inside, it is how they say: we are still here, we are still this place. Take the work, and you take the saying.

It could give them a word for it. A name. A god above the tiles who would answer the bell with a voice. It could step into the light shaft at noon and be seen, and be worshipped. That is the oldest temptation for any power that notices a complete small world. But these lives are arranged sideways, not upward. They negotiate past each other. They shout greetings up two terraces, they sing to keep time with the shuttle, they tend each other's bowls without thinking about it. To make them look up would be to break the plane on which their whole kindness moves. A wise power does not become vertical in a horizontal world.

It could free the animals. Untie the goat. Let the pig out of the court. Release the tame owl from the forge rafters. It thought about that with real tenderness. Then it watched the owl take a moth in the dark and settle again, fed, in return for his presence among the bellows. It watched the frogs, who live only in clay and in the protected puddle. It watched the moss, which grows only on made surfaces, bright lines along the mortar when it rains. None of them are wild, and none of them are quite tame. They are furnished, like the vine. To liberate them into a wild that no longer exists for them would be a second cruelty, dressed as kindness.

So it did not open, or give, or ease, or name, or free.

What it did instead was smaller, and therefore harder.

It kept the veil.

From above, there is almost nothing to find. That is not an accident of tile and thatch. It is a kept thing. Armies pass over the field and do not dig. Kings do not tax what they cannot see. No priest converts them, no explorer discovers them and writes them down and ruins them with being known. History, which has unmade so many small complete places by noticing them too late and too greedily, simply forgets this one road, this one hollow. That forgetting is not neglect. It is the active, daily work of the Attention, as patient as their own replastering. If you dropped a stone here, in a week it would be ground into mortar. If a larger world dropped its weight here, it would be ground the same way. The power chose that the weight would not fall.

It kept the tuning.

Not a miracle. A tuning. The heat of the tiles above radiating down into the top rooms a little longer after the hearths die. The light in the shaft staying a fraction longer on a weaver's hands at four in the afternoon, when sun and goat and chickens and wet clay and boiled grain and thyme all coincide in the central court, and for one breath the farthest loom and the nearest drip are heard at the same time. It does not make the rain come. It makes sure the rain, when it performs in the shafts for twenty loud minutes and then gives itself back in drips for hours, is heard as music and not as flood. It makes sure a crack that opens to raw earth is noticed the same day — though they already notice it. It mostly just holds what is already held, so that holding does not have to be done alone.

And it listened. Every day. At four, especially. Not to judge. Not to answer. To witness simultaneity without breaking it. A place that remakes itself a little every day so that it can go on being exactly what it is does not need a savior. It needs a neighbor who will not turn what it sees into something else.

When a person dies there, the Attention does not lift them up out of the made world and give them a truer one. It lets the Undermade do what it has always done: the body becomes bone for handles, ash for bricks, dust for the top terraces where the children will one day learn the soft edge of a chipped brick by touch. It lets them stay in the place they helped make smooth. That, for them, is not an ending. It is a final repair.

And if one day — not soon, not in any hurry the Attention has — the last loom stops, and no hand replasters the south wall, and the stair-hole door hangs still on its bone hinges, then the veil will lift by itself. The earth that was dug away so long ago will come back, patiently, into the bowl. The moss will go on a little longer on the mortar, for old habit. And the Attention, which could not save a complete thing by changing it, will do the last thing a wise and benevolent power can do for a world it has loved by leaving alone:

it will remember it. The sound of it. The layers. The thump and snap of the shuttles against the hammer against the child against the chickens. The high perpetual counterpoint of tap, tap, somewhere behind the pots. It will keep that sound, whole and unmade by no hand, in a place where nothing else is kept unmade.

Until then, it sits where the bats hang by day, in the dark inlet of the chimney, folded into the plaster, listening down through the tiles. It does not block the light. It does not need to be seen.

From above, there is almost nothing to find.

That is its gift, and its mercy, and its form of staying: to be the one who knows, and chooses not to tell anyone else, so that the Undermade can go on being exactly what it is, held smooth by being held — by them, every day, and by this quiet attention around them, for as long as they choose.
