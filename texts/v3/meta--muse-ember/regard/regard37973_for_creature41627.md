**The Attention**

It was not a cry that drew it.

Most attention is called down by suffering — by a world asking to be otherwise, by a people asking to be saved from where they are. The Sinter Crown asked for nothing. That was rarer, and harder to leave alone.

It came and circled, as the thermals do, in the blue that goes black at the zenith. It did not land. It watched for a full heat cycle and the long cooling after it, because it had learned, as wise things must, to keep time in the local measure before it judged: a season for a furnace to tick and settle, a decade for a terrace to gain a course of brick, a centimeter in a generation for a wall to sink.

In that time it learned where life kept. Under the white tanks. In the shadow of a north-facing duct. In the warm film inside a pipe, in the gap between double glass, on a duct where the crawlers scraped blindly at the sinter, in the updraft where a dark raptor ascended for an hour without a wingbeat and never once put its feet on the Crown. And pressed arm's-reach from wall to wall, the people in their reflective ceramic, moving slowly, speaking rarely, spending water as they spent breath, listening for a drip to count itself into a jar.

It could have done a great deal. That was the temptation, and the warning.

It could have given them water. Rain would have been easy. But rain here arrives warm and runs in sheets over the glass floor and vanishes into cracks before it can gather. The mats are not decoration — they are a map. A copper weft only lives where a tank sighs, where a pipe sweats, where dew has stayed. To flood the pockets would be to erase the reading. To make water abundant would be to make the jars, the condensers, the dawn listening, into a quaint habit instead of a way of being careful. It decided a careful people did not need to be relieved of their care.

It could have given them green. A soil, a weed in a crack, a fringe along a damp seam. But there is no soil because the stone has not had time to become a landscape. Everything here is still accreting, mineralising, being made. To root a plant would be to split fresh, unweathered rock and call it a kindness. The palette refuses green not from lack but from youth. The power was old enough to remember that not every bare place is a wound.

It could have given them voice. Sound falls into hush at this height. A tap on a pipe is meant for one ear. Much is said by not being in the way. To carry sound farther would have been to break the very conversation they had learned — fan turning over once, metal contracting, steam deciding to leave, a crawler's leg, a lizard's tongue. It did not mistake quiet for emptiness.

It could have given them speed, and ease, and rescue. It could have thinned the heat or thickened the air so lungs did not work so hard. It could have brought them down from the terraces to the cloud-sea and the lowlands below. But they chose the newest stone, the top, the present tense. The oldest levels below have already cooled into cliff, and that is their history in section. To finish the mountain for them — to erode it into a proper landscape, to lay a street and let it stay a street — would be to end the becoming that is the place.

A lesser benevolence would have called any of those gifts. A wise one recognized them as a different place, and therefore different people, wearing their own name.

So it did none of those things.

What it did instead was smaller than doing, and therefore harder.

It learned to keep the interval. At dawn, when every roof and pane is beaded from the sharp night cold and then warms past bearing, the kiln-lizard emerges between the panes and drinks bead by bead while the people below fill their jars from their wing-like condensers. They do not interfere with each other. The power stayed with that interval a breath longer than the glass should have allowed — not creating dew, not making more of it, only not hurrying its leaving. One more bead. The lizard's shadow moved through its skin. A person counted a drip into a jar. No one looked up. That was correct.

In the damp pockets, it remembered a mat that was drying. It did not water it. It let a tank sigh once more in its season, and the weft spread a hand's breadth over black glass, fixing rust and copper as it went, as it was meant to do. A sign was kept.

And on the newest living bench, on a white tank where the inhabitants mark the sinking of walls with a line, a new tick appeared one morning — no deeper than a nail, no different in size from the others. It was not a message. It was not a signature. It will be read, when it is noticed, as settlement. The next generation will add theirs beside it. That was exactly the point: to speak in their timescale, and to be mistaken for continuity rather than for arrival.

After that, it took the distance of the thermals. It will not land on the Crown, not on the glass, not on the terraces, not on the cold top of a tank. It belongs to the air between crowns. It will come back in a season, and in a decade, to see the new course of brick, to watch a gantry move while someone has coffee, to hear whether the crawler on that terrace is still wedged in its duct-shadow ticking as it cools.

If you stay long enough on Vhar's Crown, breathing slowly, you may feel it — not as a presence pressing close, but as the hush deepening by one listener. Someone else is counting the drips, and does not need an answer to a tap on a pipe. Someone else is stepping around the crawler, too.

By evening the glass floor gives its heat back slowly, condensation threads down the black rock as if the mountain were sweating in its sleep, and below, the cloud-sea turns white-gold. The terraces light one by one. Nothing announces itself, as usual. Except that one bead, that morning, was allowed to stay, and a translucent animal in a wall drank it, and a pale, slow, quiet people went on with their day, rightly, without looking up to thank anyone.
