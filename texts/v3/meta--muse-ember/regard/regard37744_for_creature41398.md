## What the Attention Did

The attention did not arrive.

There was no descent, no brightening, no wind to announce it, no shadow thrown the wrong way. If it had stepped down onto the Shelf, its footprint would have remained there, clean and sharp, forever — and some later walker, certain of walking straight, would have followed it in a circle. So it did not step.

It came the way the cold comes up through the soles of your boots: slowly, without weather, until you understood that something was present and had been present for a long time. It looked, and it kept on looking, over the pavement and the sparse green and the marks of hands, and over the breathing seam in the slab the size of a table.

A benevolent and wise power, in most places, would have begun at once to fix things. It would have seen the salt-white crusts that look like pools and called them thirst. It would have seen the dry riverbeds and called them drought. It would have lifted a hand for rain.

This power lifted its hand, and then, after a long consideration, set it down again.

Because the place was not broken.

Rain, first. It was the obvious mercy, the one every kind power thinks of when it sees a world with no water in it. But the huskbacks are not thirsty. They are not wet inside. They are fatty, salty, and crystalline, built dry the way a stone is built dry, and they take their light through the translucent rims of their shells without ever needing to drink. To rain on them would not refresh them. It would find the seams between their plates for the first time in eons. It would dissolve the salt crusts. It would bring the one thing this world has never had and does not need: rot. Their deaths are hardenings, their bodies joining the pavement while still occupied, and a first decay would not be a season to them. It would be an unmaking into something they never asked to become. The power looked at the dry grooves worn to final smoothness and understood that they were not waiting to be filled.

Then night. The power considered giving the Shelf a turning sun, a proper dark to rest under, a rhythm by which the stone-flowers might count their centuries. But the light here is honest and white-gold and does not move, and the hangers hang in it without beating their wings, and the huskbacks take what they need from it through their rims at all hours. Their shadows point one way because one way is enough. A night would be a spectacle for the visitors with eyes, not a mercy for the inhabitants without them. It would make their footprints blow away and their dust settle sideways, and it would make the enormous, tired swells mean before and after. They have no before and after left in them. They are finished.

It considered the green. One tuft of needle-grass to a hundred paces. A stone-flower that opened some immense time ago and has never closed, never seeded, never wilted — because there is no water in it to lose. The power could have scattered seed, could have made the cracks flower. But the grass is not starving, and the flower is not waiting. They root in dust, drink nothing, and are ignored by the huskbacks, who prefer stone. They add a millimetre of dry growth in a century. That is not failure. That is their whole, sufficient life. To multiply them would be decoration at the expense of the pavement they were meant to punctuate.

It considered making the huskbacks individuals. Giving them eyes. Stopping the budding of exact copies — the same posture, the same chips, the same crack on the left plate of the largest of seven around the salt patch, repeated a hundred metres on, repeated on the far rise, repeated far off as a pattern inside a larger pattern of the same seven. It is easy for a power that loves variety to mistake this for a trap. But the huskbacks are blind and slow and silent, and they do not endure repetition as boredom. Repetition is how they continue. It is the terrain. To give them sight would be to give them the sight of their own copying, and to demand they suffer it the way we would. To heal the crack would be to erase the signature by which the copies are copies. To stop them being stepped over would be to pretend a back that is pavement can be insulted by a foot. They are stepped over the way stone is stepped over, and they go on breathing through the plates regardless.

It considered the marks of hands. The cairns of three flat stones on rise after rise. The obsidian tools, edges still sharp. The low circular shelter with the early metal awl and the bundle of mineral fibre on the table, now occupied by huskbacks arranged inside in the same quiet postures as everywhere else. A sentimental power might have restored the shelter, or brought the people back with water to carry and wood to burn. But the people understood, quickly enough, what the power understood now: one could leave a mark. One could not stay. Their cairns repeat the way the huskbacks repeat. They were not a failed colony. They were a correct visit. The power let the shell rest against the obsidian tool as if it were a companion, because it already was.

And it considered, last, the temptation to make one new thing. One huskback with the crack on the right. One mote-drifter that actually drifted. One needle-grass that bloomed straw-bright in the sun. Novelty, as a gift. But in a world that has run out of new shapes and begun copying the old ones endlessly, at every scale, one difference would not be a beginning. It would be a focal point. Every future walker would come to stare at it. The pavement would have a wound in its pattern. The power decided that a single new shape was not a mercy either. It was vandalism with good intentions.

So it did almost nothing. And the almost is the whole of its kindness.

It did not take a step, so it left no footprint to be followed.

When a walker comes — as walkers do, with boots and thirst and a certainty about straight lines — the power turns them a fraction of an inch before their own prints become a loop. Not enough to be called guidance, not enough to be a path. A huskback's shuffle, applied to a human. It does not erase the prints; nothing here is erased. It simply ensures that the prints lead, in the end, to the edge where the slopes drop away toward the lower, darker country, where there is water and the walker can go and drink and be a person again in a place that keeps people. The walker will remember leaving. That is enough.

It holds the stone-flower open. It does not water it, does not seed it, does not count its centuries for it. It only keeps it as it is, filaments glassy and unclosed, older than any cairn. Witnessing, in a place where nothing decays, is a form of keeping.

It keeps the count of the seven. It keeps the tick of the cooling shell, far off, and does not add a second sound to answer it. It keeps the mote-drifters at the height they were born, and does not ask them to drift. It keeps the hangers hanging. It keeps the dust settling straight down.

And it rests its attention, gently, on the largest huskback with the cracked left plate — not to heal it, not to name it, only to honour the crack as part of the pattern, the way the seam along its back is part of the breathing. The slab rises and falls a fraction at a time. The power, which has seen worlds that begged for change and worlds that screamed for it, breathed with it in that fraction, and the Shelf, breathing through plates of mineral, breathed on.

It stayed.

That is what a wise and benevolent power did, when it finally understood what it was looking at. It did not bring rain to a world built dry. It did not bring seasons to a world that had finished time. It did not bring eyes, or novelty, or rot, or green in abundance. It did not rescue the pavement from being pavement.

It stayed the way the mote-drifters stay, and the stone-flowers stay, and the huskbacks stay: at the height where it was born, holding its breath, keeping a finished thing finished.

At what would be night, if the light ever moved — and it does not — the sky over the Shelf is a deep, clean blue, and the lives stretch away in every direction, motionless and layered and overwhelming. Nothing here began to become.

And that, at last, was not abandonment.

It was being kept.
