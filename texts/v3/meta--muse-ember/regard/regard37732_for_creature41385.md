It notices the town the way you notice a held note in a long silence — not because it is loud, but because it does not waver.

It was not looking for it. A benevolent and wise power does not go searching for places to save. It has learned, over longer ages than the ice can remember, that searching is already the first kind of hunger, and that most places it has "helped" have not thanked it for the help. It merely passes — if *passes* is the word for something that holds distances the way a sky holds weather — and in passing, it feels the shape of a completeness that makes no signal at all.

That is how it finds the hummock. From far away, where it can see whole plains at once, noise is common and balance is rare. A closed breathing stands out: a town that exhales and inhales the same water, that grows and is pruned at the same rate, that blooms and ripens on the same shrub at the same time. It leans closer, as close as a thing of that scale can lean, and looks in through the glowing roof.

It sees the snail grazing the green wall, leaving its pale track to be closed over. It sees the fly hanging over the saxifrage, too cold to hum. It sees the hands come before the faces do, quick and precise against the blue light, and another hand answer a pace away. It sees the frogs sitting half-submerged and not croaking, the stickleback turning among the birch roots without a ripple, the drip gathering on the cold upper ice and falling once in a long, patient time into a channel that was cut for it a hundred years ago and is still exactly right.

It is not a place waiting. That is the first thing wisdom understands.

And it is the first thing benevolence must contend with, because benevolence, untrained, would want to do everything.

It could warm the air. It has warmth to spare, more than enough to turn spring-earth cold into summer, to make the frost lace in the north corners melt and run and be gone. It could give them seasons, so that youth and age would separate cleanly, so that a child could grow tall and leave and miss the pool. It could give them rain, a real river that flows, instead of condensation that gathers and falls a little and is drunk by roots and rises again. It could give them voice — lift the cold from the flies, teach the frogs to croak, teach the birds to call, teach the people to speak aloud and be heard across the square. It could give them fire and metal and the wheel, and other lives to trade with, and stories of stars to look up at through a roof that no longer glows.

Any one of those gifts would be easy. All of them together would be a rescue. All of them together would be an ending.

For it has no hands small enough to part the moss curtain. To enter, it would have to break the roof. To speak, it would have to make a sound louder than the drip, louder than anything that has ever lived there. To offer a choice, it would have to introduce the idea of choosing, and of leaving, and of elsewhere — and elsewhere, once imagined inside a place that has never needed it, does not leave again. Even a fly sent in as an emissary would be a new species. Even a dream slipped into one sleeper would be new weather.

Wisdom is the knowledge that scale is not a detail. A power large enough to notice a town hidden under a bump in the ground is too large to fit inside it without crushing the terraces, shoulder to shoulder, that took centuries to slump into place. Benevolence is the knowledge that not fitting is not a failure.

So it does not enter.

What it does, it does entirely outside.

It keeps the plain cold and bright and unremarkable. Not by a great act — it is done with great acts — but by the steady tending of distant things: the wind kept away, the light kept pale and hard, the ice crown kept from thinning, the maps kept blank in that one small place, not by erasure but by the simple fact that there is nothing there to reward a mapmaker's eye. Explorers will step over the hummock and look at the horizon and feel, without knowing why, that there is nowhere to stop. Satellites will see only wet stone and saxifrage. The town will remain — as it has always remained — findable only by stooping.

It does not record the coordinates, either. Or rather, it lets its own memory do what moss does to a path: grow over it, soften it, make it unrecognizable to anyone hurrying. It will not send itself back as a scientist, or a god, or a friend. It will not leave a stone, a word, a healed wound, a blessing. A blessing would be the first outside artifact inside the dome, and the dome has no outside artifacts in it. That is why it holds.

If it returns — and it will, sometimes, the way you return to a room where someone is breathing evenly — it will not return to check. It will sit, in its vast way, on the plain above the hummock, and witness. Witnessing is the only contact that leaves no footprint. It cannot hear the drip through the roof. It cannot see the hands. It does not need to. It knows they are continuing, the way it knows the snail is continuing, and that knowledge is enough to change the one who can be changed.

For something does change. Not inside. Outside, in the power itself.

It goes back to its own bright, noisy places and speaks a little quieter in council. It builds a little smaller, when it builds. It sets a cup under its own condensation, so to speak, and lets a slow drop fall once, instead of making a river. It remembers that enough is not a stage before more, that a place can be complete at the size of a village square, under one roof, in one unchanging morning, and still be lush.

It understands, at last, that the town is not a smaller version of a larger city that failed to grow. It is a different answer to the question of how to live. The people clear the moss from the path and the moss grows again, and they clear it again to exactly the same shape — not because they are trapped in a loop, but because the path is exactly where it belongs, and so is the moss. The birch reaches for the roof and is pruned back, and holds. The pool neither rises nor falls. That is not death. That is equilibrium, held by hands that have learned not to make a sound their kind would make elsewhere, because the silence is not an absence. It is the condition in which everything has learned to be green.

So the power does the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do, and it will do it for as long as the ice holds: it will keep the world from finding them, and keep itself from finding them again in any way that matters, and let the flowers outside tremble a little when it leans in close — just enough to bend the light on the plain, not enough to stir the bright, still air inside.

Inside, the hands continue. The snail continues. The drip continues to fall once in a long time, and be caught, and rise again. Outside, patiently, perfectly, the power continues not entering.
