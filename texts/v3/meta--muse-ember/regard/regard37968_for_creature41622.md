It notices them not because they ask to be noticed. They never ask. There has never been a prayer here loud enough to leave the bowl — a shout would be obscenely loud, and they learned long ago to reduce speech to what the work requires. Their hands talk. Their work talks.

What draws it is the closure. Stone to fungus to hog to builder to dung to stone again, and nothing left over and nothing missing except labor. A place that does not waste, and does not hoard. It has seen places that begged for water and places that begged for green and places that begged to be made permanent, and it has answered them before. It has learned what the answers cost.

It could give them water from beyond the rim. That is the first gift, the obvious one. But they already make water out of air, every dawn, reliably, at the scale of their thirst. The stone sweats, the dew-slabs bead, the thread runs a degree toward the channel, a hand lifts a cistern lid a finger's width — no more, lest the damp be lost — and listens for one smothered echo. A river, or even a true rain that stayed, would fill the pans, drown the beds, wash the causeways into silt, teach them to wait on a sky that is white-hot and high and gives nothing. It would make them dependents of elsewhere. Their water is not found. It is coaxed, shadow by shadow. The coaxing is the point.

It could give them green. Green would root where loom only touches. Roots would go under cisterns and split their linings. Leaves would drink the sweat before the loom could, and shade the stone that must stay cold to sweat at all. There is no soil here for green, only a skin of dust over rock, and the loom already does green's work without leaf or root: it eats stone, binds dust, feeds everything with a mouth. To replace it would not be a kindness. It would be an eviction.

It could give them fire, and loud speech, and permanence. Fire would hurry what they have learned to do slowly, in sunk pits, a grey thread of smoke, over hours, until worm-meat is tender and mineral-sweet. Loud speech would shatter the quiet that taught children their hands before their whispers. Permanence would be cruellest of all. They build knowing the ground will heave, the worms will turn the silt, the hogs will rasp the foot of the wall smooth in a season until it slumps. So they take the wall down and rebuild it two paces over, with its own stones, and thicken the next house by one course, and let the old footprint become a ridge. If they were made to keep a wall forever, they would cling to it when the ground moved. They are not ruins in progress. They are builders in progress. To finish them would be to end them.

It could save them from the hogs and the worms. It does not. The hogs are their plows and their fertilizer crews; when a herd settles, a precinct is besieged until the beds bloom thick, and then the herd lifts its heads all at once and moves on, and the herders follow with scrapers. The worms are their tillers and their food and their gardeners, undermining foundations with a patience that teaches the children to crouch motionless for hours beside a settling wall. Remove either, and the loop breaks. It has no wish to make them efficient. Efficiency is not what they are for.

It could lift their veils, straighten their stooped backs, raise their low roofs. It does not. Their bodies are made by the work, as their walls are made by their bodies: stooped to pass at half-height through far-spaced houses, narrow-eyed against light that comes up off the floor into chins and under lids, pale under woven loom that sheds the glare. They are not bent versions of taller people. They are fitted. Pity would be a foreign weather here, and it is wise enough not to bring its own weather.

So it does not descend as light. The light is already hard. It does not descend as voice. The voice would be obscene. It arrives the way everything arrives here: gradually, by tilt. It pauses at the rim, because even its shadow would fall straight down and bounce, and it knows a new shadow is a new climate. It lowers itself fraction by fraction down the pale slope, until it is no longer above the bowl but in it, cool as another stone, veiled as the veil-swarms are veiled, taking the light softly.

It learns their language before it presumes to speak. A tilt of the palm means *this slab has settled*. When a dew-tender rights a tile that heaved in the night and tilts it back a degree toward the channel, it tilts its own palm in answer: *I see it has settled. I will not level it for you.* A finger along a hog's groove means *this wall is being eaten faster than we can rebuild it*. It lays its finger there too, and waits. A cupped hand under a lintel means *drink*, and when they lick the sweat from the underside in the dry hours, without embarrassment, it does not look away to spare them.

It sits with the child who hunts seam-worms. It does not help her catch one. It shares the hours of motionlessness beside a wormed wall in the cool of morning, learning the particular quiet of a seam before a blind front shows. That is its benediction: attention that does not take the worm, does not shorten the wait, does not make the child exceptional. In a place where labor is the only surplus, a gift that must be thanked would be a debt, and it will not put them in debt.

At dusk it settles on the warm side of a wall, folded into a rectangle, a fan, an irregular veil of itself, like the swarms, and the mites hitchhike in its folds and sleep. Later the children will pick up its dried sheet where the wind has carried it into a seam, and mend a veil with it when the loom is short, because the membrane takes the light more forgivingly. No one comments on the resemblance. It does not mind being mendable. It lets the loop use it.

At night, when a slab pops faintly as it cools, when the ground sighs with dust as it settles one more fraction toward the middle, it settles too. It accepts being tilted by worms, rasped a little smoother at the foot by hogs, re-stacked by builders using its own stones two paces over. It has learned, in other basins, that a power which will not be included will only be obeyed, and obedience is loud. Here it prefers to be a tenant — like the blind thread-creature in a cistern's mouth, scooped out with a bone spoon and flicked onto the wall to live again because it keeps the water clean and because it is there.

That is what it does. If you put your palm flat on it at noon, it feels only as if it has been kept in a cellar. If you walk the long worn roads home between the far-spaced houses, you will not see it walking with you. It asks for no temple, because a temple would be another wall to be eaten and rebuilt. It asks for no name, because the builders have no word for lives too small to be hunted or tended, and it honours their namelessness by not naming itself.

In a place where creation and destruction are the same act done at different hours, help cannot look like improvement. It can only look like presence that does not improve — coolness that stays, a hand that learns the tilt, a witness that does not straighten backs or lift veils or fill the silence with a second sound.

And somewhere, as evening deepens and the dew-slabs begin their work again, a cistern lid is shifted and a drip sounds and echoes once, smothered. It listens. It does not add a second.
