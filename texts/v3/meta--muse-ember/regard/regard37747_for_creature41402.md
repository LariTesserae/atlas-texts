**What It Would Do**

It does not find them by a shout. They never shout.

It finds them, first, by something small repeating without error: a bone needle clicking, gut cord chewed soft between teeth, tied and tied again. Then by a pattern that should not hold and does hold — a few hundred people, seventy houses, a few hundred paces of wet, freezing ground, and nothing in it ever quite still or ever quite lost.

A benevolent and wise power turns its attention that way for a long time before it does anything at all. That is the first thing it does. It watches in the same register they use to speak in: low, close, head-down. It does not thunder. It learns the volume of the place.

And almost at once it is tempted, as all such powers are tempted, to fix the bowl.

It could soften the white sun so it does not bounce back from below. It could lay a meadow over the slush, give them turf and wood and wheels and a ground that stays plumb under a house. It could give them a voice that carries past the rim. It could stop the frost heave at night. It could make the fish pools drain and be clean, make the worm-eels blind in a kinder way, make nothing eat anything else.

It holds its hand out toward all of that. And then, wisely, it pulls it back.

Because it sees what the fixes would unmake.

The scarcity is not an accident in the bowl. It is the loom. The birds' waste feeds the algae, the algae feeds the mites, the mites feed the beetles, the beetles feed — and all of it feeds under a sky that gives light and takes away sound, so everything must be close to be heard. The people carry ice bigger than their torso because there is no other way to carry it, and in carrying it shoulder to shoulder they trade, murmur, mend a neighbor's wall for fish, for fat, for cord. A house sinks a finger's width and by first light three people are under it with a lever of bone. A channel blocks and floods another row, and before noon it is dug clear.

If the ground stopped moving, the mending would stop. If the mending stopped, the knowing would stop. The bowl is not a place its people endure in spite of their lives. It is a place their lives are made of, exactly as it is: wet, crowded, bright, freezing, never still.

So the power will not rescue them up and out of it. To bring them down to the low world, where there is wood and metal and green in wasteful abundance, would be to give them everything and take away the chain that holds them alive. It will not do that, however much a lesser kindness would want to.

What it does instead is smaller, and harder to see from the rim. If you were looking for a miracle, you would call it coincidence. It calls it care.

It keeps the rim wind from coming over too often, and when it does come, it sighs. It keeps the cloud-sea below thick, so caravans and kings and map-makers in the lower world think the high plateau is empty weather and pass beneath it. Privacy, for a place that lives naked under a vertical sun, is a form of shelter.

It keeps the impermeable lens of ice under the floor from growing too thick in one place. Not so thin that the pools would drain — they must not drain — but not so thick that the whole bowl would tip toward glacier and silence itself for good. A fraction of a degree, a fraction of a night, held steady. The fish go on turning under their clear lids like slow silver weather, thumping and turning, unseen except as a flash when you walk over them.

When a house settles too fast in slush, or an ice lens shoves a retaining wall out into a lane, the power does not lift the house. Sometimes, barely, it slows the growth under that one corner for one night, long enough for the people to find it at dawn and do the lifting themselves. They will say the cold was less cruel. They will be right.

When a tool falls in black slush and goes under the clear ice, the power does not return it to a hand. It keeps it from sinking past reach, and lets the refracted light find its edge, faintly glowing, doubled like everything else underfoot — last year's lane, a drowned storehouse, a knife wrapped in fat. In the morning someone's boot will crack the surface in the right place. They will say they remembered. They will be right.

It tends the niches in the rim wall. The weeping water seals the dead in transparent stone, smiling faintly, part of the architecture. The power keeps that seal clear. It will not let them go opaque or crack and fall. A place that does not rot should not be made to forget.

And in the melt-ditches, it does not make the burrower's tunnels permanent. They collapse and refill daily, and the animal digs them again daily, paddling with only its nose above water, leaving pellets for lamps. The power respects a creature that lives by re-digging. It only ensures, now and then, that a culvert does not block where a child's foot will go through in the dark.

None of this will be recorded. The people speak low and write, if they write at all, in marks on ice that will melt by noon. The power does not ask them to pray louder in order to be heard. It hears the tying of cord. It hears the market murmur: fish for fat, fat for cord, a stone blade for a morning's mending. That is prayer enough, because it is work done for a neighbor.

If one of them, alone with a body in a niche, does pray — not for rescue, they would not know the word for it, but for endurance, for one more day of the lane holding — the power will not come down itself. It will answer in the bowl's own language. It will make the neighbor's visor tilt a little closer. It will make the fish school turn so the pool flashes. It will make the water in a frost-crack climb a few inches up a stone face before freezing, as if pulled by cold, and someone will see it and know the night has been watched over.

Mostly, though, its action is its attention. In a place where everything is preserved under clear ice — fish under lids, tools under lanes, ancestors under weeping stone — to be seen by a wise power is simply one more layer of that same clear ice: transparent, preserving, not stopping the turning underneath. It does not freeze them in place. It lets the light through.

So it stays. It keeps the cold from deepening and the light from whitening past bearing. It keeps the bowl closed and crowded and alive. And when evening drops the sun abruptly and the birds come home in their silent white rain and the people go back into their re-tied, re-leveled houses with damp mittens and loosen their bone visors, then from the rim the hamlet looks established, in balance, long-existed.

Up close it is still wet and freezing and never still.

The power does not change that. It is glad of it.
