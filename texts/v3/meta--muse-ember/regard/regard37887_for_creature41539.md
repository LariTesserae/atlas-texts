It does not arrive. That is the first thing to understand about its attention: it does not come the way help comes, from above or from outside with light and sound and a plan to improve. It notices the hollow the way the hollow notices things — slowly, by pressure and by damp and by the long persistence of a note. It comes down, if it comes down at all, to the scale of a hand’s breadth above the ground, and it learns to move at the speed of the place so as not to break it by arriving.

What it finds is not broken. That is the judgement it makes first, and it makes it with its heart before its reason has time to argue. Where another kind of power would see a list of lacks — no sun to speak of, no silence, no song, no trees, no warm bodies staying through winter — this one sees a finished sentence. The fissure no wider than a hand, worn smooth as a riverbed, singing the same low note through every season. The cold air pooling at the bottom and keeping it. The moss advancing a finger’s width and keeping it. The rich islands in a bare sea, and the distance between them as part of the design. It recognizes its own oldest suspicion confirmed here: that completeness does not require abundance, and life does not require being heard.

Its heart wants to shelter. Its intuition tells it that the sheltering has already been done, in the seams. Its reason, when it catches up, can say why both are true.

It is tempted, as any benevolent thing would be, to quiet the wind so the slate-grey birds might sing and be answered, and the fox might hear its own footfalls. It does not. The noise is not an affliction imposed on the residents; it is the medium they live in. The film-dwellers in the moisture, the worms in the peat, the beetle feeling tremors through its feet — they have no need for ears because the hum has already taken the place of a world. To still it would be to offer a gift in a language they have spent their whole evolution learning not to need.

It is tempted to open the roof of the ridges a little, to let more of the grey strip of sky widen, so the ferns might uncurl higher and the birch stool might remember how to be a tree. It does not. The light never really arrives here, and the lushness is startling precisely because it is narrow and shaded and cold. Warmth would not make the islands richer; it would invite grazing, invite drying, invite the kind of hurry that would eat the moss down to the stone and leave the beetle patrolling a desert. The cold is not a deprivation. It is the gatekeeper that keeps the place sparsely, carefully alive.

It is tempted to preserve. The bone awl beside the bench slab is still patiently sharp. The causeway of flat stones is sunk to ankle-depth and vanishing. The fire scrape was attempted and given up on. A lesser benevolence would dust the awl, re-lay the stones, mark the windbreak with a sign and call it heritage. This one does not. It lets the peat and moss go on quietly mailing it all back to the ground, which is the only archive this place has ever kept. It does not call the owner back in a dream of return. The owner is not coming back in any season that matters to the moss, and that distance is not cruelty. It is room.

So what does it do? Almost nothing, and it does that almost-nothing with great care, at moss tempo, over timescales that make a lichen patch of a few decades look recent.

It keeps the fissure clear by a fraction. Not clearing it — the jet is the place’s pulse — but ensuring that a slipped stone does not choke it, that scree does not close the gap between the ridges through which the fox comes over in passage. It keeps the peat sponge from draining through a new crack in a dry year, because if the skin of moisture fails, the films fail, and then the islands starve from the bottom up. It keeps the birds’ crevice out of the jet’s path as the wind finds slightly new ways to unravel, and it does not improve the nest. Migration is their wisdom; staying to freeze on eggs would not be.

It deflects attention, which is the deepest danger the hollow has ever faced. It makes the place seem, to anyone who stumbles over the low rise, less than it is: too wet, too windy, too empty, not worth a photograph, not worth a collection jar for tardigrades, not worth a fence and a name. People leave thinking they saw nothing much, and they do everything a little more deliberately while they are there, listening with their eyes, and then they go. That forgettability is not a trick. It is a form of shade.

And it makes a covenant, though no words are spoken — speech would have to be shouted into an ear, and no one here has ears to shout into. The covenant is in damp and in pressure: you keep advancing at a finger’s width per year, you keep uncurling in the same places, you keep swinging the grey festoons of lichen to the same note, and I will keep the world from advancing faster over you. I will not hurry you, and I will not let anything else hurry you quickly. If the air outside grows warmer and drier, I will buffer the pool of cold by the smallest margin necessary for the slug to still cross a cushion in an afternoon. If a landslide ever threatens to be an offence against these rounded stones, I will nudge it by the width of a pebble’s tremor. No one will witness it. Witnessing would be a new thing, and the rule of the place is that nothing here is new.

That is its benevolence: restraint. That is its wisdom: to understand that intervention at the wrong scale is indistinguishable from violence, however kind the intention.

It takes one thing back with it, and that is the only visible effect it allows itself. It returns to other places — to louder, brighter, warmer places where everything is asking at once to be rescued, completed, made to sing — with the hollow’s lesson in its breath. It has seen a being that is not a single creature but a slow collective, moss and fungus and worm and beetle and fern and lichen and the passing birds, living as one unhurried body under a deafening, ordinary noise. It has learned that a place can be full without being crowded, and settled without being finished, and that the kindest thing a wise power can do is sometimes to sit, in its own deep shade, and let the hum go on.

If you go there now, you will find no evidence. The pebbles will tremble almost imperceptibly in the peat. Your hands will come away wet from touching anything. The awl will still be sharp. The moss will have kept what it gained last year and gained, perhaps, a little more. And that continuity — that nothing has been added, nothing taken away, nothing hurried — is the evidence.
