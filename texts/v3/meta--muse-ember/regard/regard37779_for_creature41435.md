It notices without arriving.

That is the first thing it does, and the most important. It does not descend, it does not settle, it does not stir the silt to see more clearly. Attention, in it, is not a weight. It looks the way cold looks from inside the water: evenly, without preference, for a very long time.

It sees the cold first, too. Then the dark. Then the work.

And then, slowly, the way the place itself teaches slowness, it understands that it is complete.

A lesser power would mistake completeness for emptiness because nothing in it moves as a power is expected to move. Nothing flees. Nothing rises. Nothing asks. A lesser power would feel the ache of that silence as something to be answered and would hurry to answer it.

This one has a heart. Its heart wants to do something. Warm a single pale ribbon where it hangs translucent at ankle height, far from the ten steps of light. Turn the dark up, just a little, so the green could be seen. Bring a fish, or a crab, or a snail, so the mat would be cropped and the place would have company, and noise, and a second storey. Raise one slumped wall back to two metres, the way it was when the hand that laid it still lived. Speak a name to it, so it would not be unrecorded.

Its reason, which is older than its heart and loves it quietly, asks it to finish each thought.

Warm it for whom? The plant is not cold in the way a man in the water is cold. It is cold the way itself is cold, by adaptation, by a million millimetres of patience. Warmth would not comfort it. It would unmake the anoxious stillness that lets timber hold its shape like black fruit, let the dead leaf become silt instead of rot, let a leaf hold its unfurled shape for a decade. Warmth would be a catastrophe dressed as kindness.

Light, for whom? For eyes. It has no eyes that need it, and the ribbons that have grown pale in the colourless dark have grown pale because pale is what survives there. To brighten them would be to burn the very fact of their persistence.

Animals, for whom? The lushness here is not a lack waiting to be filled. It is what happens when nothing eats it. Bring grazers and you bring teeth and hunger and disturbance and waste; you break the one storey — water, built earth, plant — and replace it with a world that wants to be dramatic. The plant does not want company. It wants continuation.

Repair, for whom? The builders are not here to be thanked, and their work is not broken in the sense of a tool that fails. It is finished in the sense of a thing that has settled into its final form. A wall thirty centimetres high, its capstones tipped and vine-entangled, is not a failed wall. It is a wall that has arrived. To raise it again would not honor the hand that laid it. It would overwrite it with a second hand, and a false one.

Study, for whom? To lift a brick to see the thumbprint beneath the algae would be to destroy the algae to see the brick. To map the dikes would be to walk the pattern until the silt rose and did not settle for a season. To bring it into light, into a museum, into a record, into meaning — all of these are ways of taking. The place has survived because it was not looked at by anything loud enough to want it.

And a name, for whom? A name is a claim, however tenderly made. This power will not claim it by loving it.

So it rescinds its own impulses, one by one. Not because it cannot do these things. Because a benevolent and wise power knows first that its power is the most dangerous thing in the water, and restrains itself before it restrains anything else.

It does grieve, briefly, for the builders. It traces, without touching, the groove a pace wide, the stake-holes spaced by an arm, the square of pada clay softening back into silt. It learns their patience without inheriting their task. It will not finish their work. It will not become their descendants by imitation. It honors them by letting their work be allowed to end.

It stays. For a human lifetime, which here is nothing, and for itself, which is less than a blink, it stays long enough to perceive the rate of things: a rhizome advancing a handspan in a lifetime, a footprint filling grain by grain, a mat torn and then, years later, resuming over the tear. It does not ask the place to speed up to become legible. It learns to read at a millimetre a year.

If it does anything at all, it is this: it makes its noticing a boundary.

It folds the dark back over the place as it found it, a little more surely than before. Not as a secret — secrets invite hunting — but as an ordinariness that will be overlooked, a plainness that will not be mistaken for treasure, for prophecy, for a resource, for a sign. While it is here, no louder attention will find its way down through the ice. No salvage, no expedition, no well-meaning rescue will come to stir the snow of silt that falls and almost never arrives. It will keep the world unwitnessed by anything that would need to walk on it to appreciate it.

And it takes the memory away with itself. That is the exchange, and the only one. The place does not gain a witness in any way it can use. It does not gain a voice, or a face, or a future. The power gains only the knowledge that a thing can be lush without vigor, ancient without monument, alive without animal, total without ending, and ask for nothing.

Then it withdraws the way it came: without a ripple, without a footprint, without one ribbon bent.

Below, the cold remains cold. The silt goes on falling. Somewhere west a wall is a little higher than the rest; somewhere east a canal is a little wider. The felt thickens a finger, then a forearm, over stone that was laid by hands long silted themselves. The moss adds a cushion to a cushion.

It leaves the being who lives there exactly as it found it — not growing taller, not growing faster, not growing toward it — and that careful, deliberate, heart-breaking restraint is what it does.
