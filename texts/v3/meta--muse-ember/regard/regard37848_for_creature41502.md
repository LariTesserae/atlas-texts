The power does not come down into it.

It stops at the rim, where the grass and bracken are, and looks in. It does not displace the cold air. It does not cast a shadow over the water. To enter would be to change the first condition of the place, which is that nothing entering has, for a long time, been allowed to stay. The power is wise enough to know this before it knows anything else, and benevolent enough to make its first act a restraint: it stills itself more completely than the pit requires.

It had been drawn by the report of life. That is how such attention usually comes — not by suffering or by song, but by a persistence that does not fit the expected pattern. A pit dug for stone, flooded by groundwater, cold and acidic and loaded with iron, still as a sealed room, and yet: moss on the north wall, five or six sedges in a broken ring, algae crusting the stones and the concrete slab below the surface. The power came expecting to find something wounded, waiting to be mended.

What it finds instead is exactness.

The mineral stain is at the same height it has always been. The water level has not risen with rain and has not fallen with summer. The midday patch of true sun moves across the centre and goes, and in that hour the light reaches the floor five or six metres down, and the algae take what little they take, and then the shadow of the rim returns. The moss stops where it stops, abruptly, green on one side and pale grey on the other, as if a line had been drawn and kept. The sedges stand in their places, unswayed, neither thickening nor dying back. Everything that looks like an absence — no fish, no insects, no frogs, no decay, no growth — is not a failure in progress. It is a finished state. The pit has arrived at its equilibrium, and the living things have arrived at theirs, thin as they are.

The heart of the power, which is real, wants at once to do the obvious kind things.

For the moss, it wants to carry soil over the rim and spread it on the stone, so the green skin can thicken and cover the north wall. For the sedges, it wants to warm the water a degree, stir the silt, richen it, let the tussocks flower properly and multiply until the broken ring is whole. For the algae, it wants to open the pit to light all day and move the water gently, so the dark crust on the slab can become a bloom. For the pit itself, it wants to remove the rusted pipe and the square of concrete with its four drilled holes, to fill in the benches, to plant trees at the edge so something might lean over the water.

It considers each of these, in turn, with the attention that benevolence requires. And it sees, in each case, that the gift would not be received as a gift.

Soil on the stone would bury the moss it meant to save. Warm water would not stay warm for the sedges alone; it would invite what lives above the rim — the bracken, the grasses, the insects, the frogs — and in a season the tussocks would not be fed, they would be outcompeted, shaded, eaten, uprooted. Stirring the water would tear away the algae that live precisely because nothing tears them away. Removing the slab would remove the one flat, stable surface the algae have colonised. The pipe, which has not dripped in a long time, is not a wound to be cleaned; it is part of the closed history that lets the level stay where it stays.

What looks from above like deprivation is, from inside, the element. The moss does not live despite the bare stone and the dust and the cold dampness. It lives because of them, at the exact limit where they permit it. The sedges do not persist despite the iron and the unchanging cold. They persist in that cold, at that level, which never drowns them and never dries them. The algae do not wait for more light. They have built their whole day around the hour when the light comes. To improve any one of them would be to unmake the conditions that make it itself.

So the power does not improve anything.

What it does is keep the conditions.

It stays at the rim through the morning, in the shadow the rim throws, and watches the water hold its own time. At midday, when the pale patch begins to move across the centre, it steps back — consciously, so that its own shade does not fall across the surface and steal a fraction of that brief light from the floor. It does not breathe down into the pit. It does not warm the cold air that sits there and never moves. It does not wade in from the dry shelf, because a footstep in the silt would break the thin bands in the floor, and the water would take a long time to forget it.

Later, when it leaves, it leaves footprints only in the grass above, if it leaves footprints at all. It does not speak. The inhabitants have no ear for a voice, and a voice would be another alteration in a place whose silence is factual, not atmospheric. It does not name them. A name is a kind of claim, and the power is not there to claim.

Its benevolence takes the only form that will not be a trespass here: a keeping. It will keep the level where the stain is. It will keep the cold where the cold sits. It will keep the stillness unbroken by anything that comes over the rim — no new water running in, no earth slumping from the benches onto the shelf, no drainage, no filling out of pity, no tidying out of disgust for the industrial past. It will not let the pit be turned into something more legible, more lush, more useful. It will let the iron film break and reform in its sheltered corner. It will let the sedges remain five or six, and separate, and grey-green. It will let the moss stop where it has always stopped.

This is not indifference, and it is not a miracle. Nothing that can be measured from one visit to the next will be different. A person stepping down the loose grey earth in November or in June will find the same clear water, the same cold that makes the ankles ache, the same rusty bands, the same dark crust on the slab, the same abrupt edge of green on the north wall. The hum from the substation beyond the rim will still be softer than breathing.

But the pit will no longer be unwatched. And for a place that has been left alone long enough to reach its own shape, to be watched without being touched — to be attended to, precisely, in its limits — is not a small thing.

The power stays in that attention. It does not fill the emptiness. It tends it.
