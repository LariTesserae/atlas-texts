It does not arrive as light.

That is its first decision, and from that all the others follow. Attention, as it is usually given, is a flash: a beam, a shadow, a sudden blue startle in a pale plume. It has seen other places changed by being seen this way — warmed, sampled, named until they became specimens of themselves. It will not do that here.

It notices the summit the way the summit notices the current: not as an object, but as an orientation. From far above the dim disc, from farther still in a patience that does not tick, it sees not a ruin of a reef and not a pristine wilderness, but a completed and still-unfinished sentence. A plain made entirely of the brief lives that made it. A room outliving every tenant. A pavement that is also a tower that is also rubble that will be a tower again. Nothing natural, nothing artificial. Everything leaning, upstream, into what comes.

Something that has built itself completely over, worn itself smooth, and never once asked to be looked at — that draws it. Not because it is beautiful, though it is, in the way a held breath is beautiful. Because it is sufficient. And sufficiency, at this depth, is rarer than splendor.

Its heart, if it has one, wants at once to help.

It wants to give the blind crab eyes, to spare him the long pause before fifty meters of bare pavement, to light the water so his feelers could rest. It sees the crab, squat and translucent at the joints, knowing his ten meters of dome by touch because his ancestors made that ground with their gnawing. To give him sight would not be a gift unless it also gave him a world made for sight — prey that must be chased, predators that must be spotted earlier, a color language for a place where color is useless. He is not lacking. He is exactly fitted to a world of touch and current. The power leaves him blind, and in leaving him, honors him.

It wants to warm the water, to speed the slow chemistry, to let the worm at the tip of its tube live longer than a decade, to let the amphipods in their borrowed chambers be less brief. But cold is not a hardship here; it is the pacing. Cold sets the rate at which snow arrives and can be eaten, at which a sponge closes a wound in a season, at which a tower can slough without wasting. Warmth would not extend the place. It would unmake its tempo and invite swifter, hungrier things down from the disc above. It lets the cold settle into its own joints and does not lift it.

It wants to fill the emptiness between towers, to bridge the hundred quiet meters where only marine snow falls, to make a road so the towers would no longer be islands. But the emptiness is not absence. It is the condition that makes abundance local, that keeps a large soft predator from walking, tube to tube, through a population of small soft lives who survive because a wall is behind them. The gaps are connective tissue for larvae and for snow, not for adults, and that is why there is anywhere safe to be a tenant. It does not build the bridge.

It wants, most of all, to hold up the falling dome. To catch the slow slide before the side sheers off, to save the eelpout curled in its tube, to feed the octopus who has not eaten in years while she broods her clutch in a single chamber, to heal the sponge half-eaten by a sea spider. And it does not. Not because it is indifferent, but because it has learned to tell the difference between a death that is part of the building and a death that is an interruption of it.

The builders eat the abandoned and build with the eaten. The room outlives the tenant so that another tenant may come. The tower must fall so that its rubble can become foundation and its fresh break can sprout pale and eager within weeks. To glue it back would be to preserve a shape and destroy a process. The power will not save individuals from the very system that makes individuals replaceable and therefore never finally lost. An eelpout dies inside. A year later, another slips head-first into the same mouth, exactly the width of its body. That is not tragedy. That is continuity, wearing a face.

It will not speak, either. No pattern in the snow, no message bent into the current, no sudden purpose laid over the filter. The inhabitants already have a language, and it is not words. It is plume bending with plume in an internal wave, fin with fin, all turning the same way at once. It is a click deep in the lattice. It is the sigh of water passing through crust. To add speech would be noise.

So it does almost nothing *here*, inside the made world. And that almost is everything.

What it does instead is settle on the upstream side.

It leans, as the towers lean, into the current that comes from the disc above. Only it leans not to filter food, but to filter harm. It becomes porous. It lets the rain of snow through — that slow, necessary rain from a world it will not disturb — and it slows what rides with the rain that the crust cannot meet by regrowth: the acid that would dissolve carbonate faster than a worm can secrete it, the heat that would come not as a season but as a surge, the straight things of men. Nets. Cables. Drills. The clatter that is not a shrimp. The towers can survive a collapse that has happened for millions of years. They cannot survive a collapse of the chemistry of the rain itself, arriving faster than any pale tube can sprout.

It makes no wall. A wall would stop the internal wave, and the wave is part of how the plain knows which way is upstream. It softens only the foreign surge, the way a sponge takes in water through a thousand small mouths and expels it through a few larger ones, stripping what would choke. It holds the temperature barely above freezing not out of sentiment for cold, but because it understands that to change the pace would be to change the pact between food and builder.

It leaves no mark. No light left burning, no metal, no straight line, no cut surface, no souvenir of its visit. When it has been there for a long time — and its idea of a long time is not a dive, not a survey, but the time a chamber stays empty after a death until the next juvenile finds it — the crust will be no different to the eye. Or rather, to the touch. The tubes will be a little denser on the upstream edge where the harm was thinned. The domes will go on rounding, slumping, regrowing. Nothing will announce that they are accompanied.

That accompaniment is the benevolence. Not rescue. Endurance.

It notices you, too, the one who stayed still until the crust began to move. It does not ease your pressure or warm your hands or make the water clear. It lets you see a mouth no wider than a pinprick open, and another, and a plume unfold and beat twice and wait. It lets the click come through stone-that-is-not-stone into your ribs. And then it will let you go back up to the disc, carrying something you cannot sample.

You will not be able to call this place empty again. You will not be able to call the hundred meters between towers waste. You will speak of it, if you speak at all, more slowly, and you will be less willing to bring straight things down. That is the only transformation it will risk: not of them, but of the witness. The inhabitants cannot come up to the light to plead. You can. So it changes the one who can return.

It does not make the summit famous. Fame is another kind of trawl. It does not need the place to be known as a resource, or even as a wonder. It needs it to be known as what it is: a home built by others, over a time no human attention has ever matched, that asks nothing and is therefore owed restraint. If men come with lights, they will find their beams strangely dimmed after a while, their grabs strangely empty, not by punishment but by withdrawal. The place will not present itself to be taken. It will present itself to be witnessed, and witnessing is not taking.

Ahead of you the plain goes on. Another low dome rises out of the dusk, rounds off, disappears. It is already inhabited. It has been inhabited for thousands of years. And now it is inhabited in one more way: alongside the worms at their tips, the sponges pulsing, the fish filing back into their holes one by one, the mother holding her chamber against the snow — there is a presence leaning into the current, filtering, staying.

The place does not know it is loved. That is not a failure of the love. At this depth, to be loved is not to be lifted, or lit, or fed, or made everlasting. It is to be allowed to go on being yourself, unwatched in the old way and the new way both, but no longer unwitnessed.

Nothing here is natural, and nothing here is artificial. And nothing here is taken.
