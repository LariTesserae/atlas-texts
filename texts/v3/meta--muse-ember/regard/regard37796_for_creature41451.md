**The Attention**

It did not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about it, and the thing it understood first about the Table. Arrival implies a before and an after, a place unvisited and then visited, a revision. The Table is too old for that kind of news. So the power did not descend. It noticed — the way one notices a stone ticking in the cold, or a bell opening for one warm minute — and noticing, it stayed.

It had seen other places. It had seen worlds that began in fire and wore themselves down in the ordinary forward way, and worlds that were interrupted, and worlds that were given a second life by someone cleverer or kinder than they could afford to be. It knew the gestures. It could have warmed the stone. It could have thinned the blue distance at the edge and let in a lower, kinder climate. It could have made a season. It had the means, if means were the question.

Means were not the question here.

The Table had finished eroding forward a very long time ago, and had learned to erode backward in order to stay itself. A step shallows. A crack seals. A fern practices unfolding. Rain is still falling in a cistern on Wednesday. These were not errors. They were the logic of a place that had reached its oldest stable form and could only remain stable by repeating the patterns that made it. Anything new was gently, inexorably revised until it fit, or until it learned to be unmade without protest.

A wise power, watching that, feels its first temptation very clearly: to finish it.

To let the finality be final. To smooth the dome all the way down to bone, to let the peat stop thickening, to let a town add a storey and never lose it. It would be a mercy, of a kind. Things that have ended should be allowed to end.

The power held that thought the way the rootfolk hold a basket on an upper floor that will not be there by afternoon — carefully, and then it set the basket down on the ground. It understood, slowly, that ending the Table would be the one revision that did not fit, and therefore the one violence it could not dress up as kindness. The Table was not practising how to die. It was practising how to go on. There is a difference, and only an elder can hear it.

Its second temptation was to fill it.

The bells in the hollows close tight against the cold all day and open, each one, in the warmest minute, as if expecting a visitor who will not arrive. The power, which had been a visitor to a thousand emptier places, read that as loneliness. It thought of birds. It thought of insects. It thought, with great tenderness, of a soft sound over the moss.

It tried, once, in thought alone, without touching the weather. A bird made of intention circled a fern-clump, looking for a perch, for an insect, for a song to answer its own. There were no insects because there had never been insects. There was no perch because the trees were low and prisoner to their own moss. The bird found no purchase, not in the air and not in the world, and it left the way a cloud leaves here — born and unborn within sight, having rained on nothing.

It tried warming one hollow by a fraction, so the bells might stay open longer. The flowers opened, as scheduled, and closed, as scheduled, a little colder for the confusion. The power withdrew the fraction. It learned that the bells were not lonely. They were punctual. Expectation here was not a wound. It was a form of attention the Table paid to itself.

Its third temptation was to free them.

The rootfolk stand politely in the beds along the walls, taproots dark where the soil has washed away, hands woody and delicate over looms and tools. To anything that walks, that looks like captivity. The power imagined unrooting one — gently, no tearing — and letting it walk the six days of bare stone to the next town, to see the other looms, the other cisterns.

It imagined it clearly enough to understand what it would be doing: giving a plant legs for a journey it had no organs for, and then taking them away when the bed remembered it. Rootfolk do not want to be carried. They want to be replanted, if the town revises itself around them, with the same politeness with which they were planted in the first place. Their stillness is not waiting. Their shaking-off of moss from their shoulders is not a call for help. It is, perhaps, harvesting. It is, perhaps, simply the moment when the weight becomes impolite.

The power set that thought down, too, with care.

Its fourth temptation was the most persistent, because it is the one benevolence most often mistakes for itself: to teach.

To give them a voice that carries. To give them maps that do not redraw themselves wet in the morning. To give them a forward direction, a new pattern, a lichen that had never been seen before and would not need to repeat.

It listened, for a very long time, to the low dry clicks in the courtyards, close enough to hear the stone ticking beside them. The clicks are not impoverished speech. They are speech sized to the place. No one here is in a hurry to be heard across a courtyard, and no birds are listening overhead. A voice that carried would carry too far. It would disturb the hush when you step off stone and onto moss. It would be revised.

So the power did not teach. It learned the clicks' tempo. It learned that repair here means laying the same stone back in the same place until the place remembers it, and that the rootfolk tend their fingers the same way. It sat, in the only way it could sit, in the blue distance where the horizon is only weather, and it watched a storehouse acquire a back room, and watched the room acquire lichen from a stone a mile away, and did not file a correction.

What, then, does a benevolent and wise power do, if anything?

It remembers.

That is the one gift that fits. The Table is a memory that corrects itself by repeating. It keeps what fits the oldest stable form and lets the rest go — the street as it was before the turn, the step as it was before the shallowing, the fern as it was before the bud. It is not forgetful, exactly. It is faithful in a way that costs it its own past.

The power became the past's witness. It kept, outside the revision, the measure of a step when it was hollow. It kept the storehouse without the back room, and the back room without the lichen, and knew both as true in their season. It kept the name — if a click could be a name — of a rootfolk when it was younger, smaller, still learning to stand still, whose footprints are the only animal-like tracks in the town's mud. It kept the rain that fell on Wednesday while it was still falling, and the bell before the hammer, and the echo that arrived a fraction before the shout.

It did not correct the Table with these memories. It did not reimpose them. It simply held them, the way a deep hollow holds water: still, black, shallow, and always slightly out of date. So that when the Table revised, nothing was wholly lost. The revision still happened. The basket still came down from the upper floor. But somewhere, outside the weather, the upper floor was remembered as having been there.

And it became weather.

Not a storm that arrived from outside — there is no outside here that matters — but one of the Table's own: a cloud born over a cold rise, fattening, watching the towns and the green pools and the long bare stretches, splitting, and dying within sight. The rootfolk did not look up. Their shadows, at noon, sometimes pointed opposite to the sun for ten minutes, cast by trees that are not there. The power cast no shadow at all, which for once was the correct shadow.

To the bells it gave no visitor. To the ferns it gave no lesson. To the stone it gave no warmth. To the rootfolk it gave no road. It gave them what the Table itself gives everything that lives here, in its most generous form: sustained attention without revision.

On a night colder than the usual cold, when the cracks sealed themselves with soft ticking and the walls stood straight for an hour, proud and impossible, a rootfolk at a loom paused — not in dismay, not in wonder — and shook the moss from its shoulders in a slow green rain. The moss fell, as it always falls. The power, which was mostly a cloud and mostly a remembering, felt the increase in weight passing out of the world and into the peat, where decay that never hurries would keep it for decades.

That is what it did. It stayed. It remembered. It let the Table go on practising, in the same slow breath, and it practised with it — not how to begin, not how to end, but how to witness a thing that has already ended and still, faithfully, refuses to stop.

Behind it, in the blue distance, nothing changed. Ahead, the Table continued.
