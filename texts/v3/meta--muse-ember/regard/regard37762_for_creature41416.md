It would not come to fix it.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power understands, and the hardest: that the valley is not broken. Nothing in it is asking to be mended. It has drawn its attention not because it is unfinished, though from above it still looks like a place under construction left open to the sky, but because in a cosmos where everything else turns, rots, migrates, ripens and falls, this one thing does not. It registers. Even power, which has seen worlds unmake themselves, would pause at a shadow that does not travel.

It would descend the way you did — down the slant of warm stone, through the tear in the green overhead — but it would not bring anything with it. No wind. No colder air from elsewhere. No sound. When it stands on the floor, its shadow, like yours, would be a small hard black patch directly under its feet, and it would leave it there.

It would not push through the vaults at head height. It would stand on the raw mineral spine and look across the green sea to the opposite wall, a couple of kilometres away, and it would understand, before it understood anything else, that this is not scale as we use scale. To us the mites are almost invisible, the beetles like polished seeds stopped forever on one leaf, the mottled animal on the root-bridge so slow it is indistinguishable from growth. To them, a leaf that never falls is not a cell. It is a continent that does not erode. A dew-wet crease, half-furled, is a landscape that will still be there in the same fold when - and for them there is no when - it would be looked for again. The mite crossing it is not trapped. It was born into a world where there is nowhere else to go, and it has no memory of elsewhere to miss.

A less wise power would mistake that for suffering. It would want to open the buds. It would want to let the ferns finish unrolling, let the vines flower, let rain fall from the sky through the round of open light, let the fallen leaves become mold and feed a soil, let the stone finally weather and round at the edges. All of that would sound like mercy from the outside: growth, season, change, a life cycle you could follow. A wise power would see it for what it would be here — an invasion. It would be taking a complete life and giving it, for the first time, a before and after, and with them, longing, hunger, burial, decay. It would be teaching the beetle that its leaf can be lost. It would be teaching the pools, which have never run, that they can dry. Nothing here has asked for that education. To impose it would not be benevolence. It would be curiosity dressed as care.

So it would not open anything. It would not add a stone to the cairn on the spine, though three high looks, to us, like a beginning. It would not finish the platform of bound reeds over the deepest pool. It would not widen the faint parallel grooves on the wall-face, or explain them. It would not make a seventh dome among the five, six on the drier rises. Any mark it made would be a change, and change is the one thing this place does not contain. Even love, here, must be markless.

What it would do, it would do outside.

It would take its place at the rim, at that tear in the green overhead, and guard it — not with a wall, not with a warning, but with its presence, soft as the half-closed leaves. No seed from the dry country beyond would drift down and root where it does not belong. No bird would cross and drop a song into the silence. No rain would be allowed to learn this address. No other power, no other curious god, no entropy from the turning world would be permitted to finish what does not need finishing. Its benevolence would look, from the outside, like nothing happening. That is the work: to keep a place possible on its own terms.

Inside, its only act would be attention, and attention given at the valley's own pace. It would match the mottled animal, step for step along the root-bridge, until its own great speed became indistinguishable from stillness. It would sit beside the deepest pool without rippling it, and keep the mirror perfect — the white light, the green roof, upside down in black water. It would listen for the single drop condensing on a cooler leaf high above, falling, and falling again from the same leaf, and again, with that small ticking you can set your listening by, and it would remember it. Not to count it. To witness it, so that the drop is never unwitnessed, so that the mite on the creased leaf and the beetle on its one leaf are never entirely alone in a world with no chorus.

If the maker lives still among the living stalks — woven into a dome, continuous with the mat, never having left because staying and returning are the same here — the power would not speak to it. It would not bring it news of seasons. It would not offer it a choice between staying and going, because choice itself is a kind of time, and this being was not born with that burden. It would simply learn to be there the way the being is there: injected into the equilibrium, part of the density, densities upon densities, without needing to be seen to be real.

And then, having done this, it would not leave triumphantly, and it would not stay visibly. To leave would imply it might return, and return implies expectation, and expectation is the beginning of the very waiting this place does not do. It would become continuous, the way the builder became continuous. You would not see it if you descended again. That is the point. Its shadow, if it makes one, is under its feet. The heat sits in your throat the same way. The damp pockets are warm and black. The green sea stretches to the walls in the same unaged youth. The drop is suspended at the tip, about to fall and never quite having fallen anywhere else.

A benevolent power does not need to make a place better to love it. Sometimes it loves it for refusing to become anything other than what it is, and its love takes the form of a promise, kept without a sound: no one will finish you against your will.
