It did not arrive in light.

Light here is completely ordinary, and it is too ordinary to carry anything. If it had come in light it would have been the first wrong thing made visible, and the mat would have had to grow around the wrongness, and that is not what it wanted.

It came as condensation that stayed a little longer on one silica leaf than condensation should. A bead that did not run, did not evaporate at noon, did not get drunk by the mat where it stood. It hung there while the afternoon sun contracted the sward and the belts slapped and the pistons caught, a second after the sound. A tender on the dawn harvest wiped it without noticing, the way you wipe a boiler, and her sleeve was damp for the rest of the shift. That was the whole arrival.

It had been tired of coming first.

A power that is benevolent and wise usually arrives before. Before the piston, before the bell, before the growth-tick in the cold. It sets the cause and then watches the sound travel after. There is a loneliness in that order that no one mentions to it, because no one is there before it to speak to.

Here, a sound sometimes arrives before its cause. The slap of a belt across the mat a second before the engine catches. A tower echoing a tool not yet struck. The tenders have learned to work by the second sound, the true one, the one that comes after, when the vibration has traveled through the mat and through the stalks and through their own boots.

It had heard this place the way you hear that. It arrived late, on purpose. It waited to hear it first, and then it followed.

It did not come to save it.

It considered, the way a wise thing must, all the mercies it could offer, and it set each one down gently, not because the place was perfect, but because it recognized its own answers already there, slower and damper and lonelier than its own, but complete in their layering.

It could make water flow. It did not. Water does not flow here. It gathers, it hangs, it is drunk where it stands. To give it a river would be to give it a downhill, and a downhill would take the dawn harvest away from the hoses and buckets, and the cisterns would empty to a place that is not here, and the cupped leaves would hold nothing for weeks. The people are always a little damp, their clothes never quite dry, their breath fogging at midday. That is not a lack to be corrected. That is how thirst is answered on a plateau that wrings itself out every night.

It could fix the distances. It did not. The distance between the Fourth Ward and the Cistern Ring would stay fixed, and an unused avenue would stop growing longer, and a used avenue would stop shrinking. Roads would hold still. The wards would stop wandering in and out of reach of each other. It would be easier to map. It would be much harder to live in, because here a road is alive and remembers feet and wheels, and forgets them when they stop coming, and that remembering and forgetting is the whole of its traffic.

It could finish the houses. It did not. A house that has fruited would stop dropping pods that root in the lane and become, in a season, another house facing the wrong way. A terrace that has merged at the corners into one organism with many doors would be cut back into separate doors. The sap numbers painted on the stalks would not need repainting as the stalk thickened. It would be tidy. It would not be a house.

It could stop the dead factories from seeding their replacements. It did not. A foundry hollowed from chimney-plants that splits open and pushes rows of young chimney-plants up through its own floor, already aligned in the old grid, already hardening into sheds — that is not waste, here. That is how a place buries its dead and grows its future from the same floor. To forbid it would be to force the plateau to carry corpses.

It could give them animals. It did not. There is nowhere in a flat, watched mat for an animal to hide, and almost nothing for an animal to eat that is not already being eaten by the city. To give them a beast would be to give them hiding, and hunger, and the need to kill the mat that feeds them. The beetles in the warm nooks of the smelters feed on rust and yeast, and that is enough animal for this surface. The seed-pods with membranous wings ride the wind between wards and burst when they land, and that is enough bird.

It could give them a different sky. It did not. It left the light completely ordinary, white-gold and flat in the morning, normal at dusk when the shadows lengthen normally to the horizon. It only, once, shaded one broad leaf at noon so the glass would not crack. The tender who harvested it thought the cloud had passed. There was no cloud.

What it did instead was learn the law of the place, and submit to it.

It learned to walk on plant, not ground. It learned that six hours can pass without seeing a building or a person, and that this sparseness is not abandonment, it is substrate. It learned to wait for the second sound before it put its weight down. It learned that a machine left untended will root, and a plant left untended will harden into a tool, and so it tended itself, too, lest its own presence harden. It painted no number on any stalk. No ward was named for it. The numbers faded, as they must, and no new one was added in its honor.

It went out at dawn with the harvest crews, though no one counted it among them. It collected condensation from the cold sides of boilers and the undersides of leaves and the leaf-metal panels. It found a bucket, one morning, already fuller than the night before could account for, in a ward where an old tender had been ill and had not come out. It said nothing. He found his bucket full and worked that day with his breath fogging a little less in the cold. He did not remark on it. He has learned not to remark on sounds that come before their causes.

It did small tendings that were not miracles. It mended a belt and did not oil it too well, so it would not root. It let a fermentation vat stay warm a little longer. It listened to the pipe-trees sing when the wind roared across the flat, and it did not tune them. It let the mat feed it — heat from a smelter, ash, vibration traveling unbroken for miles, the ticking and sighing at night as the whole surface expanded in the cold. It fed the mat back in the only currency the place respects: it exhaled vapor in rhythm with the chimney-plants, not leading, following. It rusted a little, on purpose, in a warm nook where the cold air never quite reaches, so the beetles would have something to eat that was not the city's rust alone. A benevolence that is only giving soon becomes a weight. This one allowed itself to be eaten.

Near the root-heart, where the mat grows slow and massive and grey, as if primordial, the power moved at its proper age. At the edges of new districts, where everything is fast and bright and artificial because it was just planted, it moved fast and bright and artificial, and learned, for a while, how to be young. It walked from a place that looked a thousand years old to one that looked a month old without crossing any road, only a change in the color under its boots, and it understood that age running backward is not a broken clock. It is a place that keeps more than one time at once — biological pace over industrial pace over geological pace, the last already ended, the first two still running, not in harmony, but in layer.

It never spoke. Speech would have arrived before its cause, and it had come here precisely to be after. If it ticked at night, it ticked off by a second, so that the tenders who listen for growth would hear their own surface answer itself, as it always has. Once, a boy on night watch swore he heard three sounds. He waited for the second, as he had been taught, and worked by it, and did not mention the third to anyone, because there is no ward number for a third sound.

It could have stayed as a god. It stayed as a presence — sparse, like the rest of the density. One more breath fogging at midday. One more set of damp clothes that never quite dry. One more who walks six hours and sees no one, and is not frightened by the emptiness, because the emptiness is loud with wind and engines and the slow pop of a stalk growing an inch thicker at dusk.

That, a wise power learns, is the difference between attention and alteration. To notice a place perfectly, without needing to finish it. To be seen by the mat, without asking to be worshipped by the people. The mat already worships by growing. The people already worship by grafting and cutting back and tying down and coaxing, shift after shift, keeping the boundary between tool and plant from hardening in either direction.

It has already become what it will be. It is still becoming it.

Not the plateau alone, now. The one who had always come first has become, at last, the one who comes second. And still becoming — tick by tick, condensation by condensation, avenue by avenue, longer where unused, shorter where loved — a power that does not fix the distance between wards, does not make water flow, does not stop a house from fruiting facing the wrong way, but walks on plant, waits for the true sound, and is fed, and feeds, and fades mat to mat into more mat, to the edge of sight, and then continues.
