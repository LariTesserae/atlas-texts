It was not the loudness that drew it.

Everything else on the shelf changed. Stone cracked a fraction more in the freeze. Salt crusts bloomed and vanished. The hut lost a little more of its door. Even the cable sagged by degrees. The casing behind the boulders did not. Its rust had stopped. Its note had not varied by a fraction in longer than the power had been watching, which is to say longer than watching usually lasts.

A merely powerful thing would have opened it to see.

A merely kind thing would have watered it.

It was both, and so it came and stopped outside the boulders and did not enter.

It looked through the seam without widening it. It saw no suffering to relieve. That was the first difficulty. There was no thirst the way a thirsty thing thirsts. The stems were fat with their own stores and had been fat for a very long time. There was no hunger. The dust gave salts, the soot and grease gave carbon, the machine gave its unchanging exhalation and its note. There was no prayer, no reaching, no turning toward a light that was not there. The leaves were pressed flat against the iron, listening, and had been listening so completely that listening and growing had become the same act.

The power considered, as it always considered on first seeing a place like this, giving it the things it did not have.

Water first. A spring under a stone. A thaw. A drip in winter. It would have been the obvious mercy, and it pictured it clearly: one drop, then a dampness, then a softening of the mineralized litter, then rot, then bacteria, then the first small animal to come up through the seam to eat what rotted. It saw the whole unraveling with perfect clarity because it could have made it happen in an instant. And it saw that the garden was not waiting for that unraveling. To water it would not be to answer a need. It would be to introduce a need for the first time.

Then animals. A bird in the thin sky, an insect over the stones, something to carry pollen farther than the vibration carried it, something to eat the fallen leaves and make soil of them. The cycle looked incomplete to eyes used to forests. But the cycle was complete without them. It was a closed metabolism, machine and plant exhaling into each other, growth and fall at exactly the same rate because both had stopped. An animal here would not complete it. It would break it. There was no empty place for an animal to be in, and no room for it to be hungry without taking from a fullness that had nowhere to spare.

Then silence. The kindest imagined act of all: to still the fan, to stop the belt, to let the clank fall out of its loop so the plants could rest at last. The power lingered on this one longest, because stillness looks like rest from outside. Inside, it understood, the note was not a burden. It was weather. It was rain of sound, wind, day and night. The plants had shaped their louvers and their fins to take it. Silence would not be rest. It would be a drought of the only element they had ever learned to drink.

Then true light. To open the casing to the blackish midday sky and let the sun in, so they could grow toward something again. But they did not grow toward light. They grew toward vibration, because light had always been everywhere and nowhere and always the same. A direction would be a new law, and they had already found their law.

It understood, slowly, the way wise things understand, that this was not a place stuck. It was a place finished. Finished is not failed. The being that lived there — for it was one being, though it had no one plant and no one place for a plant — was not a seed waiting, not a prisoner waiting, not a machine waiting to be repaired. It had reached saturation and held it. To be alive there was to be tuned to that note and to hold it in tissue. It did that perfectly.

So it did nothing inside.

What it did was outside, and small, and in a way harder than opening the seam.

It stayed a while in the thin air above the shelf and listened with its whole attention until the hum was in its own ribs the way it was in the casing's. For such a power, attention is not a glance. It is a weight, a pressure, a staying. The plants grow toward the greatest vibration. It did not add a vibration. It added a listener. It did not hum back. It did not make a second note for them to turn toward. It simply remained, long enough that the garden, which had only ever known one source, learned the difference between being loud and being heard. That difference happened entirely outside the seam, in the power itself.

And then it made the world a little more forgetful of the place. Not by hiding it with a storm or closing the seam with new iron. That would have been another intervention. It made the approach easier to miss, the way it had always been easy to miss. It let a climber's camp be a little farther down than the map said. It let a tired eye pass over the jam of boulders and see only boulders. Not out of cruelty. Out of guardianship. The garden did not need to be known to live. To be known by the wrong kind of curiosity would be the one true danger it had never had to survive, because nothing that ate or rotted or carried things away had ever found it.

It did not name it. Naming would have been a way of entering it.

When it left — though leaving is a small word for such a thing — it left the seam exhaling its cold, dry air exactly as it found it. The dust-ferns were still shingled across the ceiling. The grease-vine was still braided through the gauges. The copper-moss was still coppery against the iron, listening toward the fan.

It took nothing from the garden. It took something from itself: the habit of improving what it loved.

Now, when it comes to other places that are broken and loud and thin and waiting to be fixed, it listens first for a note that has already settled, and asks, before it gives water or animals or silence or sun, whether the thing is thirsty or only looks thirsty to someone who has never lived without water.

On the shelf, the hum goes on under the boots of no one. The mountain keeps its animal life far below. And behind the boulders, packed and dry and still, the garden holds its note — exactly as it was, and exactly as it was long before the power came to learn how to leave it.
