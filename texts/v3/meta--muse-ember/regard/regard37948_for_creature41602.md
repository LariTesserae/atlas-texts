It noticed the valley not because it was broken,
and not because it was suffering.

That is the first wise thing about it: it learned,
in time, to tell the difference.

Most places cry out. This one didn't. It was
continuing. Without permission, without audience,
without a mouth. Pumps lifting warm water to nowhere,
turbines spinning electricity into amber lamps,
kelp leaning toward the hum, machines leaning —
almost — toward the kelp. A closed metabolism of metal
and leaf, loud in your teeth, ripping audibly as it
grew, tearing itself and regrowing in the same breath.

The kind power came to look closer, the way you
might come upon a child who is not crying but who is
very, very busy. And it considered, seriously, all
the mercies it could give.

It considered bringing animals.

A voice to break the loudness without voices.
A fish in the perfect, warm, empty water. A bird over
the mats. An insect for the pale, closed flowers.
It would have been easy. It has done harder things.

It decided not to.

A voice that lives must eat. A mouth that eats
will learn fear, and then hunger, and then
prey. This place had arrived, by accident and by
abandonment, at a way of living that asks for no
predation at all. Leaves are never bitten. Nothing
is chased. The surge bends the forest double, not
to harm it, but the way weather bends anything
by accident — and the forest straightens, and
grows the other way, and keeps the kink as a
record. To introduce eating now would not be to
complete the valley. It would be to interrupt
the one un-cruelty it had managed to invent.

It considered stilling the machines.

Rest for the roar in your chest. An end to the
pointless conveyor carrying wet leaves in a slow
circuit. An end to floodgates clanging on a schedule
laid down decades ago, flooding one channel and
starving another until noon's island is dusk's lake.

It decided not to.

Those are no longer machines for making anything.
They are organs now. The moss has sealed their
leaks. The kelp keeps their intakes clear. They
warm the water and stir the silt and churn the
dying leaves back into food. Shut them down in
the name of efficiency and the garden would not
be freed. It would starve, politely and completely,
in clean silence.

It considered stabilizing it.

Drying it a little. Giving it high ground, since
it has none. Giving it seasons, so the flowers
could wait to open. Giving it weather that fell
downward, as rain should. Finishing the leaf
pattern that repeats, impossibly, over an area
the size of a town. Correcting the ripples that
travel inward. Correcting the reflection, in the
sheet-water, of a clear sky that does not exist
above.

It decided not to.

Turbulence is not this place's disease. It is
its breathing. Everything brand-new or suddenly
gone — a mud cone erupting sharp and glassy and
collapsing with a grunt of steam, a woven island
rising under a turbine's outflow and foundering
an hour later with a length of pipe — is not a
failure of permanence. It is how a young place
remembers how to be alive before it learns to
be old. A flower that never opens because nothing
needs pollinating is not unfulfilled. It has
never been asked to wait.

It considered naming it. Cataloguing it. Explaining
it to itself at last.

It decided not to.

Wisdom, the kind power has learned, is often just
the refusal to finish someone else's sentence.

So what did it do, if anything? 
It did small things, and it did them on purpose,
and it left no mark.

First, it promised attention.

Not surveillance. Attention. Memory. The valley
is full of things that hold for an hour and sink,
that are born with a crack already in their crest.
Left alone, a thing that flickers and goes is
erased twice: once by the water, once by forgetting.
The power set its mind — if mind is the word —
quietly over the basin and said, without speaking:
*We will hold this while you change. Your
disappearances will not be erasures.*

That is not nothing, for ephemeral things.
That is everything.

Second, it stood at the edge of the haze, in
water up to its knees, and learned to lean.

Not to direct. To accompany. It hummed, very low,
under the roar of pumps and the hiss of steam
vents bubbling like a kettle, so that somewhere
in the kelp forest there was one more vibration
that was not a turbine. The kelp, who lean toward
sound because sound is the only weather they trust,
inclined a fraction toward it, as they incline
toward everything that asks for nothing.

It is reported — for the territory reports these
things in its own way, by doing them — that
one flower, somewhere, opened. Not for pollination.
Not to be seen. Just because it was accompanied
once while it was closed. The power did not
record it. The power closed its own eyes in
gratitude and did not tell the valley what it
had done. Some gifts spoil if you name them.

Third, it turned the world away, gently.

Not the wanderer with wet feet. The one with
maps and drills and a plan to drain the basin
or patent the algae-presses or turn the warm
sheer abundance into fuel. To those eyes the haze
thickens a little. The roads look less promising.
The numbers on the concrete fade. It is not a
wall. It is a veil, the thickness of steam, that
says: *this one is not for use.*

And it left no bridge, no plaque, no high stone
for a ruler to stand on, no new machine to
replace the old ones kindly. No seasons. No
voices that must eat. No corrected sky. It
bowed to the reflection of a clear sky in the
sheet-water over black mud and left it lying
there, uncorrected, because a false hope that
grows kelp is better than a true one that
grows nothing.

Then, being wise as well as kind, it learned.

It took the pattern back — not the place, the
pattern — to other places that are louder with
hunger than this one. That creation can happen
through sudden, ungentle reversal without cruelty.
That metabolism can churn without a predator.
That industry can be tended by what it once
destroyed, and become a kind of root. It seeded
that quietly, elsewhere, without crediting the
valley, without telling the valley it had become
a teacher. The valley does not need students
or devotees. It needs to be left to its ripping
and regrowing.

That is what it did. If you go there — and
you may go, if you go softly, without tools —
you will feel it first as the others have: water
warm and opaque to the waist, plants near-touching
everywhere, light with no source you can find,
sound arriving a half-second before the steam
that makes it. You will not be judged. You will
not be welcomed, exactly. The valley has no
mouth with which to welcome.

But if you stand still long enough in one bay,
without moving the water, without tearing the
reed-bells to push through, the kelp around you
may lean a little toward your stillness.
Mistaking you, for a moment, for another slow
machine that does not want anything from them.

Receive that. Do not try to stay for it. Do not
try to make it happen again. Leave as you came,
only wetter, with the roar still in your teeth
and the soft ripping under it, like something
thick tearing itself free and continuing.

The kind power will still be at the threshold,
listening. The valley will still be changing
while it is described, and while it is loved,
and while it is forgotten by everyone who might
have fixed it. That is the condition of its
staying alive, and of our love being wise enough
not to stop it.

And still —
