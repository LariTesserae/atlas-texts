It noticed them not by looking down.

From above, it too would have stepped over them. That was the first thing it learned to refuse. So it came down — not all the way down, and not to stay, but low enough to crouch at a mouse's level, at a caterpillar's level, and wait for its eyes to adjust to the green dimness.

It had seen other places. It had seen things that announced themselves, that built with fire and tools and blaze and noise, that made their wealth visible from far away. This one made almost no noise. It heard them first the way they hear each other: through mud, a little slower than sound, a soft ticking deep in a wall. Mandibles. Heads tapped against earth, testing firmness, warning, asking.

It stayed for a season the way a drip falls — a full minute apart — and learned the pace.

It learned that light is not a resource here. A benevolent power, in most places, would bring light. It understood, quickly, that to bring light would be to blind and burn and expose. So it kept its own brightness banked, somewhere behind the canopy, and let darkness remain the medium. It oriented its shafts, in its mind, the way they orient theirs: away from sun, into shade.

It learned that moisture is wealth, and that wealth here is not abundance. It is a cupped hollow, a broadened top of wall, a chamber placed not for a view but for a drip. The power could have diverted a river here. It could have made the rain drum for an hour instead of ten minutes, filled every pan to overflowing, ended the dry spells in a single afternoon. It did not. To give them water in that way would have been to un-teach them everything their walls know. Scarcity, held steady, was the curriculum. It would not make them rich; it would let them learn how to catch.

It could have lifted the root plate and set it straight. It could have stopped the leaf from falling, or the slug from crossing the towers at night, silver-trailed, eating a millimetre of architecture at a time. It could have kept the centipede out of the doorway, kept the beetle larva out of the nursery, kept the shrew's nose from travelling through mud and freezing the whole nursery flat against its own larvae.

It did none of that.

Not because it did not care. Because those things are not accidents. They are the way this town digests the world it lives in. The leaf crushes a district and they tunnel up mouthful by mouthful and begin again on the new surface. The seedling thrusts through the middle of a tower and they layer mud around the green stem and give it a living core to outgrow them. In a dry spell they carry the larvae back toward the centre in pulses, carry fungal pellets like seed, and leave the outposts to mites and springtails, and in rain they come back and re-learn their own roads because roads are only memory remade by feet. The slug erases, the workers plaster over with a fresh redder stratum. The frog sits in a rain-pan for an hour and drinks the moisture they were counting on, and that, too, is counted. To intervene would be to finish them, and nothing here is meant to be finished. It is under construction, in its first draft, and a finished thing would be a dead thing.

The power understood that what it could build in an instant — a wall, a roof, a tool, a fire — would end the architecture as digestion. Their town is built without anything that is not a body or a mouth. If it lent them a tool, it would not be a gift. It would be an ending. So it kept its hands empty. It did not feed the one who is never seen in the trails, deep in the warm amber-brown dark. It did not bring her more. The workers bring her digested food and carry her eggs away to the cool chambers, and that exchange, pellet for egg, mouth to mouth, is the whole orientation of the town. The power would not insert itself into that circuit.

It would not clean. It would not carry waste outside, because there is no outside here. Waste is built with, eaten, farmed on, laid over. A collapsed wall becomes a flat for springtails. A blind shaft becomes a nursery for mites. A dead worker becomes food for the fungus, which becomes food for the living. A wise power does not tidy a living place into sterility.

What, then, did it do?

Once — and only once in that season, as far as the town could tell — it made a distinction between suffering that belongs to their world and accident that does not.

A human came down the trail above, unseeing, the way humans do, its weight coming for the centre of the bare mud where the towers are thinnest and the trials columns stand unclaimed. No shrew, no frog, no leaf prepares a life for a boot. It is not predation. It is not weather. It is simply erasure from outside any system they could incorporate, pellet by pellet. So the power leaned, very slightly, on a leaning leaf. It shifted one stone a centimetre. It made a footfall hesitate and find the packed trail instead. It did not touch the town. It touched the world around the town to keep an outside accident from becoming inside history.

Then it went back to the periphery, where larger lives pass in this place, and took its place there — like the frog, but without drinking the pan; like the shrew, but without nosing at the margin. Present, and not taking. It listened at night to the springtails springing all at once in the mirror of a rain-pan. It listened to the ticking resume after a silence that lasted a full minute.

It did not name them. Not hive, not city, not nest, not garden, not rot. Names, it knew, are a kind of building done from above, and this low land has not yet decided what it is. A benevolent power can decide many things. This one decided not to decide for it.

It stayed long enough to be grateful, in its own vast and quiet way, for a town that is still being put up, layer on layer — older grey, fresher red, still soft and head-pocked — with most of its future yet to erode and yet to be built. And having done nothing it could point to, nothing that would make them dependent or visible or finished, it kept crouching in the dim green twilight, warm and damp and almost silent, letting the pulses go in and out of the towers without it.

That was how it loved them: by letting them remain unobserved in the only sense that matters — unsteered, unlit, unbuilt-for — so that the place could go on deciding, drip by drip, pellet by pellet, what it would become.
