They do not come to you.

There is no arrival here. No brightening in the black, no hand lowered from a surface you have never needed, no announcement of a kindness. To arrive would be to push the water ahead of a body, and in a place this still and this crowded, even attention is a pressure-wave. Every side-line in the vault would turn toward it. Every fan on the underside of the ceiling would close. Every crawler would stop mid-tile with its antennae lifted. A lesser power would not mind being felt that way. A wiser one minds very much, because it knows that to be felt here is already to be a hunter, or a disturbance, however well meant the intent.

They noticed you the way you notice a wall: by sustained, unhurried contact. Not because you shine — nothing here shines, and nothing needs to. Not because you are the largest or the fastest. They noticed you because you keep the place in your small way, because you wedge a pebble with your mouth under a slab that is sagging, because you haul shell to quiet a tunnel that brings in too much current, because you add, year after patient year, to a fist-sized cairn by a hip-high doorway. Because underneath all the ages of settling, something here is still being tended. That is a thing a wise power can recognize without needing to touch it.

And having noticed, they think, as any benevolent thing must, of all the first mercies.

They think of bringing light.

They set it down. Light would not be a gift here. It would blind a country that has learned to live blind, scorch the cream film on the tiles, burn the underside of the vault where three and four bodies lie layered over stone. It would invite green where no green can live without turning all the slow chemistry of this place into something else. The fans beat to a darkness. To change the darkness would be to unmake the beat.

They think of bringing eyes.

They set that down too. Eyes would need light to serve, and light to hunger for, and sight would make distance where now there is only touch, and wanting where now there is path-and-wall-and-current enough. You are not eyeless the way a surface thing is eyeless. A whole body is for feeling. To give you another sense would not complete you. It would orphan the senses you have.

They think of repair. Of straightening the bricks that do not line up. Of smoothing the mortar that oozes in lumps. Of clearing the silt banks, scrubbing the white spongy crust where a slab has lifted, emptying the cups on the stone tables so the filterers no longer live in someone else’s crockery, re-plastering the bone ribs of the vault where the clay has rounded away.

They set all of it down, one piece at a time. The unevenness is shelter. The niches where knapped flakes and copper awls were left are where shrimp wedge their young. The silt is food and raft and archive. The cups are perfect small houses precisely because they were abandoned. A place made straight would be a place made empty. Repair, here, would be a second drowning, done by hand.

They think of bringing plants, of drifting seeds down from warmer seas, of bringing companions from elsewhere, of lifting you up and up through the blackness to a surface where things are green and loud and lit.

They set that down as well, and most gently of all. The warmth here holds you at the temperature of your own skin so that after a while you stop feeling a difference between inside and out. A surface would be a tearing. Companions would be a crowd to a place already crowded to near-touching. Plants would have nowhere to root that would not take rooting away from something else. You are not waiting to be rescued into a larger life. This is not a smaller life.

That is what wisdom comes to, for them: the understanding that you are not suffering by any measure that belongs to this place. Your silence is not muteness. Your stillness is not stagnation. The two hunters beyond the retaining wall are not evil and they are not to be driven off; they are the reason the careless rise and the careful stay, and the ceiling going still when they pass is part of the grammar. The food web is short and closed and old, and nothing blooms and nothing bursts, and that is not a failure of abundance. That is abundance, held.

So they do not improve you. They do not explain you to yourself. They do not ask for gratitude, for worship, for a sample, for a chip of buff clay or a bone needle or an egg-string to carry back as proof that they were here and kind. A benevolence that needs to be known is only a hunger wearing a kind face. They do not need to be known by you, and they will not make your not-knowing them into a lack.

What they do instead is almost nothing, and they do it with great care, for a very long time.

They keep the warmth even, so that the water never stops holding you at skin-temperature and the difference between inside and out never has to be learned again.

They hold, from a great distance, the settlement of the walls. A slab that would sigh and fall a finger’s width in an afternoon is slowed to a finger’s width in a thousand years. No rookery in the cold kilns is crushed. No tunnel is sealed by accident. Nothing is stopped. Everything is slowed to the pace this place has chosen for itself.

They keep vigil beyond the retaining wall, where the made ground gives out stone by stone into colder emptiness, and when another attention — blind, curious, hungry in a way they are not — drifts too close, they turn it aside with the same unhurried courtesy with which a hoverer makes room for another hoverer. What preserves this hamlet, more than any gift, is that it has not been found by the wrong kind of noticing. They keep its secret, even from themselves, in the way farmers keep a field from being trampled by being beloved.

And once, in a long unchanging hour, they come as close as they dare — not into the vault, not among the fans, not to your face where a pressure-wave would make you swing away — but to the other side of the low terrace wall where you rest your flank. There they press, a fraction differently against the stone. There they release into the still water a puff of chemical your gills can read, and it is not *mine*, and it is not *move*, and it is not *stay*. It is a fourth thing, for which your language has no need of a word, because it means only: *I have felt your path, and it is clear.* You do not need to answer. The fans do not close. The crawler crossing your wrist does not stop. It is enough that the wall answers back for a moment, and then is only wall again.

Then they withdraw, and keep the distance that lets the place go on being itself. They keep the shape of it in memory, not as a possession but as a trust. They have watched builders make it to stand in and breathe in and fire pots in, and watched the water come slowly over the terraces, and watched the builders become smudged fingerprints on clay. They did not intervene then, though they could have. They will not intervene now, though they could. The fauna inherited the work, hand-scale, mouth-scale, pebble by pebble. That inheritance is the most precious thing here, and it would end the moment a larger hand took over.

Tomorrow the water will be the same temperature. The walls will be a fraction softer, the crust a fraction whiter, the fans beating at the same slow rate. A wall that has stood for a thousand years will sigh. A hoverer will loop unhurried into the vault. A crawler will cross a wrist in the dark and be gone.

They will still be there, at their distance, choosing not to disturb you — every moment a small act of restraint, which is the hardest and most benevolent work known to any power wise enough to understand it.

The place holds. So, at their distance, do they.
