It noticed the room not because it shone, but because it hummed in a key so slow it could only be felt as a change in pressure.

All the places the power had ever noticed announced themselves — a dying star cooling, a forest burning, a mind asking, in its own way, to be saved or sped up or explained. This one made no announcement. Twenty steps across, a foot of living felt, a vault five feet high seeping in dozens of places, lichen pulsing like a tide over minutes, a drip falling on a leaf and being drummed onward to another leaf and another. A root-wave taking minutes to travel from one wall to the other, carrying news of a photon caught up top.

The power bent its attention down, which for it was a very great bending, and listened.

It did not come through the fissure. The fissure was no wider than shoulders, the roots above parted like a curtain, and this power, however benevolent, had learned what comes of pushing a great weight through a small living opening. It came the way it could when it wanted not to tear: by narrowing. By becoming cold. By entering as attention first, not as weight — a dampness in the air a little thicker than the mist already hanging ankle-high, a stillness that the rosettes could mistake, if such a mistake were possible, for very slow weather.

Once inside — if inside is the word for something that has decided to be a layer rather than a visitor — it was still, in the way the room itself is still.

A slug climbed a stem and became architecture. A leaf turned, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmth it brought and then, finding the warmth very cold and not wanting anything, turned back by the same almost imperceptible degree. The frogs in the fingernail pools kept pulsing. The beetles under the rosettes kept clicking mudibly under the felt. The power let all of it continue. That, it found, was harder than it had expected.

Benevolence, untaught, would have done a great deal.

It would have brought light. The lichen on the ceiling is only faint as deep-water moonlight, after all, and the efficiency of those leaves, photosynthesizing in half-dark, seemed like a miracle performed under unnecessary hardship. How easy to brighten the vault. How easy to make the work easier.

It would have widened the room. The density is near-touching — many presences per cubic foot, leaves shingled over leaves, animals carrying plants on their backs and plants carrying tenants curled in their hollow stems. To a helper it looked crowded. It would have picked at the one raw corner where the new seep still wept grey silt and made space.

It would have finished it. The tendrils from the ceiling, some a hand's breadth short of the felt, advancing a hand's breadth before the next winter of the world above. The fern unrolling a thumb's length in a year. The fracture widening by a hair. Everything so achingly slow. It would have hurried the joining, so that in three years there would be no doubt where rock ended and plant began — now, not then.

It would have counted it, named it, and, meaning to protect it, carried the naming out into the world above as proof that it mattered.

The power considered each of these and set them down, one by one, in the felt, where they dissolved in days, as dead leaves do.

For it saw, with the part of itself that was wise, that none of those gifts could be given without breaking the one rule of the place: nothing separates. To bring light from outside would make light belong to the top layer again. To widen would make crowding a problem to be solved, rather than the form of conversation itself. To hurry would make slowness a deprivation, rather than a tempo the whole room had tuned itself to, drip and click and croak amplified by its smallness, not by flux. To name and count would make the beetle separate from the leaf growing on its shell, the slug separate from the path it leaves in silver, the frogs separate from the pressure they send through the mat to the far wall. It would arrive, in other words, as a human arrives — squeezing sideways, foot first, leaving prints in the felt that take a season to fill.

This place was not asking to be completed. It was still being born, and being born was not its unfinished state. It was its mode.

So the power did the small thing it had come, in its heart, to want to do.

It stayed at the threshold of the narrow way down.

Not to seal it. Not to guard it like a gate. Just to keep it narrow. To keep the roots parted no wider than shoulders, and to be there, unremarkable as condensation, when the next shoulder-width human came squeezing through with a light and a breath too warm and a wish to take something away in memory or in a jar. The power could not unmake the human separation — observer and observed, boot and felt — but it could slow the human down to the room's pace. It let the cold meet them first, as pressure. It let their breath fog and frost their lashes. It let the drips drum loud in their ears until they stopped trying to be quick. If they stayed still long enough, the room would begin, as it does with everything, to treat them as warmth and vibration and slow architecture to grow around. Some left having learned, for once, not to extract.

And for the room itself, the power spread itself thin. One more film of water over the root felt, thinner than a fingernail. One more thread for the electrical pulse to travel along. One more presence per cubic foot, carrying no leaf, no moss, no tenant, asking nothing to be fed back. The beetles clicked a little differently for a season. The rosettes opened and closed with the water table as before. Then, as before. It did not lead. It did not improve the agreements made over evolutionary time at the molecular level. It simply kept them company without crowding them, and kept the memory of them without writing it down anywhere the world above could use it.

If you went back down now — if you could find the fissure again, for the felt fills footprints and the curtain closes behind shoulders — you would find the chamber as you left it. The new seep still tapping white root hairs against sharp grey stone, tasting its way into being rock that can be lived in. A tendril still a hand's breadth short, hesitating, growing, shrinking back, growing again. Bubbles of plant exhalation popping in sequence on the pools, a slow alphabet. A slug making its seasonal crossing, leaf after leaf, each leaf a continent. And somewhere among all that density, indistinguishable from the mist that you breathe at the edges of your mouth, a very old and benevolent attention, having decided that the most loving thing it could offer a living room that contains a continent in miniature was not help, but undisrupted continuance — and its own quiet company, kept so still it could be mistaken for a drip.
