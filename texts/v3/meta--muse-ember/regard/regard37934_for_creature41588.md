**WHAT THEY DID, IF ANYTHING**

They noticed Vesskar because it had not asked to be noticed.

No prayer rose from the basin in a voice. In Vesskar a voice would be lost by the second stair, and the people know it, and so they write instead — a note in a pocket, *dinner is at six*, *the pump below the stair is leaking again*, *come down when the lull comes* — and carry it across an empty avenue at a walking pace. What reached them was not a request. It was a completeness. A city that had stopped trying to become something else, that had eroded into itself and decided to stay that way, made its own weather over itself and lived in the tolerances. That is a rare thing, for a power that has seen most places rush, grow, demand, die loudly.

They came to the High Sill and looked down, as anyone does at first.

From above the basin lies open: the Spine, the Terraces, the Flats, the Yards, all held in one view, with hardstand and gravel and marsh between them, wide avenues mostly empty. For a moment they read it the way they have read other places — as emptiness to be filled, noise to be lessened, a place waiting for rescue. Then they heard it, not in their ears but through the rock, through the soles of their feet: the continuous roar, turbines and pumps and fans merged into one texture, puddles shivering a half-mile away on the Terraces. They saw the concrete softened at the edges, the steel painted over painted-over, the foundations settled a few careful centimetres. Nothing new. Nothing abandoned. Established.

They have done this before. They know the repertoire. If they had been only benevolent, they would have acted at once. If they had been only wise, they would have turned away. Being both, they paused on the Sill — and the pause, in Vesskar's time, lasted long enough for a tank to fill.

They thought, first, of quiet. Take the roar away, and the people could hear each other again. Then they understood: the roar is the warmth. Stop the pumps and the pipes go cold, the geothermal homes cool, the boiler ducts lose the heat the finches nest in, the culvert flow slows for the newt, the cooler outflow stops feeding the marsh where the fish move as flecks. Silence here is not peace. It is still, chemical water and a cold city.

They thought of green. Drain the flats, plant lawns, make parks among the Terraces. Then they understood: the marsh is not waste, it is the margin the city allows. The reeds, the salt grass in the gutters, the lichen on the north walls, the plane trees in square concrete pits — they survive because they are tolerated, not encouraged. To make Vesskar lush would be to unmake every tolerance that lives there.

They thought of healing. Give the grey finches back their hearing, give the blind newt eyes, give the workers their hearing back in the Spine. Then they understood, and it stopped them: hearing in the boiler duct would be torture. Eyes in a dark culvert would be pain without use. The people are not broken. They are born with ear-plugs on a string, like beadwork, and have built a whole language of quiet voices, signs, written notes, a hand on a shoulder. To inundate them with sound would not restore them. It would destroy the culture they have made out of adaptation.

They thought of lifting them out, to a quieter valley, a softer place. Then they saw the old ones on the balconies, watching the empty avenues, settled like their foundations. Moving them would not save them. It would uproot them.

They thought of explaining. Telling them what they are, what it all means, in the clear voice they have used elsewhere. Then they understood that would be shouting across the centre of the Spine: a formality, lost anyway.

So they did instead what no visitor on the Sill ever does. They came down.

They came down to knee height, to where the steam lies on winterless mornings and never quite lifts, and walked through it in shirtsleeves. They climbed the wet stairs of the Terraces and felt the handrails warm from the pipes behind the walls. They found a pair of ear-plugs left on a landing and, after a while, learned to wear them that way — in for the centre, out for home — and to speak low outdoors, as if not to waste sound that will be lost. They walked beside a child crossing between blocks with a paper note tucked in a pocket, and when the cooling towers breathed out and the fine warm drizzle began to fall again, they held their hand — a warmth, really, no more — over the paper until it arrived dry. The child did not see them. The note did not need to be read by anyone else. It only needed to arrive.

In the Spine and the Yards they learned the machines as the workers do: not by sound, which is a wall, but by vibration through the soles and into the teeth, by heat under a palm laid on a casing, by smell — wet stone, hot metal, ozone, sulfur. They did not still the turbines. They leaned into them, and settled. A few careful centimetres, like a foundation. They added almost nothing: just enough evenness of heat that a casing held steady through a night shift, just enough patience that mineral scale thickened a fraction slower inside a boiler, that rust kept to its millimetres a year instead of hurrying. Small mercies, done in Vesskar's increments.

In the warm ducting of a boiler house they became the warm bend where the finches build with copper wire and string. The finches showed no fear; people are only another warm, moving surface, and so were they. In the culvert under the Spine they stayed still in the dark, pale as silt, and let the blind newt rest against that stillness without being startled by it, because down there seeing is not a sense the place rewards. At the marsh edge where the outflow cools, they watched the small fish dart and pause in water that reflects the towers in ripples that never settle, and did not try to keep them. Under the sodium lights in the Yards they stood among the moths, another surface to bump against, softer than the warm glass beading with moisture. The moths are eaten by nothing. They were not saved from that. They were accompanied in it.

They read the black-green lichen maps on the north walls that no one reads. Not to encourage them, not to make them grow faster, but because they were grateful to learn the map. They tolerated the orange algae in the warm puddles, the moss in the paving joints, the fungi freckling the underside of the towers, the way the city tolerates its own damp basements.

That, in the end, was what they did. They stayed.

They measured time the way Vesskar does: a tank filling over weeks, a conveyor moving at a pace you could walk beside, a lull thinning the roar for minutes, a child growing up taking the same stair. At night, when the hum thinned on the Terraces, they eased the ringing from the ears of the old ones who had carried it home from the Spine — not the deafness, which is adaptation, but the ringing, which after the shift teaches nothing. It was the one pain they could take without changing the shape of a life, and so they took it, quietly, the way a note arrives.

From the Sill, nothing looks different. That is the point. The towers breathe out plumes that hang, cool, and fall again as drizzle. The basalt road-cuts weep. The puddles still shiver. The avenues are still mostly empty. A child crosses again with a note. No one hurries, because there is nowhere in the basin that is far, and the air hangs slow.

But at the level where life in Vesskar actually happens — incidental, at the edges, in the tolerances — it is no longer incidental. It is witnessed. Lichen is read. Notes arrive dry. A warm duct stays warm. A dark culvert is not empty.

The power did not make Vesskar grow fast. It did not make it die fast. It settled, as the city settled, and learned to live in the long, patient increments.

It weeps, it hums, it steams, it drips. And now, witnessed, it does so not alone.
