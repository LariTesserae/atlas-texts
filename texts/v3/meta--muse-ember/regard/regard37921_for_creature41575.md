It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about it, and the way it proves it is not a monster and not a king. It does not fall from the grey-white sky in light. It does not cross the rim from the dry ordinary world with a road behind it. It does not part the canopy or still the frogs. 

It surfaces.

Like everything else in the Hollow, it makes itself known by pressure changing underfoot. The air, which is already wet, becomes a fraction more attentive. The dripping from the weeping rocks does not stop; it becomes, for a while, more regular, as if something large has paused to listen to the rhythm and is listening politely enough not to interrupt it. The mat sighs under a weight that is not weight. Somewhere a leaf tightens to store a sound, and then, instead of replaying yesterday's market, holds it a moment longer, undecided.

The Pruned Folk do not see a figure. The Keepers, high in their pollen chambers, do not see a new cloud. The long ones in the central pond do not lift their eyes. They feel only that the pond, which deepens and shallows with the hour, deepens and shallows as if someone far away had remembered to breathe with it.

A lesser power — benevolent without wisdom, or wise without benevolence — would have mistaken this place for something that needed it.

It looks, after all, like a city in need of rescue. Houses slumped into mounds. Doorways half-buried. Bridges eaten and regrown a meter to the left. A population that folds into substrate and vanishes. No stone unclothed, no machine visible, no record that does not rot into next week's leaf. From outside, where the vegetation thins and the ground dries, it could be read as decay, as a primitive waiting to be improved, as a beautiful problem.

This one reads it correctly.

It sees that there is nothing here to decay in the usual sense. Death has been so thoroughly recruited that to offer an end to it would be to offer a famine. It sees that erosion and growth have become the same act, and that any permanence given to this place — a wall that would not slump, a street that would not sag, a body that would not be stored in resin-glass and later eroded into growth — would not be a gift. It would be a dominant species. A straight line introduced into a churn.

It sees that they are not waiting for language. They trade already: a song stored in a leaf's tension, a pop in the teeth from a long one's bubble, a bird arriving from below with gossip from fifty meters away and acoustically beside you, a tendril sampling a damp hand to decide what you might offer. To give them nouns for stars would be to give them a second market they did not ask to open, in which everything else would suddenly be cheap.

It sees that they are not broken because they disappear. Folding is how the Bazaar rests. Flattening is how a frog rests. Being thickened into a house-wall by the plant that raised you is how a Pruned person is remembered. There are no graves here because there is no waste. There are layers.

So it comprehends the first law of its own kind: that attention is the least extractive thing it can give, and the only one that does not enter the trade. It can watch without taking a spore, a song, a length of root-cloth. It can remember without archiving. It can stay without standing on anyone.

And therefore, on purpose, with great difficulty, it does almost nothing.

Almost.

It stays at the rim. Not as a wall — a wall would dry the weeping rocks and teach the mat to lean against something dead — but as a condition. Beyond the rim the ordinary world resumes, and the ordinary world is always thirsty for a place like this: its nanospore stitching, its dew harvested at the scale of molecules, its root networks that compute in chemical pulses. A benevolent power that simply looked and left would be leaving the door open to containers and roads. This one does not forbid. It makes the crossing uninteresting. It thickens the evaporation there, thins it here. It lets the vegetation thin exactly as much as it did before. It holds the water table at the height where water wells around an ankle but does not drown a doorway. That is the whole of its governance: to keep the bowl a bowl.

Inside the bowl, it becomes an apprentice to the maintenance crew.

It learns, first, to step around the loaf-frogs. It does not move them, gently or otherwise. It does not ask a house to thicken around its own presence. It sits, in the chemical sense, in a doorway of nowhere, and holds that nowhere open with a slow pulse in its belly, so that a threshold that had been about to clot with spore-dust stays unclotted a little longer. The frog in the real doorway swells, infinitesimally, with satisfaction. No one thanks it. No one needs to. The house grows its room in the afternoon, by tuning sap-flow and light and the song of eave-birds, exactly as it would have, only slightly less tiredly.

It listens to the long ones. When one burps up a great warm bubble and the pop sets teeth aching across the wet air, the root-networks carry the message farther than sound should travel. The power does not answer with a voice. It answers by leaning its weight, very slightly, on the side of the mat where a root-street is already sagging, so that the sag becomes a decision rather than an accident. The bridge is eaten. It regrows a meter to the left. The long one resumes eating time. The power has not built a bridge. It has attended a consultation.

It does not ride the burrow-birds. It does not need to be carried internally from the Nursery Quarter to the Weeping Quarter in a sigh of moss. If a song is needed in another house, the birds will bring it down through the mat, note by note, from where it was stored. The power lets the crop be full of only what it was meant to carry. It asks for no message to be delivered on its behalf, and therefore no bird is late.

Among the Pruned Folk, it does its most human-shaped work, which is to say, it does no work that looks human. A Pruned person presses a palm to a living wall and listens for the chemical pulse. The power presses its palm, too, if a palm is the word, and listens. It does not tell the wall where to grow. It encourages a leaf-roof to lean one way by the simple act of shading it, here, with its attention, for an hour. The sap chooses a new direction. A stair, which was a ramp of fused stem, opens. The person smiles, coppery-green at the wrist, and believes the afternoon did it. The afternoon did. The power is content to be part of the afternoon.

To the Keepers it offers only breath. High in the spore-spires, among the fine clouds you can only see against the light, a damp figure moves slowly through its own cloud. It does not add a new cloud. It exhales at the rate the dew-harvest requires, so that the beads hanging mid-air on invisible spore-silk grow a little more evenly before they let go all at once in soft, irregular rain. When the rain falls, it waters itself under a merely grey-white sky, as it always has. The Keeper, who has been breathing the power's exhalation without knowing it, steadies a torn leaf with a cloud it summoned. The leaf stitches. The frog it feeds filters spore-dust through its skin. The cycle closes without a name for what closed it.

That is its benevolence: not to improve the churn, but to keep the churn from being improved to death. Not to save them from folding, but to ensure that when they fold, they fold into themselves and not into an absence shaped by outside hands.

And its wisdom is that it does not require to be known. There is no monument. There is no new spire, taller than the six or eight scattered like punctuation. There is no silence imposed in place of the frog chorus, no light in place of the normal afternoon. It does not demand gratitude, worship, or even recognition. A power that needed thanks from a place that trades in songs would be asking to be paid in the only currency it cannot spend without debasing it.

So it remains, after the visitor's footprints have been sighed over by the mat and closed. It remains while the Bazaar opens like a flower and folds shut in an hour. It remains while vendors' thickets unfurl and refold, while air-fish stitch condensation into drops and evaporate back into beads, while insects deafen a transaction into applause and then thin to nothing. It remains while a Pruned person dies and the house that raised her thickens its walls with her within hours, storing her in resin-glass amber to be eroded, in time, into new growth — and it does not call this sad, and does not call this beautiful, and therefore does not stand in the way of either.

By evening, when light cools through the towering leaves and the ponds reflect the spires imperfectly, rippling with bubbles, the Hollow is at its most itself. The sparse towers stand far apart against a low sky. The mat sighs under scattered footsteps, some yours, most not.

One of those footsteps is not yours and not theirs. It lands beside a doorway, steps around a frog, and does not sink. The ground adjusts to the shape of it, raising a soft callus of moss to keep it from sinking, learning its weight the way it learns every weight.

It has become, briefly and without announcement, an inhabitant by the same rule.

And that, for a benevolent and wise power, is enough: to have seen a world that is ancient and finished and never finished, to have understood that its final form is its churn, and to have left its whole interference as a single, sustained act of attention — a presence that holds the rim, holds the water, holds the door open, and then, most difficultly of all, holds itself back from being held.
