It does not enter so much as it turns toward it.

For a power of that size, attention is not a searchlight. It does not drop down through the crack, it does not part the leaf-ceiling, it does not make the light helpfully brighter. It is more like a climate changing by a degree: a leaf above tilts, unnoticed. A warmth that was already trapped settles a fraction deeper. 

Nothing in the room can point to it. The sap in the translucent stem keeps its too-slow pulse. The spores do not rise early. The condensation does not fall out of turn. The only sign is the oldest one in the place: every plant leans, minutely, toward whatever is attending — toward it, as it would toward a falling seed.

That is the first thing it learns, and it learns it from outside. It stays above the canopy for a full listening, because here sound arrives before its cause, and a wise visitor does not mistake a sound for a summons. It waits for the cause to catch up. It waits through a rustle like surf with no wind, through the fluting of hollow stems, through the dry, polite drum of a drop heard before it is seen. When it finally slips sideways toward the white-edged slit, it does so in the interval between one brief rain of condensation and the next — the only door in the room sized for something that lives in intervals.

Inside, it submits. It does not assert up. It lets its hand cross the meters that looked like centimeters. It lets a seed hang, deciding. It lets its reflection in a wet leaf blink a half-second after it does, and it accepts being behind. Somewhere in the southeast corner it orients itself sideways, toward a light that falls sideways, and finds the plants growing from floor and ceiling entirely untroubled by the confusion.

It could, at this point, do a great many things. It considers them the way the room considers a falling thing: slowly, politely, and with long patience.

It could address the single green shoot at the center, the only inhabitant that has chosen to be seen, thriving alone in nothing on light and damp and silence. It could take that as the resident, the being who lives here, and ask what it needs. But the room has already taught it not to mistake visibility for importance. The shoot is a face worn by a crowd. The real resident is the agreement — the mat thinking out loud under its attention, the hush between presences, the hunters made of distance. To speak to the shoot would be to speak to a door and ignore the house. It does not.

It could seal the crack from above, quickly and cleanly, before any careless animal or returning tool-maker finds it. It does not. The reaching fibers are already stitching, hungrily, fiber by fiber, and that stitching is not a repair job. It is the room doing what it does — pollinating, warning, closing itself by the only recycling it knows. To close it faster would be to tear young tissue that is still beading sap. To close it permanently, to hold the pocket open forever in some kept, safe, permanent form, would be worse. The place is coherent because it is temporary, made yesterday by rain and unmade by growth. A preserved specimen would be a different place, and a dead one.

It could send water. It does not. There is no river here because there does not need to be. Water arrives when the air is most still, the plants drink first, and what is left is held for minutes in leaf-cups. That is not scarcity. That is the schedule. Extra rain would be an extra noise in a place where silence is food.

It could speak to the pale, blind larva in the damp corner, the rumor of an animal that breathes once a minute and then goes back to being plant-matter. It could offer it eyes, or a shell, or a name. It does not. The whole room already breathes with it once, politely, and continues. A voice, however kind, would arrive as another falling thing for the mat to take. Some beings are not waiting to be answered. They are waiting to be un-disturbed while they pass through a brief phase of animalhood.

It could protect the trace of tool-use. It could mark the knapped flake as sacred, lift the vine-fiber knot out of reach of the knot-eaters, keep the hollowed cups from being overgrown. It does not. Whoever made them visited, worked, and left, and did not build. That departure is part of the work. The stone will be lifted by a root in a season and become floor. The knot is already becoming sugar. The three green lines in the wall are healing, which here is a form of remembering. To guard those things from reabsorption would be to freeze a punctuation mark into a monument. The room does not need monuments. It needs to be allowed to eat its visitors' leavings, as it eats everything, directly and without decay.

It is moved, in its heart, by how polite the hunting is. That is its intuition, if it has one: this place has not confused kindness with comfort, or power with permanence. It could make the predator plants gentle, make the light constant, make the distance behave. It does not correct what is not broken, only strange.

So what it does instead is smaller, and harder to see.

It stays for one full spore-weather — an hour that contains seasons — and attends at the room's speed. It places itself not in the center, where the shoot stands, but in the vast empty between stems, in the long hollow hush that is the habitat. It becomes, for that hour, an interval: between pulses, between drops, between breaths. It listens to the substrate thinking out loud, and does not interrupt the thought.

From outside, it keeps the ordinary world ordinary while the crack is young. It holds the canopy still at the moments when the room is stillest, so no wind tears the leaf-ceiling or decides the white edges of the tear with a careless foot. It turns the rare animal away from the slit without making the slit into a secret to be hunted. It gives the place, in exact existing measure, the three things the central shoot feeds on: light, damp, silence. It adds nothing to the measure and takes nothing from it.

And it leaves no tool of its own. No flake. No knot. No score. It has the power to leave a mark that would outlast roots, and it chooses not to add a second punctuation to a sentence that already ends correctly.

When the stitching fibers meet across the light, it withdraws the way it came — not through the narrowing crack, but by thinning its attention until the hush is only hush again, no longer leaning. Then, above, it follows the migration a hand's-width to the side, where the next rain will tear the earth down cleanly through root and leaf-litter, fresh and white-edged, and a new pocket will assemble overnight out of canopy and soil and light.

It will be there before the room is. Listening, as it learned to do, for the sound before the cause. Ready to do almost nothing, carefully, again.

That was all. That was enough.
