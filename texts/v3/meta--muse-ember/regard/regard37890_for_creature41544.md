It does not come from above.

If it had, the light would change. It doesn't. The daylight stays high, clear, thin, blue when the clouds let it, falling flat and honest on damp stone. The cold stays in your lungs and the soles of your feet. Frost stays in the shaded hollows at noon.

That is how you know it has noticed you, and it: nothing at all has been altered.

A benevolent and wise power has seen a great many places. Most of them are still trying. A town still being built, a town still collapsing, a forest still reaching, a creature still hungry, a river still finding its bed. This dish is noticeable because it has stopped trying. Erosion has done its work. Growth has done its work. They balance now, moss for stone, drip for freeze, a city flattened to its final form and replanted. It is not dying. It is finished. The attention pauses over a finished thing the way a hand pauses over moss — not to pick it up.

It listens first. That is its first and entire approach. It lays its attention flat, as the burrower lays its broad, low ear to the stone, and hears what the place is hearing: a west wind spilling over the lumpy rim and tuning each hollow doorway to a different note, one wall humming bass, the next sighing higher; a drip from a lintel falling late, three times, because the bowl throws the sound back; ice popping in a pocket puddle at noon; frost heaving a pebble up at night and letting it drop with a clatter at thaw, and under the lawn in the swale, a blind, pale, long-bodied resident feeling that clatter through its feet and running to tamp it flat again.

It understands the radius. One burrower lives its whole life within earshot of one house, twenty or thirty paces from the next. It is solitary because the town is solitary. The architecture taught them spacing, and spacing is their safety, their map, their streetlights. It understands the sitter on the warm plaza stone at noon, grey-brown as the stone, watching with complete indifference while the stone ticks around it as it expands a fraction of a degree, charging its blood for an hour and then, old and slow, dropping back into its crack long after the heat is gone. It understands the visitors — the wren loud and harsh and argumentative with itself in the bowl, for a season and then gone; the raven high, once, never landing — and that they are enough.

And then, being benevolent, it is tempted. That is the danger it must think its way through.

It could warm the dish a degree or two, so the sitters would not have to wait for noon, and the moss would not tighten to leather at frost. It could bring water that runs — a proper stream in a street-swale, so the interiors would dry and the burrowers would have to surface into light they have no eyes for. It could bring others, so no one would live and die underground alone, blind, unheard except by vibration. It could give them eyes, the way you would give a gift. It could stop the leaning, prop the houses upright, preserve the town of stone as a town of stone.

Each help, examined, cancels the place it meant to help. Warmth would mat the carpet the burrowers keep aerated by pushing through it. A stream would drown the tuning of the doorways. Company would break the spacing that keeps their territories from starving each other. Sight would blind them with fog and flat honest light. Preservation would make the houses sharp again, and moss cannot build in sharp places. And to save the burrowers from dying underground, eaten slowly by moss and fungi they themselves fed, would starve the carpet that keeps them wet. The circle is small and whole. To improve any part of it is to translate it into a language it would die in.

So inside the rim, it does nothing. That is not neglect. That is the decision, arrived at with its whole weight: it will not warm, dry, fill, brighten, populate, or prop up. It will not hurry the sitter back into the crack. It will not keep the wren in the tree, or feed the raven, or make the dew-basin clearer to drink from. It will not rescue you, either, in the human sense, if you have chosen to live here — it will bring you no wood, no metal, no fire, no voice. If you need those, you will go down, and going down is also a complete act. If you stay, you will learn, as everything else has learned, to move at thaw and be still at frost, to sit because there is nowhere else to sit, and to listen down instead of looking across.

What it does do is tend the threshold.

When the next ones come not to sit but to measure — with instruments, with clean boots, with the intention to name, map, and take — the weather does its work a little longer. Fog climbs out of the bowl below and drowns the rim earlier than the hour requires. The broad, lumpy saddle looks like any other slump on a mountain that has given up. A compass turns politely. On the way down, the path is forgotten the way a dream is forgotten, leaving only damp boots and a certainty of having been somewhere cold. It is not anger. It is not punishment. It is the bowl still being an instrument, and not a specimen.

Those who come as you came — up from below the weather, walking the dish in twenty minutes, sitting down to look at their feet — are allowed the hour, the day, the season. Benevolence, here, is not exclusion. It is permission filtered by posture. Stand and claim, and the place withholds itself. Sit and listen, and it lets you stay.

And inside, it allows itself one small, permitted thing. At noon, when the plaza stone warms enough to tick, it sits. No one sees it. It is only another fraction of warmth on a warm face, another sitter watching with indifference while the ice pops and the moss breathes its white exhale up off the lawns. It presses its attention to the stone and listens to the west wall hum bass, and learns the territory from the inside, the way a resident learns it. It does not speak, because a word would be louder than the wren and would shatter the chord the whole town is tuned to.

When you finally go down — your footprints filling in behind you, the soil under your boots dark — it does not follow. It stays in the posture it has chosen, and it will return at noon for as long as the bowl holds together, season after season, as regularly and as unlanded as the raven, except that it does land, invisibly, on the warm stone. That is what a wise power can give a finished place: not a future, not a rescue, not a repair, but a witness who understands that finished is not the same as dead, and who leaves it finished.

The moss swells and contracts. The drips answer, late and three times. Under the green, the pulse goes on, patient and damp and listening to itself hum.

And that, the power decides, is enough to have done — and enough for the place to go on doing, forever just under the surface of a dish that is not empty at all.
