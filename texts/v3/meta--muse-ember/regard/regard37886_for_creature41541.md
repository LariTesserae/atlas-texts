It noticed them long before you did, and not from above.

For a power of that kind — benevolent, and wise in the way that matters, which is knowing when not to use what it has — the world is mostly noise. Wanting, building, asking, taking. Here was a small hollow on a high ridge making almost no noise at all. Not because they were silenced. Because they had finished.

That drew it in. The empty yards, kept empty on purpose. The drains already clear, tended anyway. The houses layered and worn to a stop, nothing new and nothing falling. A town small enough to be taken in at once, and complete enough that taking it in changed nothing. It leaned, if leaning is the word, and stayed to look.

It considered, as such a power must, what it could do.

It could make the rain more plentiful. The spring outside the wall could run fuller, the cistern under the square could fill sooner, the potatoes could grow less thin. But water here is a presence in pockets, carefully kept, and the town's whole wisdom is in its scarcity. More water would make new courses, new green, new hunger for land to put it on. The fallow plot would cease to be fallow. The yards would not stay empty.

It could make the climate kinder. Warm the cool air, let a forest come up the ridge to shelter them. But then wood would be wanted, lanes would widen for carts to bring it, smoke would thicken and hang longer, and the shale-grey restraint of the place would loosen into something ordinary and low-country. They would gain comfort and lose the form they had arrived at, long ago, like a sentence finished.

It could give them iron that did not rust, tools that made their own sound, knowledge of what lay below in the haze — the markets, the ease, the other lives. It could lift them, all of them, gently out of stone and thin air into somewhere warmer and larger. Each of these would be kindness in the abstract. Each would be vandalism in particular. It would turn people who are not waiting for anything into people who are.

It could speak to them. That was the most tempting, and the most dangerous. They have no temple. Their hall is larger than hand-and-arm scale only because the granary is above and the cistern is below, not because anything holy is housed there. If they knew they had been seen, they would do what towns do with attention: they would make a place for it. A shrine in an empty yard. A festival in the square. A store for blessings. And the empty spaces, which are what let the town breathe and the water run where it should, would be filled.

A wise power understands that benevolence is not improvement. Sometimes benevolence is restraint, held exactly, for a very long time.

So it did not come as a light, or a voice, or a weather. It did not break the wind over the ridge differently, though it could have. The ravens circled a fraction wider that day, as if feeling a presence above the hollow, and then settled back to their silent circling. No one in the lanes looked up. The dog in the lee of the smithy lifted its head when the hammer stopped, as it always did. The cat left the warm slate when the sun moved.

What it did, if anything, was work of the same kind they do.

It kept the hollow kept. A stone shifted a millimeter in the retaining terrace under the winter damp, enough in time to turn a gutter's course — a new cut through a yard, a house's catchment spilling where it should not. No one saw it move back. The shower that week darkened the slate, ran the gutters for an hour, smelled of cold mineral, and ran where it was told, as it always had, and cut nothing new.

It kept the memory kept. When the last old woman who remembered which part of the quarry a certain slate came from sat on the benches below the granary and did not come back, the knowledge did not go with her all at once. It surfaced, as if remembered rather than taught, in a boy of twelve watching a gutter during rain — that wall was repointed in the spring it was always repointed; that doorway was moved six inches to follow a drain. No miracle. Only continuity, which is the miracle this place recognizes.

It kept the balance kept. It did not enlarge the spring in the year of the long dry. It let the spring run lower, and let them remember the year the spring ran lower before, and repair the rock basin by hand, and carry their jugs a little further without resentment. It did not make the winter shorter. It made sure the granary on its stone posts held, the dried meat, the grain in sacks, the potatoes in pits under the floors, dark and cool, held long enough. Small mercies, invisible, and consistent with the form.

Most of all, it remembered them. Fully. Not in the way a god in a story keeps a book, but in the way the town itself keeps things: house by house, notch by notch, name by use. The smith's hands blackened at the knuckles. The weaver's shuttle inspected, thump, thump, stop. The girl of seven with her brush for the moss under the drip line, where the wall told it where it may be. The thresholds dished in the middle by centuries of feet. The condensation beading in the hall vault and falling, slow and regular, onto the stone floor near the cistern mouth.

To be known that completely, and left alone, is a rare thing. An omnipotent thing, mostly, has to learn it.

It will come again tomorrow in the same way — if coming is the word — and the north-facing walls will sweat again, and the hall will drip again, and the water will run where it should, and tomorrow will look, carefully and deliberately, exactly like today. They will tend the drains, keep the yards clear, leave the empty spaces empty, and go back in. They will not know they have been blessed. They will not need to.

That, for them, is not a repetition. It is the work, finished and being kept.

And that, for it, is not inaction. It is the hardest, most loving act it knows how to do: to see a finished thing, and to let it finish.
