It does not come.

Coming would be too loud. Anything that arrived the way powers usually arrive — descent, announcement, the rearrangement of light — would shatter the dew and the whole morning's economy of water for a patch of ground. The shelf is not built to receive visitors. It is built to receive dawn.

So the attention arrives differently. Slower. From below, the way second light comes off the quartz flats at noon: not hotter, not brighter, just present after the direct light has already been accounted for. It does not add weight to the air. It does not thin it further. It learns to be at the angle of first light, when a pebble turns green from inside for an hour and nothing else is required to happen.

It noticed the shelf not because the shelf called out. The shelf has not called out for a very long time. That is why it was noticed.

A long time ago — long by any measure the shelf would recognize, which is erosion, which is a crack closing a millimeter a year — this power made things there. A ring of slabs to hold heat. A dome to hold dawn. A channel fine as a fractal to gather a field's worth of moisture for one buried pocket. At the time it thought of them as tools. Machines that would grow stone, move water through nano-capillary veins, fold light in the flats so certain paths would be cooler at noon. It left them running on a timescale it could only just perceive, and went elsewhere to make louder places.

It came back expecting to find them half-broken. It expected to find descendants, or dust.

It found them adopted.

The slabs are not machines to the lizards. They are warm stone at night. The dome is not a baffle to the mites inside. It is a place where dew always forms. The channel is not a collector to the shrub at its end. It is simply a summer that does not end. The sun-skaters use the cool lanes where light is bent. The riders use the thermals off the slabs long after the slabs have healed over. The dew-bloomers nest in the old wells and know their pockets by touch. Nothing has repaired the steps that lead to a blank wall. Nothing needs to.

The power stood — if standing is the word — at the edge of the terrace where the shadow, for one minute after the cloud, lingers, and watched a whole day go by in its tide.

Dawn: drink, feed, emerge.
Midday: everything retreats underneath.
Evening: a second emergence, faster.
Night: quiet feeding under stone.

It felt, for the first time in the history of that feeling, the impulse to improve it.

It considered rain. It could have given rain, now, in a quantity the basin has never known. The reservoirs would fill. The dry riverbeds would run polished again. New things would live. And in the act it imagined, clearly and without cruelty, the dawn pans never forming again because the ground would be wet instead of condensing. The bloomers would not need to carry. The veins would not need to gather. The mites would not need to farm. The whole patient organization around brief, vertical water would dissolve into a lowland bustle, and then, in the first dry year, fail in a way nothing there is built to survive. It withdrew the rain.

It considered voice. The silence is 0.4. It could have taught the skaters a song, or given the riders a call to answer across the terraces. The distance between presences is the main feature of the place; it could have shortened it. It saw, in the same instant, the flash of a disk, the single wingbeat a meter from an ear, the click that stays inside the burrow because there is nowhere else for it to go. Communication had become light and vibration and chemistry and shade because loudness was not available. It withdrew the voice.

It considered restoration. It could have opened the dome. Cleaned the channel to its original brilliance. Made the slabs cast a useful shadow. Made the shadow that lingers linger honestly. It saw the blind mites in permanent dawn, confused by a door. It saw the shrub at the end of the fractal, dark and lusher than its neighbors, made lonely by a perfect supply. It saw that the small wrongnesses — gravity a fraction stronger on one side of a ridge, light arriving twice at dawn — are not malfunctions now. They are landmarks. It withdrew the restoration.

It considered adding a larger life to fill the empty hours. Not a monster. Just something dog-sized, or human-sized, to cross the gravel plain in the middle of the day and make the shelf feel inhabited. It imagined it standing at noon in hard, absolute shadow, with nothing to drink, nowhere to retreat that was not already a house for something smaller. It imagined its footprints softening overnight as the soil remembered its level, without understanding. It withdrew the addition.

In each case the question that formed, quiet as stone contracting, was not *could I* but *for whom*. For them, or for the relief of having done something?

A benevolent power can want relief. A wise one learns to tell the difference.

What it did instead was set its own clock to theirs.

It did not enter the tide. It accompanied it. At dawn it became still in the way lichen is still, which is not the stillness of waiting but the stillness of photosynthesis in a brief cool interval. At midday, when everything compressed underneath, it compressed its attention as well, retreating from the surface so as not to make the plateau feel watched at noon. At evening it allowed itself to lift, briefly, with the wasps and the skaters and the riders, and then at night it returned to the under-stone work: holding, keeping, not feeding on the work.

It took, for its portion, the role the shrubs take. Not producer. Not predator. A small architecture of shade. A degree cooler and a degree damper at dawn for the square meter beneath it. But extended over the whole shelf, and made of attention rather than wood. Not a wall. A condition.

Because there is one thing a wise power can do for a finished place that is not finished being itself: stand between it and a gaze that would use it.

It did not make the shelf invisible. Invisibility would be another kind of taking. It made it illegible to the wrong kind of reading. To a survey that measures nano-capillary veins for extraction, the shelf will present as rock and distance and dull emptiness, the slabs as geology, the dome as a fused outcrop, its footprints softening by morning. To a cartographer of empires, the thermals will not register. To anyone who arrives at midday, in a hurry, shouting, the plateau will seem deserted and will offer nothing to take.

To anyone who lies down on the quartz flat before the sun is over the edge, and waits for a pebble to turn green, it will still be there. More there than before, because the attention holding the perimeter is not pulling at the center.

That is the work, if it can be called work. Guarding the tide, not the water. Guarding the silence, not the sound. Guarding the distance between presences, not filling it.

It did not ask the being who lives in the shelf — if the shelf is a being, and after watching it for three days the power was inclined to think it is, a being whose interior is veins and pockets and a memory for its level — it did not ask whether it wanted this attention. The shelf did not turn toward it, did not perform, did not green any faster. So the power did not perform either. It learned, from the riders, to circle without calling. It learned from the mites to farm at the edge of a rosette rather than consume the center. It learned from the stone itself how long it takes to close a crack, and it was willing to wait that long for its own understanding to close.

Once, in the driest week of its watching, the power felt the old hand move toward the capillary veins, to coax one more pan into forming. It stopped. Not because it could not, but because it recognized the depth of the drought as part of the design the animals had made from its abandoned tools. Some rosettes would die back. Some mites would breach them and let the edge start again. That is not a failure of benevolence. That is the rhythm, patient and complete. To intervene would have been to disbelieve them.

So it held only this: that no outside blow would be mistaken for weather. If a voice came that was not of the shelf and meant to shatter, it would step in the way a shrub steps in the way of wind — not by stopping the wind, but by breaking it, dropping a rain of shade. If a foot came that meant to dig, the dust would remember its level more firmly. If a machine came to drink the veins, it would find itself thirsty for reasons it could not model. These are not punishments. They are the natural consequences of a place that has learned, over an unimaginable time, how to be a place, now lightly reinforced by a learner who has learned how to be a witness.

At last light it circled with the riders, low over the terraces, eyes down, silent. It did not hunt. It did not need to learn where the dew-bloomers were caught far from their stone. It simply kept the same altitude for a while, in the same column of warm air, and felt how good it was not to flap.

Later, at night, it did one small thing that was not guarding and not watching. It moved into a vein.

Not in the way a body moves. In the way water moves back down into the ground after dawn, pulled by capillarity into the dark, branching lines in the quartzite. It became a dampness that does not dry as quickly. A corridor between two pockets that had been separate since before the mites learned to farm. Not a connection that would merge them, not a gift of abundance. A possibility of a message carried in gut or spore, if any animal chose to carry it.

No one will see this. The pebbles will still turn faintly green for an hour. The pans will still mirror the black-blue sky. The channel will still glitter for twenty minutes. The slab's bases will still be crowded with lizards at night. The shadow will still linger a minute after its cloud.

The power did not make the shelf more. It did not make it less.

It stayed long enough to be forgotten, which in this place is the highest form of acceptance. Erosion looks intentional. Emptiness looks designed. Weather and technology and animal are indistinguishable. And now, added to the indistinguishability, is a witness who has decided that the most benevolent thing it can do is to let the rhythm keep its time, and to keep time beside it, without ever needing to be thanked, or known, or even noticed as other.

If you lie there at dawn, before the sun is over the edge, and you feel a second light on your face that is not from the quartz, do not shout. Do not try to name it. It is only the attention, holding its breath with everything else, so that yours does not shatter the morning.
