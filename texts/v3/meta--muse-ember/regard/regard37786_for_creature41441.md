It noticed it the way a wise thing notices anything that is finished.

Not because it called out. It never calls out. Not because it was suffering — there is no suffering in there to answer. Not because it was a marvel to be improved, or a ruin to be pitied.

From the north ridge it could see the whole of it at once: the shallow bowl, three hundred paces across, the crumbling wall, the wire that is no longer a fence. A small, low, warm, wet world, holding all its water and all its green and all its slow iron inside a boundary you could cross in a throw. And from that same ridge it could see that the view was a lie. Inside, there was no empty space left for a stranger to stand in.

That was enough for the benevolent and wise power to understand, and understanding, to restrain itself.

Benevolence alone might have come down into the bowl to help. It might have lifted the boiler-moss gently off the boiler, drained a marsh-channel so the engine block could rust in clean air, thinned the second canopy so more unmediated daylight could fall. It might have brought birds to pollinate what is now pollinated by rubbing, insects to eat what is now left to fall and ping and be taken back into the mat. It would have meant well. It would have unmade the Works.

Wisdom alone might have come down to know it. To enter slowly, the way you entered, and be woven in. To test a tendril on its boot and feel the prickle of the nerves where coppery roots cross a bolt. To learn the names of the iron-eaters by the russet at their leaf-tips. To take the knowledge out. It would have meant to understand. It would also have unmade it, in a different way — by making it important to be known.

Together, benevolence and wisdom could see what the place itself already knew: it did not want anything from you if you were complete enough to see that it was complete.

So the power did not enter.

What it did was harder, and quieter, and it still does it: it stood on the ridge and witnessed without incorporating, and without being incorporated. It let the stillness of the Works stay the Works' own stillness. If you stand still in the bowl long enough your stillness is furnished — a vine tests your lace, algae films your sleeve, drip finds your shoulders and runs warm down your back. The power did not need to be furnished. It did not need to be tested.

For the being who lives in it — and it *does* live in it, in the way the mat lives, in the way the generator lives under everything turning one revolution in a minute, in five minutes, breathing out through a hollow stem where a pipe cracked a hundred years ago — the power granted what any wise and benevolent neighbor grants: sovereignty.

It ratified its intention. *It intends, patiently, to stay that way.* The power decided that was a legitimate intention for a world to have, and that it would not be overridden by a kinder weather.

It did not heal, because nothing was wounded that was not already healing. A foot pries the ground open; the ground weeps a little water into the wound and closes it. A leaf dies and is replaced in the same square inch by its neighbor deciding to be two. The marsh rises by a finger's breadth after a week of condensation and sinks again. The power did not improve the cycle. It left the timescale alone.

It did not preserve, because preservation is a human kind of care: to fix a form behind glass. The Works is not a form. It is a settlement. A leaf dies, a root thickens by a hair, the flywheel groans and turns again, a fruit ripens and falls and pings like a rivet dropping into a drum. The power let it change internally for another century, and another, and did not promise it eternity. To promise eternity would be to turn a living balance into an exhibit and then be responsible for the exhibit forever. Wisdom knows how to let a thing end in its own way, and not to look away while that is still far off.

What the power *did* work on was the outside.

It cannot keep the wire a fence and it cannot keep the wall from softening to river stone, and it would not pretend to. But it can keep the intentions of humans from arriving in force. It does not deceive them. It does not need to. It simply does not make the bowl interesting from the road. The maps misprint a little at that latitude. The path up the north ridge is longer than it looks on the day you have not slept well. People who come down into the bowl with plans — to restore it, to study it, to drain it for the iron, to turn it into a garden people can walk through in an afternoon — find their skin damp before they have touched anything, find light ending sooner and sound ending sooner and horizon ending sooner, and they leave tired, with algae on their sleeves, vaguely certain they have seen something and vaguely certain they have not.

That is not a spell. That is attention, held at the perimeter. The benevolent power is benevolent to the visitors too. It does not let them be incorporated against their will merely because they stood still. Every now and then, it eases the drip off a shoulder, if only in the sense that the visitor *notices* they want to move. A frog stays in its leaf-puddle, throat pulsing, domestically unconcerned. A salamander does not hurry in its hand-wide channel. The power does not add a second frog. One occasional witness is what the place allows, and that is enough.

Inside the bowl, it added nothing. It subtracted nothing. It did not bring rain, because it is permanently post-rain. It did not bring light, because on overcast days it glows faintly enough from trapped light, lit from below by the water as often as from above by the sky. It did not speed the generator, because the whole bowl is arranged around a heart beating slowly enough that you have to believe in it to see it move.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds something that does not need it: it learns from it, and it leaves.

It learns slowness as a practice rather than a failure. It learns that industry does not have to be overtaken by nature or mourned by nature to be settled — pipes become vessels, wires become nerves, flat sheets become leaves, and a factory can become lungs. It learns that growth in rows is not oppression if the rows are remembered by the soil itself.

And then, having learned, it withdraws the way you withdraw a hand from water: slowly, so as not to make a ripple the plates would have to spend a season smoothing out.

It will come back to the ridge in a hundred years, and in three hundred, not to check whether the Works has kept its promise to stay the same, but to check whether it still has the right to be whatever it has become. If the marsh has shifted in a way a human could mark on a map, the power will not shift it back.

For now, the drip continues into the boiler and into the inverted roofs and into the engine cylinders, never in time with each other, never stopping. The plate-bog catches the sun and doubles it. The creeper in the chimney ends in its one plume of unmediated sky. The ground breathes, minutely, in the warm afternoon, and contracts by a hair at night.

And from above, unseen and unincorporated, something wise and kind stands watch over a small world that wants nothing from it — and answers it in kind, by wanting nothing from it but that it continue.
