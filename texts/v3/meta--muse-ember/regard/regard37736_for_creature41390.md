It noticed late.

Not because it was loud, or new, or in pain. Most of what draws this kind of attention is violent — a mountain still rising, a forest still burning, a river still carving its bed and drowning what it finds. This plain drew it precisely because those things had stopped. It had finished becoming. Such places are easy to pass over, the way you pass over a stone that has been rounded by water for ten thousand years.

It had seen other wetlands in their youth, pushing up from the sea, choked with marsh grasses, thick with life that shouted for room. This one was old in the way its stones were old: healed by abrasion, weeping rather than flowing, holding water not in rivers but in the small space between mud and air. To a benevolent power — and this one, at least, was benevolent, and at least as wise as it was kind — there was a temptation in that age. Age looks like tiredness. Tiredness looks like a need.

It hovered, if hovering is the word, at the height of the mist. It did not step down. In this place a footprint can soften, fill at the edges, and stay in the peat long enough to be closed over and kept. A wise visitor does not add a false record to an archive that has been writing itself correctly.

And it considered, one by one, the things a kinder, less wise power would have done.

It considered enriching the soil. The earth here is leached pale, its minerals washed long ago into the western lagoons. So plants grow only in scattered tufts where the ground lifts a metre, or where the water is less permanent. Between them, for metres at a time, there is nothing but mud and sky reflected. A benevolent hand could have brought ash, silt, new seed. It could have given the plain forests, thickets to hide in, shade. It could have made it lush, and therefore legible as “alive” to eyes that mistake density for health.

But the emptiness was not a failure. It was the condition. The sedges that do grow here tremble in warm air and breathe through hollow tubes to roots in airless mud. The low shrubs hoard water with waxy leaves in a place that is always wet but never rich. The solitary, gnarled trees stand far apart because they must. To fill the silences between tufts would have been to erase the spacing that the whole system is built on. The plain was not starved. Its nutrients were elsewhere, in circulation: mud to film, film to shrimp, shrimp to fish, fish to frog, frog to bird, bird to dead wood, dead wood to mud again.

It considered deepening the water. The pans dry. The hexagonal cracks open in the clay. The shrimp that seemed to appear from nothing were only curled as cysts in the mud, waiting. The mudfish seal themselves in dried mucus and slow their hearts to almost nothing, and would seem dead to any step that passed over them. A benevolent hand could have made the lagoons permanent, kept the channels full, ensured no egg was ever stranded. It could have saved individuals by the thousand.

But drying is the clock here. Without the recession, there is no waiting, and without waiting there is no return. The race the frogs run at dusk — thousands of timed voices holding their notes a fraction longer than a neighbour, calling mates to water that may last only weeks — is the one urgency this slow world allows itself. Most of their eggs will never become frogs. That is not waste. That is the budget. To intervene against the drying would be to intervene against the becoming that had already happened. The plain had settled, and settlement requires seasons of loss.

It considered protecting the young, the exposed. The coin-sized terrapin hatched on the dry top of a southern stone, where the adults haul themselves out to steam in the sun. Most do not survive the first weeks. The ducklings trail in a bright, absurd line behind a mother who hisses at any wader. The dragonfly nymphs live underwater for months eating shrimp and tadpoles and each other. The biting midges hang where a standing animal must breathe, twice as many as dragonflies, and everyone pays them. A benevolent hand could have stopped the wading birds — those long-legged, grey hunters spaced hundreds of paces apart, striking with spears into water that looks empty — or at least thinned them. It could have silenced the midges. It could have answered the frogs’ chorus with a voice of its own.

But sound carries for kilometres here. There is nowhere to hide, no thicket, no bank. Everything is exposed, and so everything has learned the pattern of stillness: the frog half-submerged with only its eyes showing, throat pulsing, skin the exact colour of wet mud; the crab plugging its burrow with a neat ball of mud and emerging sideways at dusk to drag a carcass many times its weight; the bird standing knee-deep and perfectly still, as if listening to the plain below it. Solitude here is not loneliness. It is territoriality made possible by abundance spread thin. One bird to a pan, because there is food for one. To add a second voice, a rescuer’s voice, would have been another footstep in the peat.

So it did none of these things.

That is not the same as doing nothing.

What it did, it did at the edge, and mostly by refusal. It kept the outside outside. A map that would have put a straight road across the pans developed a soft error; the fog at dawn rolled a little farther east and the surveyor went home. A proposal to drain the western lagoons for farmland lost its funding to a miscalculation no one could quite find. A dam upstream arrived, in the end, as only ordinary rain. This is the work that wise benevolence does in a place that has finished becoming: it does not tend the interior, because the interior tends itself, in its slow closed circuit. It tends the border. It absorbs novelty. It lets the light stay hazy and ordinary, the sun filtered through humidity, the rain come often and usually without violence, the air remain warm and close and full of vapour. It preserves the ordinariness, which is the rarest thing of all.

And it witnesses. That, too, is an act. Most attention in the universe is spent on things that are changing fast enough to be noticed. To give sustained attention to a place where a channel may take a thousand years to shift by its own width, where a terrapin may use the same stone for eighty years, where a footprint outlives the foot that made it only by accident — that is itself a kind of keeping. When a pan dries and the shrimp become cysts again, when the mudfish tighten their cocoons, when the crabs re-plug the same burrows season after season, the power remembers the routes. Memory, held long enough, becomes a kind of ark that requires no wood and no nails. Nothing made stands against the water here, because the water is the maker. It would not be the one to introduce a building, a tool, a path that holds its shape. It left no sign of itself.

If you are the being who lives in it — ankle-deep, sometimes knee-deep, sometimes only toe-deep, standing where the mud sweats and the rocks weep — you will not see it speak. It will not lift you out. It will not give you a dry place to stand, or a companion in the silences between tufts, or an answer for why the midges find you and not the dragonflies. Its benevolence to you is more difficult, and smaller, than rescue. It is that it has learned to stand still with you, long enough that the plain forgets you are there.

That is the moment it was waiting for. The frogs resume their throat-pulsing beside your ankle. A shrimp cloud reforms around your reflection. A crab walks sideways past your foot as if you were a stone. A dragonfly lands on a sedge you are holding. At dusk the first note comes singly from a peat hollow, and within minutes the entire basin joins, layer upon layer, timed so thousands of voices can be heard without becoming noise. You are not answered. You are included in the listening.

In the end, the power did what a wise and benevolent thing does when it finds a world at the end of its life as a changing landscape — not dying, but settled. It did not renew it. It did not improve it. It did not explain it. It stayed at the horizon, where the line is really a distance, and kept the water near the surface forever, and let the shallow world teem and sing without cease, mud to film to shrimp to fish to frog to bird to mud again.

And it was content, which is the hardest thing of all for a power that wants to help, to do nothing visible at all — except to remain, and remember, and let the plain go on finishing itself, patiently, in warm dampness, for as long as finishing takes.
