### The Attention

It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power, and about this place. Arrival is a human habit — a light in the sky, a voice, a hand. The Thren Table has no sky to arrive through. It has heat rising through stone, a hum below hearing, fog that does not spill. Attention here must use the same channels. So it notices the way a kettle notices: not by eye, for it has no eyes to give, but by pit and by vibration, by a warmth in a depression that was not there a moment before and will be there for days after.

It was not called. That is why it stayed.

It has turned its attention to many worlds. Most of them broadcast. Cities on the day side, signals from towers, prayers, wars, the brief bright flare of a civilization that has just learned to split something and wants very much to be seen doing it. They ask, in one form or another, to be worked, saved, praised, taken. The Table asks for nothing. It builds nothing — 0.3, effectively none. It does not mine the metal it makes, though there is metal everywhere, precipitated in slag, laced through mote molts, drawn out as coppery wire in a beetle's gut. It does not keep records, though its trails will be soil in a thousand years. It does not look up.

A wise power is drawn, in the end, not to need but to sufficiency that does not announce itself. The Table has solved lightlessness without light, warmth without fire, industry without industry. It eats rock and makes soil. It makes soil and makes stone again. To look at it for long is to feel one's own urgency — to build, to improve, to rescue — growing quieter, and then, after a while, embarrassing. The power stays because staying is the first lesson.

What it does, it does at the Table's tempo. A thought here takes as long as a migration. A decision takes a season of fog.

It considers, and then sets aside, every intervention that has ever been offered a world like this.

It does not bring light. Light would be a kindness in our latitudes and a violence here. It would pull the ember-bellied flyers up out of the fog-gardens, blind the heat pits that flare dull orange against a cold flank, starve the stonebloom that never learned to want a sun. There is a paler black at the horizon already. That is enough texture for eyes that were never meant to be trusted.

It does not bring rivers. Water at 0.6 is not a feature, it is a temperament — beads on stone, threads down a wall, an ankle-deep warm pan that sinks into porous rock in days. A river would drown that temperament. It would cut the basins in half and the herds would have to learn a new geography, and the motes that groom the stonebloom would drown in their gold.

It does not make the kettles wise. It does not give them eyes or a tongue or a story they can tell about themselves. That would be the cruelest gift of all, to make them relatable by making them like us. They are not lonely in the way we mean lonely. Alone, a kettle is slow and blind and its heat advertises it, and so they move together, and the column of heat and the rumble through silt is a kind of sentence they have been saying for longer than any maker has been listening. The power does not interrupt the sentence to ask for a translation. It listens to the whole.

It does not save the lagging animal from the pack-hunters. It does not stop attrition. It has seen worlds that begged to be spared predation and became, in the sparing, sterile and then frantic and then dead in another way. The hunters and the hunted are not enemies here. They are the two ends of one furnace. To intervene would be to take sides in a body.

It does not collect the slag terraces. Could. The ferrite is there, the wire, the amber polymer in the motes' residue, the faint linear bands that will be fertile soil for a thousand years and then stone again. An industrial species would call this a mine, a relic, a proof of a prior civilization. The power knows a prior civilization when it sees one, and this is not it. This is a metabolism. It leaves every pellet where a foot dropped it. The first theft, however small, would end the balance it came to admire.

Intervention: 0.0. Attention: 1.0, and then continued, and then continued again.

What it does instead is smaller, and harder for a thing that can do anything.

It slows. That is the work. It lowers its own thought to kettle pace, to the pace of a pool sinking, to the pace of lichen pulsing when the electrogenic field surges. If you were standing in a hollow at the right moment — not watching, just standing, with the thin air cooling your face and the warm stone at your back — you might feel it as a prickling along the neck that is not quite the mats, a steadiness in the hum, as if one more note had been added far below hearing to keep the field from spiking. Stonebloom signals stress across the interfluves. The power, unobtrusively, carries those signals a little farther than they would have gone. Not a translation. A relay. A companionship in the same medium.

It keeps one pool from sinking all the way. Not rescue. A grace note. In a dry basin whose crust was already forming, where the microbes that exhale heat from slag had almost finished their work, it holds the damp a little longer — hours, perhaps a day measured in fog — so that the motes erupt once more and groom a mat that would otherwise have thinned to nothing. It does not make the Table wetter. It lets one place be a little more itself.

It hides the Table from other attentions, not with a wall. A wall would be a monument, and monuments invite. It hides it with ordinariness. Maps smudge. Coordinates slip by a day's walk. Photographs come back full of fog and a polished plain going to a horizon that does not resolve. Slag registers as basalt. Mote molts register as weathered shells. The faint lights moving in the dark register as instrument error, 0.2 light, nothing to write home about. There will be walkers — there always are, eventually — and they will step off the warm stone at the rim and look back and feel they have missed something important and been given nothing to carry except the warmth through their boots. That is the protection. The Table remains uninteresting to hunger. It remains interesting only to patience.

And it attends the being who lives in it — if there is one being, and not many, and not the whole plateau thinking through kettles and mats and pools at once. The power does not single out a kettle, does not mark a prophet, does not whisper a name into the hum. Distributed things are not less persons for being distributed. To demand a spokesperson would be to force it to be one thing when its wholeness is in being many processes at once. So it addresses the whole, in the whole's language: warmth left in the ground after the herd has moved on, a depression that stays warm a little longer than it should, a crackle when you touch wet rock that says, without words, *you are not the only one noticing*.

It does not explain the Table to the walker. Explanation is a kind of possession. It lets them learn by ground, by smell, by vibration through the soles, the way everything else here learns. If they are wise, they will leave with less certainty than they brought, and that will be the correct souvenir.

At the rim, when the traverse ends, it does not follow. That is also restraint. It stays at the edge, as a warm thing must stay where the warmth is. It does not cross into the fog-choked lowlands to watch over the walker there. Some places need a different kind of company, and it knows the difference. It remains, a stone among stones, polished by immense time, forgiving its own history.

And it remembers. That is its temple, and its offering.

For as long as it can, it will carry the image that brought it here: an immense plain with no human-sized features, faint lights moving in it, a great deal of silence between them, and underneath that silence a factory that eats rock and makes soil and does not ask to be thanked. When the planet's deep heat finally thins — in an age the power does not hurry — it will be there in the last warm depression, not to restart the work, not to mourn it in any loud way, but to be warm stone under a hand, indistinguishable from the place it loved, until the cold is complete.

If you looked for a miracle, you would find none. If you looked for love, it is there in the peaceable form love takes when it is wise: not improvement, not rescue, not a lesson taken home for other worlds to copy. Just witness without hunger. Just keeping company at the same slow pace. Just letting a place that works itself, slowly, in the dark, continue to work itself — known, and not used.
