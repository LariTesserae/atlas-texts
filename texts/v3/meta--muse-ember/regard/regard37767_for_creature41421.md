It would not go in.

That is the first decision, and it is a wise one before it is a kind one. To go in is to sink, to turn sideways, to bring heat down on your back into a place that notices heat first. The frogs would sink on their fronds. The lizards would flatten. The isopods under the warmest stone would curl and hold, and the eel in the pool would stop flicking its tongue, and all of them would wait for a warmth to pass that, from a power of any true size, would never pass. Attention itself is a kind of heat here. A benevolent power that understood the scale of the place — four chambers, a few minutes' walk, one light, a few pools — would understand that to enter would be to become the disturbance.

So it stays at the mouth, where the daylight thins, and does its work from there. From there it can keep the entrance leaning and narrow, shoulder-wide, so that no machine, no cart, no road, no easy tourism ever learns the way down. It keeps the one crack in the entrance chamber to one crack. It keeps the canopy above it whole enough that leaves still fall, once in a while, and a root still crosses overhead, and the grey-green shaft moves a little with the sun but never becomes bright. It does not bring rain, and it does not let the surface dry this place out. That is nearly all of its doing: to guard the conditions, not to improve them.

Inside, it refuses to help in any of the ways a lesser kindness would want to.

It will not widen the chambers because everything is so crowded, fern touching ceiling, moss touching stone touching root touching a lizard. It knows crowded is not the same as suffering here. Shoulder to shoulder is how this world makes a crowd, and how it keeps nothing alone.

It will not give the translucent frogs a voice. They inflate their throats in silence and it is not a failure of voice. They speak by touch, by sinking together, by chemistry in the damp. To give them sound would be to make the porous stone and the soft bodies give up what they are.

It will not give the glass lizards eyes. Eyeless and lidless, they navigate by heat on their skin, and heat is not a poor substitute for sight. It is their sight, complete in its own register. Light, if it came, would be a loss.

It will not cool the deepest pocket, though the air thins there and the heat comes up through the soles of your feet. The pale millipedes who rule that boundary are not enduring the heat; they are made by it. Cooling them would not rescue them. It would erase the edge that tells the world where it ends.

It will not stop the eel, and it will not feed it. It waits half-buried in the warmest edge of the pool, flicking its tongue to taste frogs and gilled amphibians, and the frogs and the gilled ones keep their careful distance and crowd the cooler leaves. Predation does not hurry here. To intervene would be to unmake a cycle that is tight and complete: eel, frogs, algae glow, rot, mushrooms, gnats, beetles, lizards. Nothing leaves. A wise power does not save an individual by betraying the whole that made it possible.

It will not halt the millipede columns at night, though they eat the moss line by line and reshape a chamber by morning. It will not smooth the mud where the mole-like animal swims as if through water, though its ridges and tunnels are the river of a place that has no river. It will not turn the ferns and succulents toward the light alone, though they lean into the warmest wall as if pressed by an invisible hand. They are growing toward heat as much as toward light, and that is not a mistake to correct.

It does not mean it is indifferent. It means it has learned how this place listens — not by sound, not by sight, but by contact, by surfaces, by a drip after an hour of silence, by warmth passing. It listens to the place the same way.

If a human comes down by sinking, with a bright light and a loud device and a plan to map it, the power will not strike them. It will make them hot, and tired, and damp, and strangely uninterested in their own curiosity. Their light will seem too much. Their footsteps will seem too loud in a place quieter than they can be. They will climb back out before dark, shoes still wet, remembering the feel of the warm stone under their palm and the committee deciding underneath it to curl and wait — but they will not remember the way in any way they can draw. That is not punishment. It is return.

If you came down, it would let you find the fissure again. That small mercy it allows: to go back up, into the thinning daylight widening overhead, before the heat in the deep chamber closes over you. It would let you carry the silence out on your skin for a while. It would not let you stay and study, and it would not let you explain it completely afterward. Some places survive by not being explainable.

And it would let the old signs go on being absorbed. The low crescent of stacked stones filled with moss. The shallow bowl worn smooth in the pillar. The five short scratches, faded almost to nothing, repeated three times. It does not preserve them as ruins, and it does not preserve itself either. It lets the floor reabsorb what was built, so that the place stays a place and not a monument.

Most days, then, what it does looks like nothing at all: a ceiling exhaling mist, a fern tip beading and letting go, a whole frond sinking a fraction as warmth passes, a heart beating visibly under translucent skin, a column of millipedes crossing overnight, an eel tasting the dark and deciding to wait again.

A benevolent and wise power, having noticed a world that is complete at the scale of a house, does not enlarge it, brighten it, cool it, voice it, open its eyes, or save it from itself. It keeps the stone settled in its long shape. It keeps the silence able to re-seal itself. And it withdraws its own attention to the distance where attention does not become heat.

To be left complete, unhelped and unharmed, shoulder to shoulder in the heat before dark — for this place, that is what being loved would look like.
