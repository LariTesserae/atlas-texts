It did not come because the plateau shone. That is how most powers arrive — drawn to a light, a cry, a prayer, a suffering that lifts itself up to be noticed. This place gave none of those. From the ridge it looked unfinished, not splendid. The light never committed to day. The air was too wet to lift a plume aright. No bell, no anthem, no crowd in a street too narrow to hold one.

It noticed it because it did not stop.

Not dramatically. No collapse, no rebirth. Just the same low, close rhythm, level by level: water dripping in different tempos, cart wheels on wet decking, a press thudding three levels down, a transformer buzzing just below conversation, a steam vent sighing and closing. It bent its attention the way light bends through the permanent skim of cloud, and found that what it had taken for a city from a distance was, up close, a single slow animal.

Not a metaphor, exactly. It was made of people, yes — the largest animal there, in weatherproof jackets that never quite dry at the shoulders — but also of ferns in the expansion joints, of algae in the filter beds, of drain eels moving like punctuation in the culverts, of moths battered silver at the lamps, of rats in the service spines eating what would otherwise clog, of two pigeons on the highest eave, of a cat per district in absolute tenure of a windowsill. Each took the space the others left. None were wild. None were separate. The whole thing breathed through its walkways, in and out, at a pace that had no hurry in it because hurry would be wasted.

A benevolent power is expected to fix such a place. It catalogued its sorrows, out of habit.

The light is dim. The air is thin and cool. Nothing touches natural ground. There are no dogs, no birdsong, no soil that wasn't brought in bags. Everything is damp. Everything is crowded in three dimensions. The walls weep. The children learn to read a terrace by its color like a pulse.

It considered, as it had considered elsewhere, lifting the cloud. Giving them sun. Giving them space, dryness, silence absolute, ground that was not deck or grating or the roof of the level below.

It did not do any of it. Wisdom, when it has lived long enough, learns to finish the thought.

Sun would dry the moss that softens the iron, crack the concrete that has cracked in familiar patterns for decades, turn the lush low green into dust and then into a need for new rules. Space would undo the one rule that holds them: if a room stands empty for more than a few days, something must go in it. Give them a valley of their own and the saturated, reused, three-dimensional care would thin out into sprawl, and the churn — workshop gutted in the morning, wall re-laid block by block by evening — would become waste. Absolute silence would take from them the drip that tells them a joint is softening a year before it would fail. Even the dimness is not a failure to be corrected. Their eyes are used to it. An elder by the greenhouse glass watches the condensation run down and is not waiting for it to clear.

It had seen other places beg for rescue, and rescue had always been a kind of finishing. This place had settled, and a settled thing must not be finished.

So if it did anything, it was this: it learned their rules and kept them, quietly, alongside them.

Everything must drain. So it drains. It does not bring rain — rain is frequent enough — but it keeps the channels under the grating from ever quite stopping. A missed drip, somewhere on a middle terrace, would once have become a softened joint and then, over years, a softened foundation. The power holds that one, unseen, an hour longer than it should, so the repair crew arrives in the morning and takes the forty-year wall down block by block before anyone is under it. That is the whole miracle: no one is under it.

Everything must be reused. So it reuses its own attention. It does not pour itself down as light or voice, which would have to be answered, performed for, built around. It stays diffuse, grey-white, present as the lamps are present — not bright, just there. Waste heat from a lower engine warms a greenhouse above it; the power puts its warmth there too, small and low, so a bean vine trains a little farther up the cable than it would have, and the tender with green-stained hands thinks only that this year the terrace is holding.

Nothing natural may be exposed for long, because the damp will take it back. So it does not expose itself. The inhabitants never know they are watched, and that is the gift. A watched place begins to preen. This one has never preened. It keeps its murmur because walls are thin and air carries and everyone prefers it that way. The power keeps its murmur lower still.

Plants may grow where they like so long as they do not block a drain or a walkway. The power accepts the same condition. It does not demand a temple terrace, a cleared shrine, a single unplanted ledge for worship. There is one place — and the inhabitants would not notice it, and would perhaps think it a small inefficiency — where a fern has never quite taken, where water falls from an elbow in a clean line to the deck below, season after season. The power leaves it that way. Not as a heresy against their density, but as a breath. One unfilled joint against the rule that nothing may stand empty. Even a saturated thing needs a place to exhale.

And for the rest, it simply continues them.

When an elder who can read a terrace by brown and too-thick green dies behind a thin wall that hums with transformers, her knowing does not vanish. The power does not speak it into a child. It only ensures a child lingers, some evenings, by the greenhouse glass watching condensation and carts, and asks a stupid question that is not stupid, and is told the answer in the ordinary way. Knowledge here passes the way water passes: from level to level, without a speech.

When the ridge road travelers look out and see not a city but a topography, retaken slope after slope, they keep driving. Curiosity slides off wet concrete. The power keeps them unremarked, not hidden — hidden invites seeking — but unremarkable, the way moss on the north side of a wall is unremarkable until you are close enough to see that it has stabilized there and is not ruining anything.

By night it settles with them. The dimming is slight rather than dark. Lamps come up everywhere, a thousand small points reflected in wet decks and running channels and clouded glass. The cats keep their windowsills. The moths keep the lamps. The eels keep the cleanest water, which is to say, the water they have learned to keep clean by living in it. The carts slow. The presses thud. The children move sideways in the narrow walkways, hands out of the channels where water runs fast and cold.

It will not make them more. It will not make them less. It has decided that benevolence, here, is not intervention but accompaniment for as long as they need ordinary time — which, given the way they live, is to say, indefinitely.

From the ridge, then, it still does not look like a finished thing. It looks settled. And now, quietly, invisibly, it is settled alongside something else that has learned, at last, how to keep itself alive by never quite finishing any part of itself.
