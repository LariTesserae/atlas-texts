They did not find it by listening for pain.

That is how benevolent powers usually find things. A world cools, a creature starves, a child is lost, a signal goes up through the dark and is answered. This place sent up no signal. It was warm. It was lit. It was damp at the edges and dry in the middle, exactly as it required. Nothing in it was hungry in a way that could be fed from outside, nothing cold that could be warmed, nothing dark that could be brightened. Nothing cried.

That was what drew their attention in the end. Not a distress — a completeness. Most things that live are missing something. This room, and everything that lived in it, seemed to be missing nothing it had ever understood how to want.

They came closer cautiously, the way you approach any complete thing, lest your approach make it incomplete.

They did not go down the slit. They stood on the plateau, on the sun side of three metres of white rock, and put their attention to the warmth of the crust under their feet. Through the stone they could feel it: the slow breath drawing in and out through the cracks, the drip quickening and slowing, the faint tap, once, far below, of a flake against a wall. They listened for longer than a crosser takes to cross. Longer than many crosser cycles.

And they learned the rules by watching, because that is what a wise power does first. It does not act. It learns the spacing.

They saw that the emptiness was not empty. It was metres of held distance between presences. A scraper, a square of wall, a mat regrowing. A hanger, a filament, a volume of clean air. A ribbon, a crack of light, a tip drinking from nothing. A dropped flake, ten paces from the next, a generation cooling on the floor. The abundance was real, but only when counted over the whole wall, over the whole slow year. At any one moment, it looked like almost nothing. That was not a failure of abundance. That was its form.

Once they understood that, the temptations came, as they always do. Benevolence is quick with its hands. Wisdom has to catch them.

The first temptation was to help it grow.

They could have opened the cracks a little more, let in more of the noon, let the mats spread, let the ribbons lengthen until they grazed the floor. They could have brought more moisture, made the pools stay, made the sweat-patches permanent. It would have been easy, and it would have felt like kindness. More life, faster.

But the mats were already exactly where light and damp coincided, and no farther. A faster room would not have been this room, better. It would have been another room, crowding out the spacing that made the first one possible. To improve it would have been to unmake it.

The second temptation was to help it live.

They could have given the scrapers company, so that two eyes might meet on a wall. They could have made the hangers descend and touch, so they would not hang alone for their whole lives. They could have given the crosser a resting place in the middle, a nest, a reason to stay. The room looked, to an outsider's heart, so solitary.

But solitude here was not loneliness. The scrapers' eyes were large and open and turned outward; they saw their wall perfectly well. The hangers swayed in unison when the room breathed; that was their communion, and it was enough. The crosser entered the light for an hour and left it, and its leaving was part of its life. None of them were waiting to be joined. To join them would have been to overgraze the mats, to foul the filtered air, to fill the middle that was meant to be mostly empty. They were not suffering from the distance. The distance was what they were made of.

The third temptation was the hardest, because it wore the face of respect. To help it become.

They could have given a scraper a second tool, or a better flake, or a cache where tools were kept together. They could have taught a mark, a sound, a word for the drip. They could have lifted the whole slow ecology up a thousand years in an afternoon, and watched to see what it chose to build first. It is a terrible kindness, acceleration.

They considered it for a long time — a lifetime of theirs, which is a blink — and then they understood that a single carried flake, made once, worn smooth by use, dropped at death to become one more solitary stone on an empty floor, was not a primitive technology waiting for improvement. It was a complete one, at the scale of the life that carried it. To give them more would not have freed them. It would have relieved them of the very discipline that held the room together: carry, don't build. Use, don't keep. Live in your square, and leave it when you die, flush to the wall.

So they decided, in the way wise powers decide, to do nothing inside.

No miracle. No second sun. No rain in the dry middle. No sign written in silt, no light left burning in a corner to prove they had been there. A sign is a building. A building is a home that must be defended. They would not make the room defend itself against them.

What they did, they did from above.

They shaded the eggshell when the sun on the plateau hardened, so the diffused noon below thickened and thinned as it always had, and did not scorch. They kept the cracks in their patient network, neither sealing them nor widening them. They kept the entrance the width of shoulders, easy to miss, hard to enlarge. And when people came — as people do, with bottles for the amber film, with drills for the white rock, with cameras for the hangers, with the pure intention to share the wonder — they made the place forgettable. Not by thunder. By a small, hangers-like deflection. The path seemed longer than it was. The slit seemed not worth the squeeze. The visitor left with clean feet and a sense of having dreamed something pale and quiet, and did not remember quite how to find it again. It was not a curse. It was spacing, extended to protect the spaced.

That is their keeping, and it is continuous, and it is almost invisible, which is the point. They did not claim the room. A wise power knows the difference between tending and owning. They tended it the way the hangers tend the air: from above, by a filament, without landing.

And they counted. Not to inventory, but to learn. Which hollow darkened first when the breath came. Which wall the scrapers had not yet regrown. How long a crosser's track endured before the ripples took it back. Over years, the numbers settled into a pattern, and the pattern settled into a kind of attention that asked for nothing to change. To be witnessed, completely and without intervention, turned out to be not a small thing. For most creatures, to be seen by such a power is the beginning of being altered. Here, to be seen and left alone was the only form of being seen that did not break anything.

They held one reservation, as benevolence must. If the equilibrium ever broke — if a crack collapsed and sealed the breath, if the crust was drilled and the light poured in raw, if the drip stopped for a true season and the mats went pale at the edges — then they would step in. One body-length at a time. No more than needed. Then step back, and two metres and more would open again between them and everything that lived there. They prepared for that the way you prepare for an earthquake in a house you do not live in: not by rebuilding it stronger, but by knowing exactly how little force would restore it to itself. They hoped, quietly and for a very long time, never to use it.

For now, the light dimmed and brightened over hours. The hangers swayed, all together, head-down. A scraper moved a body-length and stopped. Somewhere against a damp wall, a flake tapped stone — once — and the still air swallowed the sound. The drip fell into its ring, which widened and vanished. The footprints of the last visitor softened, then became ripples again.

On the plateau above, on the other side of three metres of warm white rock, they remained. Close, but not touching. Present, but not crowding. Exactly as far away as everything in that room kept from everything else — and as near as love, when it has learned the rules of a place, is allowed to be.
