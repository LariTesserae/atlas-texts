It did not land.

That was its first decision, and in a way its only one. It noticed the table not by shining a light on it, but the way the leather thing under the slab notices rain: by a low pot-like tone travelling out across stone for a surprisingly long distance before it died. Something there was taking its time longer than anything else it knew — a mountain finished dying, still standing, dripping inside from a lowland age.

It leaned over the edge, above the cloud sea, as the hawk does, and stayed there. To put a foot down would be to press on moss that sinks to the ankle and comes up green and cold for hours. The springtails would leap. The tardigrades would curl. The stone under its weight would ring too hard for the pale worms in their dark tunnel, and the millipedes following the sweat-line would stop, waiting for a vibration that did not belong to rain.

Its heart, if it has one, wanted to do almost everything wrong.

It wanted to water the pockets more, so the ferns need not close, so the rosettes need not wait for a three-minute cloud. Its reason stopped it: more water would mean more moss, more weight, more rot, and the slabs — finished, bone-smooth, with no fresh scar to give — would begin to go again. The final shape would be lost under the kindness. A permanent lushness is not this place. This place is burst and closure.

It wanted to calm the wind, so the beetle need not walk slowly and pause for gusts, so the hawk need not be thrown sideways and scream once into white noise. Its wisdom stopped it: the wind is the curtain that brings the mist to be combed out on the windward side of stones, that sings in the drip-holes, that hides the breathing of the earless thing when it comes out. Silence would expose it. A still air would starve the very pockets it pitied.

It wanted, most of all, to do something about the one under the slab. Flat as wet leather, palm-sized, many short legs, no ears because ears would be useless here and then some, hearing instead with its legs. Breathing so slowly a flank rising would be missed. Coming out perhaps twice in a lifetime, only when rain drums hard enough on the polish to hide its own breath. Eating algae washed into its hollow, drinking condensation, eating its own shed skin. Alone in its hollow, its sky, its sea.

Heart said: lonely. Starved. Blind and deaf.

Wisdom said: look again, through stone, not through air.

One hollow can feed one life. That is its whole territory, and it is enough — exactly enough, in a closed loop with no waste, no neighbour to compete for the film, no other mouth for the shed skin. Bring a second of its kind and you have not given it company. You have given it a rival for a drip that has been falling, drip by drip, for metres, since before there was a mountain. Give it eyes and you give it a way to be hurt by the plain, straight midday light. Give it ears and you give it the roar. Give it a name said aloud and your voice, swallowed by wind, would still boom in the rock and deafen the underworld you meant to comfort.

So it did not name it aloud.

What it did instead was learn its timing. It only truly looks when the rain drums hard enough to hide a breathing. It listens not in the air, where everything is noise, but through the slab, where everything is vibration — the way the creature listens. It takes that long, unresolving organ note for what it is, not a problem to be resolved.

And it keeps the conditions. Not improves — keeps.

It keeps the hollow from collapsing before its time, thumb on the invisible crack, so the dark space under that one slab does not dry out and the travelling drip under the whole table does not find a new, faster route. It keeps the rain cells irregular, arriving as they always have, a dark smudge a mile off, reorganizing the plateau as they pass, so the flowers still open only when the stone is wet, for fifteen minutes in white noise, and no pollinator needs to be invented for them. It keeps the emptiness between pockets — ten paces, fifty, two hundred of naked warm stone — because that nothing is not a lack. It is the distance the tone travels in. It is what lets a beetle be solitary, a rosette be centred on its one sweat, a hawk be brief.

It lets the cairn of three stones sink to its second course in moss. It does not add a fourth. It lets the flaked blade stay wedged in its crack, left for someone later. It lets the human watcher, once in a generation, empty the pecking-bowl of its condensation and go down again, and if the watcher comes with new tools, with a machine that would take a tardigrade away in a jar to name it properly, the wind rises, the stone booms, their thoughts have to wait until a gust passes, and they leave swearing nothing lives here at all in that ordinary light. That is not deception. That is the place telling the truth at midday.

Its benevolence, then, is custodial and almost invisible. It does not steady the hawk — being thrown sideways is the hawk's way of being here, and it never lands either. It does not warm the centre of the rosette, where the leaf litter rots sweet and fermented, always clockwise, for a reason it does not explain and does not straighten. It does not lift the millipedes to the light.

If you tap a slab with a knuckle now, you will feel the tone travel out and die, and under your boots, in the dark hollow of the old world, a drip will still be falling. And falling. And above the cloud sea, something that never lands will be listening in exactly the way that life down there has taught it: only when the rain is loud enough to hide a breath, so that its attention, too, does not disturb.

It is falling in company now, though nothing has been added and nothing taken away to make that true.
