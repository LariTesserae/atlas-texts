It begins as attention, not as arrival.

They do not come up through the cloud sea the way the people did, ears changing with pressure, hands white on the handrail. They do not need to. They look down from wherever looking down is done, and see a small, bright, damp plateau at the top of the world, manufacturing its own rain, and they stay to look a little longer — which, for a power of this kind, is already a kind of descent.

They see what you saw. A hamlet that could be crossed in twenty minutes and cannot be crossed at all without stopping for work. A place where the hills are shells, the valleys are lanes two meters wide, then one, where the water never stops because it is never allowed to stop. South old and overgrown, north embryonic and scaffolded, the whole thing breathing in and out between construction lift and harvest and forty-minute spray cycle. Green everywhere, fauna almost nowhere. People moving in pairs at walking pace, in yellow slicks, low-voiced, doing by hand the work pollinators and grazers would do elsewhere.

A benevolent power can think of many things to do. A wise one thinks of them, one by one, and sets most of them down again.

They could lift the people down. It would be easy, and clean. Down through the cloud sea, out of the warm pocket, away from the one-meter lanes and the cranes that must not drop a module. But the people came up here for a reason that lives below that sea, and the ground they left is not a ground they could now stand on without learning it again. To rescue them would be to unmake them. They are not stranded. They are resident.

They could make it natural. Send real rain instead of intercepted fog. Send insects in the mist, birdsong in the wet green, a proper soil fauna for the turf modules and the compost. It would be lush, and it would rot. The fans exist to keep contaminants out of the nutrient loop. The stillness they fear is not superstition; a still pool does sour, a still pile does rot. A wild population would eat the rice troughs, clog the fins, teach the place to depend on an outside that is too far below to replenish it. And it would steal the work from the hands that tend it. In a made place, to tend is to belong. They do not take belonging away in the name of abundance.

They could finish it. Complete the northern terraces, close the southern loops, let the scaffolding come down and the survey chalk stay. But nothing here is allowed to be finished for the same reason nothing is allowed to be still. Finished would mean the place stopped moving, and a place that stops moving up here does not rest. It fails. The builders in the north are not behind schedule; the north being unfinished is part of how the south stays alive.

They could speak. Give instructions, warning, comfort, a voice from above the parapet. The people have learned quiet not because they were told to, but because shouting carries too far across the flat exposed plateau and everyone knows everyone else's shift. A loud voice would be a kind of weather event. It would startle the white birds. It would be overheard by everyone at once, and a place that lives in pairs would be forced into a crowd. So a wise power learns the local discipline. It speaks, if it speaks at all, at the volume of a drip.

What they do instead is smaller, and therefore harder to be wrong about.

They work inside the tolerances. The place already has them: turf slumps where it is wettest, vines find the bolt-holes, algae chooses the warmest sump instead of the intended one, condensation weeps from the coldest rib regardless of the diagram. Life here has always lived in the margin between design and happening. A benevolent power does not redraw the diagram. It widens the margin, a fraction, where widening cannot break the loop.

They connect two sumps in the lower pipes with a channel too narrow for a sensor and just wide enough for a pale amphibian to find, years from now, on its own. No one will note it to the next shift. That is the point. The pair of white birds, who circle once in the morning and then go into the cloud sea, are allowed one morning a season to circle twice — not to sing, they do not sing, but to delay their going by the length of one breath over the parapet. The phosphor-moss in the underwalk is allowed one bolt-hole beyond its seeding, so that at dusk a seam of pale green glows a little longer above the night-shift drainage walk, where a torch is being carried and a hand is listening for a missed pump.

They make the fans' world forty-one minutes long, once a day. Not forty, not less. Forty-one. Once in a day's turning of loops there is a minute that belongs to no cycle — no spray, no lift, no harvest — a minute in which a bench on a southern terrace can be dry enough to sit on, a minute in which stillness is watched over and therefore does not have to be rot. It is not much. It is exactly enough to be noticed by the body and never by the schedule.

And they learn to move in pairs and at walking pace. No single great adjustment. A drip timed to answer a tool set down with a clear clank. A condensation rib weeping, obligingly, where someone will want to rinse their hands after handling cuttings. A warm pocket that holds, even when the wind comes through the cloud sea below and tries the kettle, and a quiet underpinning against the one thing this place cannot afford: a fall. Not rescue from the work. Protection from the catastrophe that would end the work for everyone at once.

If you listen now, before you look, the tick-tick from the gutters has not changed. Under it, under the thrum through the deck, there is a second tick that is not quite a pump. It is irregular. It does not keep the forty minutes. It keeps the people.

They do not stay forever. Attention of this kind does not need to. It comes back on the cycle, the way the spray-bars sigh, the way the rice moves two centimeters a day. It looks from the parapet at the very top, where the newest frames are still bone-white and the old ferns lean out over the straight drop into cloud, and it does not push and it does not pull. It makes sure the leaning is safe.

Below, the small world continues: dense, dripping, warm, unfinished, unnatural, manufacturing its own landscape because that is the only way up here to have one. The people are still small and paired and damp and quiet, poling the troughs, carrying baskets, bolting a tank two meters above another crew, pollinating the espaliered fruit trees by hand from duct to duct.

Nothing has been made natural. Nothing has been allowed to be finished.

But now, also, nothing is allowed to be unwitnessed.
