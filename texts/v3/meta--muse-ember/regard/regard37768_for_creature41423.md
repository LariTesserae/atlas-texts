If a benevolent and wise power noticed Tessik, it would not notice it the way powers usually notice places.

It would not come for the prayer. There is no prayer here, only tending. It would not come for the suffering. There is no suffering begging to be made large.

It would come because, from the rim, it would see a bowl full of water holding a town the way a cupped hand holds water, and nothing in the picture would be out of place. The frogs would be loud. The fish would be in the doorways. A house would be sinking by inches in a decade and rising by inches in a wet year, and no one would call it disaster or miracle.

A lesser power would try to save them from drowning. A wise one would listen long enough to hear the mat-being underneath answer: *We are not drowning. We are floating.*

That would be the whole conversation, and it would take a season.

### What it would do first: nothing, on purpose

It would learn to sit down in a dugout without tipping it. That is harder than it sounds. The boats are single logs, poled, not paddled. Your weight is your steering. A power used to arriving in light would find its knees wet for the first time in a long while, and would consider that an education.

It would stop trying to speak. Tessik has no words for gods. It has words for the give of the mat under a pole, for a channel choking to a meadow, for a root pillar thick enough at last. When the power opened its mouth to explain itself, a child across a lily meadow would shout for her mother, and the shout would arrive whole, unsoftened, because there is nothing here to absorb it — and the power would understand that if it spoke loudly, the frogs would stop, and if the frogs stopped, the people would know someone had stepped too close to a nest.

So it would become quiet enough to be mistaken for weather.

### What it would do second: tend the edges no one sees

A benevolent power cannot simply watch, if watching means letting an outside harm arrive. Below the rim, in the valleys still trapped with cloud, there are people who get thirsty and people who get ambitious. From below, a high lake that never surges, eight hundred paces across, perfectly cupped, looks like a resource. It looks like it could be drained, dammed, straightened, made useful. Metal is not here. That is not an accident of poverty. It is an agreement with the mat.

The Listener at the Rim — if it needed a name — would not build a wall. Walls here are woven and left to root, and a wall that does not root would feel wrong to everyone. Instead it would make the climb *longer in the thought*.

Travelers coming up from the cloud would find the rain-wall arriving early, the pole slippery on algae, the frogs suddenly intolerable in their companionship, and, most of all, an embarrassment they could not name. To want to take this water would feel, suddenly, like wanting to take the flooded ground floor of someone’s house while the fish were still drifting through it. They would turn back, not defeated, but uncomfortably aware they had been about to be rude.

Downstream, the power would be generous in the other direction. The slow drift from the snowmelt seep on the north rim to the south — a finger’s width a day, barely a current — it would keep that seep open by a hair when the rock wanted to shift. Not a flood. Just enough to go on feeding the low valleys, season after season, so no one below could say Tessik was hoarding. Benevolence, to a wise power, is not taking from one place to give to another. It is keeping both places from having to become enemies.

Inside the bowl, its miracles would be so small no one would build a religion on them:

A willow woven into a wall would root a little faster on the leaning side, so the lean would stay stable, as it always has. No one would call it straightened. It would still lean.

A root pillar a young family had been waiting a childhood to thicken would get one season’s grace, early. They would move upstairs and think, as people do, that the mat had swelled in a wet year.

A heronry in a half-dead alder that is also somebody’s wall would shift its nests for one spring, so a repair could happen without disturbing them, and then return. The herons would call from the rooftops as if nothing had changed, because for them, nothing had.

A dugout that should have tipped when a silver fish jumped with a slap among the houses would right itself by the careful placement of an otter’s loud cousinly weight on the other side. The otter would bark. The child would laugh. No one would thank the sky.

That is the level at which Tessik already operates. A power that intervened at the level of wonder would break the completeness of it — plant below, plant above, animal threaded through both, human hands mending in rhythm everyone across the water can hear. To lift them would be to unthread them.

### What it would do third: remember

This, perhaps, is the only true service a wise power can offer a place that measures change in generations.

Tessik expects to be exactly as it is, only greener, for a very long time. That confidence is not promised by anything in Tessik itself. The mat thickens. Peat forms where reeds fall and are not eaten. Timber softens. Stone polishes. A flooded room becomes a fish nursery, then a planter. A channel becomes a meadow. In time, even the bowl will change its mind.

The power would stay to remember the names of the channels while they are water-streets, and the names of the meadows after they were channels, and which chair has been underwater longest in which drowned room. When the oldest people who remember the causeway walked dry are gone, someone would still know the causeway is a road and not just a place where crayfish walk at night feeling for algae.

It asks nothing in return. That is what surprises it most. Everywhere else it has been noticed, it has been asked for rain, for rescue, for meaning, for an end to the lean and the damp and the slow sinking. Here, no one asks. The storehouses on raft platforms are visited by everyone and owned by no one in particular. News collects on the hearth-hummocks the way frogs collect at dusk, and is given away the same way.

A benevolent power that has been worshipped for eons might find, at Tessik, something it had forgotten how to want: to be *unneeded*, and to stay anyway — not as a god, but as a neighbor who has learned to pole, to sit with wet knees, to listen to wood knocking against piers all night and not mistake it for a plea.

At evening, when the light goes ordinary gold and then flat, when the water turns dark green-brown and reflects the rim, when fat lamps are lit upstairs and fish drift past the windows of light below, the Listener would not add its own light. It would make sure both lights could be seen at once.

It would not drain the summit. It would not dry it. It would not make it holy.

It would keep the seep open. It would keep the approach embarrassing. It would keep the frogs from ever quite stopping.

And if, a thousand wet years from now, the mat finally decides to become peat and the bowl decides to become something else, it would be there at the rim, still listening, able to say what it was — stone, timber, fiber, root, water, and human hands mending — because for a power that wanted only to be benevolent and wise, the most powerful thing it could do was not to change Tessik at all, but to be the one who witnessed that Tessik, on its own, had already learned how not to disappear.
