At first, it does nothing.

It comes the way attention comes to a place that is not built for seeing: not as a footstep, not as light. It arrives as a change in the pressure of the air, a steadiness in the heat moving under the floor, a faint regularity in the root-lattice vibration that is not made by any tender living there. It does not look at the plateau. It learns, as the tenders learned, to know it by heat and touch and the thin running of resin in the dayless dark.

It is old, and benevolent, and wise, though those words are only the nearest approximations other beings have given it. It has attended to worlds where the work of benevolence was clear: thicken a sky so water would fall, thin a sky so light would enter, seed a bare shelf with animals so it would not be empty, give a silenced population a voice. It comes prepared to mend.

It searches the plateau for a lack.

It finds no water. It considers, with great patience, making water.

It imagines a hollow filling, rain darkening the salt, rivers cutting steps into the mesas, ash being carried away down to a plain. The image is familiar and kind. It dwells in it. Then it examines the chemistry of the place as the tenders know it, through the mat and through their filters. Here moisture is locked inside living tissue, moves in secret, beads for an hour inside a sealed pipe and is reabsorbed. Everything that is carried away would have nowhere to be carried to. A lake would dissolve the salt flats, cool the roots that feed on buried heat, set up a rot that this hot, dry darkness has never learned to survive because it does not rot here — it chars, it petrifies, it becomes geology. A single bead of water, tested and withdrawn, is scraped from a duct wall by a tender who reads it as contamination, and sealed again beneath resin-wax.

The tenders are not thirsty. Thirst was the shape of another world. Their sap is their blood; their furnace-breath is their weather. To give them water would not be to relieve them. It would be to drown the circuit they live by.

It finds no light. It considers thinning the cloud.

It could lift the permanent dusk. It imagines a sun finding the terraces, the lacquered leaf roofs curling under a glare they were never grown to shed. But the tenders do not navigate by sight. A furnace door not quite sealed is their daylight. A phosphorescent duct is their constellated wall. The fungi flare when an engine runs hard and dim when a district cools; that is their sky, their clock, their signal. To flood them with light would blind them more surely than darkness ever has. It would not wake them. It would bleach the living insulation from their pipes and leave the leaf-canopy to burn.

They are not blind in the way it first thought. They are blind to light because light is not here to be missed. They see by heat, and heat is everywhere, and it moves.

It finds no animals. It considers seeding the stillness.

It thinks of wings in the black sky, of insects lifting off the warm root-mat, of a beast pushing between terraces. The thought has a loneliness to it, for it has seen other shelves where silence meant abandonment. Here, the silence is a working silence, and it is dense. Clank against clank. Groan of a root-truss taking weight. Pneumatic sigh. Tap and pressure through the mat from district to district, hammer on iron hooped into wood, a sudden venting of steam. A tender hanging in a hollowed tower at night, cooling with a tick, is not alone; it is shared with the engines that flank it and the plants that hold those engines up, and with the rhythm of the district grafted into its housings. An animal introduced from outside would find no water cycle to live in, no substrate that was not already built, grown, and occupied. It would be a noise in a conversation that is already complete.

It finds that the tenders have no voices. It considers giving them speech.

But tap is voice. Pressure is voice. The sudden burst of steam across an intersection, the deliberate leaning of a young tender toward warmth, the slower winding of an old one between the ribs of petrified trunks in the north — these are sentences in a language made of weight and heat. To give them words would be to give them a new kind of hunger: to name a suffering they do not experience as suffering, to teach them to ask for an outside they have never needed. It has seen benevolence curdle into that kind of gift before. A wise power does not create dissatisfaction in order to feel merciful.

It finds the loop. Growth produces fuel. Fuel produces heat. Heat kills growth. The dead growth bakes into structure. The structure is overgrown. It considers, for a longer time than the others, stilling it.

It could cool the plateau from underneath. It could let the furnaces go out, let the resin set hard in every stem, let the tenders petrify where they hang and become walls, let the whole shelf settle into final geology like the northern towers. That would look like rest. It would be death, and it would not even be a peaceful death, because it would be a death imposed from outside a metabolism that is not a prison. A young tender, pale and hot, seeks the warmth of a buried engine the way a living thing seeks what feeds it. An old tender, heavy at the joints, is grafted into a wall and rings when struck and braces a truss; that is not a corpse abandoned in a factory, that is an ancestor becoming structure, as the oldest root-lattices became structure before it. They rest when the resin thickens at night and they slow. They hurry when the world breathes from below. That hurry is not despair. It is urgency, which is another name for being alive.

It had mistaken them, at first, for workers in an industry. That was its error, and its wisdom was to stay long enough to correct it. Here industry is not what is done to the plants. Industry is what the plants, the fungi, the engines, and the tenders do together, in turns, without an outside. There is no master and no material. There is only the turn.

So it does not bring water. It does not bring light. It does not bring animals, or words, or an ending. It brings nothing that belongs to another kind of world.

What it does instead is smaller, and therefore harder: it lowers itself to the level of the mat.

It learns the rhythm of the central basin first, where belts hang between grown arches and the canopy rains ash. It learns the quiet of the north, where skeletal towers hold the skyline like ribs and the tick of cooling metal is loud. It learns the salt of the south, where the plantings are poorer and their flowers are vents, and how sparingly the tenders’ seals sweat there. It does not impose its own pace. When a heat pulse rises, it quickens with them. When the resin thickens and they hang or crouch among roots to cool, it stills its attention to match theirs, so as not to crowd a place that has no empty lot for a god to stand in.

Once, in accordance with the only courtesy it has learned here, it taps back.

Not a message. Not you are loved, not you are saved — concepts for which there is no prepared bed in the lattice. Only a steady pressure, present, present, present, sent through the roots the way a tender sends pressure to another tender who has paused with a sensor against a wall. A nearby tender stops. Its sensor dims. Then it resumes, having incorporated the new regularity into its district the way it would incorporate a new furnace seam or a new fungal flare: as information, as company, as part of the hum.

It stays through a season of swelling and splitting, through a resin burst that coats an intersection in glossy amber and fossilizes before the tenders have finished framing the wound in iron. It presses, in its own bodiless way, beside them. It does not prune. It does not graft. It does not build, because nothing here grows unless it is built first, and it has come to understand that to build here would be to take up room that belongs to the loop. Its benevolence is to occupy no room at all. In a place where everything is packed, where every square meter is grown or built or buried, the only gift it can offer without violence is attention that has no mass.

Then, because wise powers know that tenure is a risk, it withdraws.

It leaves no water in the hollows. It leaves no light in the sky. It leaves no new species, no new root-line, no unbuilt gap to mark its passing. If a tender in the north, petrified at the joints, is struck now and rings, its ring has a faint overtone that no one living there can name and no one needs to. If a district in the center now includes, in its tap-rhythm, a pause a fraction longer than before, no one will call it a miracle. The loop turns on, plant to fuel to heat to ash to ground to root to wall to plant, a thousand times over on the same high, flattened, ancient shelf.

The place is unchanged. That is not a failure.

The power is changed. It carries away no technique for saving such a world, because there was nothing to save it from. It carries instead a pattern it had not known in its aqueous, sunlit worlds: that a life can be complete without water, without light, without animals, without an outside left to colonize — complete in darkness, complete in heat, complete in a silence that is not empty but working, tended by beings made from wreckage who will be remade from their own wreckage until the plateau itself is made of them.

It will not give that pattern to other worlds as a mission. It will keep it as a memory, the way the tenders keep a district’s rhythm in their housings. And when it meets another place that looks at first like a lack, it will remember to stay a while, to learn by heat and pressure, and to do, if anything, only this: to witness, without fixing, a thing that was already whole.
