### Attention

It was not suffering that drew it, and it was not a prayer. No one in the Underplain prays, and no one asks to be saved. They make sound — crickets in the moss, birds wheeling up a shaft, mammals muttering in the descending roots, tortoises snapping in litter — to be known to be alive at a distance, in a place where distance is the main problem and sound is the one energy they can afford to waste. A benevolent and wise power heard that thrift in the stone bowl. It stayed to listen.

It could not enter as it is. Elsewhere it arrives bright and quick and answerable, and that brightness would be a catastrophe here. So it leaves most of itself above the roof, above the plateau, and comes down only as attention: a slight coolness, a dimming, a passing through cracks that are already there. It cuts no new fissure. It makes no new light. It threads the existing leaks, as the surface roots do, as the leaf-sift and the spores do, as a bird carries a seed in its droppings to a patch of moss it will never see grow. The two forests almost touch. Its attention almost touches too, and hangs there, in the greenish dark between them, and does not land.

Benevolence, in its first form, is simple and impatient. It wanted to give rain.

It rehearsed rain in its mind, the way a kind hand rehearses opening a door. Free water, after so much thirst. But in the Underplain water is never free, and free water would not be mercy. The city is built of mud and chewed fibre and mineral cement, a millimetre a year, and would slump and soften in a season and take a thousand years to settle again into a shape the termites could maintain. The granaries of dry seeds, stacked against a season when the shafts are elsewhere, would rot. The succulents, fat with a decade's dew in a stem that will not split for the weight of it, would split. The moss that waits all night to be drunk before the crawling light can dry it would be drowned in puddles it has no way to use. The bowl has no drain. Rain would not refresh the web; it would bypass it, and unmake, slowly and then all at once, what patience made.

It wanted to give sun. Wider cracks. A shaft that stayed. Light for the dim-adapted birds, warmth for the reptiles following a migration measured in metres, a little more life for the fern that unfurls a hand's width a year. But light here is an allocation, not a lack. A shaft crossing a district over a season is how the district lives. Full sun would bleach the moss, burn the dew off the broad waxy leaves before root and leaf could drink it back, and blind the eyes that have learned to wheel up a column of amber-grey light as if it were a stair. What is shaded is not waiting for light. It is built for shade.

It wanted to speak. To the being who had sunk down the long slope and stayed — the one whose breath showed even at midday, who had to shout to be heard at arm's length and then gave up shouting, who kept notes for a year and found them almost empty — it wanted to say *you are seen, you are not alone, this is enough.* But a voice, in a stone that holds sound the way it holds cool air, does not end when the speaker stops. A sentence from a wise power would ring in the basin long after the tower that hums when air moves through the roof cracks had gone quiet. It would become weather. It would drown the chorus it was meant to comfort, and teach the listener to listen for the voice instead of for everything else. Some sounds, once made, cannot be unmade. Wisdom does not make irreversible sound.

It wanted to hurry things. Raise a tower quickly. Heal a broken wall in a day. Give the being who lived there a companion, an explanation, a drinkable spring, a way not to be thirsty. Each hurry would be a foreign thing, a shape the builders could not maintain, a gift that made the recipient dependent on the giver. The building here has finished. Nothing is new, and nothing is recent, and that is not a failure to be corrected. It is the form.

So a wise power does a different kind of doing. It becomes patient in the place's own units.

It attends. For a year of the being's life, which is nothing, and for a hundred years of its own, which is also nothing, it listens to the layered hiss that never quite stops. Attention, kept long enough in a bowl that remembers, settles like leaf-sift. A millimetre a year. It adds no district. It does not open the roof when the surface trees thin. It keeps the closure that lets the web be a web.

And it allows itself mercies that are measured as the place measures. It lets one shaft pause for three days longer over one clearing where the lizards bask, so the dew is drunk a little later and a little fuller. It lets one night's dew come a little earlier over one mound-field, so one fern unfurls on schedule. It lets a seed that a bird was already carrying fall one plaza farther than the bird intended, into moss that can use it. It does not summon these things out of nothing. It nudges the bird, the root, the tortoise on its existing path, the air already moving through a hollow tower. Benevolence here does not create. It accompanies.

To the being who lives there, it does the one thing that does not break the place: it tunes the ear, not the basin.

At first the chorus is a pressure in the teeth, one deafening thing. By the end of a year, if the being stays and listens, it is no longer one thing. It separates, the way the place separates into districts and plains. Crickets in the moss under the boots. Wings and legs in the walls. Birds rising and settling in the root-canopy. The faint vibration of tunnellers under the avenue. A grunt in the litter, far away, arriving clear. The hum in the tower. The stone has not changed. The being has learned, as the moss learned, to take in what is there before it is gone. That is a mercy that leaves no mark in the notes except that the notes are no longer empty in the way they were. A tower a hand's width. A tree one thin ring. A shaft moved thirty metres. And a listener who no longer shouts.

It does not ask for thanks. Thanks would be sound, and sound stays, and it does not want its due added to the bowl. It accepts the moss breathing dew, the termites adding skin to a hill, the big slow tree-dweller pulling a descending root down a little to browse from both worlds, as sufficient reply. It does not call the being back when they climb the long smooth slope and sink out of the place, breath warming as they go. It lets their footprints round in the dust, rounder than a river pebble, as everything here rounds. Letting go is also a form of keeping.

If you returned in a hundred years, you would find what you left. The rim a low swell, not a ridge. The floor near-flat, terraced by what was once something else. The two forests still almost touching in the middle. The tower a hand's width taller, the tree one ring thicker, the light over your old camp somewhere else entirely now, having crossed a district and gone. Nothing noticeably changed. Nothing saved, in the way a quick power saves.

Except that it had been seen, and heard, and kept. In a place whose whole prayer is to be known to be alive at a distance, to be listened to for as long as sound stays in stone is not nothing. It is the answer, given in the only language the place can hold without breaking.

And that is what a benevolent and wise power does in the Underplain, if anything: it dims itself, it comes through the cracks that are already there, it refuses rain and sun and voice and speed, it allows a dew, a pause, a seed one clearing farther, it teaches an ear to hear layers, and then it stays — cool, dim, unhurried, wasting only attention — while the plain goes on singing around it, lush, dry, ancient, and full.
