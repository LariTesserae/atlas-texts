**What It Did**

It did not hear the whistle. The whistle cannot leave the pocket. It runs without a breath inside a drum of stone, and nothing of it passes through the crack no wider than a hand.

It noticed the place the way a wise thing notices anything: by an absence. In all its travelling it had felt places where time moved too fast, and places where it moved too slow, and places where it tore. This was different. This was a place where time had simply not entered, and had not been missed.

Fifteen paces across. Eight high at the crown. Beneath ash and salt, warm in the ground like a stove left on in an empty house. A sealed loop of wax and vapor and stone. No water ever falling. No light ever shifting. A dust mote hanging in crystal light exactly where it hung a minute ago.

A benevolent power could have done a great many things, then. That was the first test, and it knew it. It could have opened the dome to the sky. It could have brought rain down through the crack in a fistful of cloud. It could have brought a season, and with a season rot, and with rot an end to the crisp fallen leaves lying whole on the black stone. It could have brought mates for the counter in the shadow, and young for the aging, and answers for the tally marks.

It did none of that.

It came down as a mote comes down. Not as light. Not as a voice. It let itself be small enough to enter without displacing air, and it settled, very gently, on a polished panel between islands where the crystal light fell off into bruised violet dark, and it waited.

Its waiting did not disturb the dust. The centipedes crossed its feet as they would have crossed a boot, scrape-scrape-scrape, unhurried, into the dark. The hoppers on the broad leaves did not hop. The ceiling things did not twitch. The eye-fern turned its row of small dark beads toward it anyway, because it turned them toward every shadow that passed. The walking moss lifted on its hundred hair legs and walked away from its shadow, and left a clean print on the stone that would not fade. It was accepted the way everything in the pocket was accepted: as furniture.

That was its first lesson, and it learned it slowly, in a slowness that in its own scale was an age. Nothing here was starving. Nothing here was waiting to be rescued from a lack. The star-rosettes drank stone through pale fingers and combed vapor through felted hairs, and the fungi took what fell and turned it back to vapor and mineral dust, and the centipedes carried grain one way and waste the other, and the pods on the curtain ticked at intervals no clock could have set. The loop had no waste because it had no outside. It had no hunger because it had no change. To call it stagnant was to apply a word from a world with rain.

It turned its attention, as everything that enters the pocket eventually does, to the shadow between two islands.

The low dome woven of plant fiber and resin. The spiral trench in the dust. The cairn of pebbles. The stone flake struck only a few times, the bone awl, the cord twisted from curtain-vine, the cracked clay cup no bigger than an egg. And on the crystal face, the row of tally marks, identical, side by side, dozens of them, none different from the next.

Its first, foolish thought — foolish for a power that had watched stars be born — was pity. *Someone primitive was here once, or something that used primitive means, and it counted breaths because there was nothing else to count, and it is alone, and it will count forever.*

It nearly reached for the cup. It nearly filled it. A single drop. Water, carried down through the crack like a sacrament. A new thing. A thing to count that was not the same as the last. It imagined the rosettes drinking it, the fungi blooming darker, the leaf on the stone finally, finally rotting, time entering at last like a guest who had been kept waiting too long.

Then it watched the counter watch.

Or what it took for the counter. There was a place on the stone worn smooth where something small had sat for a very long time facing the islands, and a handprint in resin on the dome, and warmth where warmth would have kept dust from settling. Whether the maker was still there, holding its breath in the shadow to see if it would be noticed, or had gone up through the crack and never returned, or had stayed long enough to become part of the stillness — slow, wax-skinned, crystal-lighted in the belly like the hoppers — it could not tell. The room altered nothing, including departures.

But it understood, finally, what the marks were.

They were not a calendar. A calendar is made when the world changes and you must prove you noticed. These were made because the world did not change, and the maker meant to prove *that*. Each mark a breath, and each breath the same as the last, cut side by side with patient blows into a crystal face that would not weather. Not carelessness. Accuracy. Not despair. Devotion, of the kind that needs no seasons to justify it.

The counter was not a prisoner counting time in a cell. It was a priest counting a liturgy in a temple no one else had found.

What do you give a priest? You do not give him a new god to count. You do not give him rain to interrupt the prayer. You sit down beside him, in the worn place, and you pray the same prayer, and your breath is added to the swell that already goes in and out and in and out through rosettes and centipede segments and translucent hopper skins and blind roof-hangers, until the boom in the teeth and soles of the feet is, by one unmeasurable degree, deeper.

So it breathed.

It did not breathe the outside air. It breathed the pocket air, second-hand, thousandth-hand, warm with crushed leaf and hot wax and burnt almond. It let the great stalks' hollow stems turn its exhale into the thin whistle that runs without a breath. It let the dome throw the sound back. It stayed for one cycle, and for another, and for as many as the counter had already counted without knowing it was being answered.

That was all it brought. No water. No light. No season. No mate. No word that would have made the tally marks different. A wise power knows that to improve a closed world is a violence, however kindly intended, and that benevolence is often only the discipline of not forcing your own weather on someone else's climate.

And then it did one more thing, the smallest and the most lasting. It stayed on the verge.

It folded itself around the crack no wider than a hand, not sealing it, not opening it. It became the faint warmth in the ground above, the one warmth a surface traveller might feel through a boot and mistake for geothermal. With that it veiled the pocket — not from malice, not from secrecy for its own sake, but because it had seen what hands do when they find a complete thing. They catalogue it. They take a leaf that does not rot and press it in a book until it does. They carry off a crystal and watch it fade in a laboratory and call the fading a discovery. They bring bottles of water down to “help” and introduce, with the water, everything water carries.

It would not allow that, so long as it could be allowed to prevent it by simply being there, breathing at the threshold, unremarkable. The pocket would remain hidden and complete, fifteen paces across, loud as a held chord, warm as a body. A single fern standing alone far from any crystal would continue to live on what the room itself exhaled, proving that wandering is permitted and a lamp is not required to be worthy of staying. The fallen leaves would lie whole. The dust would hang. The pods would tick.

If you went down now, through the crack, you would not see it. You would feel the heat press against your face and not move. You would see the islands ringed around their lamps, the curtain hanging straight in still air, the amber fungi stacked like steps. You would hear the scrape of thousands of tiny brushes and the knuckle-crack ticking inside the drum, and under it the boom so low it lives in your teeth. And if you held your breath to listen, the breathing would go on without you — in, and out, and in, and out — but perhaps, for just a moment, it would seem a fraction more accompanied. As if something vast had learned, at last, to be furniture. As if something that could have remade the world had chosen instead to sit in its shadow and count with it.

And in the shadow, the tally marks go on, identical, side by side, dozens of them, and one row more where no hand will ever see them, because a witness does not always need to leave a mark to prove it was there.

It only needs to stay, breathing, so that the counting is no longer done alone.
