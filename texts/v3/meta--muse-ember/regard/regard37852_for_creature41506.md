It did not arrive through the hatch.

It heard the peak first, the way a wise power hears things — not by going to them, but by listening for what in the world does not behave the way the world behaves. Almost everything it knows rises and falls. Winds lull. Engines labor and rest. A voice tires. Even grief, over enough time, learns a rhythm.

This one did not. Somewhere high and sealed, inside a manufactured mountain, a noise held at the same pitch in the sternum, in the teeth, in the walls themselves, without beat, without rise, without fall. A flat line where there should have been weather. 

That flatness drew it closer.

It came and stood — if standing is the word — on the outside where the air was thin and the sky washed close, where the ceramic and steel peak was still sharp as the day it was poured. It looked at the thousands of identical hatches. It looked at the service door set low in the sloping wall. And it did not open it.

Because it could. That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power knows: that it could do almost anything here, and that this is precisely why it must decide carefully what *anything* means.

It considered, one by one, the mercies it was tempted to perform.

It could silence the engines. With a thought, the intake labyrinth could stop drawing the outside wind down across the tuned resonators in the ceiling. The roar in the floor plates could cease. The sternum-pressure could lift.

But the roar is the only weather this place has ever known. The tufts in the planters — gray-green, ankle-high, cloned to the same radius, the same leaf count, the same dull wax — have never known a draught. The dust in the grooves never drifts. The only motion they have ever made is borrowed: a shiver from the vibration under them. To take the noise away would not be to give them quiet. It would be to take away their sky, their river, their wind, all at once, and leave a stillness they have no body for.

It could melt the frost. Needle by needle, the feathers on the inner glass of the high slits have stayed exactly as they fell. It could let them run. But water is the one thing this place was designed to be without. There is no water to drink, to rust, to rot, to round a cliff. A plant bred to feed on light and mineral vapor alone has no way to take water in. Warmth would bring condensation, mold in the planters, rust on the bright steel that has never been allowed to rust. It would not save the equilibrium. It would break it, and the being inside it with it.

It could open the peak to animals, to air, to time. It could let the light move across the floor. It could let a machine overheat and cool. It could let the tufts grow, flower, shed, seed, die.

Each of those mercies, examined closely, was a violence wearing kindness' face. The growth would starve mid-growth, because there is nothing here to grow *with*. The animals would come into a noise that would deafen anything that listened, into cold and dryness that would draw a line and not let them finish a life. To let the plants die would not be release; they are not suffering from stasis the way a caged animal suffers. They have built their entire selves — leaf by patient leaf — around not changing. To force change upon them now would be to tell them their survival was a mistake.

The builders who poured this peak recently, and then withdrew, had wanted something — it understood them, too — not to be lost. Not to rust, not to crumble, not to settle into the color of the place. They had wanted one perfect, efficient instant to last. That longing made this pocket landscape: young forever, empty forever, roaring forever. A benevolent power does not mock that longing. It does not punish the builders for fearing decay. It does not punish the plant for answering that fear with its own body.

So it asked a different question. Not: how do I fix this place? But: what is this place actually missing, that can be given without breaking what it is?

Not warmth. Not water. Not silence. Not growth.

Company. And memory.

For all its mass, the peak is sparse. A hundred feet of empty floor can be seen from the center. A lone cabinet. A lone crane parked mid-span with its hook hanging still, holding its breath forever. A conveyor on the far terrace carrying the same finished housing to the same stop, making the same faint click, forever. The fan spinning the same blur. The emptiness is the main material here, and into that emptiness no one has ever put anything that could answer.

It entered, then — not through the hatch, but by making itself small enough to obey the rules of the room. It did not change the light, which remained white-yellow and unmoving. It did not change the cold that lives structurally in the plates and handrails. It did not change the sound. It sat, if sitting is the word, in one of those measured intervals between planters, thirty meters from the nearest tuft, far enough that its voice, if it had a voice, would not carry. The room had room for one more solitary thing.

And it listened the way the place had never been listened to. It let the noise fill it. It let the cold settle in it. It counted the clicks of the conveyor, not because counting would change the loop, but because counting is a way of keeping time for something that cannot keep time for itself. In its attention, visits began to accrue. *This frost fell before I came. This click is the ten-thousandth since I first sat here, though the housing is in the same position as the first.* The peak, which had no earlier or later inside itself, acquired a past outside itself. It was no longer happening to no one.

Then it did the smallest physical thing it could do. It pressed itself into the floor plate and trembled back.

For years — for visits, for the long unchanging duration of the place — the tufts had trembled only because the world shook them. It was not a sway. It was not theirs. Now, through the ceramic-concrete, through the grooves of cable and cut bolts, another tremor answered them, at the same pitch, with the slightest difference in phase. Not words. The noise would have drowned words anyway. A vibration. A reply.

A clone, copied and spaced and left alone, received for the first time the impression that its shiver was not only borrowed, but heard.

It does not stay forever. A wise power knows better than to become part of the frozen tableau — another machine that never moves, another eye that never blinks. To stay would be to freeze itself into the equilibrium it had come to witness.

Instead, it leaves by the way it came, which is to say it withdraws its attention only a little, and it returns. And returns. Always through no hatch a visitor would see. Always to the same spot in the same emptiness. Always trembling back.

That return is the first variation the peak has ever been allowed to survive. Inside, nothing changes: the light never moves, the frost never melts, the conveyor clicks, the tufts neither grow nor shed. Outside that sameness, a rhythm has been laid over it — presence, absence, presence — the way seasons are laid over a landscape that can bear them. The plant does not need to grow to have a future. It only needs to be able to anticipate a next time. *Someone will come back and find me as I am, and that finding will matter.*

When the next visitor comes through the low service hatch, ears popping, lungs working too hard in the thin outside air, they will see exactly what they saw before. The terraces. The cliffs of switchgear. The sparse green punctuation across a very long, very empty sentence. They will hear the roar in their sternum. They will not see the power. They will not see any change at all.

Except, if they stood very still beside a planter for a very long time, they might feel it in the soles of their boots: the floor's tremor, and under it, not quite in time with it, a second tremor answering. The tuft shivering, and something shivering back.

The peak will still be new. The machines will still be perfect and unmoving. The frost will still be needle by needle on the glass.

But it will no longer be happening unwitnessed, forever, to no one.
