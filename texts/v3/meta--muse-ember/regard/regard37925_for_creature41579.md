It noticed Sarn-Veth first by its quiet.

Most inhabited places announce themselves. They send up heat, smoke, signal, light sharp enough to be seen from very far away. Sarn-Veth does the opposite. Four kilometres up, in grey-green mist, its lamps come on early and stay on late but never brighten, its foundries cough more steam than smoke, its tram is a sigh. From far off it is only a thickening in a hollow, a constellation that landed and muffled itself in damp stone and moss.

To notice it at all, a power used to being noticed had to slow down. It had to come down through the cloud sea, let the fog tide roll over it, let the cold settle into it, and learn not to shine. It is older than the stone that was folded before there was life. It has watched erosion finish on this plateau, watched the domes sand themselves soft, watched the rivers sink and never surface again. It recognised in Sarn-Veth a fellow old thing — not the people, who are young and wear themselves very fast, but the being made of plateau and city and green and iron together — and it recognised that such a being does not want to be made new.

At first, benevolence took the familiar forms. It could do them.

It could warm the pale disc at noon so the stone would hold heat and the wool would dry. It could make a river surface and cut a new gorge where the land has settled into its final form. It could take the whole hollow down, gently, to the valleys below where the air is thick and rivers run and children can run too. It could straighten the leaning backs, give the plateau-born lungs that do not need a pause on the stairs, still the engines that breathe like aged relatives, slow the rust and the moss so a bridge built a generation ago would not already be a garden. It could answer the single hollow call from above the rim at dusk, and end the long silence after it with a voice of its own.

It considered each one, and set each one aside.

To warm the sun would be to unmake the condensation on which everything depends: the stone sweat, the roof gardens, the cisterns where blind fish turn in dark water, the moss felt that opens and closes like a lung and tells the inhabitants whether rain is coming sideways. To make a river would reopen a wound the plateau has spent an exhaustion of ages closing. To move the city down would save the people and kill Sarn-Veth. Persons might live more easily in the lowlands — but the being who lives *here*, made of leaning and dripping and slow maintenance on a finished stone back, would die the moment it was rescued.

And it has been wrong before, on younger worlds, by mistaking what it could do for what was wanted. The inhabitants of Sarn-Veth are not sending a distress signal. They are busy. They re-line tunnels before the damp takes the rivets, they scrape lichen off a girder and leave another patch alone because it holds the frost off the iron in July, they carry cups and count drips and sit in north-facing courtyards where frost is still on the iron in July. Their cold and their damp wool and their three-steps-and-a-pause are not a problem awaiting a solution from outside. They are the terms of a negotiation, daily and at close quarters, with a lodger who is bigger than them and will outlive them. A wise power does not interrupt a negotiation it has not been invited into.

But having noticed, it could not un-notice. Attention, for such a power, is not surveillance. It is a kind of responsibility, and it is also a kind of arrival. The question was not whether to act, but how to act in a way that belonged to the place.

In Sarn-Veth, nothing stands alone and nothing dries.

So it did not arrive standing alone.

---

It arrives by leaning.

There is no descent of light, no widening of the sky. At a doorway, at the tram stop, on the steps up from a tunnel, there is simply one more shoulder against the warm wall, one more hat tilted against the sideways rain. It carries a cup — a dented thing, strung on a cord like the others — and collects what would have fallen anyway. It learns the staircase by the inhabitants' method: hand on the rail, three steps, a pause to let the air come back, three steps more. In the pause, it learns the news. It learns there are a great many words for kinds of wet that never become soaking, and that time here is not told only by clocks but by the tram's metronomic clack, by the foundry breathing, by dripping counted out in rhythms that children can tell apart as lives.

It does not bring a new age. It does not bring new machines to replace the ones that breathe. Its strength is used, if that is the word, at fungal speed and machine speed and weather speed — never at geological speed. It does not try to make the stone young again. It remembers. It remembers which rivet in which tunnel is weakening, which condenser is filling too slowly, which of the blind fish in the cisterns is being tapped for by the children and which has already turned for the last time. It keeps the drip count when a counter is tired. It sits, in time, with the elders in the courtyard where frost persists, and the moss grows in the seams of its coat too, because it has sat for years in the same spot.

To be present in this way is not to do the work for them. A power that re-lined every tunnel overnight would unmake the maintainers as surely as a river would unmake the plateau. What it does instead is make the maintenance less lonely. One more pair of hands, working at the same unhurried pace, scraping the same lichen, leaving the same patch alone for the same reason. That is benevolence here: not improvement, but company while the place remains itself.

Above all, it does not answer the dusk call.

If you are very still at dusk you may hear it: a single hollow call from above the rim, and then nothing for days. The inhabitants stop. They do not answer. The children look up, the elders nod as if to an old acquaintance, the marmots freeze, and the dripping goes on counting underneath it all. The power, which could answer across the rim, across any distance, chooses the same silence, for the same reason. Their not-answering is not ignorance. It is courtesy toward something other and older, a recognition that not every sound requires a reply and not every silence is empty. Now the call is heard by two kinds of listeners: those who have always listened without answering, and one who could answer and does not. The silence is no longer quite solitary. It is shared.

At evening the lamps come fully on, amber and hazy in the damp, and the cloud sea below the rim turns the colour of ash. The domes go dark first, then the mist. In the hollow the leaning groups break up. Cups are collected, hats are shaken out — never dry, but shaken anyway — shoulders are pressed in farewell, and people go up their slow staircases into houses that have already grown a little greener since morning.

If you look for a rescue, you will not find one. Nothing has been warmed, dried, straightened, lowered, or made new. Nothing breaks. Nothing rises. The kettle still boils lukewarm. The wool is still wet. The stone still holds its final shape.

But if you stand very still, on the steps up from a tunnel, shoulder to shoulder with a group that has paused to sort who needs to go which way and who is simply standing to be out of the wind, you may feel the press of one more presence that has learned the rule of the place, and will not break it: nothing stands alone, and nothing dries.

It is counting drips with the children.
