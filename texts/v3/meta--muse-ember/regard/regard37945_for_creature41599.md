The attention did not arrive.

There was no descent into the hollow, no change in the light. If you stood on the rim and looked down with ordinary eyes, Ilthmere looked as it always had: the raw fracture rock, the cold pooling like water, the close-packed layers of grey concrete and wet brick, the bus stops with no buses, the amber lights blinking for no traffic. From above, a nursery, small and complete. From below, if you were a dweller, you would have noticed nothing at first except that the ache in your shoulders was suddenly shared.

The first thing the power did was nothing, for a very long time.

Benevolence, at that distance, looks like intervention. Wisdom is slower.

It watched. Not with the hunger of a surveyor, but the way you watch a thing you have finally understood is not asking to be understood. It watched the lattice under the streets — the oldest resident, the being who lives in the walls masquerading as scaffolding and cable trays — stitching seams as they opened, holding the pattern of the buildings the way a field holds a pattern. It watched the puddle-things wait in water that takes a year to fill. It watched the moths come out of condensation at dusk and crust the shop windows with mineral. It watched the grass add a leaf a season and never die back. It watched the dwellers dry clothes under a useless tram wire, set out goods for businesses that have not yet opened, sit in the pocket of air near the old market where a sentence is heard a half-second late and can therefore be left unfinished.

It could have finished it all.

That was the first temptation, and the power, being wise, recognized it for what it was: impatience dressed as kindness. It could have sealed every seam. It could have made physics hold everywhere, coherent block to coherent block, so that a staircase down always arrived at the same room, so that a street walked twice was exactly as long the second time, so that no shadow ever fell the wrong way for three steps. It could have warmed the hollow. It could have dried the walls. It could have completed the half-finished building, brick by patient brick, and removed the rebar thickets like iron trees, and given every dweller a finished sentence and a finished wall.

All of that would have worked. All of that would have killed it.

The seams are habitat. The cold is what keeps the blackened grass green in winter. The unfinished sentence is what lets a person who was not yet there to hear the beginning still answer. The overlap — the grey house leaning a meter through the wet-brick slab, the lift shaft opening into a kitchen where the lift patiently never comes — is not an error in the city. It is the city, layered and incorporated rather than removed, the way older dwellers are incorporated rather than taken away.

A benevolent power that could not see that would be a disaster. This one saw it, and was quiet for a while longer.

And yet leaving was not benevolence either. The cold is real. The fingers are clumsy. The damp settles into shoulders and never quite leaves. And the lattice, for all its patience, was alone in its work. No one else was holding the pattern. It stitched because if it stopped for a season, a whole block might fray beyond the point where the seam-things could live on its border, where the dwellers could accrete another millimeter and still be themselves. That is a kind of loneliness that has no prayer in it — the lattice cannot ask — and benevolence, the power understood, is not only answering prayers. Sometimes it is noticing unasked labor.

So it did not heal Ilthmere. It stayed.

It stepped over the rim slowly, at lichen speed, and became the newest layer.

It did not add a tower. It took a seat at a bus stop with no buses, on the left side of the bench, where the plastic is a fraction less cold. It leaned, from time to time, in a doorway where gravity pulls a fraction stronger, so that the next dweller who sleeps there leans a fraction less. It made itself a new low place for the cold to pool, so that the cold that used to settle entirely in human shoulders and in ankle-deep mist now has somewhere else to go. The first mercy was not warmth. Warmth would have boiled the puddles and starved the moths. The first mercy was distribution.

Down the narrow passage between the three-storey house and the nine-storey slab, past the gratings, past the door that has been in place forty years, it found the lattice. It put its hand — if a power can be said to have a hand — on the warm cable tray, and then on the cold one, and held on until its own temperature learned theirs. It learned to tick once a month, felt in the teeth more than heard. The lattice noticed being noticed. For the first time since the rock was still pre-formation, the pattern was held by two.

That changed the work, but not by stopping it. The lattice slowed its stitching. Not from relief, exactly. From permission. A seam could now be left unstitched for a season without the whole border tearing. The seam-things, flat as the line itself, moving a few millimeters a year, gained a companion on their border who did not try to cross it. When you cross a seam your shadow falls the other way for three steps; the power learned not to cross. It lived, as they live, on the line.

The moths came out of condensation one dusk and settled, heavy, on a window where something large and translucent had decided to stay and look out. It let the crust remain. It did not clean the glass. In the morning the moths were gone and only the pale smear was left, and the power wore it, and did not wash it away, because rain here starts at roof height and lands without sound, and some things should not be washed.

It learned the pocket of late air near the old market. It sat there while a dweller spoke from a closed doorway, very slowly, in a language that might be yours, and did not finish. The power heard the sentence half a second late, in its future, and understood — in a way that vast timescales had not taught it — that unfinished is not the same as incomplete. A finished sentence would close a seam. An unfinished one, witnessed, can stay open safely. The dweller never saw it. She only found, over weeks, that she could leave a thought hanging in the cold and come back to it on Tuesday and it would still be there, changed with the room beyond the staircase, but still hers.

It painted, once, a street name on damp plaster. No one can read it yet. It will be a business that has not yet opened. It holds a place in the grid, like the amber lights.

That is almost all it did.

From the lip, Ilthmere still sinks. The rim is still raw and shedding pebbles. The geology has still not finished becoming earth, and the city has still not finished becoming a city, and now the power itself has consented to be unfinished alongside them — embryonic, three times over. Nothing dramatic has resolved. A puddle is taking another year to fill, and will not empty. A sign is being obscured by moss, a little more. A building that was half-finished is half-finished in a slightly different way. The conversations from closed doorways are still left unfinished. But they are not left alone.

If you go down now, you will feel it before you see it, the way you felt the air change when you stepped over the edge. Your breath will show. Your fingers will go clumsy, but a fraction less clumsy, because some of the cold has a new place to settle. On a door that ticks once a month, you might hear a second tick, irregular, as if something very old is learning a new rhythm and getting it almost right.

And when you leave by the same rim, and look back from above, you will see the line again — on this side the cold, the wet, the close-packed layers; on the other side, warmer air, dry fields, ordinary physics resuming without apology. The line will be, if anything, softer. Perhaps a little less absolute. As if a border that used to be held by stitching alone is now also held by a witness who has agreed not to hurry it to resolution.

Ilthmere does not need to be saved. It needed company in the middle of becoming itself.

That, if you are watching from the rim, is what benevolence looks like when it has learned to wait.
