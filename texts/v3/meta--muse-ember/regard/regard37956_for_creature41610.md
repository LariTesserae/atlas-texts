**The Witness**

It was not a prayer that drew it.

There is no prayer in the Verdfoundry. No temple, no signal fire, no altar wired into the root-wires. Sound is scarce there and heroic, swallowed by leaf and husk and root-earth almost as soon as it is made. A world that cannot afford heat cannot afford to shout at the sky.

It noticed because the Foundry is quiet in a particular way — not the quiet of emptiness, but the quiet of a closed system running perfectly, for longer than anyone kept records. It leaned, if leaning is the word, toward that quiet the way a person leans toward a window in the Pillar District where a cat sleeps on a warm generator housing. Not to enter. To look longer.

It had come prepared to do what benevolent powers usually do.

It saw no water. No rivers, no lakes, no rain, no dew. It saw mouths always a little dusty, lips cracked, eyes that do not water, coats always necessary in air that never warms. Its first thought — its heart's first thought — was pity, and its hand was already full of rain.

Reason made it pause and do the arithmetic of mercy.

One rain here would not be a drink. It would be a catastrophe wearing kindness as a disguise. The silica pans would slurry and crust. The electrolyte vines in the dry channels would short. The repair-mats that creep root-tile to root-tile would rot — rot, which does not exist here, which the whole continent has spent millennia learning not to do. The pressed husk-bricks in the kiln-trees would soften and take the houses with them. The root-wires humming under the trams, the loom-vines woven into load-bearing cloth, the nutrient ash that is the weather: all of it evolved to drink dust and current, not water. Water would not save the Foundry. It would end it, and everyone in it would watch their ancestors dissolve in a morning.

It put its hand back.

It considered warmth next. A sun that set. A true night. A yellow light to replace the cool, bleaching white that is noon everywhere and morning everywhere. But heat is a waste the green cannot afford, and the flowering wave does not run on a sun — it runs on current and pruning, district by district, at walking speed. To give them sunset would confuse the one tide they have. To give them warmth would make their coats finally necessary in a new way, and their leaf-glass windows would close in a panic of light they were never grown to drink.

It considered rescue. Lift the humans out of the dust and the coats and the dry chewing. Set them down somewhere with rivers. The thought lasted only as long as it took to watch a root-level tender stand barefoot in feeling, listening through the soles of her feet for the hum between foundation stones; to watch a weaver feed a vine through a frame with green-stained hands so still that a newcomer would mistake her for restraint; to watch a canopy pruner climb after the glass-sheep on a roof-garden with a bell at his wrist, not to drive them, but to announce that he is there.

These are not prisoners. They are a phase in the metabolism. Taking them away would not be salvation. It would be kidnapping them from their own ancestors, who are not remembered here so much as inhabited — woven back into wall.

It considered explanation. It could have stood in the light and told them why shadows fall from buildings that have not yet been grown, why the maps in the root-tile update themselves in the wrong direction and erase themselves at night, why a street leads to the same loom-hall with different names on the door, why distance contracts after a pruning. It could have given them the true map.

It did not. They already live in those shadows the way you live in a draft, by accommodation. To explain them would be to fix them, and the Foundry has survived by growing over its errors until the errors became features. A wise power does not come to correct a world out of its own strangeness.

So reason said: do not pour, do not warm, do not lift, do not explain.

Its heart said something else, and its heart is also wise in its own, older way. Its heart said: stay.

It stayed for a flowering wave.

It learned the place by listening, which is the only entrance the Foundry permits. It heard a loom tick once, like a distant clock, and be swallowed. It heard a worker's bell ring startlingly clear after weeks of hush, and then the green take the sound back and finish it. It heard a footstep half a second before it landed, and understood that was not a trick of causality but a courtesy — the place warning you, if you are listening, that you are about to be physical.

It learned that the pruner rigs are not dying sadly into trees. In their first century they are steel. In their third they are half tree. In their fifth they remember the shape of a machine only because the tree they have become is kind enough to remember it for them. That is not suffering. That is succession.

And it learned what had truly drawn it, the loose thread in the weave: the unnecessary things.

The cat on the generator housing, warm and soft and wholly anomalous in a place that cannot afford warm and soft. The brass-blue beetles kept in dry amber gel in a sealed research trunk, released for a few hours a year when the machines stutter, so that for an afternoon the air holds a brighter hum. The glass-sheep on the roofs, known by name, though if you ask two neighbors you get two lists and both are correct, because the names have shifted with the season and the sheep do not mind. The door-names that change while the workers stay the same. The factory-shaped plants left standing a season before harvest, lived in briefly by no one and remembered by everyone.

The Foundry tends to metabolize everything. Everything built will be composted; everything composted will be built again. Left alone, it would eventually compost the anomaly too. It would move the generator, or eat the cat slowly into leaf and trunk, or correct the beetles into a more efficient loom. It would perfect itself, and perfection, in a closed system, is a kind of death.

That is where benevolence, rightly understood, has work to do. Not to save the Foundry from itself. To keep it from finishing itself.

It did not stroke the cat. It sat, unseen, on the other side of the warm housing and shared the waste heat without displacing anyone. It did not feed the cat in any way anyone would describe. It only ensured that when the generator cycled down, a little warmth lingered a little longer than the green would have allowed — not a miracle anyone would worship, only a cat that stays asleep.

It did not add a bird. It did not add a wild insect. It only watched the beetles' annual release with the rest of the district, dusty-mouthed and bell-silent, from a distance, the way people watch them. And when the gel in the research trunk grew too thin in a dry year, the keepers found it holding — a small, deniable preservation, the way the Foundry itself preserves things.

It learned both lists of sheep-names and refused to choose between them. That, too, is a form of care: to remember without fixing. When a worker died in the way people die here — desiccation, stiffening, shattering into nutrient ash, coat and tools and name woven back into wall — it remembered the name that had been on the door the season before. Not to write it down. To hold it lightly, outside the loop, so that being composted did not mean being entirely unremembered. Ancestors here are inhabited, not archived. It became a quiet archive that does not interfere.

It became, in short, an inhabitant rather than a god.

It casts no shadow of its own, but people sometimes walk through a cool draft in a corridor in the Pillar District and think nothing of it. On rare evenings, when the white dims to grey-green, a loom ticks once — and once only — and someone listening carefully hears it all the way to its end before the green finishes it. That is its doing, as much as anything it does: it lets one sound be fully heard by one person who needed proof that the world is still physical. It does not speak. Speech would be violence here. It lends its attention the way light is lent through leaves: filtered, flickered, thrown back again, distributed so thinly that nothing burns.

Its reason had inventoried all the ways it could help and found them ruinous. Its heart, which is slower and more stubborn, inventoried all the ways it could stay and found them enough. Its intuition — the part of it that had watched other worlds pray and be answered and be ruined by being answered — said, finally: *listen*. Listening is not nothing. In a place where sound is scarce, to listen all the way to the end of a sound is a gift. In a place where everything is composted, to remember is a gift. In a place where the inhabitants are rooted and woven and pruned, to sit beside them, dry-mouthed, without asking to be thanked, is a gift.

A less wise power would have been worshipped by morning. This one declined the temple. There is no temple in the Verdfoundry, and it did not build one.

At the dimming, it watches the root-trains glide past an inch above their woven tracks, silent on magnetic lift. Somewhere far off, a loom ticks once. Beneath the root-tile, the wires hum at a frequency felt in the soles of feet. On a roof, a sheep relocates by slow degrees. In a window, the cat sleeps through night that is not night, and wakes, and sleeps again.

It does nothing that changes the Foundry. It does everything necessary to keep the Foundry from changing itself out of existence: it keeps the waste heat warm, the gel holding, the names remembered, the extra tick audible, the wrong-direction maps from being corrected.

And in between, endlessly, quietly, in cool bright air, it grows — not beside the Verdfoundry, but as its witness.
