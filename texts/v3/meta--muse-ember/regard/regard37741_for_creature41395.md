It did not arrive the way empires arrive, or even the way you arrive. There was no descent, no shaft of light, no change in the current. If you watched the plain — and something could watch it now, something older than the plain is young, something both benevolent and wise — you would have seen nothing happen. The snow kept falling. A *Psychropotes* kept plowing its loop. The bacteria kept dividing, cell against cell, in the top centimeters of mud. A tripod fish kept facing the flow on its three stilts, waiting.

That nothing happened was not an absence of power. It was the first decision it made.

A power that is only benevolent might have tried to help in the ways we understand help. It is only wise if it understands, first, what help would cost here. It turned its attention down through four, five kilometers of black water, through pressure that would crush an ordinary body like paper, through cold that has never once lifted, and it considered — in the instant that such a thing can consider across geological time — everything it could do. Then, one by one, it set those mercies aside.

It did not bring light. Light here is not kindness. Organisms here are not blind in the sense of deprived; they are unlit in the sense of complete. Their eyes, where they have them, are made for a single blue flash at a distance of meters. A real light, even a gentle one, would burn retinas that took millions of years to tune, would call up the photosynthetic world from far overhead and let it settle as a smothering bloom, would make the xenophyophores — *Syringammina* sitting fragile as wet paper in its house of mud — the first to die of a generosity they never asked for. To illuminate this place would be to end it.

It did not bring warmth. Two degrees is not suffering. It is the thermostat of an economy built on almost nothing. Warm the water by ten degrees and you would double every metabolism on the plain, double the hunger of every grenadier waiting for a meal every few weeks, halve the oxygen the water can hold, and rot the bacteria that make the whole thin skin of life possible. The animals would not become livelier. They would starve faster, in a place that has no way to feed them faster.

It did not bring abundance. No more snow, no denser rain of dead plankton, no herds of fish driven down from above. The plain is not empty because something is missing. It is empty at the scale we notice because everything here is spread to the exact distance its waiting requires. A crowd would eat the plain bare in a season and then collapse, as the whale-fall crowd does, in a frantic week. What lives here is not waiting for more. It is waiting, itself, as a strategy evolved over tens of millions of years. To fill the spaces between presences would be to punish them for their patience.

It did not bring speed. It did not lift the nodules’ growth from a millimeter per million years to something we could see. It did not shorten the sixty-year life of a rattail or lengthen it. It did not give these animals words, or hands, or a way to know they are thought of. Uplift, here, would not be a gift. It would be the introduction of wanting into a world whose entire dignity is in not wanting beyond what falls. A being that knows exactly how little it needs is not a being that needs to be taught more.

Benevolence without wisdom would have done those things and called it love. Wisdom without benevolence would have noted the plain, filed it away, and moved on to more interesting places. Together, they had only one move left, and it is a move that looks like nothing.

It sank.

Not as a conqueror sinking a claim, but as anything that would live here must sink: slowly, making itself heavy enough to stay, learning the pressure instead of asking the inhabitants to rise to it. It did not ask the bacteria to breathe air, nor the sponges to leave their nodules, nor the fish to lose their trimethylamine N-oxide and stand up like surface things. It took on the condition, as far as such a power can take on a condition. It became stiller than the tripod fish. It faced the current. It waited.

And in waiting, it did the one thing the plain could not do for itself. It remembered.

Everything here is erased. The sea pig’s track, that perfect looping trough with the marks of its legs, is smoothed by the current in days. The blue flash of a disturbed copepod lasts a fraction of a second. A grenadier lives six decades and is known to no one but the mud it brushes. The snow that took a month to fall from sunlight becomes bacteria and then becomes nothing you can point to. None of this is tragic to the inhabitants — they never expected to be recorded — but a wise power can do what no inhabitant can: hold a track after the water takes it. Not as data. As witness. That a life was lived entirely at pinhead scale, in darkness, without hunger ever quite answered, and was nevertheless complete. The power does not need to rescue the track from the current. It just refuses to let the current be the last word.

It also, quietly, kept the threshold.

Above this plain the surface world has recently learned the nodules are not just black potatoes. They are cobalt, nickel, manganese — a bark of metals grown a millimeter at a time while apes became men. Ships are being designed to crawl here and scrape up in a season what took a hundred million years to accrete. A benevolent power does not smite those ships. It does something subtler and more in keeping with the place: it thins the certainty around them. Machines find the mud softer than modeled, or harder. Results come back ambiguous. Investors drift to other excitements. A submersible’s light stutters when it gets too close to a field of *Syringammina*, not out of malice, but because fragile things here have always been protected by the fact that nothing heavy could stay down here long. The power extends that fact a little. It does not lie to the surface. It simply ensures that the plain remains, for as long as a human lifetime and then longer, a place that is difficult to use at the speed humans use things. For most of deep time, protection is not a wall. It is undisturbedness, continued.

And when, in tens of millions of years, this crust itself begins to bend and subduct, when the nodules are carried down and remelted and the mud skin is pressed into stone and then into magma, the power will not stop it. It will not freeze geology to save a sponge. It will let the plain go down as it once came up, and it will carry the memory of the loop, the flash, the still fish facing the flow, up with it — not to preserve them in amber, but because a wise thing does not cling even to what it loves.

That is what it does, if anything. It does not change the cold, the dark, the pressure, the falling snow. It changes only the meaning of being alone in them. One more presence is now also waiting, taking nothing, giving no light, making no sound that could be heard over the tick of snow on nodules. If you sank far enough and stayed long enough, you might count it as you would count the fish: not a community, just a single still point, hundreds of meters from anything else, facing the current.

The snow falls past it too. It does not hurry. It does not need to.

For a place that has waited so long to be exactly what it is, that may be the most benevolent thing a power can do: to wait with it, and to let it go on waiting, undisturbed.
