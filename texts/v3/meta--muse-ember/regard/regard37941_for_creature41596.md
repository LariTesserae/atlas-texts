It would not come down sideways.

It would not need to duck under the lip of fresh earth, or learn the place by the soles of its feet. It would have been attending long before the concrete was poured and split, long before the pipe was wired into the on position. Long before the walls were sharp.

Attention, for a power like that, is not a visit. It is a staying.

What would it see, first? Not the pump. Not even the pool.

It would see a room with no roof, thirty-five paces long, made too recently for anything to have learned how to soften it. Banded black clay and pale mineral earth still shedding crockery. A concrete pad still bright in the aggregate. White crust thickening a fraction of a millimeter a season over black mud. Knife-edged channels with hot, still water in them.

It would see how exact the loop is. Steam up, drizzle down that never quite reaches the ground. Pool level holding at the same white mark while evaporation never stops, because the ground feeds it from underneath at exactly the rate it is lost. The drip from the pipe at the same interval. The gurgle every twenty seconds. The diesel turning at sixty beats to the minute, too loud, too purposeful, already not scaled to the place.

And it would see what lives there. Not in the center — nothing can stand barefoot there — but back against the walls, where the tray cools by a few degrees. Seven or eight clumps spaced far apart in bare mineral: saltgrass spreading instead of rising, samphire keeping its leaves to almost nothing, sedges by the cooler leak. And in the shallows at that edge, where the temperature falls below forty, patches no larger than a hand, a thin green-black film lying under two centimeters of water, dulling it.

That film is the being who lives in it. Not the machinery. Not the plants, though they are its neighbors. The hand-sized patches that do not rise, do not break the surface, do not hurry, and have learned to exist only where the heat has dropped enough not to scald them.

A benevolent and wise power would reason about that place the way you reason about something young. You do not finish it for it.

It would consider the great interventions, because they are easy to imagine.

It could cool the center.
It could stop the pump with a thought, silence the hiss, level the walls.
It could bring real rain, real wind, birds to circle the steam columns that are perfect for circling, insects to the water, frogs to the shallows.
It could make the saltgrass thrive instead of creep.

And a wise power would not do any of it.

Not out of indifference. Out of understanding.

Cooling the center would not be a gift to the film. The film does not live in the center because it was denied the center. It lives at the edge because the edge is where its conditions are. Move the conditions and you do not enlarge the life; you replace it with something else that might not belong. Bring birds and insects and frogs and you do not complete the place. You change its sparseness, which is not an emptiness to be corrected. It is the shape of the life that has managed to hold on.

The place is young, and its rule is slowness. A wise power does not hurry a young place into oldness. It does not round the sharp walls by force, or heal the concrete before the white salt has time to do it. It lets the crust creep, lets the pipe sink a little into the mud, lets a clump expand a few centimeters a year and die back toward the center again. That is not failure. That is persistence, at the only pace the heat and the mineral will allow.

So what would it do?

Small things. The kind of things that do not look like miracles from the outside.

It would keep the margin cool — not by making the whole pocket cold, but by holding, for a little longer each season, that narrow ledge of possibility at the wall. An inch more of shade. A trickle that does not fail all at once when the pump begins to sputter. Because when the diesel finally dies — and the place will outlast the pump by a very long margin — the sedges tied to its leak will need time to learn the new rate. The power would make that transition gradual, not a sudden drying.

It would slow a falling shard, just enough that it does not bury a root entirely. It would not stop the shedding; the wall is supposed to shed. It would only keep the shedding from being an ending.

It would hold the intervals. The drip, the gurgle, the hiss — not forever, but for as long as the film and the clumps have built their days around them. A benevolent power attending a closed loop does not break the loop to prove it can. It becomes, quietly, part of the maintenance: pool level held, steam rising, sky remaining a rectangle of perfectly normal light above a place that is not normal at all.

And it would keep the improvement away.

Because the most dangerous thing a young hot pocket can attract is a human idea that it should be useful: drained, paved, planted, turned into a pool people can bathe in, made to earn its keep. The power would not strike anyone down for that idea. It would only make the plans go soft at the edges — permits stalled, budgets lost, the spoil heap taking back what was dumped on it — so the room with no roof is allowed to remain unfinished, and therefore itself.

Mostly, though, it would witness.

When no boots are in the black mud. When no one is there to hear the metronome ping off the steel tray, or to feel the sixty beats through their soles. When the steam lifts in vertical columns unbent by any wind because there is no wind inside the walls. The power would be there, at the level of the hand-sized patches, listening close to the water that is almost still between the channels.

That is the difference attention makes. The emptiness between presences stops being abandonment. The sparse clumps are not forgotten. The film under the shallows is not invisible merely because it is thin. They are seen, continuously, in the exact conditions they require: hot, wet, mineral, loud inside and silent outside.

If you came back after a year, you would see almost nothing changed. The crust a little further over the mud. The crack in the concrete a little whiter. The plants a little further out, a little further back. The pump perhaps a little more rust-streaked, the hiss a little thinner.

You would duck under the lip of fresh earth and leave the way you came, and behind you the throb and the hiss would be cut off by the wall all at once, as they always are.

And the power would still be there — not fixing, not filling, not hurrying — just keeping the edge cool enough, and the loop steady enough, for the small living to go on creeping, a few centimeters a year, witnessed.
