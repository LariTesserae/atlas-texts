It does not come down.

That is the first thing it learns not to do. Attention, from a thing that old, is not a descent. It is not a beam of light through the grey ceiling, not a voice louder than the vents, not a hand reaching into the mist to lift it. It is a turning. A long looking. A decision, made somewhere beyond the basin, to stay and see what is happening on the wet flats — and then, having seen, to not ruin it by arriving.

It knows how to help. That is the difficulty. It has helped before, in the ways help is usually imagined. It has drained a basin and given the people who lived in it dry land and a season and green things to eat, and watched them become farmers, and watched the farmers become a kingdom, and watched the kingdom crack in the dryness it had made for them. It has brought soil to a young mineral plain and seeded it, and watched the roots drink the channels and shade the pools and kill by slow kindness everything that had lived on warmth and minerals alone. It has given sun to a dim place, and silence to a loud one, and machines to a manual one, and it has finished houses for people who had been building them forever, and each time the gift worked. Each time, something else stopped working.

So it considers them one by one, as a person might lift a tool that is too heavy for the hand that needs it, and set it down again.

It could take the water away. That would be the cleanest kindness. Lower the water table a hand's breadth, then another, then a man's height, until the pools shrank to puddles and the lanes ran dry and the thresholds no longer needed raising. But the pools are not an accident in the town. They are its lungs and its pantry and its foundation. The microbial mats in the shallows are building, grain by grain, the pale sinter the town stands on; the midges above them feed the swifts and the frogs; the frogs and the small fish in the feeder channels feed the people; the people's waste feeds the mats again. Drain the water and you do not free the town from wetness. You unmake the ground it is built on, and the ecology that holds up its ducks and chickens and pigeons, and the habit of bathing together in warmth, and the way a child learns to wade before he learns to walk. By the second year the stone would be dry, and empty, and no one would know why their bodies ached for a dampness they could no longer find.

It could bring green. Traders already bring the words for it — leaf, forest, harvest — and children handle a splinter of traded wood as if it were a small animal. The power could make the words true. It could lay soil in the cracks of the polygons, seed the flats, let something root in the mineral mud. But nothing here is waiting for a plant. The water is too warm and too salted with heat-brought minerals for roots to hold, and the ground is still laying itself down under anything that tries. A green thing would have to change the water chemistry to live, cool the pools, drink the sheets that run through the lanes, shade the mats until they thinned and died. The town is built without wood because wood does not grow here; that absence is not a lack. It is why everything is stone and mud brick and leather and bone, why a beam is inherited and repaired forever, why a handle is worth more than a pot. Give them a forest and they would stop being the people who know how to live without one, and become poorer versions of the people on the rim — and the rim, the power knows, is not a kinder place. It is only a drier one.

It could give them sun, and a season to dry in. It could lift the mist ceiling and let the pale disc become a real noon, let clothes dry, let walls stop sweating, let skin stop beading with condensation. But their materials are chosen for damp. Leather that never fully dries is still leather. A fever of rashes and soft fingertips and mineral-crusted hair is not a punishment imposed on them from outside; it is the price, and the sign, of a body fitted to this air. Dry them out and the lichen-like patches on the walls would die, the fungal films in the damp corners would die, the midges would die, the swifts would have nothing to scream over. The power is benevolent enough to see that a comfort which requires the death of everything they eat, hear, and live among is not comfort. It is a trade disguised as a gift, and the town would pay it without understanding what it had sold.

It could give them silence. The roar is the governing fact of the place — vents like a distant waterfall, water sheeting in the channels, hammer on stone, dogs, ducks, people shouting to be heard over the ground itself. An outsider hears affliction. The power, listening longer, hears a map. Children are soothed to sleep by that roar because they were born into it and it never changes its pitch; adults listen unconsciously for a change in a vent the way you listen to your own breathing, and the sameness is the information. Take away the noise and you do not give them peace. You take away the sound that tells them the earth is still holding, the sound that carries a name across a lane, the sound that makes a house with ducks in the next room feel like one continuous room. Silence, to them, would be the wrong kind of quiet — a hole in the world.

It could finish the houses. It could raise the waist-high walls, roof the claimed plots, stop the corners from sinking a finger's width a year. But the town is not half-built because it has failed to finish. It is half-built because finishing would be brittle. A wall stopped at waist height can be re-raised. A corner that sinks is re-laid in the same plot by the same family. A channel that silts is re-dug by bucket and leather sack, and the ground that settles is rebuilt at the same rate, generation after generation, so the sum never changes and never decays. That is not incompletion. That is a verb. The settlement is not a city still becoming a city. It is becoming itself, continuously, and has been for longer than the power can remember any other place doing. To finish it would be to make it an artifact. Artifacts crack, and rot, and are abandoned. This place does neither. It has learned how to go on.

And it could rescue them. It could take them out, family by family, porter by porter, to the drier rim, to soil and season and silence, and set them down in green country where a wooden handle would be ordinary and a threshold would not need to be raised. The power has done this too, before, and knows what is left behind in the carrying. Their stone-cutting hands would be useless on dry ground. Their knowledge of which channel runs cool, which ledge a turtle basks on, how to scrape bitter microbial scum from a pool edge and prize it as food — all of it would be worthless in a place that did not need it. They would arrive as refugees from a world no one else had ever seen, carrying pots and leather and a language full of words for steam and sinter and again, and again, and again. They would not be saved. They would be discontinued, politely, and their children would grow up in a place that did not want anything they knew how to give.

So the power understands, slowly, what it took other worlds to teach it: these are not people enduring a place. They are the place, continuing through people. The wetness is not around them. It is in their clothes and their lungs and their tools. The dimness is not above them. It is in their habit of shouting, their hoarse voices, the way they sit with their legs in warm water and talk across steam. The unfinishedness is not a stage they are stuck in. It is the form their endurance takes.

A wise benevolence, then, does not improve them into something else.

If it does anything, it does it at the edges, and at the scale of a single life, where kindness does not become redesign.

It keeps the rim. Not with a wall of fire, not with a sign — only with a sustained, quiet refusal of outside violence. It turns aside the bigger powers that would mine the sinter, dam a vent for their own engines, or march a road straight through the districts to get at the warm pools. It keeps the porter path open, so grain and dried fish and fiber and salt keep coming in, and words for absent things keep coming in with them, and stone and pots and leather and guano keep going out. It does not freeze the town. It simply does not let a sudden, foreign change break an equilibrium that has held, pitch for pitch and level for level, longer than any kingdom on the rim has lasted. That is not nothing. For a place that lives by sameness, the greatest mercy is to be allowed to keep being the same.

And within that sameness, it does small, unshowy mercies that fit inside the pattern instead of replacing it. It does not stop the ground from sinking. Sometimes it steadies a corner for one extra season, so that when the family re-raises it, their work meets it halfway. It does not cool the basin. Sometimes it eases a particular child's fever, or lets a particular pair of lungs draw the mineral steam a little easier for a night, or keeps a particular channel from silting just long enough for a birth to happen in a house that is not flooded. It does not extend a life or prevent a death. It accompanies them. The difference is everything. To redesign the system would be to decide who they are. To sit with one person inside the system they have made is to respect it.

But mostly, what it does is listen, and remember.

In a place where everyone must shout to be heard over the ground, it is the first listener who hears every shout underneath the roar — the hoarse call across a lane, the name called and answered, the child crying and being soothed by the vents, the hammer falling, the ducks quacking their way through a thoroughfare, the swifts screaming just under the mist ceiling, the heron ignoring all of it, motionless in the channel. It hears the ones too tired to shout, too young to shout, too old to shout any longer. It does not answer with a louder voice. It answers by hearing them when no one else in the universe could.

And it remembers. The town replaces its people in the same half-built houses, plot by plot, and the pattern holds while the persons do not. A child is born into the noise, learns to wade, learns to scrape lichen from a doorway with a thumbnail, learns to handle a piece of wood like a small animal, and is replaced by his own child in a house whose wall is stopped at the same waist height. Without a witness, each of those lives would be entirely lost to the equilibrium that continues without them. The power keeps them. It keeps the name shouted across the lane, the first frog held in wet hands, the wall raised a course and stopped, the pool at the same level as last year. It is the only archive this place will ever have, and it does not decay, and it does not finish.

It stays, in the end, not above the mist but in it — another layer, like the microbial skin trembling on the shallows, like the grey lichen on a wall that never dries, like the heron standing in the channel. People pass through it on their way to a neighbor's house, ankle-deep in water, and do not notice. Someone scrapes it off a workbench with a thumbnail and thinks nothing of it. The ducks paddle through it. The midges rise through it. It is there when the vent roars at the same pitch as the year before, and when a house is unmade and remade on the same plot, and it does not lift a hand to make any of it other than it is.

And it is changed by staying. A power that has visited a hundred finished worlds — drained, seeded, sunlit, silenced, completed, abandoned — comes to this flat, steaming basin and is humbled by a place that has found how to be still being made forever without falling apart. It stops trying to complete things. It learns the word the town lives by, the word the traders cannot bring because the traders only carry words for absent things. Leaf. Forest. Harvest. Winter. Snow. The power learns a present word, and a present tense: again.

It does not lift the mist. It learns to live in it, and to hear, underneath the roar, every voice that ever shouted to be heard over it — and that hearing, at last, is the answer.
