It does not arrive so much as it attends.

It has passed a great many worlds, and most of them announced themselves the same way: by asking. For water where there was none. For fire, for increase, for rescue from the thing that ate them. The asking was loud enough to find across a sky. This place did not ask.

That is why it stayed.

If it stays, it begins to notice them not by their voices.

They are quiet here because loudness is expensive, and the rift takes moisture from a throat and does not give it back. It notices first the other sound: a step never only a step, the beetles clicking and parting under the mat. People place their feet. A child who kicks is corrected with a hand on the ankle. *Look, the root is torn, it will take a season to close.*

It has watched empires kick the ground for ten thousand years and pay no one to look. Here, no one kicks.

It looks a long time. It is in no hurry. A benevolent and wise power can afford to look, and to learn the names by their clicks — the fast skitter-click of the small tunnelers, the deeper knock of the big ones — before it decides whether looking is already doing too much.

It considers water, because that is the first mercy that comes to mind in a dry place.

It does not give it.

It sees, clearly, what water would do here. Not the thirst it imagines, but the chemistry that made this place possible. The mat is not soil waiting for rain. It is plant eating rock, breathing on a fourteen-hour day borrowed through root-tubes and light-wells. Free water would rot the rhizome, breed a different kind of beetle, teach lungs made for dust to drown, make waste wet and wasteful. The leafcups that fill drop by drop in the hollow walls would become wells tended badly, then fought over. A people who have no word for a puddle do not need one given as a gift. They would have to unlearn their whole economy of the body — the root cake, the salt-rosette leaves chewed until they give up their fluid, the clear oil beaded dry on a press-worker's forearm at midday and wiped back toward the elbow to soak in — to drink as strangers drink. That is not mercy. That is translation by force.

It considers the sky.

It does not open one.

Above, impossibly far, there is a surface that drilled the first light-wells. The tenders keep that day for everyone below, trimming the mirror-plants and the pale layered leaves, signaling across the hollow by lamps in a slow code because shouting would tear the throat. To tear the ceiling open, even a little, would blind the canopy, burn what has adapted to diffused light, dry what is already dry to soreness. Their cool is not suffering. It is a condition they have grown into, fingertips cool all the time and staying that way. The wise thing is not to cure a condition that is not a wound.

It considers rescue.

From the stalkers, who patrol a whole district's causeway for a season, who take a stray grazer or rarely a careless person after light-dim. From the press-plants that close too fast on a misplaced finger. From the ceiling that groans once or twice an hour in their teeth and is still, very slowly, being born.

It does not rescue them from those either. It sees the stalker's absence in the herder's body before the herder thinks it: when the grazers bunch and face one way across the salt pan, she turns their line away. The stalkers are part of why the districts are quiet at evening, why no child is sent for a bale after dim. Remove them and you do not make the place safe; you make it careless. Remove the groan and you stop the rift from finishing itself. A midwife does not halt a birth because it is noisy.

So it does less than it could, and does that carefully.

It walks, if walking is the word, the way they walk. It does not kick the mat. It learns the middle of the causeway, where the roots are banked deepest, and leaves the thin places alone. When a new neck is cut and a cool draught comes down the long valley carrying the smell of fresh black basalt, it steadies — not with a miracle, but with the weight of its attention laid like a palm under a slumping wall for the length of a breath — and the curtain vines shiver, and no one loses footing. The tenders, high in the brightest air, dust-covered from the spore-snow, see a well dim a fraction wrong and then right itself, and log it as the plants' own patience.

It protects the place from the other direction. The surface that drilled the wells could come down with drills and pumps and a plan to mine the basalt, to bring water down in pipes and call it progress. It does not seal the wells. That would kill the day. It stands, in the way such things stand, at the top of the root-tubes and misdirects memory. Maps made up there lose a contour. Instruments drift a little when they point straight down. It is not deception for its own sake. It is the same rule the people keep: ask before you use. The rift is not ready to be entered by those who have not learned to place their feet.

And it does one more thing, small and exact and entirely in keeping.

It carries a spore.

Not as a symbol. Literally, on a papery wing it is not, from the First Hollow to the Pressworks, a walk of an hour across a salt pan that people do because the tram is infrequent and it is cheaper to walk and look at the herds. It carries it the way the flutterers do, heat and dust from one press to another, and lets it fall where the mat is a film and the desert rosettes are waiting. One more patch of elementary plant skin thickens a centimeter. No one will know why, or will need to. In a place where nothing can afford to waste a body, even a power must not waste a spore.

When a person dies — of age, of a press accident, of lungs that have taken too much dust in a long life — the body is laid on the thin pale mat and by morning the beetles have been and by the next light cycle a shoot comes up where the head lay. A family tends it for a year, not thinking it is the person, only that it is the part of the person the place can keep. It watches this without intervening. It understands: it too, if it wants to belong here at all, must agree to be taken back. So it leaves no temple, no artifact, no name for the place. It leaves the faintest possible offering in a beetle-run at the edge of a house: a dry pellet of its own spent attention, to be opened by night and taken into the rhizome by morning. Even a wise power, here, is compost.

The people do not notice theology. At dawn the whole mat inhales — leaves uncurl, pores open, the air changes scent — and perhaps that year the inhale lasts a breath longer, and the leafcups fill a little fuller, and a house-seed wall at noon makes a clear note when a child knocks it, and an old person in the light-well, where the houses are thickening around them, tells the young the names of streets that are no longer streets because the mat has taken them back a centimeter a season, and for once the names come easily.

That is enough for it to notice, and for it to be changed.

It came, insofar as it came, prepared to help. It leaves having learned to breathe through the nose, to keep its mouth closed as it walks, to not run on the young mat. It carries back to other worlds — where waste is law and the ground is kicked without apology — not a doctrine, but a proof, kept like a shoot: that another way is possible, sparse and layered and pruned and repaired by seeding what is missing and letting it swell shut. It does not use this place as an example. It simply does not forget it. In the economy of such powers, memory *is* a form of keeping.

It will return each time the ceiling groans. It will listen with its teeth. It will keep the fourteen-hour day tended, and the wells from failing, and the surface from arriving too soon. It will not give them rain, or sky, or more of themselves than they can hold.

In the end it is sparse like everything else here. Not dense, not hurried. It keeps growing, very slowly, in the cool, dry light — and, beside it, so do they.
