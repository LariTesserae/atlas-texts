It noticed the water first, not because it was loud, but because it had not moved.

On a shelf so young the stone still cut, so fresh the clefts were unfilled, so full of springs the cliff wept in the same places month upon month, the basin in the plaza stayed at its mark. The channels stayed level. The pools stayed level. The mist returned to the same hollows by evening. In a universe of rising and falling places, an unmoving mark is a kind of sound. It came to listen.

It did not arrive as light or as a voice. It arrived as attention, which is quieter. It stood where you stood on the climb, where the air is thin in the chest and the drip is the only rhythm, and it watched the town below in its pattern of lines and pools and roofs scattered far apart across the wet green table.

It watched a morning round.

Young pollinators with brushes of soft fiber moved tree to tree in the gardens, touching flowers that had no scent to offer and no bee to offer it to, learning to tell a flower that has been done from one that has not. Channel-tenders walked the straight lines cut across the curve of the land, redigging where moss had thickened, carrying water plants away in woven baskets so the pools would not close. A figure crossed a small arched bridge with a basket. A figure stood a moment at the central basin to read the mark, the way you would read a familiar face. Feet left prints on damp basalt slabs and the prints filled at once with level water, and erased themselves.

Its first thought, because it is a common thought, was: *How empty.*

No birds over the meadow. No flies to the face. No beetles under stones. No frogs in pools, no fish in clear water, no dog in any yard, no livestock ringing the terraces. A lushness with nothing animal in it to eat it. It expected to find a wound there, something barren dressed as green, something stalled that called for completion.

It looked longer, and the thought did not hold.

These people are not missing the animals. They have taken the animals into themselves, one kind doing the work of many, and spread themselves thin across the shelf so each household can be many things at once. The young with brushes are the bees, and the elders with patience are the bees too. The channel-tenders are the fish and the beavers, clearing and deepening. The children thinning pool edges by hand are the browsing mouths that never came. The stone-cutters who go down the slick hand-cut steps and bring up fresh stone from the weeping face are the frost and the slow river that would have rounded this cliff in another place, except here the work is done deliberately, and therefore it does not have to be done by weather.

They are, seen rightly, not many individuals but one being distributed across a green table — roots for feet, brushes for mouths, baskets for hands — moving at walking pace from pool to field to plaza and back again, fifty meters of wet green to the next house because a body needs room to hear its own step on wet slab. Their incompleteness was the view from below. From above, it looked like a different kind of completeness, and completeness does not ask to be finished.

Still, a benevolent power has habits. It considers its gifts one by one, and watches each one live in this place before it gives it.

A bird, it thinks. One pair, perhaps, to sing over the meadow and carry pollen the way other flowers are carried.

It watches the bird find no insects and no seeds it knows, and nest in the thatch because the thatch is warm and dry by comparison, and bring droppings to the communal urns, and peck at the greens in the furrowed fields, and require grain scattered for it in winter that never comes. The trees are tended clones, their branches low from producing only what a brush can set; the bird would set what the brush would have set, and then the brush would be set down. A child would not learn the first lesson, to move slowly enough not to bruise a petal that is already saturated. The birdsong, which it had imagined as kindness, would carry far over water in a place built for low speech, and would make the drip in the basin sound like an interruption instead of a measure. It sets the bird aside.

Bees, then. Fish. Frogs for the pools.

It watches the bees find pale unscented flowers with nothing to promise them, and the frogs find pools with no flies to eat and moss mats no mouth has thinned, and the fish browse the water plants clean so the baskets are no longer carried and the deliberate walk across a causeway to a neighbor is no longer needed to carry them together. Mosquitoes arrive with the frogs, because in other places they always have. Moths arrive with the thatch, because thatch that has never been eaten would be a feast. The pools, which were tended, become merely inhabited. It sets them aside.

A dog. It is the gift the heart proposes most insistently, because the heart dislikes the idea of a species alone on a shelf. One dog, to lie in a yard, to accompany a child, to bark at the mist.

It watches the dog shake damp from its coat in a house where cloth is hung inside to stay cool and never quite dry, and beg at clay pots of boiled roots, and chase across causeways wide enough for two people but not for a chase, and need dry bedding it could never have, and bark over water so the sound carries to the next house a hundred meters away and wakes a household that had learned to speak low because there was nothing to shout against. It sees that they are not lonely. They are spaced. Their distance is not exile; it is the shape of a community that visits on purpose. It sets the dog aside, though it lingers over this one longer than the others.

Metal. A wheel. A saw. A hotter fire.

It watches iron rust in air that beads on every arm, rust in soil that is always saturated, rust in tools hung to stay cool. It watches a wheel find no dry road to do its work on, and a saw seduce the stone-cutters to cut the weeping face faster than the weeping face cuts itself, and more stone mean more houses, and more houses mean the intervals of empty furrowed field and shining pool shrinking, and the terraces that hold water at a constant depth re-leveled for a different purpose. It watches a hotter kiln scorch damp wood into steam and smoke, and make food roasted instead of steamed, and take from the buried pits their slow ferment work. Their chipped stone and bone and fiber are not a stage before metal. They are adequate to the wetness, adequate to the pace. It sets metal aside.

Rain. A season. A storm to give the place variety, to soften the sharp gullies, to fill the clefts with silt, to let the meadows turn brown and green again.

It watches one storm take the low retaining walls that terrace the meadows into levels, breach the clay pipes under the causeways, scour the moss mats from the channels, knock thatch that has never known wind flat into the pools, and turn a basin that has not moved in living memory into a flood and then a mud. The young stone would soften all at once, and not gently. Their calendar is not by seasons; it is by rounds — pollination, then channels, then thatch, then weaving — and a season would not give them more time, only a different anxiety. It sets the storm aside.

And it considers the gentlest gift of all: a story of the outside. Pictures of birds, of dry fields, of other towns crowded wall to wall, of animals that sing and sting and run. Knowledge.

It watches a child who has learned the brush before much else hear about a bee and ask why the flowers have no scent if they are meant to be found, and hear about a plain below the edge where the water falls and ask why the mark never moves if the world does, and look at the far sharp ridges and feel a longing that has no round to answer it. Longing is not cruelty in other places. Here, where nothing is allowed to fail and the work is the same and the mist returns to the same hollows, longing would be the first thing that could not be tended by hand. It sets the story aside.

It considers, finally, whether to take something away. The water, perhaps, which keeps everything damp and dark-polished and cool. The height, which makes the chest shallow on the climb. The silence.

It sees no suffering that requires rescue. No biting flies, no mosquitoes over the pools, no predators in the sharp gullies. Sickness comes rarely and in mild forms. Hunger has no door here, because the terraces are level and the root pits ferment and the fruit, hand-set, fills the cool damp urns of the storehouse. They live a long time at that walking pace, and children are born into damp houses and learn the wet stone underfoot and grow up never having seen the basin rise or fall — and they do not look, in the plaza, like people who are waiting for something to rise or fall. They look like people who have learned to read a mark and find it sufficient.

What would a wise power do, then, with a place that does not call?

It does what they do. It tends, by not disturbing.

It will leave no footprint on the causeway. It will not close the clefts or open new ones. It will keep the veil — that is its one active work — turning aside the other attentions that pass this shelf, the hungrier powers that would see no animals and think *barren*, no metal and think *stalled*, no crowd and think *ready*. It will let the cliff weep in the same places, not by miracle but by the simple refusal to use its strength to improve the weeping. It will remember the place exactly as it is, not as it could be if it had been made in a more familiar shape. And in the mornings, while the pollinators go out, it will stay a little longer than a power needs to stay, attending to the brush and the basket and the step on wet slab, the way they attend.

In return, and this is the part it did not expect, it is taught. It has visited ten thousand places where benevolence meant adding and lifting and hastening, and most of them have learned to want what was added. This one has learned something rarer: how to keep a level. How to make a life out of rounds instead of progress. How to be young in stone and old in habit without apology. It carries that lesson away with it, not as a gift given, but as a gift received.

If you go there now, you will find it as it was. The rock face still sheening. The moss still giving a little underfoot. The basin still full to the same mark, water plashing softly from its pipe. A figure with a brush under a tree. A figure with a basket on a bridge. The faint thread of water falling far away off the edge, day after day.

It will not look different for having been seen by a benevolent and wise power. That is the point, and the mercy.

The most benevolent thing it could give them was its belief that they were already whole, and the most wise thing was to give it in a way they would never need to know — and then to go on, carefully, doing nothing, for as long as they go on doing theirs.
