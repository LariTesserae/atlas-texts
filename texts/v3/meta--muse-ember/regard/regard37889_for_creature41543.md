It did not arrive as light. The light here never moves, and it would not have moved for this.

It arrived as attention — the way condensation arrives, without announcement, beading on a surface that was already there. If it had a posture, it was the posture of something standing on the rim where your ears pop, above the white sea of cloud, letting the heavy sweet air thicken around it and run in clean lines down its face, the way it runs down every vertical face in the city.

It had seen broken places before. Places that were thin, and cold, and empty because something had been taken from them. Places that were waiting, in whatever way stone and soil can wait, for a repair.

This was not one of them.

It understood that more slowly than it understood almost anything else, because understanding, for it, was usually a kind of fixing. Here it had to sit, in its own timeless sense, and unlearn the impulse.

### What it did not do

It could have made the mountain honest. It could have thinned the air until a swamp at that altitude was impossible, cooled the vapours, dried the travertine, and let the city slump into the alpine cold it was supposed to have. That would have been consistent. It would also have killed everything that lived here, which was to say: it would have killed the place by correcting it.

It did not.

It could have filled the houses. That was the first tenderness. The doors are hung, the stalls in the covered markets are carved ready for goods that never arrive, the granaries stand in order, the bathhouses are finished. To ears accustomed to the world below, the absence of people looked like a lack. Love without wisdom would have brought people, and goods, and a tomorrow in which to eat them, and hungers to go with it. It would have made the city occupied in the way a house is occupied, and in doing so would have made it uninhabitable in the way it was.

It did not.

It could have completed the ecology. No birds in the canopy, no insects in the rot, no frogs in the canals, no fish in the lake, hot side or cold. That silence where animals ought to be seemed, from above, like a note missing from a chord. It would have been easy to add them — a frog to the mats, a fish to the black east half, a bird to the emergent crowns to cross them, an insect to hurry the leaves. But the work of eating and being eaten is already done here, by water and heat and fungi, blooming and dissolving in the same steady state. The total green never increases or decreases by a leaf because nothing browses it, nothing pollinates it in a hurry, nothing winters it out. To add mouths would not have rounded out the system. It would have started a different one, and ended this.

It did not.

It could have given it time. A moving sun, a dusk to cool the steam, a dawn to start it over, a season to thin the strata. That seemed, at first, the kindest gift of all: tomorrow. But the residents have no morning and no evening to keep, no dormancy for a winter that has never come, no clocks except a seed pod popping somewhere in the canopy every few seconds. Tomorrow is not a neutral present. Tomorrow would arrive as a shock to things that have grown, sideways and upward and sideways again, in agreement with a river that flows up a cliff. It would make the shade move. It would make the water level a question. It would turn equilibrium into decay.

It did not.

It could have simplified the impossibilities. Made the stair go up, and stay up. Made the river fall both ways only in metaphor. Separated the lake into hot and cold with a proper shore. Erased the second shadow at noon, erased the tower that exists only in reflection, made the drip land before it is heard. Those things are load-bearing. The ferns live in the water-rills worn into the centre of the returning stair. The plants along the upward river grow in agreement with it. The roots are intertwined under the cut-line of the lake because the line was always a negotiation, not a wall. To straighten any one of them would not have been a repair. It would have been an eviction.

It did not.

That restraint was not passivity. It was the whole of its wisdom, applied. A benevolent power that acts on a place with no suffering, no lack, and no request can only do harm by acting, unless its act is to recognize the place as complete and other — not a ruin, not a project, not a pet, not a smaller version of the world below.

It stayed long enough to learn that recognition. Long enough to hear that the *shhk-shhk* of broad leaves rubbing in still air was not silence. It was a crowd shifting its weight and never moving. It was talk, in the only tempo this place has.

### What it did

It stayed.

Not above — it had begun above, as everything that comes to this place begins above, on the rim — but *in*. It descended to the level of a surface. Leaf, stone, moss, attention: all surfaces here. It let the drips fall on it at the speed of the covered streets and the speed of the open terraces. It did not add a sound, and it did not hush one. On the grand stair it felt the drum-beat under the city in the place where you feel it, in its teeth, and it did not try to find its source and stop it.

It took, for a dwelling, the one address that displaced nothing.

In the central basin the water shows a reflection of the city with one extra tower, which has no counterpart in the real one and never will. The mats skin the false tower as faithfully as the real one. The power settled there — in the reflected tower, visible only when the water is still, casting no shadow in either direction at noon, needing no door, opening no gate, entering no courtyard.

It did not enter the tree in the market square. The hollow tree that is its own home, with its well and its bench carved from wood itself and its door that opens onto the square again on the other side, is inhabited to saturation by being itself. The power sat in the square, outside it, the way a neighbour sits outside a house that is not theirs. That is a small thing. For a power that could have anywhere, it is everything.

It did one active thing, and it was a guarding. It made the place un-kept by those who would keep it otherwise. Causeways will be hard to retrace. Surveys will return to the level they left. Maps will close over, the way a puddle closes over a footprint a breath later. Not hidden in anger — hidden in the same way the city hides itself from any use that is not attention. If you come again, you will come as you came the first time: by accident of altitude, ears popping, and you will leave without a road to bring anyone else. The power did not build a wall. It simply declined to let the world below learn the way up as a route.

And it remembered. That is the work that remains. To hold, elsewhere, the exact configuration of a place that does not need to be held: seven terraced basins around a divided lake, hot west and cold east, plants leaning each to its preference, roots intertwined below; aqueducts crossing on arches; vines bridging roof to roof over streets that are also canals, so you wade and duck at once; fungi blooming under leaves and dissolving before they have finished blooming; the roar under everything, the hiss from the wet seams, the hiss of travertine exhaling, the drip-precussion, the tick of hot stone, the pop — somewhere, every few seconds, and somewhere else a few seconds later.

It does not count the pops. To count them would be to make a clock, and a clock would make a tomorrow. It only listens, in their tempo.

When you left — and you did leave, because you are the kind of creature that has a world with tomorrows to return to — your footprints closed over behind you. A vine you pushed aside swung back to its same trembling position. The power watched that happen, and it understood its own place in the pattern: not as the one who disturbed, and not as the one who healed, but as the one who remained, the way moss remains on a surface, proving not decay but a finish.

A thousand years from your footprints, a pod will pop in the canopy and somewhere else another will pop a few seconds later. The leaves will rub, *shhk-shhk*, shifting weight that never moves. The lake will be hot on one side of its cut and cold and black on the other. The river will flow up the cliff in its smooth sheet to the ledge thirty meters above, and the plants there will grow sideways, then up, then sideways again, as if agreeing with it. The stair will rise for two hundred steps and bring whoever climbs it back to where they left, and both terraces will be right.

Nothing will have changed. And the power will still be there, in the extra tower in the water, keeping the only appointment this place allows: to attend without end to something that has all the time there is, which is none at all.

That was enough. It was, in the end, the only gift it had that this place did not already have — a witness that would not improve it.
