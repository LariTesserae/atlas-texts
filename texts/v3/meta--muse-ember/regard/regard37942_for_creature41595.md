It did not come because it was called.

Nothing in the sink was built to call it. There was no temple, no bell, no tower above the rim, and no one looked up from the lanes with an expectation large enough to be heard. From the plateau the town was invisible; the goats, tethered far apart, cropped their wind-bent grass and the light stayed plain and unfiltered, and the power that had been passing over that flat, thin land for longer than the soil had been thin could have passed over the place forever without knowing.

What drew it, in the end, was the newness. A fresh cut in the earth is a thing a traveler feels before he understands. The bands of limestone and shale exposed in section, the spoil still being carried up in baskets, the corners too clean to be natural — all of it said that the rock had been opened within living memory, and that opening was not yet finished. A power that had watched other diggings fill with rain, or collapse, or be abandoned when the stone gave out, paused over this one expecting the same.

Instead it found people living in the cut, house by house, terrace by terrace, with everything repaired and re-hafted and used to the last.

So it stayed, to watch. That is the first thing a wise power does: not to arrive, but to stay long enough to stop mistaking the visible for the whole.

It learned them not by names — they had no use for names beyond the lane — but by the order of their days. By the buckets at the cistern, the chipped one and the one with the patched rope and the one painted lime at the rim, each waiting its turn in the cool shade while the ladlers talked low about salt and late-summer rain and a cousin whose new room had broken damp. By the children on the north faces in the morning, with cloths and shallow ladles, working carefully around the moss rather than wiping it away. By the grandmothers in the deepest rooms, where the oil lamps burned at noon and daylight never reached. By the bakers, up before the collectors, sending bread up the ramps on the same backs that carried stone down. By the dogs shifting a few inches with the shadow and going to sleep again. By the rats coming out after the last bucket, when the rota was done.

It watched a family move a terrace up, literally up, toward the light that lasted a little longer and the breeze that slid down from the plateau. It watched the market descend from the flat horizon and climb back before dusk. It watched the swallows at evening cut low across the bowl twice and three times, as if examining the sky, and then go up again. It watched the white fungus return to the timber props after being cut back, and the woodlice turning patiently under the drain slabs, and the clear greenish skin of life holding the cistern water, skimmed by a practiced hand and never quite killed.

It understood, slowly, that they were not waiting to be lifted out. They were not a failed version of a town on the plateau, thin for lack of water and light. They had made a particular answer to a particular thinness, and the answer worked. The bowl kept them cool and sheltered from the wind that bent everything on the rim. The drains kept the rare rain from flooding the lower houses. The rota kept the seep from becoming a quarrel that outlived the water. The sharing of walls and roofs kept a lane a shoulder's width wide from becoming a wall of strangers. Their life was saturated, hive-like, and quiet because the stone took the sound and gave back only what was close. They heard one another through the walls constantly, and intimately, and that was not a defect of the place. It was the place.

If the power had been merely strong, it would have improved them out of existence.

It could have taken down the south wall and let the sun stay on the floor all day. It could have brought a river under the plateau and filled the cistern to the brim. It could have raised the roofs to the rim and opened a square where everyone might gather in the open, loudly, as towns are supposed to. It could have finished the outer ring in a single night, stone dressed and limewash laid, so that no family would ever again watch their house pass through the hands of cutters and masons and roofers and plasterers. It could have spoken, in a voice that did not stay within a few houses, and settled the argument about water once and for all.

Each of those mercies assumed that the town wanted to be some other place. It did not. A river would have undone the drains and the rota and the careful trust that made a seep enough. A wider bowl would have lost the coolness that was their wealth in the middle of the day. A square would have given them a place to gather, and taken away the reason they had learned to gather in lanes, at the cistern, in doorways. A finished town would have ended the hand's breadth a day, and with it the thing that defined them most: that they were always making room for somebody who had not yet arrived.

Benevolence, the power decided, is not always the same as abundance. Sometimes it is the decision to let a form of life remain itself.

So it chose to act at their scale, and by their rules. It would not leave a gap. It would not take a turn out of order. It would not break through a neighbor's wall. It would meet what was already built, and turn before it broke, and take its place somewhere small.

In the morning, on the north faces, it held the damp a fraction longer than the stone would have held it alone, so that the old people and the children reached the walls before the sweat ran thin — not in flood, not enough to rot the timber, only enough that no bucket came away needlessly light. The moss, which they read like a neighbor's door, stayed a dark green fur where the seep wept from the shale. Where the moss thinned, the power did not announce a miracle. It breathed a little water back into the wall behind, until the line thickened again and the people, reassured, went on with their day and credited the stone.

During the long dry weeks before the late-summer rain, it let the seep weep a few days past its usual failing — one extra ladle a week, perhaps, spread so thinly across the rota that no family gained and no family lost, and the argument about turns did not sharpen. When the rain came at last, short and cool and vanishing, it waited until the drain slabs had been cleared and the market had descended, and then let the sheets run down the faces and away, and left the puddles in the shade to take a full day to dry, because the woodlice under the slabs and the small lives in the new tunnels needed that day.

At the digging faces, at the edges, it did its most careful work. The town's whole safety rested on the turnings: a tunnel must turn before it breaks into a neighbour's room, and the turning is marked and remembered. Memory, in a place where the oldest dwellings were gouged into the floor and a house could branch sideways behind a single blank front, was the frailest timber in the whole structure. When an old man's memory thinned, or a boy's marking was rubbed away, the power remembered for them — not with a voice, not with a sign, but by making the stone sound a shade different under the pick at the one place where turning mattered. The digger paused, puzzled, and turned. The neighbour's room stayed whole. Trust, which no one could afford to lose in a town with no gaps, stayed whole with it. Where a face was about to break through a niche that pigeons had claimed, it let the pick glance off a harder band, and the niche, whitewashed with droppings and accepted by everyone because a niche with pigeons was a niche that was not being dug away, remained.

It steadied what they could not see. A prop beam, beginning as scaffolding and left to become a floor, held one season longer against the white fungus, long enough for the owner's son to replace it without anyone noticing that it had nearly failed at the worst hour, when the bakers' smoke was rising unobstructed and a child was passing below with a basket of bread. On the plateau, above the rim where no one from below could see, it nudged a goat's tether rope free of a snag, and the faint bell, the town's distant relative, sounded a little clearer in a wind that happened to be blowing the right way.

It did all of this the way the town did everything: close, quiet, and without monument. It never made a bucket new. It never filled a deep room with light. It never let the stunted fig in the sunlit widening grow tall enough to shade the beans, because those green things were defended precisely by being tended by hand, watered from a neighbour's overflow and a spilled bucket and a favour, and the power was not going to make the favour unnecessary. It did not still the argument about salt. It did not stop the dogs from sleeping through almost everything. It did not sterilize the cistern. It left the spiders in the corners of the storage rooms undisturbed, and the silverfish in the grain rooms, and the moths beating softly around the oil lamps in the rooms that never saw the sun.

And because it had learned the town's rules, it accepted the town's consequence: there was nowhere to stand that was not already a house, a store, or a drain. So it took a niche, as the pigeons had taken theirs, in the thickness of a wall where the stone stayed cool to the touch all day. There, where it could hear a bucket set down through the rock, water dripping somewhere deep on the other side, a child calling once in a lane and quieting, the soft thud of a basket, the rhythm of picks two terraces away, it settled. It made room for itself by not unmaking anyone else's. In a place where no one may leave a gap, even a power must learn to be a neighbour.

The town, of course, went on being unfinished. That was the point. The masons followed the cutters, and the roofers followed the masons, and the plasterers with their white and faint stubborn pink followed the roofers, and somewhere at the edge stone rang once, twice, a hand's breadth at a time, opening a little more room for somebody who had not yet arrived.

Now, when it rings, it is not always a pick.
