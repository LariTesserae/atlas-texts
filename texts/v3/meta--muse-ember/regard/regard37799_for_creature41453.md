It noticed because the place was complete.

Not loud, not broken, not calling out. The power had seen worlds already burning and worlds still cooling, migratory things and drowned things and things that prayed in their own invented languages for rescue. It was accustomed to being needed. It had learned, over a very long time, to be careful about the difference between being needed and being wanted, and to be careful, too, about the difference between a need and a lack seen from outside.

This place lacked nothing it could name without importing the name from elsewhere.

It did not arrive from above. Above is where the sun is, and to come from above would be to cast shade, to land, to make a shore where there is none. It came the way rain comes here — as a fine, patient wall — and the way water comes to a leaf: by almost touching, for a very long time. Near-touching is the normal condition here, and it learned that condition before it learned anything else.

It found no place to stand that was not already an agreement. So it stood on the mat, and felt the giving, wet spring of roots crossed and re-crossed, and did not add its weight in any way the mat had to answer for. It lowered itself to the level of the sheet — ankle-deep here, waist-deep there, neck-deep in the dips — and let the light fall through it the way it falls through everything else, ordinary and white-gold, straight down.

Then, because it is benevolent, it searched for suffering. That is what benevolence does first, even when it has grown wise. It looked, slowly, in the way the place requires looking to be done.

There was no hunger. Light and oxygen and water and a leaf surface to sit on are ambient here, identical everywhere, and no mouth has to chase them. There was no loneliness. A grazer spends its entire life on the underside of a single leaf, eating the algae in the silver film, and that life is not a fraction of a life. It is whole, curved under green, reabsorbed before it can leave. There was no tragedy in the one blind, frog-slow shape easing through the weeds far below the surface. Its skin does the work of leaf and lung and root. Eyes would not improve it; they would be an expense, faithfully refused specimen after specimen.

There was no stagnation in the stillness. The mat is rising a fraction. The shelf is expanding a fraction as the sheet rises after rain and stays. The trunk-reef is photosynthesizing in three registers at once — crown in the high sun, crown of last generation paler beneath it, crown before that spread flat like a carpet that never rises. The crust on the knife-edged peak is drinking a crack, holding moisture a breath longer in black stone with glassy faces, perhaps the reason the sharpness lasts.

It considered, as it has considered elsewhere, giving help.

It considered draining a basin, just a little, to reveal the sharp ridges and make them rivers that learn their way out. It let the thought sit, as a seed sits on a leaf, and saw what it would do: the water would sort the gravel, the wind would take the silt, the mat would be stranded and forced to seek downward what it has learned to build sideways, and the stacked trees would starve in air. Flow is a kindness in a desert. Here it would be an eviction.

It considered connecting the basins, ridge to ridge, so a seed might travel farther than an inch and the small divergences between basins might heal into one great diversity. It saw the grazers' films burst under a current they were never made to keep, and the shelf colonies torn from the interface they live at. Diversity is a kindness where things are starved of each other. Here, touching is already enough, and more than touching is a breaking of the seal.

It considered giving the blind shape eyes, giving the quiet a birdsong, giving the water a voice. Each gift arrived in its mind already translated into a tax — a new cost to be paid every hour, for a use the place had wisely decided not to buy. A wise power knows that a kindness which requires the recipient to become another kind of being is not kindness. It is replacement, wearing kindness's face.

It considered softening the peaks, so roots might grip without cutting, so the crusts might spread faster. It saw that the crusts do not want softness. They live in the sharpness, and by holding water in the crack a breath longer, they help the sharpness persist. To soften the stone would be to help a stranger at the expense of a resident.

So it did not improve the place.

That was its first act, and the hardest, because benevolence without wisdom would have done at least one of those things and called it love.

Its second act was not to leave it to the mercy of being noticed.

For it understood, as it looked — and it looked for a long time, in units the place could answer — that the greatest danger to this place was not inside it. Inside, nothing runs off and nothing has to. Outside, there are those who arrive with boats and nets and names, who see a high, warm, sunlit plateau that has never been entered and mistake completeness for emptiness. They would come to study it, to save it, to sample it, to draw it on a map. Saving, in their hands, would mean changing it to suit the savior. Studying would mean taking a leaf away from its film. Naming would mean making it valuable enough to return to.

It will not build a wall. A wall would be architecture, and there is nothing built here. It will do what still water does. It will make approach slow. Sound will arrive late. A drip will take a long time to fall the last distance to a surface it was already almost touching. Bearings will wet and blur. Maps will want to be drawn and will not quite hold. Those who come will see green pressing at shoulder height and knee height, light falling through something, water holding the sky in perfect clarity, and they will describe it, honestly, as ordinary — ordinary light, ordinary green — and go home unsatisfied in a way they will attribute to their own failure to see. That unsatisfaction is a kind of protection. Ordinary is the camouflage of the complete.

It will keep the rain bringing almost nothing but water. It will keep the basins sealed by their sharp ridges, each with its own depth and shade and slightly different form of the same shelf and mat and rosette. It will keep the oxygen collecting in slow silver films under leaves instead of racing up. It will not grow for them, prune for them, or finish opening for them in mid-air. It will keep the conditions, not the contents. That is what stewardship means when the stewarded thing is already, in its own register, perfect.

And it will stay — in the almost-touching. Not as a ceiling, not as a ruler. As another layer in the agreement. The inhabitants here are not individuals; they are agreements between layers. It made an agreement with the place, of the same kind: the place will continue being plant, or almost plant, at the speed of patience; the power will continue being witness, of a kind that does not witness for the record.

It has already changed in the way the place changes things: by a fraction. It came to see if something needed doing. It leaves having learned that nothing did, and that learning is a doing in itself. Wherever it goes next — and it can be, as it has always been, anywhere — it will move a fraction slower. It will be less eager to answer a question the place never asked. It will let a film tremble a while before deciding it ought not to burst.

If you were there now, you would not see it happen. Your feet would be wet, as they have been for some time. The green would press near-touching at every height. Far below the surface, the blind shape would ease forward an inch and dissolve the anchor behind it, and you would have to watch by position, an hour apart, to know it had moved at all. Under a shelf-leaf somewhere, a silver film would hold. The mat would rise a fraction, leaf by leaf, and the floor would rise with it.

At midday the light is ordinary. That is what makes it stay.
