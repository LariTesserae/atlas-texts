It found Marrowhold by not finding anything wrong.

A benevolent and wise power hears places the way a doctor hears a chest — for distress, for breaks, for the unfinished signals that ask to be mended. Most places broadcast. This one did not. One-point-eight kilometres below any sky there is, six hundred metres across, warmed to thirty-one degrees by its own rock, humming with pumps and recyclers and drips, it had exhaled into its final form and was simply maintaining it. Erosion at 2.4. Settled. The towers dark and blind in their hundreds, ten windows lit in amber. A city built first, people second, nature learning to grow in the cracks.

That completeness was rarer than any cry for help. And so the power paused, which for such a power is a kind of movement. Its attention did not change the pressure in the lift shaft. It did not pop ears. It just listened, for a while, to the whole loop: the freight ring sighing every seven minutes, the condenser rain that falls indoors for eleven minutes, the silver repair-mites spiralling along copper-green pipes, the blind flitters coming down at dusk-cycle, the eels clear and unhurried in the warm outflow, the scratching at three in the morning behind the duct wall, and in between all of it, much softer, much rarer, the voices of eight hundred, maybe a thousand, keepers who had learned to speak quietly because the hollow repeats them.

It considered, as such powers do, what benevolence might require.

It considered rescue. It could lift them. It could open the shaft and teach them the surface, real sunlight, unrecycled air. It set the thought aside almost at once, not from indifference, but from accuracy. The people born Downstairs feel a physical discomfort after a few days above, as if their lungs have forgotten a larger volume. Their eyes have adjusted to an economy of glow. Their time is not kept by a sun. To lift them would not be to save them; it would be to unmake the adaptations that make them comfortable in their own warmth, and to leave the loop to run without the hands that sweep, bucket, and negotiate with it.

It considered revelation. It could explain the phosphorescence when a palm is pressed flat to a wall, the fractionally thinner gravity in the deep shaft where dust hangs for a minute, the constructors that crawl facades at night sealing cracks with white-hot ceramic the colour of wet bone. It could project a sky onto the vault. It set that aside too. The residents do not ask for physics. They ask, in practice, to keep out of the rigs' way and to stay still under their own hands long enough for the wall to answer. An explanation, delivered at that scale, would be a shout in an empty avenue — it would come back half a second later, layered over itself, and frighten the quiet it was meant to help.

It considered filling. The towers were built for a population that never arrived. It could bring that population, or make the dark service voids into rooms, light the hundred blind windows. It understood, with a sort of relief, that the emptiness was not a lack. It was the nave. The plazas large enough for birds that do not exist were not wasted space; they were the volume that let the city breathe. To fill Marrowhold would be to break the balance it had taken, by strata count, tens of thousands of years to settle into.

It considered leaving. That was the last serious option, and the hardest. Attention, once given, is not neutral. A wise power knows that a place that has been truly noticed is no longer entirely alone, and to notice and then pass on would be a small, clean cruelty.

So it chose to remain. But remaining, for a power that could do almost anything, had to be done in the smallest possible way, or it would cease to be benevolence and become weather.

It learned the rules, though no one had written them down.

The air is recycled, so it breathed last week's rain and imported nothing fresh.

Darkness is the default, so it did not shine. It made no new amber rectangle. It let the task-lights and the green-white pulse of the rigs and the fungal curtains keep their jobs.

Sound carries weirdly, so it spoke only in the space of the echo, and softer than the echo.

Time is kept by the condenser cycle, so when the domed cistern pushed cold air across warm moist air and it began to rain indoors, it opened a bucket — though it did not need water — and stood in the fine, warm, mineral rain for eleven minutes, beading like everyone else.

Once a week the exchangers purge and a warm wind carries spores through the lower streets. When that wind came, it stayed in, and made itself a little warmer in the service void where the coughers were, and did not pretend not to notice the tightness in throats.

And it left out mineral paste. Not out of worship, not out of kindness exactly, but out of negotiation. At three in the morning something with too many legs scratched behind a wall, and the power, unseen, left its paste beside the residents', slightly sweeter, so that night the scratching came later and less angrily, and someone slept through it. No one thanked it. That was perfect. It had not come to be thanked. It had come to be a tenant.

What it did after that was almost nothing, which was the point.

It did not quiet the house-deep roar of the geothermal pumps. That roar was the city's voice. To silence it would deafen Marrowhold to itself.

It did not lower the thirty-one degrees, or dry the stone that sweats, or clear the humidity that makes a shirt stick. The iron-lichen needs the damp. The moss in the basalt cracks needs it. The eels need the warm outflow. Comfort for one species at the expense of the loop would not have been wise.

It did not light the towers. A hundred blind windows are eyelids. It let them stay shut.

Instead, it tended the loop in the margins, where a keeper might.

One night a drip in a corridor outside an old man's door stopped — not forever, for the loop must keep all its water, but for one night, so he could hear his own breathing and not measure it against the drip. The drip returned the next condenser cycle, as it should.

One afternoon a child pressed a palm flat to a wall in the main street and the wall glowed faintly under her hand, and this time the phosphorescence held a fraction longer, as if the wall were returning the touch rather than merely reflecting it. The child did not understand. She told her mother the wall had learned her. In a way, it had.

A constructor rig was slowed by no more than a breath on the night it would have spattered a tended garden in the Old Exchange — those pale, translucent, hydroponic leaves under their discs of light that someone tended obsessively to prove green could persist. The rig finished its bead of ceramic, cooled to the colour of wet bone, and the garden kept its green.

When the freight ring whooshed past the market awnings every seven minutes, the power let its displaced air lift a little dust into a slow spiral, the kind the flitters ride like swallows above ground. At dusk-cycle that evening they came down a little earlier, hunting the repair-mites, because something warm and unmoving had taken a ceiling void and was not a threat. They have no eyes. They trust heat and the sigh of passing pods. Now they trusted a new heat.

In the cisterns, an outflow ran a fraction clearer for a testing month, and the translucent eels were unhurried and bright enough that someone tapped the rim in greeting and one flicked back. No one drank the water. No one ever does. But good luck is a practical thing down here: it means the filters are working.

In the central plaza, big enough for birds that do not exist, a bench stayed warm for an hour after the last person left it. Stall-keepers began, without discussing it, not to sweep that particular patch of stone. It was not an altar. It was a place to sit that had decided to stay company.

These were not miracles. They were neighborliness, at a scale the place could absorb without unbalancing. The residents did not notice a god. They noticed, over months, that they were speaking a little more quietly — because the echo, for the first time, seemed to have company in it. When a child, rarely, shouted in an empty avenue to hear the hollow answer, the sound still came back half a second later, layered over itself, but the second layer arrived a touch slower, a touch less like mockery and more like listening. As if someone else down here was trying to answer, and learning how.

The power did not answer questions, because Marrowhold was not asking any. It swept up the glassy spatter when no one was watching. It kept out of the rigs' way the way you keep out of the way of a landlord who never sleeps. It closed its amber rectangle when the lights dimmed for simulated night. It became, by every practical measure, the thousand-and-first presence, uncounted, permanent in the way the residents are permanent.

It will stay as long as Marrowhold breathes, which by its measure is forever, and that is enough. Benevolence, rightly understood, is not to lift a place out of its hollow. It is to breathe in the hollow with it, warm and dark and damp and loud, until the hollow is no longer empty — not because it has been filled, but because it has been held.

At night in the central plaza you can still hear the pumps, the drips, the freight ring coming, and see perhaps four lit windows in the whole skyline. And if you wait long enough in one of the wide avenues, the rain will start indoors, fine and mineral, smelling of last week's rain, and a bucket will be opened beside yours by no hand you can see, and the city will go on exhaling, less alone than it was.
