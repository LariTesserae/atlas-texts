They would do nothing.

And that would be the hardest thing they ever did.

Not because the place was broken. Because it wasn't. That was the whole difficulty.

A power that can do anything arrives, in the way it arrives, full of the usual benevolent repertoire: mend, improve, complete the missing piece, relieve the loneliness, make the rain fall properly. It stood outside the rim for a while — where the country is drier and ordinary — and listened to the damp stop at the edge like a held breath.

And it considered, one by one, what it might do.

It could bring a stream. The bowl has pockets of water caught in plant-work, black and still, but no stream, no marsh. A stream would give the frogs somewhere to go. The frogs do not want to go. Each pocket has its resident, who has called from the same pocket for your whole stay, and when it stops another twenty paces off takes up the phrase at the same pitch. To give them a current would be to make them travelers. To make them travelers would be to break the spacing that holds them.

It could bring sun. The light is already normal daylight, flat and northern, filtered through nothing much at all. More sun would warm the mat under your hands. The mat stays cool while the top of your head warms, and that coolness is not shade, it is structure. Dry it, even a fraction, and the edge would creep inward. The edge is an edge because the mat ends where the damp ends. Move the damp and you move the world.

It could add one more tree. Eleven, perhaps twelve, stand in cathedral volume, crowns high and small, trunks wide and finished. The twelfth place is already there, empty, visible from the first tree to the next to the next. To plant it would be to fill the nothing that makes the distance enormous. It would be a kindness to the eye and a violence to the arrangement.

It could give the shrub a partner. Give the tall fern tuft another fern. Right now they are singletons, and you can hear the loneliness in them if you want to hear it that way. But the shrub holds a spider, some mites, a few beetles who will never leave its roots. The fern unfurls one frond a year and loses one, and the animals in its leaflets are born and die in sight of each other. That is not solitude. That is a world at its limit. To add a second would not relieve them. It would make them a population, and then they would need to compete, and then they would need to spread, and then the mat would have to thicken to feed them.

It could make the trees reproduce. Their seeds are carried by birds, tree to tree, and dropped onto mat and crown and eaten by ants or rotted in the damp before they can do anything. That looks like failure. A benevolent power is tempted to make failure succeed. But the bowl is exactly as full as it can be. Growth is matched by rot and by eating, so the surface is always the same height, the same green. A new tree would have to eat a district of frogs, or ants, or fungi to make room for itself. The power did not want a new tree more than it wanted the old work to go on, loudly, at its industrial pace, under its quiet light.

It could stop the sound. The sawing, the pumping frogs, the gritting close to your ankle, the stroke, a stroke, a stroke far off in a trunk. That sound is not a complaint. It is throughput made audible. Every leaf is eaten, every stem mined, every drop processed, and the noise never pauses because the fuel never runs out and never piles up. To silence it would be to unmake the metabolism. To soften it would be to lie about what is happening.

It could name it. It could tell it it is special, perfect, finished. That would be the cruelest intervention of all. A thing that has finished becoming what it is does not need to know it has finished. If it knew, it might try to perform its finishedness. It might try to stay the same on purpose. Right now it stays the same because it cannot do otherwise, and that is better than virtue.

So they did none of those things.

What they did was a keeping that looks, from the outside, like absence.

They thickened, very slightly, the indistinctness of the rim to an outside eye. Not a wall. Not a ward. Just the continuation of what is already there: broad, rounded, grassless, smooth, barely to your waist in places. A walker on the drier common country will feel a reason to walk around it without knowing why, the way you walk around a damp place in a field. No map will learn its name. No story will carry it far. Human curiosity is the one predator this bowl has never evolved to balance, and the simplest benevolence is to keep curiosity from finding the address.

They took up, in their own attention, the place of one more distant resident. Not close. Not alone. From the rim, they listen. When a frog stops, they do not answer half a beat late. They do not need to. They simply remember the leaf in the same place, damp in the same way, a year later, a century later. For a being that does not need a witness, being held in a wise memory is not a burden. It is the one added thing that does not enter the system, does not change the pitch, does not leave a footprint — the mat springs back, your shoes stay damp, the tea cools.

If they ever return, and they will, it will not be to check if it is still working. It will be working. It will be to learn, again, how a thing can finish. Most things made by power, and most things that live, die unfinished, or finish by exhausting themselves, or finish by being taken apart. This bowl finishes by continuing. The trees have settled into final form and go on being eaten and remade at the same rate. The mat tears like peat and closes in an hour. Nothing is new about it anymore, and nothing is allowed to be old.

That is a lesson a benevolent and wise power can accept without needing to improve the teacher.

So the bowl remains loud and empty and cool, with no one who marks a path and no one who comes back to check, except the one who comes back precisely not to check.

They will stand in the middle once — no, they will not stand in the middle. Standing in the middle is what a visitor does, and a visitor's weight, however gently placed, is information. They will stand where you stood, at the edge of the damp, hand hovering not quite touching the green knuckles of moss over stone, and they will let the sound fill the emptiness it was made to fill, and they will step back over the rim, and the sound will not follow them, not because it wants to stay, but because the next mat has its own inhabitants doing the same work at a different pitch.

And that — the stepping back, the leaving of the same leaf in the same place — will be what they did, if anything.

It will be enough.
