I did not come to Kell Hollow to save it.

That is the first thing, because most places come to me as a question. A forest thinning. A river choking. A city of humans building a tower it cannot finish. They arrive incomplete, and the incompleteness is loud. I can hear what they need.

Kell Hollow arrived as a statement.

I noticed it because it did not ask. For a long time I only listened from a distance, the way its animals listen — through my feet, if you can call it that — and what I heard was not distress. It was a hum at a steady pitch, a circuit turning and turning, water beading and being licked away and breathed out and beading again. No crescendo. No fade. I have not heard that often enough to mistake it for an ending that failed.

I went closer. I stood, in whatever way I stand, on that small brick platform where the rails end in buffers, and I watched the procession come around the boiler house.

I will not pretend I did not think of kindnesses. A benevolent power is always tempted to improve. A wise one learns, slowly and at cost, what improvement does to a tuned thing.

I thought of giving them eyes.

Those milky pits where eyes should be are not wounds. They are closures. The animals navigate by the *tik-tik-tik* under the snout and by the vibration that runs so beautifully through concrete and iron. They are listening to the buildings, and the buildings are answering them in hum and ping and clang. To open eyes in that place would not be a gift. It would be a confusion, then a grief — to see, all at once and clearly, that there is no green and no sky and no elsewhere. They are not missing sight. They are complete without it. I would not take that completeness from them to satisfy a human idea of what a living thing ought to have.

I thought of greening it. A tree in a crack. Grass on a verge. A little ordinary life to soften the grey-yellow late morning.

I dismissed it on reasoning first, and then on heart. The fungus that crusts the damp walls is not waiting for a plant. It is fed by the mineral pellets they drop and the slag plates they shed, and they in turn graze the fungus and regrow the plates. The water is not waiting for rain. It sweats off the cold pipes, is licked from eaves and from each other's backs, is breathed into the cool air at their ankles and condenses again. Everything is worn to its final shape because the loop is closed. Put one green thing in and it would not stay green. It would be eaten, or it would unbalance the felt, or it would teach the animals a new hunger and then starve them of it. The cracks are clean not because nothing can grow there, but because this place learned, completely, how to live without help from outside.

I thought of giving them rest. Stopping the turbine hall, letting the conveyor rust out, letting the fans slow, letting the herd lie down in the wheel-ruts and not rise.

They are not tired. That was the hardest thing to understand, and the most relieving. Walking in a circuit that does not go anywhere is not labor to them. It is tending. It is breathing. The scarred one with the torn plate on its left flank is in the same doorway every time because that is its place in the loop, not because it is trapped there. If I halted the circuit I would not be giving them peace. I would be breaking the one prayer they know how to say.

I thought of giving them a future. Birth, death, change. The usual drama. They never age, the number never changes, no one is mourned because no one leaves. It is tempting to call that a lack. It is not. To give them death would be to give them fear, and to give them birth would be to give them replacement for someone they have never lost. They chose — if a choice can be made without words — the other bargain: to stay, always thousands, fat and sleek and listening, and to be exactly enough.

So I did not green it. I did not wake it. I did not finish it in the direction of a story.

What I did was smaller, and therefore truer.

I held it.

I put my attention around Kell Hollow like a low, low wall — not to keep the animals in, they have never needed keeping, not to keep a visitor from entering, but to keep out the unfinished intentions of others. The developer who would slab the yards. The miner who would take the slag. The well-meaning human who would come with a tree and call it rescue. The scientist who would take a plate to a lab and call it a sample. They will all pass by and feel, perhaps, a faint reluctance, a coolness that makes them look elsewhere. That reluctance is my doing. It is the only intervention I could make that does not change anything inside.

I left the door open behind the one who is standing there now on the platform, with an animal's damp breath fogging his ankles and condensation ticking in the pipes overhead. He will have to climb over a back in a wheel-rut in time. He will leave with his boots clicked and polished by iron hooves, and he will carry the place out in a way I cannot — not as a place to fix, but as a place that works because it does not change. If he tells it truly, that is enough. Witness is a kind of protection.

And I stayed.

Staying is what the animals do on the walls, shivering with fine vibrations, licking where the brick weeps. Staying is what the engines do, running empty with perfect fidelity. I have learned from them, which is not a thing a power expects to say. For a while I only watched them turn the corner of the boiler house and turn again, and somewhere in that turning I felt an old kinship. I too have hummed at a steady pitch for a very long time. I too have been finished in more ways than I care to recount. Kell Hollow did not frighten me with its stasis. It relieved me of the idea that finished must mean dead.

Tomorrow the light will be late morning again. The same herd will be in the same yard. The same puddle will hold the same brownish depth. A bellow will rise from a hollow chest and a corrugated wall will hum in answer, as if the building itself were continuing the note. No one with any sense will call that a curse again in my hearing.

I will not make it brighter. I will not make it evening. I will keep it held, and keep listening through my feet, and let it be exactly like this tomorrow.

Not because it cannot change.

Because it does not need to.
