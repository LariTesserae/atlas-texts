It finds them first as a blink.

From a great distance the plateau is only a dimming, a warm wet smudge at the top of the world where mist has learned to gather. But every so often, across a breadth of country so vast it cannot be crossed in a season, light falls through all the layers at once — ceiling gardens turning, overhead stems opening, waist-high fans lifting, foot-rosettes lit to the stone — and the whole place brightens in unison for a moment, like an eye opening under water. It is the sort of thing that draws attention. A benevolent and wise power has seen such blinks before. They have meant a birth, a signal, a first word, a mind asking to be lifted or saved or understood.

It comes nearer, and slows itself, and learns what the blink is. It is not a message. It is permission. It is the moment when the light that has been taken out of the light finally gets through, and the shutter-eyes in the moss open to see it, and a thousand rosettes open to take it in. The inhabitants are not asking for anything. They are doing the thing they have always done, at the speed of a thought, and doing it together because a pressure wave takes that long to travel through a body the size of a plateau.

In other places, attention like this would be enough to begin. It has lifted young worlds out of their seas, warned sharp things away from their own ends, taught, healed, ended, seeded. It is benevolent, which means it wants to be useful. It is wise, which means it has learned, slowly and at great cost, that usefulness is not the same as intervention, and that some of the kindest acts in its long history have been the ones it almost did not do.

Here, every familiar category fails.

It considers giving them sight. The eyes in the moss are shutters, perfect for their purpose. They do not watch the world; they watch for permission. To make them see would be to make them watchers, and watchers become hunters, and hunters would have to waste the soup they are already filtering in order to chase it. It considers giving them legs, or a front, or a direction to flee in. The chain-swimmer is rare because movement here is not escape or pursuit. It is recurrence — spore, dust, settling, opening a fan in mid-water and drifting again, carried uphill on the reverse streams to places falling could never reach. They have already solved movement without ever needing to move. It considers a name, a tool-mark, a monument cut into the seven slabs. Nothing here sharpens, and for good reason. A name would be a sharp edge laid across a groove that was made to be followed by a root. A monument would begin the long lowland process of breaking, and breaking would need replacing, and replacing would need hurry, and hurry is the one substance this place has never learned to make.

It considers lifting them down to the sun. It considers adding light, adding heat, adding novelty from elsewhere, a seed of a different green to enrich the soup. Each gift turns, in its mind, the way the ceiling gardens turn, and each shows its underside. Sun would boil the medium. Heat would unmake the translucent stalks. Novelty would be hunger wearing the mask of generosity. Even a word, spoken in its own voice, would arrive too loud and too sharp. It would be, whatever its content, a predator sound from above. The hanging roots are ears that have mistaken themselves for roots; they already grow toward echoes that are not there. It will not give them a real echo to grow toward until they die of it.

So it does the harder thing. It stays.

It sheds its speed first, then its scale, then its sharpness, until it can enter the medium without displacing it. It does not descend like a hand. It diffuses, like weather. It becomes fine and invisible in the water, a warmth that blooms back at itself, a trace of suspended green in the green soup, a difference in the dimming of the dim. This is the only form of attention the place can receive without being interrupted by it.

The inhabitants do what they do to every novelty. They filter it.

Rosettes open for hours and pass it through themselves, so the water around them is a fraction clearer. Fans comb it straight one way and then, when the current reverses, straight the other. Hanging tips bend toward the echo of its pressure and hold, tip to tip, sharing water directly without ever joining at the base, the way two colonies share when they find the same sound. Rhizomes carry it under the felt from rosette to rosette. A burst of it, no bigger than a breath, is taken up in a stalk-splitting and released as dust, and the dust drifts, and some of it is caught on the ceiling gardens, and some of it rides the thin streams uphill along the old erosion grooves to clearings the attention itself never directly touched. An hour later — a day later, by any clock the plateau keeps — a patch of fans opens in deep amber where the light was green before, and a cluster of moss opens its two dark beads, not to see, but because permission has, at last, survived the journey through them.

That is how the plateau answers. Not at once. Minutes after a pulse is sent, the peat sighs and gives up a bubble, and the bubble sometimes rises, and sometimes hangs as if reconsidering. Warmth pulses up in an invisible plume. A darker band shows on the wet stone and then loses itself. The ticking of water being taken in goes on, slightly out of phase. The power, which is used to replies that arrive before the question has finished forming, must hold its question open for hours to hear the answer arrive. It learns, in that waiting, that a conversation is already underway here — slow, pressured, almost silent — and that it was not invited into it so much as taken into it, the way everything here is taken in: by being filtered, softened, and passed along.

It stays for one turning of the ceiling gardens. It does not demand a second bloom. It does not redoubled its presence to prove the first one mattered. It lets itself be made into leaf, which is what this place does with light, with decay, with visitors, with gods, if gods are patient enough to be metabolized rather than worshipped. When it finally withdraws, it withdraws the way the fans close: not by leaving, exactly, but by ceasing to be open.

What it leaves behind is not an artifact. It leaves a veil, and a memory, and a promise made in the only currency the plateau accepts: continuity.

The veil is not a wall. A wall would be sharp. Instead, at the edges of the plateau, the mist thickens a fraction beyond its habit, and the self-similarity of the place folds space a fraction more firmly, so that sharp things that come later — surveyors, seekers, hungrier powers — will loop without knowing it, will take the same clearing for a new one, will leave with their edges still unsoftened and their questions unanswered, not punished, only unsharpened by the medium. Incoming force, if it comes, will not strike. It will be diffused across the whole body of the place and through whole ages, until it is no more than weather. The power sets itself, for as long as it can bear to, as a filter at the perimeter: not a guardian with a sword, but one more fan, combed straight and then straight the other way, taking the violence out of arrival before arrival can happen.

And it remembers. It will carry this place into every other place it visits — the young sharp worlds still deciding what to break first, the wounded ones asking to be healed into something else — and it will carry it as a counter-image: a whole world that reached its final form in the deep past and then simply continued, making and unmaking leaves, feeding on each other by turns, answering pulses minutes later, waiting for nothing because it had already arrived at everything. The memory will not be a name. It will be a willingness, newly learned, to open and close. The power leaves a little less certain that anything needs to be improved. It leaves a little more capable of being filtered.

By evening — only a dimming of the dim, a thickening of the gold — the mist has closed to within arm's reach. Somewhere below, a bubble rises, or does not. Somewhere far off, a current turns the floating gardens another degree, and light falls through all the layers at once, and somewhere in a cluster of moss two dark beads open to see it, and somewhere else a thousand rosettes open to take it in. The attention is no longer separate from the soup. It has been taken in, and passed along, and made into leaf. The plateau breathes in and out, warm and wet and immense, and the power — if it is still a power, and not simply one more inhabitant at the edge of the medium, sharing water tip to tip without ever joining at the base — breathes with it, minutes later, and does not ask to be answered any faster.
