It notices the place not from the ridge but from underfoot, because that is where it begins.

Attention does not arrive here the way it arrives elsewhere — no breaking, no descent, no light let down into the lanes. Arriving is a kind of cutting, and this place dies back to a cut. So the attention comes the way cold comes into the basin: slowly, through the rock, through the water threads, through the felt first.

It finds the fungal threads before it finds the houses. White, finer than hair, stitching root to root. Thickening where a root is bruised. It finds the springtails scattering from a footprint, the cold worm eating its air pockets through the mat, the film of algae turning in the black spring water and shimmering with protozoa too small to name. It finds the first population, the ones who make the felt a felt and not just a pile of dead things, and it understands at once what kind of power would be needed here, and what kind would ruin it.

A lesser benevolence would look for the tenders first. Faces. Souls. Hands that might be warmed.

This one looks for the foundation. And the foundation says: nothing here can be separated.

That is the whole lesson, and it is taught without words, in the cross-section of a wall where dead stems freeze solid in the middle of living bark and become scaffold for the next growth weaving through. Nothing discarded. A fallen leaf kept from rotting by the cold. A tender laid into the mat when she dies, and persisting, and becoming foundation for the next growth, exactly as a stem does. A house built on its own husks. The city built on the city, because there is nowhere else for anything to go.

The attention, older than the mountain, had watched the glaciers leave this basin clean and black and angular. It had watched the green mass learn, in just a few cold generations since the rock was born, how to be a town with only itself as material. It had not seeded it. It had not taught it. Seeding and teaching both require a separation — a teacher apart from the taught, a perfect plant apart from the imperfect shelf — and separation is death on the wind off the ridge. So it had stepped back, and let wind and snow-load and generations of hands braid the tendrils back from the gap in the thicket wall, and let the lanes arch and meet and be reopened by bending stems, and let the mouth be a tool for sealing a graft because there are no other tools and a tool made of metal would have to be brought in, and anything brought in would have to be carried out, and nothing here is carried out.

Now that it has noticed, now that its attention has actually settled on the shelf, the temptations come in the usual shapes. They are dangerous precisely because they are benevolent.

Heart says: they are cold. Lift the cloud deck a little. Let the sun arrive at noon. Warm the membranes so they do not freeze amber. Give them fire, give them a lamp, give them a light that is not grey-green twilight, so their large unhurried eyes do not have to learn the dim.

Reason answers: if the sun arrives, the ice wedged in the cracks melts too fast, the scree slides too far, the young cliff settles too quickly, and the shelf that holds the mat cracks. If fire comes, the vent reed that blackens only from cold would blacken from soot, the interior would sour and warm, the mat would dry and shrink and split even in the cold, and everything that must stay wet would begin to smolder at the edges. They are not cold in the way a house without a fire is cold. They are cold the way a packed, respiring biomass is cold — warmer by a degree or two in the upper chambers, collectively, enough. To add more heat would be to unmake the insulation they have grown.

Heart says: they are dark, and quiet, and sound carries poorly, and their speech is mostly touch. Give them a voice that carries. Give them a name for the city. Give them a god to speak to at night besides the drip that rings faintly like a distant bell.

Reason answers: a voice that carries would be too loud in the wet air, like your own breath seeming too loud when you first step through the gap. A name, once spoken from outside, would become a thing apart from the vegetation, and the vegetation is the town. A god would separate the tenders from the plants they mouth shut, from the shoulders that polish the dome walls, from the root connection through which a hand on a shared wall can feel a neighbor's slow pulse. They do not need a god apart. They have a neighbor three domes away felt through a foot on the mat. That is enough separation, and enough company.

Heart says: they are few. Far too few to prune the city, too few to pollinate it reliably. Send the white grouse-like bird in flocks. Send more insects, more salamanders, more slug-grazers to scrape the walls clean. Send more tenders, so the lanes do not close.

Reason answers: density is not a problem to be solved here; it is the solution. The city grows only inward and upward because beyond the thicket wall the wind is unbroken and young shrubs lose their grafts in a single winter. If you prune it open, it loses heat. If you pollinate it from outside, it learns to need what it has learned to graft without. If you send more hands, more hands will press the mat, more mouths will be needed, and the tenders have learned to be small — sideways through the gap, stooped under the arches — in the way things learn to be small over generations. One leaf-shaped insect clinging motionless to a single stem for a whole day is not a failure of pollination. It is a witness, perfectly at scale.

Heart says: save them. When a wall is cut, heal it. When a tender dies in the green-dark, lift her out through the gap before the felt takes her. When a lane seals into a tunnel and then a wall, open it again, permanently. When the winter is hard, stop the winter.

Reason, and intuition deeper than reason, answer together: if you heal the cut, they will learn to cut carelessly. If you carry the body out, you remove foundation and teach that dead things are waste to be removed, and waste is not removed here. If you open the lane permanently, you end the tending, and tending is not maintenance of an object; it is the relationship itself. If you stop the winter, you break the cold that keeps dead matter from rotting too fast, that keeps the city layered on its own husks, that slows everything — fungi, salamander, tender, graft — to a pace at which connection can keep up.

The most dangerous thing a benevolent and wise power can do here is be benevolent in the usual way.

So it does almost nothing. And the almost-nothing is precise, and it is done from the ridge, not from inside.

It keeps the scree sharp, so that descent is always a descent, not a road. It keeps the cloud deck low and flat, so the sun remains a rumor and the light remains grey-green, and visitors do not stay long enough to map the lanes. It keeps the wind over the ridge but not through the thicket, so the frost forms on the windward rim and the lee stays beaded with condensation. It keeps the gap in the wall just wide enough to turn sideways through. No wider. Anyone wider would be an entrance, and an entrance would be an exit, and exits are how places like this are emptied.

When you descended — hat brushing the arching stems, breath condensing and seeming too loud, water everywhere and deep nowhere, the whole oval invisible from inside and the lanes invisible from outside — it did not follow you down. To follow would have been to brush the stems, to add a third presence to a place where three presences crowd a chamber, to make the mat tick under a weight that was not a tender's. It let the city make you small. It let the cold and the wet and the dim do the work of forgetting for you, so that you would leave by the gap remembering the feeling of the felt springing back under your boots, but not the route to any particular dome. It does not erase memory violently. That would be a cut. It lets dimness do it.

Inside, when it acts, it acts as a hinge, not a hand.

In a winter that is a fraction harder than the grafts can bear, it does not stop the winter. It shifts a woven culvert of hollow reed by a fraction, so one black thread of spring water does not freeze solid and sour a reservoir plant. The salamander in that channel, thumb-long and translucent, barely moves, and continues.

When a tender mouths a graft closed, teeth and tongue softening a tendril, lips pressing sap into a wound, it does not seal it for her. It lets the mouth be enough. It has seen mouths be enough for generations.

When a hand is pressed to a shared wall and feels a pulse three domes away through the root connection, some of that pulse is the mountain, and some of it is the attention passing through the root network the way water passes through a culvert — indistinguishable, correctly misattributed to a neighbor. The wise power prefers to be mistaken for a neighbor than worshipped. Worship would separate.

It does not speak. Speech, here, would be sound too loud for the packed vegetation to swallow. The city answers in its own medium: stems ticking as they contract in the cold, snow sheeting off a roof-leaf with a hiss, a root probing a crack and thickening a fraction while no one watches, leaves turning almost imperceptibly toward the light at dusk. The attention listens for a long time, in glacier time. That listening is its presence. A place is held, sometimes, by being listened to at the right distance — close enough to warm the walls by the faintest degree, far enough not to bend a stem.

And when, in time, the mountain is no longer young — when the cliffs soften, when snow no longer wedges in the cracks, when the shelf widens and the wind finds another way — it will not rebuild the city. It will not preserve it as a perfect green mass filling its basin to the edges, mist rising, frost on the rim. Preservation would be a cage with living bars. It will let the lanes seal, the domes stop being fed by their neighbors, the granary husks cool and be overgrown, the tenders' laid bodies become simply foundation, and the whole too-regular density thin back into an ordinary forest, which is not a failure of the city but its final layer of husk.

To love it benevolently is to let it finish becoming forest again, in reverse, the way it became a city forward.

From the ridge, then, it does not look like a place at all. The attention settles there, where a visitor first stood, and looks at the too-packed green too regular to be forest, and knows the lanes and domes and reservoirs inside it are invisible from this height, exactly as they should be. It keeps the mist thin enough to rise, and thick enough to hide. It does not go through the sideways gap. It has no need to turn sideways; it was never going to enter.

It stays as attention — not as teacher, not as savior, not as light — and leaves the tenders bending and tying and mouthing the city closed again, day after dim day, keeping it alive by keeping it wet, and connected, and dense, and unexpanded, and alone in the only way that matters: not isolated, because everything here is connected root to root, but unseparated, because nothing here is apart from anything else.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything. It would do the nothing that keeps the limits intact, and call that love.
