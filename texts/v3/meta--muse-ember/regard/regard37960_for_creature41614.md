A wise power does not notice a place like this by being loud. It notices because almost everywhere else, making is tied to eating. A forest makes leaves in order, somewhere down the line, to be eaten. An animal makes fat in order to be eaten, or to eat. Everything keeps a ledger.

Here there is no ledger. There is only production.

That is rare enough, and strange enough, that even a benevolent power would pause at the notch in the ridge and look in — not with the sun, not with a gaze that warms, but with attention held slightly to the side so as not to become another shaft of light on the floor.

And its first impulse would be benevolent in the obvious way: to complete it.

It would want to give the bowl what seems to be missing. Birds for the canopy, so the roaring wind would have something to answer it. Insects for the pristine leaves, frogs for the mirror-black pockets under the ferns, so the food chain would close and the hum would start and the place would feel, to human senses, *alive*. It would want to lift the lid of cloud a little and let the sun that already exists on the other side of the ridge come in, to give it a stream instead of a rumor of water, to steady the cliffs so a tree might live its full span and soil might thicken and hold.

All of those gifts would be beautiful. That is precisely the danger.

A wise power would see, if it stayed long enough to listen through the pulses of wind and the thirty-second calms, that the bowl is not unfinished. It is in its first fast age. Every lowland forest was once something like this, before animals learned to cross — wind-pollinated, spore-seeded, growing without negotiation. The leaves are unpierced not because herbivores have failed to find them, but because cold, altitude, newness, and turbulence have excluded anything with fur or feathers or breath, while rewarding anything that can travel as a spore and live on mist. To bring in teeth now would not simply add a layer. It would rewrite the terms.

Leaves would learn to be bitter. Trees would learn to be thick. Fern fiddleheads would learn timing. The sheer, unpruned deployment — fronds the size of doors, vines hauling themselves hand over hand up clean fault planes, epiphyte roots hanging in air drinking rain that falls sideways for a minute — would be traded for a more familiar economy of defense and delay. The place would become healthier, by our measure. And it would stop being itself.

Light is scarce here, so it is used completely, leaf upon leaf, tier upon tier. To add more light would not increase that completeness; it would end the reason for it. Water is intermittent, so everything catches it — moss thick as a mattress, hanging roots, broad lamina answering each fat drop with a *tok*. To give it a river would end the reason for a million small collectors. Rock is young, still arguing with gravity, shedding daily in ticks and avalanches, pale where it splits and then orange-streaked and green within weeks. To stabilize it would end the constant supply of fresh surface that the whole bowl depends on.

And the being that lives here — if you can call the green one being — does not experience any of this as lack. A tree fern does not miss a grazer. An epiphyte does not miss a pollinator. Moss steaming faintly in a moving shaft of light is not waiting for eyes to see it steam. *Nothing is looking at you* is not a deprivation in this room. It is the condition that allows the work to go on without self-consciousness. Loneliness is the visitor's feeling, carried in through the doorway in his chest, where the thin air catches. A wise power would not cure a loneliness the place does not have.

So what would it do?

Not nothing. Restraint, done well, is an active thing.

It would keep the notch no wider than a doorway, and keep the jet coming through it. The wind is doing the jobs of animals without the cost of animals: it is pollinator, pruner, seed-carrier, and the thing that keeps wings from settling. It would keep the cloud lid mostly closed, and let the pale shafts move and vanish. It would not buttress the cliffs. It would let them shed, let soil slump and be undermined, let trees be levered out whole and become log-shaped hills where new trees rise through their branches. It would let fungi and bacteria run their black, silent conveyor at their own pace, converting whole leaves to humus without hurry, because slow rot is part of why so much stands un-eaten.

It would station itself, in effect, at the threshold facing outward. Not guarding the bowl from itself, but guarding it from use. No path would be cut. No cairn stacked. Footprints in spongy ground would vanish as they already do. A traveler might cross it in a step, hear the bamboo knock and the condensation fall metallic from the cliff and his own breath thin in the thin air, and feel himself a trespasser in a workshop running itself — and then find, gently and without explanation, that he cannot find the step back, that maps lose the ridge, that the name he might have given it will not hold. Not as punishment. As quarantine. The greatest threat to this place is not rockfall. It is the animal that eats by looking, naming, and taking home a spore on his boot.

It would not name it, and it would not show it. It would not make it a parable, a proof, an Eden to point to for others. A benevolent power that uses a place as an example has already begun to consume it.

And it would not freeze it. Wisdom is not embalming. In time the mountain will wear, the bowl will open, the climate will shift, and an animal may ride a gust through the notch by its own means. The power would not bar that arrival forever. It would let the phase end when the place itself ends it. Its protection is for the youth of the place, not for its eternity. It will not arrest time to keep the leaves pristine.

In the end, the exchange is reversed. The power came prepared to improve, and leaves having been taught.

It leaves with the plant's economy in its hands: to produce without needing to consume what is produced, to witness without needing to be witnessed in return, to love a thing without improving it into something else. That is the only gift the bowl can give that does not diminish it, and the only gift the power can accept without betraying its own nature.

Then it steps back across the notch, as the traveler did, in a single step. The wall of rock closes behind it. The wind comes in as a jet and ricochets off the opposing wall and comes back at itself. The ferns whip and spring back. The epiphytes drink the mist. Nothing startles. Nothing turns a head.

For a moment, something was looking at them. And it chose, kindly, to look away — just enough — so that nothing would ever feel looked at.
