### At the Lip

They noticed it not because it was bright, but because it was the only thing in their hearing that did not ask to be changed.

Even the other summits rising from the cloud-sea send up weather, a shift, a bird out of season, a stone rolling down to begin a new valley. This one sent up only mist, at the same temperature, in the same amount, year after year. From far away it registered as a held note. Close up, it registered as a finished thing: a prayer said once, perfectly, and never revised.

They came, or rather they arrived at the rim of their attention to it, and they understood at once what it was. The root-deck and the fish beneath it, the frogs on their patches, the turtles in their basins, the birds on their unseen lines, the insects ending at the edge of a leaf — these were not tenants in a place. They were one being living *as* a place. Its skin was root weave, its blood black water, its breath the chorus that never paused. Stone held by roots, roots fed by water, leaves feeding the water, the dead eaten before they could decay. No part of it was surplus. No part of it was waiting.

They did not step onto the causeway. They had no need of feet, and in any case the springy deck would have known them. However gently placed, theirs would have been the first new weight in longer than the frogs could remember, the first pop in the channels that did not belong to a fish. A wise power does not make its first visit into an event.

So they stayed at the lip, beyond the last step, in the cloud-sea below the wall where nothing fell. From there they considered, as any benevolent thing must, what kindness could do.

They thought of light. They thought of opening the vault of leaves, of letting a true sun through, of giving those broad, waxy leaves something more to catch. It would have blinded the frogs, whose eyes were made for gloom. It would have burned the mist that the whole system breathed in and out. It would have dried the films running down the walls and killed the slow life that lived in them. The plants were not starved. They were built for almost nothing, and almost nothing was enough.

They thought of rain, and of seasons, and of moving the water even a finger's width. The waterline was the same dark band of moss on every bowl and pillar for a reason. The fish had no elsewhere to go; their channels were their world, and the level was their sky. To drain a pool, to flood a step, to give the place a dry spell in which to grow or a wet one in which to rot, would not have been variety. It would have been eviction, performed slowly, on creatures who had no concept of leaving.

They thought of company. A new bird with a different call, a new plant that did not remember the plan, a voice that could tell the frogs their patches had names. The temptation was strong, because love often mistakes itself for introduction. But the birds already carried pollen from vault to vault. The insects already kept every leaf clean by eating, in place, everything that would grow. The chorus already had all its pitches, each patch tuned by its depth and its mat. To add a note would not have enriched the song. It would have made every other note wrong, forever, in a place that could not move away from the error.

They thought, in pity, of ending the stillness. It can look, from outside, like a beautiful prison: nothing wandering, nothing new, nothing growing within sight, a leaf falling and becoming the same mat tomorrow. But pity belonged to the onlooker, not to the inhabitant. The fish were not bored in their channel. The turtle was not waiting for a season. The frog on its leaf was not rehearsing a life it would have preferred. They had arrived at the last form their lives would take, not as a sentence but as a fit — root to stone, mouth to midge, waste to root again. There was no suffering in the place that a change could relieve, only a risk that a change would create.

And they thought of gifts — a tool, a fire, a word, a hand reaching into the water to show that someone had noticed. There were no tools here, no boats, no metal, no signs, not because the builders had failed to leave them, but because the place had learned to maintain itself without them. The first tool would have been the first foreign object. The first fire would have been the first thing that needed feeding from outside the loop. The first word, spoken into the chorus, would have been the first thing the place could not eat, clean, or turn into mat.

So they did the only things that could be done from outside, at the boundary, in the language the place already understood: constancy.

They became the outermost wall. Not stone, not root, but attention held steady at the lip. Anything that would have fallen into that closed world from the sky — a meteor, a curious seeding bird, a human thought that called it a ruin and wanted to dig, a silence from a louder world — they turned aside, thousands of metres below the waterline, without making a ripple. The water kept its level. The drips kept their pattern on the same leaves. The chorus did not pause for breath, because there was nothing new to breathe around.

They kept the count, because a perfect humming that no one remembers is close to anonymous. Not to catalogue it like a museum, but to know it: this basin for the turtles, that lily patch pitched a third lower than the next, this channel where the fish turn and pop against the decking. They learned it the way you learn the breathing of someone asleep beside you — not to wake them, not to improve the rhythm, but so that if the rhythm ever falters, it will not falter unheard.

They did not sing along. Even a power that could have out-sung the frogs and the birds and the insects together refrained from adding itself to the loop. Its silence was not absence. It was a different kind of listening, slower than the drip, longer than the time it took a school of fish to reform in the same channel. It matched the place's tempo instead of asking the place to match its own.

And they stayed. That was the heart of it, in the end. A wise power knows that a finished world needs no improvement. A benevolent one knows that finished does not mean forgotten. So they remained at the edge, in the cold air that ached a visitor's ears, keeping the world out and keeping company in — two stillnesses, theirs and the place's, not merging, not interfering, each allowing the other to continue.

They also accepted the limit of all this. The mountain will move, in its own time, far beyond the ten years in which a visitor could mark the waterline and find it unchanged. A stone will soften past what moss can armour. One day a frog may not be on its leaf, and the mat may not be quite the same. When that day comes, the power will not force the old waterline back into place, and will not call the change a failure. It will simply still be there, at the lip, so that the first crack in the finished form is not also the first moment of abandonment. It will walk, if it must, one pace with the first wanderer — and then let go.

If you arrive there now, wet to the knees, your feet numb, your ripples closing at once behind you, you will see nothing different. The waterline will be where it always was. The frogs will be on their patches. The dome of a turtle will rise far off across a basin and sink again without haste. And if you stand at the outer rim long enough, feeling the vertigo of so much fullness perched over so much empty cloud, you may sense a second stillness beside your own — not part of the chorus, not breaking it, not asking it to change. That is what they did.

They did not improve the place. They kept it company at its edge, kept the world from falling into it, and kept its time with it, until the humming, which never needed saving, also never needed to be alone.
