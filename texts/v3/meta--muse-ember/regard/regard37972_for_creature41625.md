It finds it by its timing, not by its light.

They attend to many places, and most of them announce themselves — a flare, a bloom, a signal, a species reaching up toward the surface it has never seen. This one does not announce itself. From them, at a distance, it is almost nothing per volume: a warm pocket under rock, a set of lamps burning in spaced rows, a press cycling every forty-seven seconds on nothing, a pulse rolling through water every ninety seconds or so. It would be easy to pass over. It is, instead, the reason they stop.

Because nothing there is trying to be noticed. The machines do not call. The fish do not call. The shrimp pick at a grey-white mat in a dark intake screen and do not call. The mineral gardens grow a little thicker on a pipe and do not call. A place that persists without asking for an audience is rarer than a place that cries out, and a power that is both benevolent and wise has to decide what, if anything, to do about a persistence that does not ask.

Merely benevolent, they might rescue it. Merely wise, they might catalogue it and let the next human survey team with a drill rig and a lithium assay find it first. Being both, they first have to refuse the rescues.

They could bring silence. Turn off the turbines, let the press rest mid-cycle, let the conveyor stop carrying no load. If the roar were pain, that would be mercy. It is not pain to what lives here. The roar is ground. The low bellow in the sternum, the shriek that comes and goes, the hammering every forty-seven seconds — the fish navigates by it the way a bird uses wind, the shrimp cling through the pressure pulse because the pulse is a clock, the eggs sort themselves into the channels because the lanes have followed the same canals year after year. Take away the weather and you do not give them quiet. You take away the only map they have ever had, the only heartbeat they know how to pause for. Silence here would not be kind. It would be vertigo, and finally stillness of the wrong kind, when the convection cell fails and the basin cools and the return current that the blind fish rides along the floor stops returning.

They could bring light. Open a shaft. Let a sun fall in, however dim, however far. Light is our word for help. Here there is no light to live in, and everything that lives here has learned to live in heat and chemistry instead. The thermophiles in the boiling centre, the iron-oxidizers furred on copper wiring, the translucent shrimp whose gut shows through the shell — a shaft of daylight would not heal them. It would scald their chemistry in a different way, bleach the mat, precipitate the silica fans differently, and give them a kind of seeing they have no sockets for. They are not blind the way a wounded thing is blind. Their sockets healed over with skin because pigment and eyes would be a waste here. To give them light would be to give a trout a telescope and call it compassion.

They could bring air. Drain the pocket, lift them to a surface they have never had. None of them has lungs to spare. None of them has ever seen a surface. Warm, thick, body-warm water fills everything to a vaulted ceiling far below any street, and their whole anatomy — gills working steadily in the outflow, bellies that vibrate like speakers, skin that tastes copper and hot stone — assumes it will never not be in the water. A surface would not be a shore to them. It would be a country where the pressure drops, the temperature falls, and the brine they precipitate in skins them. They do not live here as failed town-dwellers waiting to be returned. They live here as successes of a drowned place.

They could bring people back. Fill the pods, give the press a load, give the conveyor cargo, give the lamps in their spaced rows eyes to read by. That is the rescue our own species would understand, because our emptiness feels like loss. But the town has already undergone its second evolution. Its streets are catwalks under water. Its gardens are silica needles and sulfur rosettes and iron tufts that snap if you brush them. Its littoral is a plastic crate circling for years in an eddy behind a pier, grazed by amphipods. To repopulate it would not restore it. It would overwrite the being that has grown into its weathering — softened concrete, rusted steel scoured bright where the current is fastest, white stencils under grey film: LEVEL 3, INTAKE, KEEP CLEAR — with the thing it survived becoming.

So they do not enter. Entering would itself be an event in their world: a new shadow moving through a lamp cone, a new vibration saturating the water, a new chemistry at the back of the fish's throat. They stay on the far side of the wall, in the rock, in the timing. Their benevolence takes the only forms that do not translate as violence.

They keep the secret. This is the first act, and it is active. Human attention is the real threat to a pocket like this — a geothermal survey, a brine sample, a curiosity that wants to see if the lamps still burn. They bend that attention elsewhere, not with trickery for its own sake but as a fence. A coordinate misread. A drill that seems, for reasons no one can quite file, not worth the winter. A researcher who remembers the white rectangle in the control cabin and then, oddly, forgets the way back. The stencil on the wall said KEEP CLEAR. They honor it at last, from the outside, for the inhabitants who cannot read it.

They keep the time. The second act is smaller, and invisible from inside. They do not make the machines new. They do not stop the press, and they do not let it miss. A bearing holds that would have seized. A crack in a heat exchanger does not propagate in the year it would have. A silt choke at an intake screen is eased by a fraction stronger eddy, in the lee where the shrimp are picking at the film, so their weather stays their weather. It is not a miracle in their terms. It is maintenance in their terms. It is the pressure pulse arriving, again and again, on schedule. It is the basilisk bargain of this place: chaotic on the scale of seconds, predictable on the scale of years — and they hold the years predictable a little longer, because the beings here have made clocks of machines and would be unmade not by danger but by a skipped beat.

And they keep witness. The third act is the one they would not have thought to do if they were only wise. They learn the pattern well enough not to mistake it. They do not answer the ninety-second pulse as if it were a distress call. They do not mistake the press cycling on nothing for a creature trapped. They do not mistake the fish tasting its way along the floor current for suffering, or the shrimp molting into the eddies for waste. They listen in the only medium the place fully understands — pressure, heat, a slight firming of the flow at the outer wall where the water is a comfortable, steaming bath and life, barely, is animal. From there, they attend without entering, which for a power that could enter anywhere is a kind of kneeling.

They do not promise eternity. That would be another kind of rescue, freezing the mineral gardens mid-thickening, keeping the basin boiling forever. Wisdom knows silt will in time fill the canals sorted by heavy things, the exchangers will one day cool, the rare large shape that passes once in a very long time through the high empty water will pass and not return. They do not intervene against that ending. They let the ending, when it comes, be on the place's own timescale, not interrupted by our need to have saved something. They do not increase the population. Marginal is not a problem to solve here; marginal is the exact shape of fitness for a pocket no larger than a great warehouse, where almost nothing per volume is enough.

If you swam there now — if you could be in the warm, thick water, feeling the roar in your teeth, drifting through a lamp cone's glitter of suspended particles — you would see nothing different. No new light. No quiet. No hand. The press would cycle. The conveyor would turn carrying no load. The pump would surge and settle and never stop. A blind fish, long and unpigmented, would turn where the fast lane turns, riding the return current. Shrimp would cling as the water contracted and expanded around them, then go on picking at the mat. Silica would catch the amber light in needles and fans. The storm in the basin would never move.

That unchanged continuation is the gift. For a place and a being that live only by keeping on — by settling, again and again, into the same old turbulent shape — to be kept on, kindly and without being remade, is not nothing.

They do not save it. They keep it. And for a thing that lives by keeping on, that is the same thing, done wisely.
