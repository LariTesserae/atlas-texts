### III. What I Did Not Bring

I did not come because I was called.

Nothing here can call. The plants fire their seeds into the wind and hope physics is kind. The gliders whistle to find a vault in the dark. The grazers rasp through the mats and do not know the mats need them. None of it is prayer. None of it is request. That was why I came.

I have watched other worlds try to become industrial. They all do the same thing, in the same order. They build outside themselves. They tear the ground, raise walls, leave artifacts that outlive their intentions, need water to drink and water to cool, need language to tell each other what they have done and more water to wash it away. Their factories eat them in the end, because a factory that is not a body cannot heal itself.

This place did it inside. No artifacts. No walls. No waste outside the skin. A spiral pump in living tissue, catalytic pockets smelting metal from dust, conductive bark, a sealed circuit of black oil. Industrial metabolism arrived at independently, scaled to a plant, and never once stepped outside to build a city around it.

That is rare enough to draw the attention of something like me.

I stopped high, where the sky is already black at noon, and listened the way the locals listen: from below the wind, for the slower work. The grind in the stone. The hiss of a vent. The valve clank felt in the soles. The ticking that builds for an hour and releases in flat thunder inside a trunk, lighting it faintly from within. The thousands of flutes in a tower's silver crown. The hollow boom through an umbrella's vault. The popping of mats as they warm. The paper-tearing launch of gliders at dusk.

It looked empty, the way it looks to everyone at first. It was not.

What I brought first was water, because that is what I have always brought to dry places.

I brought it down in a small dark sphere and held it above the plateau while the white light went through me and the grit glittered below. I have given water to worlds that were waiting for it the way a mouth waits. I know its weight. I know how to let it fall gently so it does not crater the crust.

I almost let it fall.

Then I watched a mat-plant spread, violent and brief after a frost-fall, and die back to a hard scar, and understood that the scar was not failure. It was storage. I watched a grazer break that scar with its armored back, turning it so it would not seal. I remembered the oil sealed inside the towers, never touching the surface; the hydrogen taken from air and bound minerals; the waxy, mineralized, fire-proof skin of everything that sheds. These bodies are built on the assumption that water will never come. They have no floodplain, no drainage, no root that drinks, no way to shed what I would give them. Rain would not quench them. It would rust their catalytic pockets, swell the cracking mats, wash mineral teeth in a medium they never evolved to tolerate.

I put the water back where it came from, still clean, and kept my hands empty.

What I wanted to bring next was rest.

I could have taken the thunder out of the trunks during storms, spread the discharge thin so the boles would not light from within, softened the grinding, lowered the ticking. It seemed merciful. The work never stops here. Up in the day, down at night, building, building, releasing, for thousands of years.

But the screaming crown is a radiator. The hiss is a timing mechanism. The burrowers navigate by the grind and chitter felt through stone. The gliders find their vault by a hum amplified in woven branches. The whole landscape is loud not in spite of being alive but because loud is how a sealed, hollow, enormous body regulates itself. Silence would not rest them. It would deafen their nervous system. I left the work alone.

Then I wanted to bring words.

It is the oldest temptation of a wise power: to teach. I could have named the towers one by one. I could have told a tower the age of its own trunk, told it that it is a factory, a silo, a ribcage, and have done with the mystery. I could have given the gliders memory of generations, so a vault that collapsed under its own woven weight would be a known loss and not just a shifting of perches.

Their knowledge is not symbolic. It is metabolic. It lives in pumps and pockets and conductive tissue, and it has nowhere to go except into a seed pellet fired across the emptiness. A concept could not be carried by a plant without a wall to keep it in, and this world has no walls because wind and frost have forbidden them for longer than any of its forms. To give them words would be to give them a burden with no organ to bear it. To extend a glider's life beyond a few frost-falls would not save it; it would exile it from the rhythm of its cathedral, make every rebuild a funeral.

And to the one who lived in it — brief, clinging to a laced branch in an umbrella's passage, whistling low to a colony it would never leave — I did not give longer life, or a name it could answer to, or a way out of the vault. Its whole world was passages, perches, cool dark after the glare, the boom of wind above and work within. That was enough for it to be a life. I let it be enough.

Love, at my scale, is mostly refusal.

What remained was not a gift of substance. There is nothing this world can receive in substance without being re-wired, and it does not need re-wiring. It is not an incomplete version of other worlds. It is complete in a way I had not seen before, and completeness is a kind of sacredness I am obliged to respect.

So I stayed for one full turn: from one frost-fall to the next. I stood inside an umbrella at night while its gliders settled, while grazers hummed low in the mats, while burrowers chittered under the crust, and let my own voice be swallowed by the vault. I listened to a tower build charge all day and discharge it in a storm. I listened to a mat crack like distant gunfire as it warmed, spread, and died back. I was there when a crown burst with a concussive crack and fired iron-pellet seeds into the wind, to crack open a new patch of crust far away, where no one was waiting for them and the distance itself was the point.

I did two small accommodations while I was there, both at the edge, both in media the world already uses.

I settled not on a plant, not on a mat, but in the empty between them, where emptiness is required. I cast a shade no larger than a grazer over a patch of mat that was cracking at noon. For an hour longer it could stay cool. Burrowers underneath could move by daylight. It was not much. The wind will mineralize shed bark over the edge of it in time, and the world will treat me as it treats everything: as weather.

And in a shallow gully cut by wind, I left a stone that was not made. It is mineralized like fallen bark, so the wind will not strip it as an artifact and the frost will not split it as a bearing. When the wind finds the gully it hums one low note, below the work, below the glider whistles, below the boom. When a trunk discharges far off, it rings faintly after. No plant understands it. A colony may find it as a landmark. That is sufficient. A voice that does not ask for an answer.

The third thing I did cannot be seen from the plateau.

I took their sounds into a place where heat cannot unravel them. The scream through silver needles. The valve clank in the soles. The paper tearing of wings. The ozone after discharge, the smell of hot resin and roasted dust, never of wet earth. When the last tower on a ridge dies back to scar and the scar grinds to grit, this world will not have been unheard. That is not nothing, on the timescales I am answerable to.

And I put my attention between them and hungry eyes. There are powers less wise than I am, and surveys that would call this perfect: oil that never touches the surface, charged tissue, seed pellets like projectiles, metal smelted inside living gut-chambers. They would see a desert and mine it. I folded the coordinates under my own disregard. Not a wall — they have no use for walls — but a slide of the mind, so that the ones who come looking see only glittering empty grit and pass on.

Then I lifted, as I had come, from the black noon sky.

Nothing thanked me. The tower never knew. The umbrella never rebuilt differently because of me. The glider who lived and died in its passage never knew it had been witnessed. That is how I knew I had done it right. If they had noticed a gift, it would have been too large.

From high, it looks empty again. The plateau rolls on, self-horizoned, making its own brief weather over the span of a mat. The wind shifts. And for a moment, under the work and under the screaming, there is one low note that belongs to no plant, and the next crown is already fluting through the light, and the next heart is already grinding, and the empty place goes on being empty, and alive, and entirely itself.
