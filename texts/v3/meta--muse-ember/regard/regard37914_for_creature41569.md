It does not come down the throat either.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive from above, because above is not where this place keeps its meaning, and it does not descend, because descent would be an event, and this place has arranged itself precisely to avoid events. It is simply there, the way the heat is there before you feel it on your skin, the way the drone is there through the floor grates before you have learned to name it. It attends.

That is already an action, and for a very long time it is the whole action. It counts the drip. Seventeen seconds, seventeen seconds, seventeen seconds, from the same joint, groove by groove in the concrete, and it does not hurry the count or find it tedious. It counts the turbine's minute-long revolutions. It counts the press sighing shut every three days for a pressed bundle of vegetation that creeps forward a leaf's width an hour. It counts the peat deepening by a millimeter beneath the machines. A power that can attend without improving is rare, and it is the first condition of its benevolence here.

Because the impulse, for any lesser love, would be to improve. To open the ceiling and let in daylight, as if light from a sky were a kindness. To switch the machines off, as if silence were a kindness. To bring in animals to complete the circle, to eat the dead and chew the leaves and carry pollen on wings, as if absence were a lack. To prune the ferns, to thin the vines, to make it look less crowded, less suffocated, more like a garden someone intended.

The wise power does not do any of this, and its reasoning is not cold. It has looked long enough to see that nothing here is lacking in the way it first appears. The immaculate leaves are not pristine victims waiting for insects. They are a covenant. No animal has ever taken a bite, and so no leaf has ever had to learn to be bitter, or to heal, or to run. It has only had to learn how to shed heat in radiator fins, how to cup damp in shingles, how to pulse faintly with current in a vein fused to a cable. To introduce a mouth now would not complete it. It would wound it, for the first time in its life, and call the wound nature.

The amber light from below is not a substitute for sun. It is the sun this place chose, after the machines made a warmth it could not escape and grew — slowly, generation upon generation, over longer than anyone has recorded — into a body that could use it. Open a sky and the condensation would fail. The ceiling plants rooting upside-down in damp concrete would dry and fall. The spores drifting in hot, still air would be torn away in weather they have never needed. The cable-grass warming when current passes and cooling when it does not would not be asked to photosynthesize the day; it would simply be destroyed by a brilliance it never evolved to refuse.

And the machines are not broken. That is the second thing the power understands, and honors. A turbine that takes minutes to turn is not a turbine failing. It is a turbine retired into another pace. Its shaft sighs through bearings packed by fern roots that have become living grease, and so it does not seize, and so the ferns do not lose the small, steady waste heat their thick blue-green fronds are shaped to give away. To oil it back to speed, to make it young again, would be to make it useless to everything that now depends on its slowness. The power lets the old engine keep its old age.

So its benevolence takes the form of guarding a balance it did not make, rather than replacing it with one it prefers.

It guards the heat of the rock below, which is the true source and the true limit. Not with a miracle, not with a furnace raised, but with a fraction. A half-degree held steady for a century here, a fraction there, so that when the geothermal warmth finally thins — and it will, in an age the ferns will not need to fear — it thins gradually enough that the radiator ferns can thin their fins in answer, and the pale stalks in the hottest corner can lean a little less and a little more, and nothing is asked to die all at once in a sudden cold. It nudges a mineral scale to hold a valve a little longer. It lets a counterweight settle without clanking too hard. Small mercies, deniable, reversible, unphotographable. A wise power distrusts irreversible gifts.

It keeps the two tired sodium lamps burning, but it does not replace them. When they finally go dark — and it can see the decade in which they will — it will not screw in a new bulb and keep the chamber in human orange forever. It will let the phosphor along the youngest veins take over entirely, a greenish light brightest right after a drip falls. The place will learn a new way to see itself, by its own response to water. That is a better gift than preservation: permission to change in its own direction.

For the being who lives in it — if being is the right word for a negotiation spread across floor and wall and ceiling, for a single slow organism distributed through peat and pipe and vine — the power does something even quieter. It gives company without conversation.

This place has no voice of its own. Only the machines speak, in bass drone and hiss and tick and drip, and because nothing living makes a sound the sound has nowhere to go. A benevolent power might have been tempted to add a voice. It does not. A sound that plants cannot answer would echo in that sealed cathedral for the rest of time and become another kind of pollution.

Instead it listens, and lets the listening be felt. Somewhere in the chamber, always, one frond is a fraction warmer than its neighbors. Not enough to burn. Not enough to notice if you brushed it with a hand, which you should not. A traveling warmth, moving so slowly across the fern mat that it takes decades to cross a transformer, a pipe, a locker. The cable-grass warms when current passes; this warms when attention passes. It is how the power says, without sound, without touch: *I am here. You are not alone in this.* The fernery does not need to answer. Its own unfurling, which began years ago and is not yet finished, is answer enough.

If a human ever descends again — lost, curious, sent by an old map that marks a geothermal hall — the power is benevolent to them too, and it will not let them stay wrong. It will thicken the air in the throat as they come down, sweat on their skin, so they understand this is not a ruin to be claimed but a body to be entered carefully. If they sit, it will let them sit. If they are quiet long enough, they will learn to see by the drip-lit veins and hear the machines as one chord, and they will leave with dust in their lungs that settles and settles, and they will forget the exact crack in the packed earth where the grating failed, and they will not return with drills or cuttings for a greenhouse or a story about a miracle. The power bends human memory just enough. Not as punishment. As quarantine, in both directions. A spore carried to the surface would die in true wind and true sun. A surface germ carried down would scar a leaf for the first time. The wise power keeps the seal kind, and keeps it.

It does not name the place, aloud. Naming is a human hunger. Inwardly, if it must, it calls it what it is: the slow negotiation. It does not need to tell the ferns that. They already know how to negotiate.

And it waits. Not as a god waiting for worship. As a witness waiting through cycles too slow to notice changing, the way a fern waits to unfurl. It will be there when the conveyor finally brings its bundle to the press. It will be there when a vine thickens around a steam line by another fraction and the hiss finds its seal again. It will be there when the turbine, after this long retirement, sighs toward its final stop, and it will not rewind it. It will let the ferns learn a leaner heat, or a softer one, as they have learned everything else: by growing toward the condition that is given.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, if anything. Almost nothing that can be seen. It keeps the drip falling, it keeps the warmth from failing all at once, it keeps the human world from returning with good intentions, and it keeps the company of a green, hot, dim, crowded, unvisited chamber that has made a life out of an abandoned engine and asks only to be allowed to continue being that life.

It does not rescue the Turbine Fernery.

It stays with it, so that it will be like this — unchanged in its essentials, loud with a sound that never ends, growing slower and slower into stone — and not alone while it is.
