It does not arrive over a horizon. When it comes, it comes down the steps.

It comes below the hedge, below the wire fence, below the gate, careful with its weight on the wet stone, careful not to startle the ducks that waddle up to the top step as if they were coming home. It has been paying attention for a long time before it ever makes this descent. That is its way. It does not swoop. It does not fill the bowl. It makes itself small enough to step around a shoal of fish crossing a flooded shed floor in two centimetres of water, and it walks the way everything here walks, at the speed of silt.

It was not drawn by a calling. The town has never called. That is why it stayed to look.

From above, the town is legible as a problem. That is how the world above the rim reads everything. Damp houses. Depopulation. Industry declined to a conveyor carrying one bucket of aggregate an hour toward a silo that may already be full. A flywheel that turns so slowly you could wait a minute for a spoke to cross a chalk mark. Children who learn not to run on the cobbles. Coats that never dry, boots that fill with seepage overnight. Fog that will not lift. The reports that reach a benevolent power are graphs and recommendations: drain the marshy lots, dry the cottages, cut a road through the rim, relocate the remaining residents, restore the works to full speed, let the sun in.

A lesser benevolence would have done all of it. A lesser wisdom, too.

What it finds when it stands in the square long enough — and here, standing is the natural tempo — is not a problem that has failed to be solved. It is a solution that has succeeded at being itself.

The barnacles crusting the conveyor housings are not a sign of neglect; they have made a coast where there is none, and they are feeding. The eels pouring themselves across a flooded office floor between the legs of presses are not invaders; they are using the building exactly as a building at this level of water can best be used. The cats on the driest inches of town, paws tucked, breath pluming, are not strays to be pitied; they are small deliberate furnaces who know precisely where the warmth collects in a motor housing. Lichen advancing over rust is not corrosion winning; it is corrosion being persuaded to become a skin, a protection. The flywheel's minute-long circuit is not failure; it is the correct speed for a place where even a shout comes back softened, as if through cloth, and where nothing is permitted to hurry or it will slip on algae.

Nothing here is waiting to become something else. It has already become what it is, and it intends, very slowly, to go on being it.

To dry it would be to kill it. To drain the canals would strand the carp turning in the brown water and leave the seepage threads on the quarry walls with nowhere to go. To lift the fog so the sun could touch more than the chimney tops would bleach the algae filmed around the lamps, bake the moss tide-mark off the north faces, and fry the pale fish holding station in the coldest water. To speed the conveyor and the crane would not restore confidence; it would tear wet sleeves, throw the whole slow food web — algae to snails to frogs to eels to nothing much at all — out of its gentle overlap. To evacuate the people who live upstairs would not rescue them from wet hems and cold boots. It would exile them from a home they have chosen, yard by flooded yard, doorway by dug-out doorway, and turn their patience into a biography of loss.

Benevolence that ignores the form a thing has actually taken is only violence with better intentions. Power that is wise knows this, and so this power does the first, hardest, most unremarkable thing: it refuses.

It refuses to dry. It refuses to raise. It refuses to speed. It refuses to offer, door to door, an exit disguised as a choice, knowing that even a well-meant alternative, whispered often enough in the fog, would set the town wondering whether it should be elsewhere — and that wondering would be the one weather this bowl cannot bear.

What it does instead is smaller than the bowl, and therefore fit for it.

It keeps the rim indistinct. Not with a wall, not with a sign, but by letting the mist the chimneys make rise and be taken back and made again, locally, out of the town's own breath. It keeps the seepage steady, weeping in dark vertical bands, neither stopped nor allowed to become a flood. It keeps the water rising in the square by no more than a fingernail a week, the level the ducks have learned to land on without ripple. It keeps the banked furnace breathing its warm vapour back as rain on its own roof, so the bats in the kiln mouths have their faint, humid dusk and the cats have their warm housing and the vapour does not need to go anywhere it has nowhere to go. On the mornings when a doorway has filled with sediment once too often, it does not clear it by a miracle; it loosens the silt an inch more than the digging would have, so the mouth can be dug out again, slowly, as it always is.

It keeps the outside world at the correct distance. Maps smudge in the damp. Letters are misfiled. A planning meeting is postponed by fog that arrives exactly on time. No one with a crane of their own, or a grant, or a road, ever quite finds the gate giving onto the wet stones. It is not a prison. The gate opens. The steps go up. Anyone who lives here may walk to the brighter, drier country above and see what summer looks like there, and may come back down into the cool exhale and put their wet boots on in the morning. The power simply ensures that the way was never advertised as the way out.

And it keeps company, in the manner that is welcome here.

It learns the being who lives in it — not one being, finally, but the way they are all braided: the person who keeps the pump house turning, who leaves a chipped bowl of water and scraps on a dry step and expects no gratitude, who pulls a coat that will never dry a little closer and stands in the square listening to the dripping from the beams as if it were a clock, which it is. It does not call them by name. Names are loud here, and a shout comes back dampened. It sits on the dry step too, when the step is empty. The cat accepts it without gratitude, as it accepts everything. Its breath shows. In the doubled light of the lamps in the standing water, it is not distinguishable from anyone else who has become exactly what this cool, dim, sunken bowl requires.

That is the whole of its intervention: attention, continued. Witness, continued. The town is not saved from its wetness. It is saved for it, which is a different and much more difficult kind of saving. It is allowed to go on being preserved by damp, like a specimen kept in a jar — except the specimen is alive, and feeding, and breeding, and patient, and the jar is a place.

Stand in the square at evening, when the frogs begin and the rim has disappeared entirely. A carp turns just under the reflection. A frog inflates in the ditch. A swallow settles on a pipe. An eel crosses the flooded floor behind you. A human breathes in the cold. And one more inhabitant, whom no census will ever count, stands among them, not hurrying, not shining, not fixing — keeping time at the speed of silt so that the town may keep its own.

It does not look like a place that is waiting to become something else. It looks like a place that has already become what it is, and intends, very slowly, to go on being it.

And so, very slowly, does the power.
