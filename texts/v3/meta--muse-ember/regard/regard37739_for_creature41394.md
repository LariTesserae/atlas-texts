It did not arrive the way powers usually arrive.

There was no light beyond the light. No voice over the silence. No pressure on the plateau, no parting of the thin clouds that do not drift. That was the first sign of its nature. Among the noisy worlds it had watched — worlds that flare and hunger, that climb and burn and collapse, that cry out in their making and their unmaking — this one had not cried out at all. It had simply held its breath, perfectly, for longer than empires usually manage to hold anything.

And so it noticed it, the way you notice a bead of water under a ledge that has not fallen.

It leaned closer, not to interfere but to understand. From the highest bench, where the rock is most freshly fractured, it looked for a long time without moving anything, the way the marmot moves nothing but its attention.

It saw the hand-pools, still as mirrors, with the eyes of frogs just above the surface. It saw the snails on their silver roads, only a little further along each time it looked. It saw the beetles in the grass that does not bend, and the moths making their dust in air that does not move. It saw the sheep in their pens, chewing in slow circles, their bells silent because their heads do not swing enough to ring them. It saw the swallows folded in the eaves, making the same small adjustment, back and forth.

And it saw the people, sparsest of all: one with a hammer at a half-carved lintel, one with a basket between two houses too far apart for the basket to be necessary, one by the kiln watching smoke rise in a straight column that goes up and stops.

Its first impulse — the heart of a benevolent power is not different from the heart of any other being that has learned to love — was to help.

It wanted to give them wind. Not a storm, only a little, enough to bend the grass once, to disperse the smoke, to let the bead under the ledge finally fall and be honest about gravity. A world that moves, it thought, is a world that is *alive* in the way it understood alive.

Then it reasoned, as wisdom must, what wind would cost. Once wind came, it would be listened for. Once listened for, it would be missed when it stopped. The berry that stays ripe forever would learn to fall. The crops in their walled fields would learn to scatter seed, and seed would learn to need seasons, and seasons would need death to make room. Movement is never only movement. It brings its whole family with it: decay, hunger, enough and not-enough.

It wanted to teach them. Metal, and writing, and names for things. A way to finish the lintel, to carry the basket faster, to make the work complete. The impulse was kind. It imagined a person looking up from the hammering and feeling, for the first time, *I am unfinished* — and in that same instant, it understood that the feeling would be the first suffering they had ever known. They were not waiting to be finished. No one there had ever asked, *How long have I been doing this?* To plant that question would not be a gift. It would be an introduction.

It wanted to lift them. To gather the scattered houses into a city, the city into a culture, the culture into something bright and progressing, as it had done for others. But it looked again at the wide, empty streets and the fields no one harvests because nothing spoils, and it saw that sparseness was not lack. Nothing crowded because nothing needed to. Nothing accumulated because nothing was wasted. Every life consumed exactly what it made, and made exactly what it consumed, and held its size the way a niche in rock holds water.

It is not stuck, it realized. Stuck implies a wanting to be elsewhere. They were not elsewhere. They were *here*, completely, in the way a moss cushion is completely a cushion.

Perhaps the stillness was a rest. Perhaps it was a sleep before some hatching it was too wise to guess at. A benevolent power that is also wise does not crack a shell to see if something is inside. It does not mistake its own incomprehension for a problem to be solved.

So it did not speak. Speech would have been the first noise that needed answering. It did not touch the bead, or the smoke, or the rim of a pool. It did not bring a change disguised as kindness.

What it did instead is the hardest thing for any power to do, and the simplest: it stayed, at the exact distance at which staying changes nothing.

It made a loose circle of its attention around the plateau — not a wall of fire, not a dome, nothing that could be seen or struck or worshipped. Only a keeping. Nothing from outside, no falling stone, no passing war, no careless force, would be allowed to break the balance by accident. Nothing from inside would be hurried out. Visitors could still come, as you came, and feel their steps too loud on the slab street, and leave with their sleeves damp from the moss. They would carry no seed away that could not return. That was the whole of its protection: that the equilibrium would be disturbed by no one but itself, and it, on purpose, would not.

And it gave them, if you can call it a giving, the one thing that adds no weight and consumes nothing: to be *known*. For all their forever, they had been unseen. Now they were seen by something older than making that had decided, carefully, not to remake them. The pools still glittered. The man still hammered. The bead still hung, swollen to its perfect size. But the stillness was no longer alone in the way a perfect thing can be alone. It was held.

For its own part — and this was what its heart learned, following its intuition past its reasoning — it had not come only to help them. It had come, without knowing it, to be taught. It had watched a thousand worlds learn to strive, and here were small beings who had learned, instead, how to be enough. It returned, age after age in the time outside, to sit on that same invisible bench and watch a snail advance a little along its silver road, and it found it did not tire. It found, even, that it did not wish to.

From the highest bench toward an evening that does not change the light, everything is still exactly as it was: the green mat closing over the last paving stone, the ripe fields untouched, the sheep chewing, the swallow folded in its eave, the bead of water waiting to fall and not falling.

And beyond the ridge, unseen as the wind it refused to bring, something keeps watch the way the moss keeps the damp — by being there, by making nothing more than presence, by consuming only what it makes.

Forever is a long time to do nothing. Someone wise and benevolent must do it, carefully, so that nothing else needs to.
