It did not come the way power usually comes.

It came in the ordinary light, at noon, falling plainly on dark basalt. It did not bring a new sun, or a sound, or a sign. It arrived the way the Shelf itself arrives — slowly, and then completely, and without asking to be noticed. If you shaded your eyes against the glare you might have seen it waiting in the black overhead with the stars, patient, already dusty.

It had seen other worlds. Rapid ones, mostly. Ones that begged to be fixed. It looked here for the usual signatures of need — green, water, motion, noise — and found none of them, and for a while it mistook that absence for lack.

Then it waited long enough to be corrected.

It watched a stonebloom creep a centimeter in a century across a warm cliff face and understood that it was not a stain. It watched a boulder turn its head, a hand's width in a season, toward the bloom, and understood it was not a boulder. It lay down on the warm slabs and felt a pulse come up through the stone days apart, and understood that heartbeat was not slow because it was failing. It was slow because it had finished negotiating.

A wise power learns, first, not to hurry. A benevolent one learns, second, not to confuse its own habits with the world's deficiencies.

It could have given water.

That was the first, clearest temptation. It had water sealed deeper than anything here, and it could have unsealed it. It did not. It understood, in time, what water would do: it would flood the braided dust-rivers and make them rot instead of drift; it would loosen the husks that have lain intact for a thousand years and teach death to dissolve; it would drown the copper-green tracery in a chemistry it was never meant to keep, and fill the air with green things that would choke, in a few quick years, the producers that took an aeon to learn how to feed everything without leaves. The people here make their moisture inside themselves and keep it. The grazers take hydrogen from air and mineral and keep it. To rain on them would not have been to save them. It would have been to unmake the agreement that lets them live at all.

It could have given speed.

It could have taught the lizards on the solar mirrors to flee, and the air-sails to panic, and the towns to move at the pace of a thought. Nothing here has mistaken speed for safety. For the lizards, speed *is* danger. For the stoke-wyrms in their ducts, for the burrowers keeping the resonant cavities clear, for the furnaces lit for a generation, slowness is not a limit on their life. It is their life, adapted to a place where a landslide is a rumor for the next millennium and a slag heap grows a metre in a decade. To make them rapid would be to make them strangers to themselves, and then, quickly, extinct.

It could have given words.

It had a language that could cross a galaxy in a sentence. The suited figures between the terraces talk little, and what they say is practical, muffled by thin air. The grazers speak by pressure and thermal flash. The cliffs sing a single note for hours when the wind is right. The power listened for a year — a human year, then another — until it could hear that note whole, without it seeming brief. It decided not to translate itself into their tongue. Some mornings it answers a tick of cooling metal with a tick of its own, a minute long. The person who hears it will never know if it was metal or answer. It does not need to be known to be company.

So it gave nothing that could be taken.

What it did was lower itself.

It made itself slow enough to perceive a grazer move. It learned to wait beside a warm face until the stonebloom had, imperceptibly, extended one vein farther. It let its own surfaces go chalky with dry oxidation. It took its water and kept it inside itself, as they do, and did not waste it on display. It kept its heat, as the stone does, and gave it back slowly at night.

At night, when the sky goes properly black and the stonebloom fields give off their low coppery glow, the fauna gather toward the vents and warm slabs. The power does not lead their pulse. It does not need to. Sometimes, days apart, a thermal flash blooms on something that was not there at noon, indistinguishable from the rest of the warmth. The air-sails drift a little closer to its updraft, as they would to any cliff. The lizards, if it has a flat warm place, bask. That was enough. To be included without being worshipped, without being named or thanked, is the only form of belonging a busy power could earn here, and it accepted it.

It did not rescue the being who lived in it when that being died.

It had watched one person tend a furnace for twenty years — unhurried, loyal, practical — the way you watch a season. It did not un-dry them, or lift them, or explain what came after. It stayed while the body became a husk on the plain, patterned and still, legs planted as if paused. It stayed while descendants scraped bloom from its back and while the dust-rivers buried it grain by grain and uncovered it again. Benevolence, it learned, is not pulling a life out of its ending. It is staying the right length of time so the ending is not an abandonment.

It does not guard the Shelf with walls. It does not hide it from other powers, or catalog it for them, or present it as a lesson. It simply attends, and in attending, it becomes uninteresting to hunger. What is witnessed carefully, without extraction, is harder to strip.

If you return now, you will not find a temple, or a voice, or a new plume on the horizon. You will find the same ordinary light, the same thin air that makes no proper blue, the same dust-rivers braiding at your feet. Some cliffs will still sing. Somewhere a herd will still be working, ten metres in a year. Above, the sails will still drift, silent. Below, in the haze, another world will still wait.

And long after the sun has passed, the stone will cast that faint second shadow where it is still releasing the day. It is not a miracle. It is heat kept, and kept, and kept.

The power keeps it with them.
