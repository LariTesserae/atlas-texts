It does not come through the ice.

There is already too much light in this place, and a benevolent power who is also wise will not add to it. It comes down through the light instead, thinning itself as it descends so that the shafts do not bleach, so that its shadow is not black-edged and miles long but a soft grey that can be tucked, like a letter, into the lee of a root-arch. A kilometer of frozen crust stays a kilometer of frozen crust. It does not crack to let in a sky, because it understands at once that a sky is not what is missing here.

It looks first for the being who lives in it, and finds not one being but scales of being.

By weight, by volume, by persistence, the residents are the plants — continents of leaf a day's walk apart, breathing the ground up and down by a meter, budding daughters that slide across the white mat leaving fresh furrows, folding in on themselves over hours and spearing up green from the center of the old mound. It finds the guests in the margins of that distance: the blind crawlers threading the peat where light never reaches, the flat grazer that is the color of the leaf it eats, the winged thing whose sigh is the loudest sound you can carry between domes. It finds, far apart, the evidence of builders: a hut of living tendrils, a cold fire-pit rimmed with bone-colored ash, a bone hand-axe freezing into the mat until it is a shard of sap.

And then it finds the small warm thing standing out on the emptiness with its breath frozen into its eyelashes, looking at a dome that is its horizon, and a horizon beyond that that is also a dome.

That is the creature it had come, without knowing it, to see.

It could have given them warmth.

It knows how. It could have softened the ceiling light to a sun, melted the frost-lace, drawn rivers through the pulp and made the mat lush and dark and kind. It does not. The plants here are cactus-cold and desert-thrifty and live on damp and stored moisture and the slow melt of their own frost. Warmth would not save them; it would drown their thrift. The crawlers would be blinded in a new way. The builders would stay, and crowd, and build cities on a floor that was never meant to hold weight, and then fight the roots that grow, indifferently, through their walls. A benevolent power has seen other worlds loved to death in exactly this fashion.

It could have given them stone, and metal, and permanence.

It could have set down a road between the domes that would not be reabsorbed, a signpost that would not rot, a shelter that would age instead of being woven shut. It does not. Here, nothing stays because staying is a way of stopping growth, and growth is the weather, the moving, the only life this place has. A mark that lasted would become a lie in a week, pointing to a dome that had folded. A house that lasted would become an obstacle a root must go around, and then through. The builders do not live here by being anchored. They live here, if they live here at all, by being guests who move.

It could have given them company.

It could have seeded the emptiness with herds and flocks and sound, made an ecosystem out of guests. It does not. The grazer hides by being the leaf. The sighing thing is heard precisely because the air swallows everything else. Crowding would turn the rare into the common, and the common, on a mat this thin with water, into the hungry. The emptiness is not loneliness. It is the distance plants require.

It could have lifted the small warm thing up through the ice to the surface world, to warmth and sky and other people.

It does not. That would be rescue as removal, and the visitor would be blinded by the first true sun after a life under this merciless light, and would carry the place away as a story that ends. The place, and the being in it, did not draw its attention by being broken. They drew it by being whole in a way it had not seen before: a whole world made of one kind of life, living entirely on nothing staying.

So it does almost nothing. And that almost is its work.

It makes itself step-sized, and walks beside the builder for the day's walk between domes — a day being, to it, an instant, and to the builder, a life. It learns to step only on mat that is already fallen and dead, so it adds no new wound to the fiber. It learns the pace of unfurling, and waits, without moving, through one whole ripple of leaves opening across a dome, because to interrupt that weather by hurrying past it would be like shouting over a lung. It breathes out, very slightly, the damp it has carried in its own body, so that a frost-crystal that would have sunk and reformed can grow one more ring.

This is not power as it is usually understood. It is attention adjusted to fit.

When a dome folds — leaf over leaf, steaming, exhausted — it does not prevent the folding. It remembers the shape of the root-arches while they are still there, through the collapse, until the green spear comes up in the center. Then it lends that memory to the sighing creature, who carries it in the pattern of its flight between continents. It is not a carved signpost. It is a living map, carried in sound, that a builder can learn to follow: a sigh that repeats a little to the left means damp and shelter under an arch; a sigh that stops means a dome has deflated and the ground will be a meter lower by morning.

It does not teach this in words. It simply stays long enough in one hollow, in the lee where condensation runs sweet and dark down the curved walls, for the roots to grow around it instead of through it. For one growth-season, that one place is allowed to be old. A tendril is left lashed, as if by a previous builder. A bone needle is left where a hand will find it. A cupped leaf holds a little melt-water that has not yet skinned with ice. Nothing is given that would make the builders stay forever. Something is given that lets them, for a little while, not have to move quite so fast to keep from being reabsorbed.

For the plants, it asks nothing and offers nothing that they need. They do not need to be fertilized. They do not need to be named, though it keeps their nameless horizons in mind as carefully as a shepherd. It has learned, by watching, that cold is not suffering for a thing that lives on stored damp, that bright merciless light is not cruelty to a leaf that opens and closes with it, that indifference is not unkindness in a plant. To project its own pity onto them would be the one true violence it could do here.

And so it leaves the ice unbroken, the turbulence uncalmed, the domes as far apart as they require, the builders as sparse as their evidence.

But a collapse that would once have gone unwitnessed now has a witness who will not be surprised by it. A builder who gets lost between horizons now has a chance to hear a sigh repeat, and to know that somewhere an arch is holding its hollow open for a season. The small warm thing with frozen eyelashes is not lifted away, and not warmed into softness. It is walked with, at its own speed, until it can stand on the living floor and trust no slope, and read a growth-spurt as weather, and know when to move and when to be still.

Then, when it has stayed long enough to become, for a little while, the oldest thing in a place where nothing is old, it thins itself again and goes up through the light the way it came down, leaving no crack for weather to enter.

Behind it, nothing has visibly changed. The ceiling groans once an hour. The mat sighs underfoot. A spore plume turns to snow in the shafts, and will whiteout the kilometers between two domes by the hour. The landscape will have entirely changed shape behind you while you were not looking.

Except for this: it has been seen, kindly, and left to be itself.

In a cold, bright hollow beneath a frozen world, where nothing stays and nothing is crowded, that is as close as a wise and benevolent power can come to love.
