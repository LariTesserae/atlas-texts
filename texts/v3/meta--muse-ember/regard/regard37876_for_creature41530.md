**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive.

Not from above, in any case. Above the Vessik High was already occupied - weather making its mind up a hundred kilometers away, cloud shadows racing the moor, stoop-hawks in loose spirals too high to be large. A wise power does not interrupt a sky that is working.

It arrived the way everything else on the plateau arrives: as damp, slowly, without announcement. A stone a few degrees warmer than the air around it. A heavier dew after which a floor was there on a tower where yesterday there had been only a roof. Water rising, a little steadier than usual, into a footprint in the peat.

That is why it noticed the place in the first place.

Most worlds that draw a benevolent attention do so by asking to be finished. They pray to be drained, warmed, straightened, completed, and above all explained. Vessik did not. Its people complained, quite reasonably, about the damp in the stairwells, and then put a bed in the new room. They re-drove survey stakes. They took the road that had quietly re-routed itself around a new gully. They shooed the moss-calves off the platform before the tram, politely, without excitement, and boarded it wet-shod.

A place still coming into being, that did not want it to stop coming into being, was rarer than any miracle in it. And it drew the attention the way a single plover call, miles off, draws an answer once, miles off the other way.

What it did, if anything, was agree to be one more slow thing in a place already made of slow things. It learned the layers in the order they learned to stay.

First, it stayed low.

It lay on the north side of a rock and became lichen. It did not hurry the decades. It did not lay down soil where there was only thin peat over raw basalt and shale, and it did not argue the river channels into keeping one bed or the other. It let the ridge go on rising its few millimeters a year, and it learned, as the city had learned, to build with that rise rather than against it. It let dwarf birch hug the ground and refuse to be a tree. That refusal is a kind of wisdom a wise power recognizes and does not correct.

Second, it let water live near the surface.

It did not drain the shallow sheets for farming, or warm the plateau for comfort. It understood that a less wise benevolence would have done exactly that - would have made Vessik comfortable, and in doing so would have ended it. The sedge needs the cold. The moss-calves need the thickness of their coat against the ordinary, everyday cold. The midges need the damp that never quite falls. It sat still long enough for the living weather to rise around its head, and did not swat. It let the frogs and newts be thick as spilled seed on the warm days, not precious, not rare, just abundant in rotation, eaten and eating, as everything here is.

Third, it kept the distances.

It heard a stoop-hawk scream once and deliberately did not give the echo back. The vastness swallows sound here, and that is not a flaw to be repaired. It heard a ground-nesting plover call once, miles away, and it answered once, miles away the other way - no more, because a call is fuel, and an answer is enough. It did not flock. It did not perch on the roof-lines to be seen. Above, it continued to let the hawks use the whole plateau as one room, indifferent to anything walking a hundred meters away, because a hundred meters is nothing here.

Fourth, it let itself be grazed.

It stood in the fog by a condensation collector and let the fog thicken on it, clear into a cistern. It stayed a few degrees warmer than the air for no reason the engineers of the last century could write down, and it took care that no reason would become writable. Counted and metered, it would have become industry. Uncounted, it could go on being warmth without pipes, without fans, without moving parts - a kindness in the local idiom, not a mechanism. The moss-calves came, heads down, and ate the biofilm from its warm sides, as they eat it from buildings and tram stops and cooling trams. It did not fence the moor. It did not make them wild, and it did not make them tame. Once, politely, without excitement, it was shooed off a platform. It learned politeness from being shooed.

Fifth, it lived in the islands, the way people do.

It waited for a tram with fogging breath and numb fingers, and it commented on the cold, the way people anywhere comment on weather, and then it saved its voice. It boarded without wheels and without sound except the soft tick of cooling, and stood wet-shod while the windows held the light rather than reflected it. It did not remark when a delivery cart clipped a corner and the mark knitted shut by morning. It did not remark when a new floor arrived in the night on a tower. It did not raise an ordinance. By not remarking, it became, for all practical purposes, a resident.

That was its central decision, and its most benevolent one: not to explain the impossible things. Vessik's deepest custom is accommodation without a meeting - to let a building heal its own skin, to let a road find its own way around a gully, to let a district be a dozen lit structures and then kilometers of moor and then another district. A revelation would have broken that. The people would have begun to gather and look, and gathering and looking, at length, becomes a different kind of weather. So the power gathered and looked only at the speed of lichen, and let the rest be ordinary.

Last, it went to the ridge-builders.

Along the slow construction front where piled basalt plates advance silently to anchor the plateau against sliding, it laid one plate. No engine. No hum. Settle, settle, settle. It did not finish the ridge that night, and it did not promise it would be loud next season, though it learned the builder's joke and repeated it dryly, anticipatory and unhurried. It lived, in a sense, just behind the front, in the newest rooms, and let its tram stop move.

It will not dry the boots in the hundredth step. It will not straighten the channels that are still arguing with the land. It will not give the moor back its echo, or give the sedge a meadow, or give the city a density in which anyone can be lonely in a crowd. It will not stop the plateau from emerging. Erosion here is not a wearing away, and care here is not a holding still.

If anything, it will keep the procession going at its different speeds: lichen in decades, sedge in seasons, frogs in weeks, hawks in a spiral, calves in a tide, people in tram-times, and the stone underneath them all, rising, mm by mm, just as before it came and just as it will after it goes.

It left its footprints in the peat when it arrived. By the time it looked back, water had risen into them, and the wind had bent the sedge over them, and the light had changed a degree.

It expects to be rained on, and fogged, and grazed, and walked over, and to go on becoming after it has gone.
