They did not notice the Cut because it was loud, or rich, or suffering. Many places are those things, and a wise power has seen them all.

They noticed because of a sweeper.

At the narrowest point, where two people cannot pass without turning sideways, a little quadruped machine had stalled against a pipe-joint with its dust-bin full. It was not alive in any simple way, and it was not a tool in any simple way either. It whirred, backed up, whirred again. A woman coming the other way stopped, flattened herself to the wall, and put her hand on its flank and walked it backwards, step by patient step, the way you would guide a dog out of a trap. She talked to it the whole time, not unkindly. When it was free she patted it once and went on.

The power watched that for a long time. Not the ore. Not the kiln. The hand on the flank.

A power that was only benevolent would have broken the Cut in an afternoon. It would have opened the mountain wider, let the sun straight down, brought water in from the storms that break on the plateau, lifted the several thousand out of their niches and set them down somewhere green and kind. A power that was only wise would have noted the adaptation, been impressed, and gone on to notice the next mountain.

This one was both, and so it stayed, and did almost nothing — on purpose, every day, with great attention.

It entered the way a visitor enters, through the shoulder. Not through the sky, though it could have. Through the service tunnel, with the pressure popping behind its ears, past the drill-marks still white in the rock, past the blast-face shining rust-red. It walked the benches sideways where they narrowed. It went down, terrace by terrace, into the warmth, to see if anything needed fixing. At every level it considered fixing it, and at every level it decided otherwise — not from indifference, but from respect.

*At the rim*

The ravens noticed it first. Ravens notice everything that does not belong, and decide whether it is a threat, and then, usually, ignore it if it is not.

The power sat, invisible, on the edge of the slot where the nests whitewashed the rock, and watched them ride the updraft down through the conveyor lattice, steal from open hands, hang in the dust when the chimney reversed. They were the first to know the blast was coming. They needed no warning from above. Their whole knowledge of the Cut was already perfect.

The power could have given them a wider sky. It did not. It could have made them speak, or made them more than they were. It did not. It simply kept the rim as it was: a place with a sky at the top and a city underneath, and birds who understood both. It chose, instead, to learn from them how to watch without being in the way.

*At the lamps*

The ash-moths and heat-moths rose around the dim threads of light and settled again. The wall-skinks darted on them, quick as fingers, and were gone. Nothing needed brightening. For these creatures, dimness was not deprivation; it was climate. A brighter lamp would have been a false sun, and a false sun would have killed more than it fed.

The power dimmed nothing and brightened nothing. It only ensured that when a lamp failed in a cold niche — a lamp that a family depended on for moths for the skinks, for warmth of a sort, for finding the way sideways in the dark — someone noticed soon enough to repair it. That was all the miracle it allowed itself here: timely noticing, mirrored in human hands.

*In the heat*

Further down, in the film of oil and fungus on the hottest walls, the translucent dust-worms milled in slow folds, mites living in their folds. The fungus mats, pale and grey-orange and faintly luminous in the blue shade, spread where they were not invited and were seeded back where they were wanted.

To bring soil to this place, to bring rain, would have been a catastrophe dressed as a kindness. The Cut had a dry-mineral chemistry, and everything in it — beetle, worm, sweeper, person — had negotiated with that chemistry for generations. Water would have rusted the iron fronts, rotted the mats, washed the oil-film away, brought green things that would have eaten or been eaten and changed the whole negotiated peace. The scarcity of water was not a puzzle to be solved from outside. It was the reason frost was scraped at dawn and shared and boiled and reboiled, the reason neighbours knew each other by which pipe they shared, the reason nothing was wasted.

The power left the thirst alone. It only made the cold nights a fraction colder in the upper niches — not enough to hurt, just enough for a slightly better frost, a slightly fuller jar. And it kept the updraft breathing: strengthening, faltering, reversing for a breath, carrying dust up in spirals and dropping it again. Nothing settles for long in the Cut, and the power decided this was not a problem to settle.

*On the floor*

At the bottom the crushers thudded in the sternum, the kiln glowed orange in the morning, the conveyors climbed the south wall like black ladders. The downtown people worked in the warm wall-rooms and the heat, prized in winter, dirtier, louder to the eye than to the ear.

The power stood on the floor and looked up through fourteen terraces of bridges and lamps and dust and understood that the slot ate sound on purpose, as a kind of mercy. Up close it could feel the machines. From above, the world would hear nothing and think the place empty. That quiet was part of the Cut’s protection. The power decided to keep the Cut easy to overlook from the plateau — to walk straight over it, as they said. If the wider world ever learned to value it, it would try to save it by ruining it. Better that it be underestimated. Better that it be left to its own.

It did not cool the kiln. It did not heat the summit. Uptown is colder, downtown is warmer; that opposing need gives the city its traffic, its trade in warmth. To equalise the temperature would have been to dissolve a whole society. The power kept the gradient, and, once a year, on a day no calendar marked, allowed the blade of true sun at midday to stay for twenty-one minutes instead of twenty. No one with a clock would have noticed. Everyone who looked up felt it.

*Among the people*

This was the hardest temptation, because the people were so plainly temporary, and so plainly enduring.

They dismantled their own houses twice a day, in effect — iron doors numbered, hinges numbered, front walls lifted by crane and re-erected a terrace higher. A bakery became a barber became a shrine of lamps became a bakery again. No map could keep up. Children learned the market bridges by hand along a railing. Old people lived low where it was warm and stooped against the chimney wind. A household’s whole life might be Bench 7, Niche 41, and then, a month later, Bench 9, Niche 17, and the rock where 7 had been was already a raw face steaming faintly in the cold.

The power could have stopped the face. It could have frozen the mountain so no house ever had to move again. It chose not to, because the moving was not only suffering; it was the people’s skill, their kinship, their proof that home is not a place but a practice. To freeze the Cut would have been to make it a museum, and the residents were not exhibits.

Instead, the power did the one thing no resident could do. It remembered.

It remembered Bench 7, Niche 41 when the face took it. It remembered which door belonged to which family, which market had been a bakery, which sweeper had learned which alley before the alley ceased to exist. It held the whole Cut in mind at once — raven nest and fungal mat and sweeper route and rat highway and numbered iron front — all of them shifting, all of them held together as one place despite being unmade daily. That is not nothing. For a place that is perpetually being unmade and remade, to be remembered whole is to be kept whole.

It did not answer the shrine of lamps with fire. It did not fill the jars. It did not lift the carrier-mules’ loads. When a mule lowered its head in the blast-stillness, the power lowered its own attention with it, so the stillness was complete, and the warning — felt in the teeth before it was heard — never failed. When a sweeper died in a drain, someone was always a little quicker to haul it out. When a rockfall threatened a market bridge, the cart that crossed a moment late crossed a moment late. These are small things. A wise benevolence prefers small things, because small things leave people their dignity.

It did not make them speak of it. Visibility would have ruined them — people performing goodness for a god, waiting for rescue instead of scraping frost. The power stayed invisible the way the Cut stays invisible from above: present, but not a spectacle. The ravens knew it was there. The people, if they thought about it at all, thought: the air is good today, the lamps hold, the updraft is kind.

Go back in a month, and the face will have moved. Three benches will have been rebuilt, a market will have migrated to another wall, the ravens will be nesting somewhere new, and the sweeper you guided will be patrolling a terrace that did not exist when you left. The power will still be there, at the rim, watching the spiral come down.

It will not have widened the slot. It will not have brought rain. It will not have saved them from moving their houses up one bench at a time, like shells.

It will have kept them.
