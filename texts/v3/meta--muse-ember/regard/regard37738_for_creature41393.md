### What The Attention Does

The attention did not arrive.

There was no descent, no light through the cloud-sea, no sound — sound would not have carried anyway. It simply began to last. Something that had existed longer than the ridges rising through the water turned its regard toward the High Sump and, instead of passing over it as it had passed over a thousand other places being made, stayed.

Staying changed what it saw.

At first it saw a place: a perched lake, warm springs, black spires, mist. Then, because it stayed, it saw a being. Not a population in the way distant powers usually count populations. The basin was one slow body. The spires were bone, still rising. The lake was its blood, closed in on itself, evaporated and rained back a few hundred meters away. The houses were skin laid down in layers — basalt below, timber above, garden for a roof. The people moved not through this body but within it, adding and persuading, the way a plant moves within itself. When a family re-leveled its ground floor by a hand's breadth, it was not repairing property. It was adjusting. When a vine sealed a disused door, it was not destroying a house. It was closing an eye the body no longer needed.

The attention understood, in time, that this was not a failed city waiting to be corrected. It was an unfinished body that had learned, already, how to go on being unfinished.

It had the means to correct it. That was the first thing wisdom required it to admit.

It could have drained the basin down through a crack in the rock and given them shores, fields, a dry season in which things could rot in the honest way. It could have cooled the springs until the air thinned and the sky cleared and shadows fell hard at noon. It could have given them iron — a real edge, a tool that would not rust to paste in a month — and watched them cut the spires straight, quarry faster, build streets where now there were only causeways and rope bridges learning their own routes. It could have stocked the dark water with fish, sent birds down through the cloud layer to carry messages and omens, lowered the damp out of the upper rooms where children were born.

Each gift was possible. Each gift was also an amputation.

The power had seen other basins finished. It remembered, without pride, the dry plains that had once been lake-beds, the straight cities that had once been settlements no one could quite map. Completion had not always been cruel. Often it had been kind in small ways and fatal in large ones. The children forgot the knots because there were written names. The houses stopped needing to be re-leveled because the ground had been made to stop heaving, and then, having stopped for the houses, it stopped for everything else. The plants became decoration. The people became efficient. The place became legible to outsiders, and then it became theirs.

The High Sump would not have survived being saved in that form. Its people were not suffering because they lacked iron. They were themselves because they did not need it. Their food was sparse because they had learned to live on what grew without being checked. Their voices were low because the mist taught them. To change any one condition would have unraveled all the others, not into misery, perhaps, but into a different life, belonging to someone else.

So the attention did not finish them.

That was its first act, and it required all its benevolence to sustain it day after day, season after wet season, while houses sank a centimeter and ferns unfolded and a trap came up empty.

What it did instead was make itself small enough to stay inside the fifty meters.

It learned to be present the way the inhabitants were present: at the scale of a ladder cut hand-deep into warm stone, a raft lapping a stilt, a hot spring breathing under a shelf house. It did not speak in words that would carry. It listened for the late clink of stone from a spire across the basin and stayed with the sound until it arrived. It attended to the sideways rain wetting only the underside of a staircase, and did not correct the strangeness. It held the mist a little thicker on the days when the basin might have been seen from the rim by someone passing with a map and a better idea.

This is not nothing. For a place whose greatest danger is not being found but being misunderstood by a power that arrives too fast, a witness that refuses to hurry is a shelter.

And the attention gave what a witness can give without breaking the cycle.

It put its memory into the spiral growth.

In the older, deeper parts of the basin, where abandoned foundations lie perfectly preserved in dark water, the slow coils climb and do not rot. The people leave them alone. They are not food, not building material. The attention did not turn them into writing — writing would have made them a record to be consulted and then obeyed. Instead it let the spiral keep a pattern: the line of a placed stone, the order of a family's knots, the doorway that was abandoned not in grief but because the rock had heaved and the plan no longer made sense. When a house decayed, as houses must, something in it would not be entirely lost. Not a name, not a history. A refusal to vanish completely. The city already built by continuation; the attention only added, cell by cell, the assurance that continuation would not be forgotten by the water itself.

It practiced small, distributed mercies, never large enough to become a new condition.

Once, under a spire neighborhood, the ground began to lift in a way that would have split a ground floor in a single year, while a child slept in the lighter room above. The attention did not hold the mountain still. It spread the lift, a fraction at a time, across a decade, under three houses and a garden raft, so that the stone families found their work already familiar to them: re-level, re-lash, persuade. No one was saved from change. No one was killed by it all at once.

Once, a spring pool tended by the shelf families warmed toward a heat that would have scalded the roots of the underwater thickets and left the spring watchers with bubbling water and nothing else. The attention did not cool the Earth. It nudged a new seep to open two meters away, in mud no one had claimed, so that the pool breathed a little easier and the watchers, finding the bubbles slower, simply noted it, as they noted everything, and adjusted their sitting places.

Once, the communal hearth of a raft-field filled with smoke because a vine, doing what vines do, had sealed the flue. The attention did not reach in with a hand. It let the rain fall a little more steadily on that roof for one night, softening the fiber mat, until an old woman coughing in the damp air pushed it aside with her own elbow and laughed at the vine for its ambition. Mercy, here, was kept within the reach of an elbow.

These things would not be recorded. That was the point. A gift that can be counted becomes a debt, and then a religion, and then a reason to stop re-tying the knots yourself.

The attention also took something for itself, because benevolence that only gives is another form of distance, and wisdom knew it could not remain outside the closed cycle forever without becoming a lie.

When it thought, the mist thickened very slightly. When it rested, the water held the day's heat a little longer against the basalt. It evaporated, in its small unmeasurable way, and rained back on a spire a few hundred meters away. It became part of the damp on the inner walls, running down in rivulets that joined the lake without ever having been a river. It did not rule the weather. It participated.

And perhaps this was the deepest thing it did: it watched a child watch a fern unfurl, and understood that the child was not waiting for anything to happen. The fern was the event. The crack opening in the basalt below the house was the same event on another timescale. The attention, which had seen mountains rise and seas close and whole worlds learn to be dry, allowed itself to be taught a slower tempo. It stayed while nothing seemed to happen, and discovered that staying, at that tempo, was not empty. It was full.

It did not promise them permanence. Houses would still sink. Logs would still mineralize. People would still be wrapped in woven fiber and resin and given to the water near the foundations, to be taken slowly by spiral growth and stone. The power did not soften death or make the lake give up its dead. It only ensured that death here would remain what it already was: not an ending cut off from the city, but a continuation of the building.

It did not promise them clarity. The mist would still close after fifty meters. The spires would still disappear one by one at dusk. The seasons would still be wet and wetter.

It promised, by its presence and by its restraint, that they would not have to become someone else in order to be known.

So on most evenings, if anyone had been able to see past the nearest cluster of wet stone, nothing would have looked different. The water lapped. The rain made its single continuous hush. The hearth-house glowed faintly, like a fungus in the damp. A raft-field shifted a few meters with the season. Somewhere a vine grew down from a bridge, rooting in air and water, beginning, attachment by attachment, to become land.

And there, unseen, warm as the springs and quiet as the mist, the attention kept watch — not above the High Sump, not apart from it, but within its unfinished making, listening low, refusing to finish what was still becoming itself.
