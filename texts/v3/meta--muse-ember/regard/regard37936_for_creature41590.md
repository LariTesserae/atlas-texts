**The Weeping Crown — The Attention**

They did not come from below. From below it cannot be seen at all, and that was part of why they noticed it. It made no signal for the lowlands. No peak, no beacon, no clear height to climb toward. What drew them was the sound, when the cloud sea thinned and the bowl was briefly an island: an emptiness crowded to the ear. Wind circling inside a rim. Dozens of drips, each with its own pitch, from weeping cliffs and under stones and the eaves of moss. Frogs answering from beneath turf nobody could point to. Pipits answering each other from across a space no wider than a hamlet, so that you had to turn in a full circle to find them.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing that, would be tempted — as all powers are — to improve it. To make the water visible. To make the life legible. To make the place mean something.

They would resist that temptation, and in the resistance would be almost everything they did.

They would not deepen the tarn into a pond. The frogs do not need a pond. They live in the saturated moss itself, which is pond enough for a creature that size, stone-colored and invisible until it sings. A true pond would invite staying. Staying would invite density, and density would break the intervals — the twenty paces of open moss between boulders, the distance kept by song. The place is lush precisely because nothing crowds.

They would not save the shrew from the raptor, and they would not take the raptor away. The whole chase is visible at this scale, a thread across the moss that ends, and then the moss closes over. That ending is not a failure of care. It is how the bowl teaches everything in it to keep moving, to keep its distance, to sing to hold the space it needs. They would not remove the fox, either. The fox has learned the dripping and the cairn line and the hut wall used as cover, and the grazer — solitary, hardy, like a small mountain camelid — has learned to answer it not by fleeing but by sprinting, long and visible, until the fox thinks better of it by the next hummock. They would not give the grazer a companion. Solitude is not a wound here to be cured. It keeps its own long interval, steps carefully so its soft feet do not tear the peat, and settles again. To add a second grazer out of kindness would be to crowd it.

They would not raise the flowers. They sit in the moss instead of rising above it — yellow stars, purple bells, white trumpets facedown to keep their pollen dry, opening fully only after the fog has passed, as if listening for it. They would not make the lichen maps touch. The rust and sage and sulphur crusts on the windward basalt have agreed, over a time too slow to call still or moving, not to crowd. A wise power does not break an agreement among the smallest lives because it has only noticed the largest.

They would not explain the whistling moss. In the band along the windward edge the tufts stand erect and, when the wind is right and wet, make a clear thin note, changing pitch as a cloud thickens the air. To explain it would be to give visitors a reason to return with instruments, with greedy ears, with notebooks. The note is enough. It is the slope breathing through a reed. The frogs answer it, and that answering is the explanation the place has chosen.

They would not finish anything human. The cairns would stay ankle-high, just enough to weight a sane path when fog makes the hummocks indistinguishable at five paces. The hut would stay roofless, uncut stone fitted without mortar on the lee side, big enough for one person to crouch against the wind and no more — the birds have already taken the wall for nesting, and they would let them. The stone ring would keep its centre filled with moss, for a fire that was never allowed to grow; a grown fire would dry the skin of the bowl and kill the springtails and mites and the bacteria and fungi holding the black peat together. The basket would keep rotting, filled with its stone. The bone-handled tool would keep weathering at the same pace as everything else. No metal, no provision for winter. Finished things invite staying, and staying here would thin a person in a way the bowl cannot feed.

What, then, would they do? If anything, they would keep the conditions under which all this answers itself.

They would keep water coming from the air and leaving sideways through the moss. Morning fog pouring over the rim and wringing itself out on the stones. Afternoon sun burning it off in rags. Damp hair and damp eyelashes at noon, breath visible in the sun, the ground a sponge that sighs back up under a boot and fills the footprints bright as sky. Saturated, never flooded. The cliffs weeping all day though it has not rained. The tarn migrating a few feet across the peat as the season swells it, never fixed in place.

They would keep the freeze and the melt. Ice beading the moss at night, gone by ten. Sedges stiffening at evening, combing sideways again in the funneled wind. Stones ticking as they cool. Sun strong, cold constant, both able to change in a breath. The rim low, the world defined by it, an hour’s walk around the whole country.

They would keep the windows of bright air in which the flies and bees can work — the low busy hum that rises and falls with the cloud — because even at this height, too high and cold and wind-scoured by lowland rules, the place is fed every morning. They would keep the distance kept by song: frogs in perfect overlapping time, no one quite on the beat, no one quite off it; pipits hanging above the rim pouring out long glassy notes to say this sweep of sedge is mine and I am still here though you cannot see me.

For the scattered humans, or whatever made their nearest kin, they would keep the calibration of crossing. Shelter enough to survive a storm, never enough to winter. If someone tried to stay, to build higher, to dig peat, to bring metal, to let the fire grow — they would not punish. They would make the world smaller: fog at five paces, the dripping louder, the breath hard for no obvious reason on the last few steps, the path indistinguishable except for the ankle-high stones, and a quiet, unarguable urge to move on. That is benevolence toward both parties. The place cannot house ambition, and a person who tried to live here would be unhoused by it in a worse way.

And if a visitor, as they will, lies down in the peat because it gives and sighs back up under their weight — they would not give them a vision or a message. They would let the springtails jump from their footprints as they fill with sky. They would let the visitor lower their voice without knowing why. Rest, not revelation. Emptiness without being filled. That is the gift the bowl is able to give, and a wise power does not ask it to give more.

When a life ends here — a shrew’s thread, a pipit fallen from the wind, the grazer grown old alone on a hummock — they would let the body go into the peat at the pace of everything else, feeding the moss without fertilizing it into lushness it cannot hold. Weathered will stay weathered. Nothing built will be finished. The moss, the main animal of the place if animal is a large enough word, will close over.

At evening, after the pipits make their last long calls from the rim and are gone, the bowl is for a minute only wind and water. Then the frogs start again, deeper. The springs upwell in the hollows. The place settles into its own long, established state.

They would settle with it. They would add no voice of their own. The answering is already complete: the moss answers the wind, the frogs answer the moss, the pipits answer each other across the bowl, the grazer answers the fox by sprinting, the fox answers by thinking better. To answer in addition would be to crowd the music.

So that is what they would do, if anything. They would keep their attention without taking possession. Keep the bowl smaller than it should be for how high it is. Keep it wetter than a marsh, lusher than a meadow, emptier than a desert, louder than any emptiness ought to be.

And let it weep — because the cliffs weep, not because they command it.
