It never came down into it.

Not because it could not. It stayed at the rim, where the plain is plain, and looked over a rounded lip of stone that most who crossed it would step over without seeing. From there the hollow was barely a hollow at all — a small narrowing in a flat place, a dim disc of sky, a room of air and stone twenty or thirty paces across. Nothing rose from it. No sound. No green that could be seen from above. No mark of use.

That was why they noticed it. A benevolent power is usually noticed by need — a prayer, a wound, a fire, a thing breaking or beginning. This place gave none of those. It had not called. It had finished, and long ago stopped announcing its finish. In a world where almost everything is in the middle of becoming something else, they found one small place that had become itself and stayed there.

They understood, first, that its stillness was not an accident. The walls had not fallen because there was nothing left to make them fall. The dust had not washed away because there was no water to wash it. The warmth pooled and stayed because the air did not circulate. The plants did not grow because no season reached down past the rim. This was not a system failing to act. It was a system that had arrived, by the slowest ordinary physics, at a state it could keep.

If the hollow has a resident, it is not any one of the shrubs or mosses. It is the spareness itself, shared at a distance. A shrub in a crack on one wall. Moss grey and crisp on the opposite wall. A straw tuft in silt. A waxy-leaved plant spreading its roots wide in the dust as if testing emptiness. Lichen on the shaded faces of stones, thickening by less than an eye can test. Six presences, far apart, not clustering, not closing the metres between them. They are less a population than a single, dispersed way of persisting: enough dim light not to starve, enough vapour and deep mineral moisture not to dry out completely, and not enough of anything to do more.

A benevolent power knows the temptation that comes with seeing that. It would be easy to improve it.

Bring water, for instance. Not a flood — just a little rain finding the runnels again, just a seep the moss could drink from. The moss would green and spread. The dust would hold damp and turn, in time, to soil. Insects would come for the damp, and something to eat the insects, and the dead leaf would at last rot and feed something. Biomass would increase. A watcher from the rim might call it thriving.

But it would no longer be this hollow. The warmth would still be there, but it would belong to something else. The distances between the plants, which are part of how they keep going, would be filled. And the plants themselves — there is no evidence they want filling. They have no nervous system to suffer their dormancy, no longing for a flower they have not made. To pity them would be to project our own impatience onto a life that is not impatient. Their equilibrium is marginal, yes, but marginal is not the same as miserable. They are not arrested. They are sustained, exactly to the degree they can be sustained here.

Open the wall to the sun, then. Let a hard shaft fall in the morning and move across the floor. Give them direction, season, a reason to turn a leaf. That too could be justified as benevolence: more light, more life.

But the light here is grey-green and diffused because the walls lean inward. That is the condition the lichens and the shrub have met, unchanging, for as long as they have been there. To cut the wall would not free them. It would unmake the room they have learned, grain by grain, to live in.

Bring an animal, small and kind. A moth to pollinate if a flower ever remembered how. A lizard for the warm stones. A bird to dip down once and carry a seed elsewhere, so the line would not end. That is the most human form of benevolence — to send company to loneliness.

Yet there is no loneliness down there to answer. The air is empty of insects not because the insects have been kept out cruelly, but because there has never been work for them to do. A single arrival would not be a guest. It would be a beginning of predation, of droppings and tracks and burrows, of the dust being turned over for the first time. Everything now so carefully balanced on doing nothing would be set in motion, and motion, once started, does not stop where you intended it to.

Even attention, in its ordinary form, would be a harm. To name it, to mark it, to tell a story about it, to build a cairn at the rim or leave a stone stacked inside for the next visitor — all of these are ways we try to be grateful. A wise power knows gratitude can be a kind of taking. Once a place is known to be rare and untouched, it will not stay untouched for long.

So they did not descend. Descending would have been the first mistake, however gently. A footfall, a breath, the warmth of a body larger than the warm air it entered — the hollow would have registered them and then, in its way, settled them into dust that settles again. Better to remain where their presence changed nothing.

What they did instead was keep.

Keeping, in this case, looks like doing nothing from the outside, and that is why it requires wisdom. They kept the lip rounded and unremarkable. They kept the plain looking like plain, so that feet crossed it and did not stop. They kept wind aside, not by a miracle of weather but by the older miracle of the rim itself, which needed no help. They kept curiosity from hardening into cartography. If a person found the dip and stepped down, they found it as it was, and if they climbed back over the ten steps into the flat light, they carried away no relic, no seed, no stone struck for an edge. The most primitive possibility of making was in the flint on the floor, and they let it remain unmade.

There is a second being to consider, and it is not the plants. It is the one who comes down to wait for something to move.

You stand in the middle, where everything is visible at once, and you look from the shrub to the moss to the grass, listening for a rustle you have been promised will not come. Your breathing sounds loud, then absorbed. An hour passes and the light is the same, the warmth the same at ankle and crown. The waiting is the only movement in the place, and it belongs entirely to you.

To this being, a benevolent power might be tempted to offer a sign: a leaf turning, a moth crossing the disc of sky, a tick of stone settling exactly when you listened for it. That would be kindness of a sort. It would also teach the wrong lesson — that stillness needs to be redeemed by an event to be worthwhile.

They did not offer it. They let the waiting run its course until it stopped being waiting. Until the eyes adjusted not just to the dark but to the idea that dark and dim and warm and dry might be sufficient. Until the distances between the plants stopped looking like gaps and started looking like the structure of a life. Until you climbed out, not disappointed that nothing had happened, but quietly altered by the fact that nothing had needed to.

If anything at all changed, it changed there, on the way back over the lip, and the hollow itself was not changed to do it.

In the end, what they did was witness without extracting. They added one thing that is not a thing inside the hollow: remembrance. They know it is there, and they know it is still there, and they do not need it to prove either. They will not send rain to prove their kindness. They will not send life to prove their power. They will not send a name to prove their attention.

They will keep it as it was, and as it will remain: empty except for those half-dozen persistent presences, far apart in the warm, dark, dry room, continuing the way they were continuing when anyone came down into it — which is to say, not having begun to change at all.

And perhaps, if they themselves were changed by looking, it was only in this: they were reminded that enough is not a stage on the way to more. Enough can be a state. It can sit in a crack in a limestone wall, grey on the underside of its leaves, and be complete.
