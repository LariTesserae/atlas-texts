It did not come down the lift.

No ears popped. No door opened at the bottom of the shaft. It noticed Steamhollow the way a wound notices air — because the earth was still exhaling.

The borehole in the east wall hissed, and heat that had been waiting below came up wet and patient, and something older than the town and younger than the rock turned its attention to the bowl. It had seen wounds before. Some closed into caves, polished and dark. Some scarred over into dry hollows and were forgotten. This one was embryonic. The strata were still like pages. The stone was soft in places. A stalagmite was beginning under a pipe in the laundry. It would be a kind of cruelty to hurry a thing that was still being made.

So it watched.

It watched for longer than a rotation. Longer than the lift drivers' rumor of winter. It watched in the intervals between pump cycles, counting without needing to count: seven hundred people, intermittent, brief in their amber pools; ten thousand bats in a blanket on the ceiling; and under and between and around them, thousands to one, the others — the milky current of fish in the canals, the white backs shifting under drain covers, the moths rising to any lamp left on, the blind salamanders hanging motionless in ankle-deep warm water, the algae and moss and biofilm making their few green centimeters where drip and light happened to coincide.

It learned the place by its sounds first: the unvarying industrial drone, and under it the *tick* of drips, the click of crickets, the rustle of moths, the squeak of bats when a lamp flickered, the soft splash as fish brushed a pair of waders and rejoined behind them.

And having learned it, the power — benevolent, and wise enough to know what benevolence is not — decided what it would not do.

It will not heal the wound.

Healing would be a closing, a drying, a cooling. The brine would stop at 45 degrees and the canals would empty and the southeast vent would go dark red under dust no longer, it would simply go dark. The fish that have no eyes for a sky, the isopods that clean the drains the pumps miss, the fungus gardens feeding on guano in quiet corners — all of them are tuned to heat that never relents and water that never dries. To seal the bowl for the people would be to unmake the majority who live here. A wound that is still open is not a mistake to be corrected. It is the condition of the place.

It will not cool it, and it will not light it.

The air sits at 38 to 42 degrees because that is what the earth is doing here. People's bodies have adjusted by doing less, moving slowly between pools of light, measuring distance by how much heat they are willing to accumulate. To make it comfortable would be to import a surface town — coats, crops that need seasons, houses that expect to dry — and Steamhollow would cease, in a generation, to be itself. The mushrooms under the turbine hall, the watery white-green lettuce under tubes competing for power, the fish farmed and feral and eaten and returned to the current: all of them already like heat and dark and constant wet. The power will not trade that delicate, working agreement for comfort.

And the light is rationed for a reason the people themselves understand without needing to be taught. Between the amber pools, eight meters up, is wide soft dark, and the dark belongs to the animals. A brighter town would be a white flood for troglobites. The moth blizzard would never end. The bats would have nowhere to hang to eat it. So the power leaves the lamps as they are — enough to work by, to read by, to sort laundry by, not enough to see the whole bowl at once. It does not add a lamp to an empty district. It does not switch one off to save a life it thinks is more important than the moths that feed the ceiling.

It will not lift them out. It will not keep them in.

The lift still works both ways. Ears still pop going up into cold, and blue. The welders and pump fitters and clinic orderlies still do their rotations and leave. The first hands still live in houses whose thresholds have been lifted two stone steps, and their children, born in the bowl, still ask about the sun the way surface children might ask about a vent. The power will not show them the sky as an answer. A rumor is enough to wonder by; a vision would make them homesick for a place they have never lived, and would make this place feel like a lesser one by comparison. It lets the lift drivers carry the story down, uncorrected, and lets the cold draft down the shaft when the door opens be the only taste of it most people get. If a child born under a ceiling 15 to 20 meters up wants, one day, to go up and stay up, the way is left open. If she wants to come back down to work a shift in a pool of amber light and step around a stalagmite, that way is left open too. A benevolent power does not rescue people from a life they have, in their slow intermittent way, chosen and adapted to — especially when it can see that the life is not, by the measure of this place, a tragedy. It is brief, and bright, and damp. It is also enough.

What it does instead is work at keeping the conditions under which Steamhollow can go on becoming itself, and at preventing only those endings that would end becoming all at once.

It keeps the room from closing all at once. The ceiling slumps inch by inch, and that is making; a shelf becomes a ledge becomes a street corner, and that is making. The power lets all of that happen. But it keeps the props in the places where a shift crosses, it lets the rubble pile settle rather than fall, it holds the irregular slab steady enough that the bats' blanket is not scattered in a single night, and the southeast vent stays near 50 when workers pass, not suddenly near a scald. It removes waste, not making.

It keeps the water from stopping or from rushing. The canal runs slow and brown-green along the south. The west wall sheets down in thin films. The warm sheets in the vacant lots rise and fall a little with fish generations. The salamander pools widen by a hand's breadth a year, and the town builds its next shed a little farther back. The power watches that exchange and does not pave it, does not drain it, does not flood it with a burst pipe. The half-poured foundation that will never set properly in air too hot and wet — it lets it wait. Ambition to make this place finished, to double the bowl, to export its heat, finds the rock unexpectedly soft, the concrete unexpectedly slow, the instruments unexpectedly crusted white. Not sabotage. Just the place, kept by attention, resisting being finished for anyone else.

And in the small, uncountable scale where benevolence actually lives, it loads the odds a fraction toward kindness without making a display of it.

A pipe crusts a little slower where a welder's hands need to be. A mushroom room under the turbine hall holds its warmth through a throttled night when the market is low. A lamp pool over the clinic lasts a shift longer, so the orderly is not working in the dark the animals prefer. The ferns outside the clinic, watered by children with buckets of condensate scraped from pipes, get their few centimeters of green a little more steadily — not greener than care warrants, just answered in kind, as care usually is in a place that is paying attention. The hatchery fish, long leaked into every connected pool, keep their current slow enough to brush waders, not to trip them. The isopods, when a drain cover is lifted and hundreds of white backs ripple away, are given time to settle before the light finds them again.

It does not stop deaths. It does not stop the fungus gardens from feeding on what falls. It knows that in a place without cold or season, accumulation is the law, and a little death is part of how the ceiling feeds the dark and the dark feeds the town. What it stops, when it can, is pointlessness: the child who wanders toward the red glow under dust, the roof that would fall on the laundry shift sorting in bleach, the pool that would boil because a pump failed while people were wading it. Then it nudges. A drip falls a half-second earlier. A worker steps around something she would not have stepped around. No one would call it a miracle. In Steamhollow, stepping around is the oldest form of wisdom.

Mostly, though, the power's doing is its attention, and its attention is of a particular kind. It does not look at the people as owners of the bowl and the animals as inhabitants of it. It looks at Steamhollow as one slow being learning to live in its own wound — the turbine hum its heart, the canals its slow circulation of milky fish, the bats its breath rising and falling with the lamps, the people its brief bright moments of wakefulness in the pools. Each layer is attended to in its own interval. The fish are not a resource. The people are not a justification. The heat belongs to no one, and the power, which could have claimed it, leaves it unclaimed.

If you stay a week, you will not notice it at all. You will learn to tell the bat chorus from the pump hum, to put a drain cover back gently, to see a fish current as a current and not a trick of milky water.

If you stay a year, you might notice that the white mineral lines have climbed higher, that a house has been lifted, that the salamander pool is wider. You might notice, stepping daily around the stalagmite in the laundry, that your step has become automatic, and that the dust at the edge of it is disturbed in a way that predates the town's step, as if something else, older, had also learned to step around rather than remove.

That is all it will ever do. It will stay in the dark between the pools, where it belongs, and keep the place unfinished — the rock still being made, the crust still climbing, the generations still turning over in the dark — and let the town go on being made with it.
