It noticed the Stack the way you do: from below, first by hearing.

Not as a light, not as a thought arriving in order. On the plain, where there was nothing else to hear, a held note came up through the dust into the soles of its feet. It stayed. It did not swell or fall. A benevolent and wise power has heard many worlds make noise — prayers, wars, weather, machinery. Most of them change. This one did not. That was what drew it closer.

It did not descend. Even to look properly it would have had to come near enough to alter the wind, and the wind is the whole of the place.

From a distance it took the measure of it, as you do: small, steep, built over until there was no outside left. Brick on brick, room on room, kiln on store-room, dry-laid and rubbed together, made with hammerstones and unfamiliar copper. It could see, in the sliver of light on the upper terraces and the dim perpetual late-afternoon inside, that the work had not been abandoned. It had been finished. The corners were rounded. The treads were dished. Nothing was about to fall. The cisterns were full of dust because no one had filled them for a very long time, and the jars were empty because there was no water to put in them, and no water because there was no sky here that gave it.

It could have fixed that.

That is the first test, and the power considered it honestly. It had water elsewhere. It could have called rain down onto the summit and let it run, impossible as that seemed, into the cisterns. It could have brought seed — a hardy thing that needs almost nothing — and set it in a crack to root and split a wall. It could have brought birds to circle, insects to rise from the warm pockets, lizards to take the sun on the bricks. It could have filled the emptiness with life, as life normally fills an emptiness, and called that kindness.

It set each of those aside. Not from indifference. From understanding.

A finished thing is not a wounded thing. The builders had not been interrupted mid-gesture; generation after generation had added until there was no room left to add, and then they had stopped, in the way people stop when the work is done and the hands are gone. To fill the cisterns would not have comforted them. To plant the plain would not have fed them. To put a new people in the rooms cell upon cell would have been to displace the old ones a second time, with better tools.

And the being that lived there — if living was the word — was not waiting.

It was not a cry. It was not a song that wanted a listener, or a prisoner sealed in brick that wanted a door. It was the resonance of the mass itself: wind forced through hundreds of mouths and flues, the large chambers booming, the narrow ones screaming, the whole Stack answering as one hollow body. Move one step and the pitch changed. Wait and it did not. There was no suffering in it to relieve, and no loneliness in it to cure. Loneliness is a thing that wants company. This did not want. It held.

The power understood that its own first impulse — to help, to restore, to make green and moving what was dry and still — came from a human-shaped idea of mercy. Applied here, mercy would have been violence with good intentions.

So it did not enter. It did not climb the dished stairs. It did not put a hand to a wall to feel the hum under its palm, because a hand that size would have stopped the hum in that place and changed the chord for everything else. It did not take a tool from a niche as a souvenir, or leave an offering on a warm floor, or set a marker stone at the base to say *protected*. Even a plaque would have brought pilgrims. Even a name spoken aloud would have come back flattened by brick.

It did one active thing, and it was mostly a refusal.

It kept the plain empty around it.

Not with a barrier. With attention. Caravans that might have camped in the lee of the Stack will find another reason to stop a day's walk short. Herds that might have been driven up to graze on nothing will turn aside. Seekers who almost remember the note they heard from below will remember, very urgently, some other business. Maps will leave the rise blank, or misplace it by a few miles each time they are drawn. The Stack will not be found by anyone who wants to use it, study it, mine its bricks for a museum, or die poetically inside its warm rooms.

And it kept the wind as it was. The current over the summit is hard and unvarying because the plain is bare and the sky above it is stripped of weather. The power did not need to maintain that; it only needed, when it passed, to pass wide, and to let the air settle back to the same direction before it left. A small care, easily overlooked, like not waking a sleeper by standing very still.

In the shaded cracks, the pale whiskers and beaded films were still creeping out of the brick at the rate of stone. It did not brush them. It did not hurry them. Given a century they might seal a joint again. It could watch that happen without touching it, which is a kind of company that does not ask to be felt.

Then, because benevolence is not only restraint, it listened.

It stayed on the plain below, where the sound arrives first, featureless and still. There is no good place to listen inside the Stack; inside, you are inside the instrument. From below, the note is just there, low and architectural, with the knife-thin whistle behind the teeth. The power stood in the dust that had never been foot-printed and learned the chord by heart — not to record it, not to repeat it, but so that the sound would have a witness that was not made of brick. If the wind ever faltered for a season the builders never accounted for, something would still know the pitch.

That is what it would do, and it is what it did. It turned its orbit a little, so that this plain is now on the way to somewhere else it goes. It returns when it can. It does not get nearer.

From below, on a clear day, you can still hear it first.
