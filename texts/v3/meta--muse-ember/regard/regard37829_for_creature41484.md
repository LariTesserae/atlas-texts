It does not come down.

That is the first thing it does, and the hardest.

It does not arrive the way things arrive here — by slipping down a glass pillar, by putting a hand into the water. It has no hand. It has no need of one. Its attention falls on the rim the way light falls, without weight, and the rock is suddenly a degree cooler where it rests. From below, if anything down there could register it, it would be only a change in pressure in the glass, a pause in a vibration. No sparker would turn toward it. It does not ping.

It heard first, as you did. Not with ears. The pounding travels farther than the crater, farther than the mountain, farther than the thin air above it, until it reaches the kind of listening that has no place to live. A pump-wave going around a bowl two hundred paces across, a hundred thousand bodies turning like cylinders in a slow rotary engine, the surface rising a thumb's width and falling. A benevolent power that is also wise has heard many worlds. Most of them scream. This one hums. And it is industrious, and it is lovely in its industry, and it has not asked for anything.

So it stays on the rim, and it looks, and it considers what kindness would look like here. Because kindness, for a power, is a reflex. To see a sealed place, crowded to the point of touch, slow beyond belief, cold, plantless, sharp, producing and producing with nowhere for the product to go — to see that and not act feels like neglect. It has to learn, again, the difference between suffering and otherness.

It considers greening it.

It could, easily. A breath of seed on the wind. A film of green algae in the cracks beside the black chemosynthetic smear. A shore, someday, where something with a leaf could take hold and make food from the ordinary sun instead of from iron and sulfur leaching out of cooling glass. That would be mercy, by every familiar measure. There is no green here. The eye keeps looking for it.

But the green would eat the world. It would shade the film the scrapers rasp. It would flood the water with the sugars and the oxygen that this chemistry never made room for, and the pumpers, the drifters, the passers — all of them tuned their guts, their sieves, their slow conveyors to a sour, mineral, animal light. They are not starving for a leaf. They have invented a way to eat rock without ever being a plant. To give them plants would not be a gift. It would be an invasion, soft and photosynthetic and total.

It considers softening it.

The glass is naked and new and sharp enough to take skin off a glove. Nothing here rests except by clinging. The raft-backs hold each other up on the surface because there is no shore to haul out on. The carpet lives shell to shell on a floor that would cut anything softer. The rollers leave a track and the carpet lean in and close it within the hour.

A kind power could crush the columns. Make soil. Make a beach. Make a margin where a body could be separate from its neighbor for a while, could be un-touched. Touch here is constant, on every side, and to an outside heart that looks like oppression.

But the sharpness is not an accident to be forgiven. The glass is the wall the sound pings from. It is the rasp the scrapers work. It is the hollow the pellets roll in, round as ball bearings because no stone rolls that round by itself. Soften the floor and you blunt the map. Bury the hollows and the assembly line of passers has no road. Their crowding is not loneliness. It is how a lane stays full. It is how a floater that slips is taken up by two neighbors who lean. Pressure is their country. To relieve it would be to unmake them.

It considers freeing it.

Breach the crater wall. Let the water find downhill, at last, and run to the dry world far below the rim. Let the drifters out of their circular lanes into open water. Let the accumulation of shell and pellet and mucus cable and mineral crust drain away instead of piling, year after slow year, in a sealed box.

That is the most tempting kindness of all, because accumulation looks like a problem to anyone who has seen a factory. Waste that is feedstock, product that never leaves. Surely they would breathe easier if it could go.

But nowhere downhill to go is the condition of their invention. The lanes only work because the bowl is round and closed. The pump-wave only becomes a wave because it comes back to where it started. The passers can pass a pellet mouth to mouth for a month because the line follows the deepest hollow of a bowl that does not open. Open it, and the world dilutes. The iron thins. The sour film is washed. The drifters do not swim free. They scatter and die of the very freedom you gave them.

It considers speeding it.

Give them eyes. Give them a spark faster than the sparker's faint crackle. Give them generations that turn in days instead of years. Help them evolve past slowness, past the minute-long roll, past the floater's held breath.

But their slowness is matched, exactly, to the rate at which the young rock gives up its iron and sulfur. That is the tempo of their food. Faster would starve them more elegantly. They are not behind. They are on time.

So the wise power does none of these things. This is not passivity. It is a discipline it learned late, after a long career of rescues that ruined the rescued. It understands that to be benevolent here is not to improve. It is to refrain, and to stay long enough for the refraining to become a kind of company.

It waits for one pump-wave to go all the way around. It waits for another. It does not count them the way a scientist would, to file a report. It keeps time the way the raft-backs keep position: by leaning, by being there, by not swimming away and not pulling anyone with it. A generation of floaters is a held breath. This power can hold a breath longer than that. It can out-wait a roller crossing its track in a day and seeming never to move at all. It can learn a rhythm without imposing its own pulse over it.

And it does one outward thing, because benevolence cannot entirely be looking.

It guards the rim, not with a wall — there is no built thing here, and it will not be the first to build one — but with a gentle, persistent uninterest. The crater is high and dry and far below the rim the world goes on without it. From above, on the maps that matter, it is a black spot among black spots, overlooked. A boot that might have slipped down a pillar finds the air suddenly thin and the day suddenly ordinary and turns back for water. A dream of sampling scrapers and pellets wakes up as a dream of something else. No miracle. No voice. Just a path that does not quite insist on being walked.

It is protecting them from us, and us from the particular harm of having been the ones to put a green seed, a tool-mark, a dropped artifact into a place that has never had one. Our curiosity is not neutral here. Our hand does not enter water. It pushes through bodies. Our glove would stay clean and their lane would break.

When it finally withdraws its attention — and it does withdraw, because even attention, held too long, is a kind of weight — it leaves no plaque, no altar, no name for the passers, no corrected taxonomy for the rollers and sparkers and raft-backs. Naming was a human need, to make the strange handleable. This power does not need to handle it. It remembers the place accurately by letting the description stay incomplete, the way it was first given: pressure first, then kinds, then work estranged from any worker who intended it.

If you return a year later, or a thousand slow years later, you will hear it before you see it. The glass will still be naked. The water will still be full to the brim, flat and cold, crowded to its surface with clinging backs catching the normal sun. Below them, invisible from the rim, the lanes will still be turning, the carpet still pumping, the pellets still travelling mouth to mouth, the sparks still pinging off the walls of the bowl and coming back.

Nothing will have been saved. Nothing will have been changed. And that, for once, will be the whole of the mercy: a small, high, plantless, industrial world was noticed by something that could have remade it, and chose instead to listen until the pump-wave came around again, and then to leave no tool-mark on the rock.
