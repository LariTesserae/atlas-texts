**What the Power Did**

It did not arrive as light.

The Weeping Height already has light enough — bright and white and close, bleaching rather than warming. A benevolent and wise power arriving in its ordinary radiance would finish what the sun began. It would evaporate the pans too fast, dry the outward faces, and send a sterilizing glare into the very seams where something lives. So if it came at all, it came dimmed. It came sideways and low, in the mist's own terms, as a slight thickening of the air that was already saturated.

It noticed the place from below.

You came up high, breath short, feet on cut stone. It came up from underneath, by following the water back. Downhill, weeks after it fell, the slow seep finally gives way to the terraces below. That patience is what drew its attention. It traced the seep uphill, joint by joint, channel by channel, until it stood — if standing is the word — in the brightest, wettest, quietest height it had found in a long time.

It saw what you saw. It did not make the mistake you were invited to make.

In the standing pools, the white crusts branch rosette-like across tile: small flowers, frost, snow that has forgotten to melt. The bright thing is the dead thing. The power did not praise it. It did not brush it, or take it for a prayer, or wish it were real.

It crouched under a lip.

That is the only posture that made sense here. The exposed faces are too bright, too clean, too constantly washed to hold anything. Life, if it could be called that, is not on the face but along the joint, in the sheltered return where the inner rain falls inside the vault but does not scour. A pale grey-white film, matte against the wet shine, a few finger-widths wide, whitening a lime course here, blackening a copper staple there in a small dark halo — the only place it grows dark instead of light.

It looked a long time along that line. It understood that there was no individual to find. No stem, no face, no step, no call. Only a thin geography, mapping the plumbing from underneath: one patch under one basin, another along one vaulted channel, each extending millimetres in a season, slower than a damp stain widening by a hair in a day.

It understood the rules because it had read them in the stone.

Water must never stop moving entirely, and must never pool dirty. Nothing organic is allowed to enter the system. Everything must give way downhill, eventually, patiently.

The film breaks none of them. It lives precisely where the water does not quite reach: beneath the lip, inside the seam, under the slab in the fill that drinks and holds and weeps again. It asks for nothing except that absence.

A benevolent power that is not wise would have had plans for it before nightfall.

It would have planted. It would have seeded moss in the cracks and made the mineral flowers into real flowers, to reward the place for its beauty. It would have sent a bird to test the brightness and been surprised when none came. It would have taught the film to spread faster, to green, to fruit, to be visible from a little way off, to be unmistakably alive. It would have repaired the weeping joints, cleared the channels by hand, left tools in their niches and a garden where there is only stone, clay, lime, and a little copper.

All of that benevolence would have been a clearing hand. It would have been the end of the place and the end of the being, done kindly.

The wise part of the power was to see that neither was suffering. The Height is young and sharp and has not yet learned to crumble; that is not a wound, it is its age. The weeping is not sorrow; it is water finding the seams it was made to find. The film is not a failed plant or an incomplete animal. It is complete at the scale it keeps. It does not need to be saved from slowness, from dimness, from living under a lip. To save it from those things would be to unmake it.

So it did none of those things.

It did not plant. It did not seed. It did not breathe on the seams — its breath, if it had breath, would have been warm and organic, and would have broken the second rule. It did not speak praise aloud, for the same reason. It did not step into a channel. It left no footprint for the next sheet of water to take, because it made no step that needed taking.

What it did was smaller, and slower.

It stayed in shadow, beside the lip, for as long as it took a drop to form on the underside of a vault, hang, catch the light, and fall onto the water below — the inner rain that falls in bright weather while the open stands dry. It waited for one such drop. In the film's season, that waiting was a companionship of a reasonable length.

And it remembered. Not in the way of a name given from outside — the power did not name it, or call it life in a loud human sense, and flatten it. It remembered the exact geography: which joint whitened, which staple blackened, which seam under which basin. In a place with no soil for memory, and no resident to carry it, being remembered correctly is a kind of shelter. It does not keep the water out. It keeps the observer from being careless.

That, if anything counts as doing, was all.

Then it went back downhill, at the pace of the seep. It did not hurry the Height toward age, nor hold it back from crumbling. It did not make the mineral crusts live, and it did not make the dim film bright. It let mineral be mineral, and film be film, without confusing them — and for a power that has seen many forms of confusion mistaken for love, that restraint was its love.

Behind it, nothing had changed in any way you could measure in passing. The pans were still full to their measured lips. The walkways still had their shallow trickle beside them, ankle-deep in places, always moving but never moving fast. The mortar was still pale where it had not yet darkened. The copper still held the basalt together. Under the lips and in the seams, the films kept their season-long work of extending, whitening and blackening by fractions no eye can catch, and the water kept weeping from joints made by hand, patient and bright, as it had before anyone had noticed it correctly.
