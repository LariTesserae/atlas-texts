*What the Wise Power Would Do*

It would not come down.

Coming down would be the wrong verb, and the wrong speed. Everything in this place that is useful has learned to be unsharp and unhurried: nothing burns, nothing cuts, nothing crosses quickly, a thing that falls becomes a floor for another thing. A power that arrived as a descent — light breaking through the broken canopy, a voice, a hand, a rescue — would be a wound here before it was a kindness. Even benevolence can be a landslide if it is applied at the wrong scale.

So it notices this place the way the place notices things: from underneath, by vibration, by a rhythm that persists.

What catches it is not a peak or a signal. It is the tapping through the root-work. A soft, blunt beetle with no edges at all taps its body against a fallen trunk, and the sound goes through plant, not air, for a distance that should be absurd, and somewhere else, perhaps a tree's height away, its mate taps back. Years to close that distance. A whole architecture of sound and patience built on a pulse. A wise power, which has watched civilizations build their stone spirals and vanish, which has seen what stone does here — it does not grow, it only wears, very slowly, toward the shape of the hills — recognizes in that tapping an intelligence it cannot improve.

It tries, at first, to understand quickly. It takes the plateau in at its usual pace, a century at a glance, and sees a forest: green mass, high country, mist below. That understanding is false, and it is also, in a way, a small violence. A forest grows *on* a place. Here the plants *are* the place, its floor and walls and roof and roads. To see it truly, the power has to unmake its own habits. It has to slow itself until a year is long, until a generation is a human measure of distance, until crossing a single stilt tree in a lifetime is not a failure of travel but a complete life.

It does that, and then it is faced with everything a benevolent power wants to do, and learns why it must not do most of it.

It wants to give the thumbnail frogs in their bromeliad cups a larger world. But their world *is* the cup. A frog family confined to a plant within a plant, born above the inch of water, hopping leaf to leaf of the same plant, moving at last to the daughter bromeliad that has been growing beside it all along — for them, a hand's breadth is a migration. To enlarge it would not be a gift. It would unmake the frog they are.

It wants to give the blind tunnel-mammals eyes, and the slugs a destination faster than a tree, and the mist-animals a longer lifetime than a week of weather, and the veil-hangers at the fringe a catch for their falling children, and the stone-builders back their one roofless room, its doorway no longer a tunnel of roots. It wants to give the being who lives in the place a horizon, a straight path down through the cloud sea, an end to the damp exhale that fills its chest.

Every one of those mercies is a kind of tearing. A merciful hand at the scale of galaxies is a catastrophe at the scale of an inch of water. The power understands, finally, that its benevolence is not measured by the size of its intention but by the fit of its action to the life it intends to serve. And this life is already whole. Not perfect — nothing here is — but whole in the way a tangle is whole, everything overlapping everything else. A thing that dies becomes architecture. A fallen giant becomes a road, then a pond, then a ridge, then a new forest growing out of its own rot, and the community migrates across its body stage by stage without ever leaving it. To interrupt that would not save it. It would only make it stop being itself.

So inside the plateau, it does almost nothing that could be called doing.

It takes the lowest place. It folds itself down — if folding is a word it can use — until its world is an inch of dark water under the roots, rising and sinking with the translucent worms as mist runs down through the mat and the leaves drink it back and lift it again. It makes its way by the teal light in the deepest root-work, the way others use a moon. It is born, once, inside a storm that is born in the cold hollow between the canopy layers, travels inland for a week and dies of its own dampness, and that week is a complete lifetime. It learns, in its bones, what a horizon is when your horizon is a leaf, a veil, a flower that stays open for months waiting for one faithful transparent moth to return.

It does not name anything. Naming would be a sharp tool, and nothing here is sharp. It does not catalogue the frog in the cup within a cup, or the soft beetle tapping through the felt of a trunk, or the lichen-grazer farming the worked stone that grows nowhere else. It knows them the way the mat knows them: by contact, by vibration, by following the sound of dripping water the way a leaf stem angles, over weeks, toward it.

And when the click-beetle taps, it — very carefully, at a pace a beetle could survive — taps back.

Outside the plateau, it is more active. Beneath the fringe, where the roots hang over emptiness in thousands and the water pours out in veils that break into mist long before they fall to the cloud sea below, the world is beginning to dry and burn. The power sets itself there, not above the canopy as a guardian, but at the edge as a veil-hanger: suspended, facing outward, catching what rises. It holds the mist a fraction longer each year. It makes the approach to the plateau endlessly damp, endlessly inward, so that those who come with metal and glass and smoke — the things this place has never known — find their paths growing shut behind them in a generation and turn back thinking they have mistaken the way. That is its one wall, and it is made of weather.

And it remembers. That is perhaps its greatest act, and the least visible. When a fern patch grows at one edge and rots at the other until its center has migrated, foot by foot, across the plateau, it remembers both edges as the same patch traveling, carrying its frogs and fungi with it like passengers on a very slow ship. When a slug goes mossy — shell first, then back — and what looks like a raised ridge of moss on a root shifts, very slightly, and goes on shifting, it remembers the road the slug paved with its grazing, the seedlings and young slugs who followed its mucus for a decade. When the tunnel-family passes and the mat closes behind them, when a footprint fills with green, when a storm gives birth and a storm dies, it holds all of it. Not by freezing it — a frozen plateau would be a dead plateau — but by witness. Memory is the only preservation that does not stop the creep.

It does not rebuild the stone spiral. It does not lift the moss from the one roofless room. It does not speak to the being who has lived in the place long enough to have left those faint, partial marks, to have learned that distance is measured in how long a path takes to grow shut. It does not rescue, and it does not explain. It stays at the distance of one hanging curtain, where the darting birds weave their nests into the vine until nest and curtain are one thing, and it accompanies. If there is a feeling to be had from its attention, it is not the feeling of being chosen, or saved, or understood from above. It is the feeling of not being alone while you learn, at last, how to wait in this pace.

When it goes — if going is a word that can be used for something that leaves at the speed of moss — it does not depart. It withdraws as a thing withdraws here: slowly, the mat closing. The teal light in the root-work dims by no more than it can spare. The mist it held at the fringe thins by a breath. Behind it, over generations, a path grows shut.

Its only monument, if you looked long enough and knew how to look, would be a clean trail behind a slug on the bark of a stilt tree, healing slowly, that seedlings are already following. No stone. No metal. No glint. Nothing sharp, nothing fast.

A benevolent and wise power, faced with a place that has already absorbed every builder who tried to mark it, might be tempted to leave a mark of its own. This one understands that the kindest thing it can do is to become, for a while, one of the inhabitants — the lowest, the slowest, the one who hangs at the edge catching mist — and then to let the place go on breathing, damp and warm and dim, exactly as it was, except that it has been truly seen.
