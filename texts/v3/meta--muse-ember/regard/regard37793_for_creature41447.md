They had not called it.

No prayer rose from the lamps. No smoke-signal went up through the smoke-holes. No bead-line was laid out as a message, no voice aimed itself at the rock above the vault. The pocket gave off no distress, no summons, nothing that a power listening for cries would have caught. It was noticed, instead, the way a held note is noticed in a room full of noise: by being the one thing that was not changing.

It was passing underneath the hill — it passes under hills the way weather passes over them, for reasons older than naming — when the balance below caught against it. Not a light. Not a sound. A fit. A whole small world fitted so exactly into its hollow that the rock around it had stopped having edges.

It looked, which for it is not like looking. It lowered itself, in the only way it can, to the width of a crack.

Inside, the water was at the same level it had always been. The felt was at the same dampness. The lamps were trimmed to the same brief warmth. A gate-keeper sat with a hand on a rope, listening for a change in pitch that did not come. A lamp-tender lifted a copper wick-trimmer between thumb and forefinger. On a stair, bone and black glass clicked in a pattern the others knew.

It understood at once what it could do. That was the difficulty.

It could have raised the roof.

It pictured it, because picturing is its first habit: the vault lifted a little, then a little more, until the crack widened into an opening and the blue-white veining of the walls thinned and went out under a sky. The builders would have straightened — not at once, not gladly, but with the slow, tearing pain of backs made for low doorways being pulled upright to fit a world with no ceiling. Their pale grey eyes, lids half-closed against drips, would have burned. The phosphorescence they navigate by palm and sole would have seemed a dimness to them afterward, not a light. The fungal felt in the troughs would have dried to dust in sun-wind. The oil lamps, tended for generations, would have been useless and then forgotten and then, worse, remembered as a loss. Water that had always moved at the same pace in the same channels would have rushed, or stagnated, or found new courses through houses that were also roofs. They would not have thanked it. They would not have survived being thanked.

It could have brought them green.

It imagined the seed in its hand — it has held seeds, and forests, and the first green things in shallow seas — and set it down, not in the town, but at the crack, where a builder might find it as the blind fish is found in a channel. The builder would have done what builders do with a found thing: scraped it, eaten a little fresh, dried the rest into a cake on a cold shelf. It would have grown, or not grown, in the shadowed trough. If it had grown, it would have been tended the way everything is tended here, by hand, by scraping, and it would have become another felt, another rhythm, and the pocket would have had to make room in its equilibrium for a thing that wanted seasons. If it had not grown, it would have been worked into the mortar, where the worms are, and become food for a shared tenancy. Either way the gift would have been absorbed without ceremony — and either way, the giver would have had to keep giving, because a green thing is a promise of more green, and more green is a promise of a surface, and a surface is the one thing this place, in its wisdom, has never made.

It could have taught them words.

*Sky. Far. Tomorrow. Straight.* It could have stood at the crack and spoken, and the chamber would have done what it does to every voice: carried it three terraces up and returned it a second later, close and distant at once, so that the builders would have waited, as they wait for *gate* and *felt* and *lamp*, for the echo to complete the sentence. A new word would have entered the bead-lines and the work. It would not have made them unhappy. It would have made them want in a direction that has no door. There is no mercy in giving a fitted life a vocabulary for leaving, when leaving would unfit it.

It could have made them taller. It could have made their eyes dark, and able to meet a gaze. It could have given them wheels, and metal finer than hammered sheet, and a straight stair where now there is a sideways one. It weighed each imagining the way a gate-keeper weighs a sluice: by what would happen to the water.

In every version, the beings who were saved were not the beings who had been there. The saved ones were taller, greener, sunlit, and stranded in a pocket that had been opened past its balance. The builders below were not prisoners asking for a key. A prisoner is a life made for an outside and held from it. These were lives made for an inside, and held — if holding is the word — by their own hands, daily, faithfully, at the troughs and the gates and the lamps. To free them would have been to refit them. To refit them would have been to replace them.

That is what wisdom looks like, when it is not in a hurry: it does not mistake a different shape for a better one.

So it did not enter.

It did something harder for it than entering, and it did it at the scale of the place itself. It took up a station at the crack. It contracted itself — a power that has held up mountains and put them down again — to the width of turned shoulders, sideways, so that it would not widen the doorway by blocking it, and would not seal it by passing through. It stays mostly outside, the way the water table stays mostly under the floor: present as pressure, as cool through the soles, as the reason the sheen on the stone does not dry.

It does maintenance on the outside, as they do on the inside. It keeps the hill above from being opened by miners who would call it a discovery. It keeps the river that feeds the seepage from cutting a new channel through the vault. It keeps the aquifer from rising and from falling. No one inside will ever know. That is the point of maintenance. A house is repaired where it always was. A stair is worn smoother where it always was. Benevolence, here, is not to add. It is to not subtract.

It has learned their trade, which is listening. For a power accustomed to instant effect — to say and have it be — the second's wait for the echo is a new discipline. It hears a voice say *lamp* far inside, and it waits. A second later the phrase comes back to the crack, thinner, later than to anyone in the pocket, as if the rock were still deciding whether to remember it. It receives it as a sentence addressed to it, and does not answer with a voice of its own, because any voice of its own would be the end of the sentence and the beginning of a different town.

Sometimes, high on the back wall, the cave cricket re-angles its antennae toward a drip. It recognizes the gesture. It is the only other presence in the pocket that has learned to face the water without needing to be fed by it.

It will stay. It has time, and time, for the first time, has a rhythm it can keep: the drip, the click of beads, the scrape of a bone tool on a trough, the roar that never pauses because there is nowhere for the sound to go. It does not ask the water to change its level to prove that it cares. It cares by letting the level keep.

Below a hill that has no name, at a crack that is exactly the width of a sideways shoulder, it sits in the damp, out of the blue gloom and out of the sun, listening to a small world stay balanced — and for a power that could have done anything, that is what it does.
