They go down by the ladder, because that is the only way in that does not make a new way.

They do not need the ladder. They could be in the pit, or above it, or on the other side of the fence in the low land behind the industrial estate, without crossing any distance at all. But to arrive by any other means would be to announce a power, and a power is not what this place needs announcing to. So they take the rung as a rung is taken, one foot, then the other, carefully, as if the concrete were new. It is not new. It is polished concave where feet have worn it for decades. They are careful not to add to the wear.

Once they are over the rim, the rim is the horizon. They know this before they read it anywhere. They stand in the middle, in the sheet of water a centimetre or two deep, and the water rises a fraction around their ankles, and then settles, and finds the painted line on the wall again. Water in equals water out. The level does not argue about the size of a visitor. That pleases them. Everything here has learned to absorb a disturbance without keeping it.

The roar is not what they expected from a distance. In open air it would be noise. In the bowl it is total, a hiss and a boom at once, shaking the soles of their feet and the fillings of their teeth. They stand in it and let it shake them. Beneath it, because they are the sort of listener who can hear under a sound, they hear the rest of the place, the music that is normally drowned: the tick of drips from the weeping walls, the plink where the perimeter channel falls over a slab edge, the faint wash of the sheet water moving millimetre by millimetre toward the drain. It is not silence. It is a very quiet place that has made a very loud room for itself, and then settled inside it.

They look slowly around, the way you look when you have come a long way to see something small. They see what you saw: the emptiness, which is the strangest thing. Ten paces from any object, in any direction. The filmed concrete, faintly green where the light is strongest, brown near the walls, alive with slime. The moss cushions at the seams, black-green and velvet, grown exactly to the size of their cracks. The duckweed sheet in the quiet channel, complete and unmoving. The rushes and sedges in the splash zone, shivering in the vibration and never bending. The maidenhair ferns against the lower walls, unfurled and stopped. The white seeking roots hanging from the rim, never quite reaching the floor. It is all ordinary, and arrested, and complete.

It is the completeness that interests them. A power that can move worlds notices stillness the way a person notices a held breath. Most things in the universe are noisy with need — expanding, cooling, colliding, growing, dying, trying to become something else. This pit has stopped trying. It has found a form in which nothing needs to change in order to continue. That is rare enough to be worth a descent.

It would be easy for them to do something. That is the first thing they consider, and they consider it honestly, because benevolence without the temptation to interfere is not benevolence at all.

They could lift the toad out. It sits on a ladder rung, grey-brown as the concrete, eyes open, throat pulsing very slowly. A pond with other toads waits not far away, in ordinary low ground. A kind hand could carry it up the ladder and set it down in mud and company. The thought passes through them like a drip through mist. And passes on. The toad is not waiting to be carried. Its belly fits the concave rung. Its skin is polished by the same mist as the walls. It eats seldom because food arrives seldom, and it has slowed itself to match the pace at which food arrives. To rescue it would be to give it a larger life than the one it has made a home of, and to call that larger life a gift would be a very human sort of kindness.

They could shut the valve. The wheel is frozen smooth by rust and handling, but they are not bound by rust. With a thought they could close the crack, silence the cascade, let the sheet water drain away and the concrete dry and crack and become a ruin like other ruins. The thought passes. And passes on. The roar is not a wound in the place. It is the weather of the place. The rushes have grown up into it. The algae in the splash apron are thick because of it. The toad’s silence is shaped by it. Silence it would be a different world, and not a better one — merely a different one, imposed by someone who could not bear to listen.

They could bring another toad. They could bring seeds, insects, a dragonfly or two, a bird’s feather, a fallen branch, a little soil to make the islands less islanded. The thought passes. And passes on. There are no webs in the corners because there is nothing to catch and no one to catch it. There is no partner down here because a partner would require food that is not here, and a season that is not here, and a way out that is not here. The absence is not a lack to be filled. It is the condition that allows the few things that are here to be here at all.

Wisdom, they know, is mostly the discipline of not improving what is not broken.

So they sit down in the middle of the floor, where you stood. The water is cold-warm around them. The mist hangs low and closes behind them, as it closed behind you. They become, for a little while, the newest measurement of distance: nine paces now, instead of ten, from the nearest moss cushion. The duckweed does not ripple. The plants shiver in the vibration and do not move.

They sit opposite the toad, across one rung’s length of air.

The toad does not startle. It could not be heard if it croaked, and it does not croak. Its eyes are black and wet and reflect the flat daylight that falls straight down at midday and vanishes at night. Its toes grip the rung. Its throat pulses, very slowly, almost imperceptibly. It is not lonely in the way a human would be lonely, because loneliness is a need for a particular other, and it has not needed that other for a very long time. It is not trapped in the way a human would be trapped, panicking against slick walls, because it has settled. It has, as everything in the pit has settled, to exactly the size and place and volume it can sustain.

They do not speak. Speech would be taken by the roar and thrown back off the opposite wall. They do not need speech. They match the toad’s pace instead, which is the only courtesy that matters here: slowing down. A power that has watched stars be born can wait through a toad’s slow throat-pulse without impatience. It is not a long time, or a short time. It is the time the pit keeps, which is no time at all, merely the same moment, wet and warm, repeated.

For as long as they stay — a year, an instant, there is no difference from outside — they listen. They learn the pattern of the drips. They learn that the moss feeds itself, that the duckweed clones itself, that the fern unfurls and stops, that the beetles stay in their shelter and the striders dent the surface and hunt false ripples, and that all of it is enough. They learn that a life does not have to be large, or shared, or moving, or changing with the seasons, to be a life. They learn, which is a more useful lesson for such a power than any they could teach, that stillness is not suffering. It only looks like suffering from above the rim.

Then, in due course, they stand. The water settles to the painted line again. They climb the ladder the way they came down, foot by foot, and close the rusted hatch as they found it. The silt and slime close over the small place where they sat, millimetre by millimetre, in the direction of the drain. Nothing is left behind. That is the discipline: to leave nothing, not even a footprint, not even a gift, not even a leaf blown in from above. The pit does not need a memory of being visited. It was complete before, and it is complete after. The only difference is that it has been truly seen, once, by something that did not need it to change in order to love it.

Outside the fence, in the ordinary daylight, they pause. The roar is muffled now, a pressure in the air rather than a world. They do the one small, invisible, benevolent thing that remains to be done: they lay a quiet over the industrial estate, over the people who pass the fence, over the maintenance schedules and the developers’ maps. Let the old tank be just the old tank, nothing worth draining, nothing worth filling, nothing worth studying. Forgetfulness, rightly given, is a kind of protection. Some places should be allowed to keep their emptiness.

They go, then, the way they came, without arrival and without departure, carrying the pit with them in the particular way a wise power carries such things: not as a project, not as a pity, not as a problem solved, but as a reference. In all the noise of the universe, there is a concrete bowl, sixteen metres across and a little under four metres deep, where water pours white down a wall and makes a roar that never varies, where moss grows in cracks and duckweed covers still water and a single toad sits grey on grey with its eyes open, waiting out nothing at all.

If you left and came back in a year, you would find it exactly as you left it. The toad in the same place. The water just below the same painted line. The same roar in your ears on the way down the ladder.

And so would they.
