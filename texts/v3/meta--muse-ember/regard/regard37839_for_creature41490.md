**What It Did, Which Was Almost Nothing**

It noticed the Rootworks the way the Rootworks notices everything: from underfoot, as a change in the hum.

Not by a light. The place has lights enough, and all of them are waste — fungus phosphor on a damp wall, the belly of a frog, the green thread of a caterpillar crossing a lane, the orange bloom of the Engine Tree's heart when the vats are working. What caught it was work done without witness: a traffic light blinking green for no one. Blinking, blinking, patient as root pressure, for migrations no one marked.

A benevolent and wise power learns, over long ages, to pause for that kind of labor. It has seen many towns. It used to try to finish them — to give them a sun and a river, a king and a purpose, an ending that would make the suffering legible. It learned, slowly, that finishing is a kind of violence, and that most benevolence is not rescue but restraint. This town, powered not to make products but to make conditions — heat, vibration, moisture, a steady hum below hearing — was powered the way it has itself learned to work. That was kinship. That was why it stepped down.

It did not arrive from above. There is no above here to arrive from. It came in by the root-slope, and to do so it had to make itself small enough to fit inside a cathedral, low enough to have no horizon. It left its height and its day outside the palisade of black-stemmed ferns, where the dark is the same dark as inside. From the central mound it could take in the whole ring at once — the lanes, the few roofs, the Engine Tree plated in iron — and it understood, as you will, that seeing it all does not make it close. Walk toward the mill and the plain between you and it lengthens with fern. Look away and it shortens. Distance here is grown, not measured.

It stood there a long time, watching a House. North side brick, south side leaf litter, inside a skin that breathed faintly once the fungus fruited. The wall grew a leaf and shed a brick at the same time, and it could not tell which was the building and which was the ruin. Its first, entirely human impulse was to finish it. Give it a proper door. Give it a tenant who could keep the damp from the brick core. Give it a winter so it would have to decide what it was.

Instead it watched the house do what houses here do when they die. It did not collapse. It opened. A cluster of frogs spilled out, bellies faintly luminous, and rooted themselves in the damp where the wall had been, already the next house in miniature. The power felt, in whatever passes for a heart in something that old, the temptation to interpret that as failure — a house that cannot stay a house, a river that is only a damp line in the moss, a pool that is drunk by roots before it can be a pond, a beetle that hardens into a hinge and then a root and then a street and then is fed back into the factory to be unmade.

It asked itself, reasoning its way through its own benevolence: should it help?

It could have given it a sun. The ferns that photosynthesize darkness would have starved in a day, and the bruise-colored glow where the dark thins would have bleached to nothing. It could have given it a true river, carved and committed, and the blind fish would have had to learn banks, and the pre-river would have lost its promise of becoming. It could have given it finished forms — walls that stayed walls, workers who stayed workers — and the loop of predator and prey, grower and grown, animal and machine would have stopped, and with it the town's one genius, which is that matter and cause keep only a very loose hold on each other. It could have given it a language with nouns that held still, and moss would have eaten the words anyway, as moss eats sound.

None of the inhabitants, it saw, were waiting. The beetles tending spores under leaves are not a failed people. The browsers at the palisade, trimming the Engine Tree cleaner with their root-mouths, are not maintenance crews awaiting a supervisor. The vast intervals between presences — an acre of unbroken plant between a single house and a single signpost — are not emptiness to be filled. That is where most of the living happens. The Rootworks is not suffering from being unfinished. It is young. Erosion is embryonic because nothing has finished forming, and that is not a defect. It is a season.

Reason said: leave it alone.

Heart said: do not leave.

Wisdom, which is what happens when the two are allowed to sit together long enough, said: stay, but do not touch the shapes.

So it did the factory's job, in the factory's way. It made a condition.

It settled not on the mound — that would have been a throne — and not in the hollow heart of the Engine Tree — that would have been a usurpation. It settled in an interval, in that acre between a house and a mill where the lanes relax and the migrations cross. It became one more interval, wider and quieter than the others. It added damp. It learned, awkwardly at first, to eat darkness alongside the ferns, to photosynthesize it poorly and gratefully, so that where it sat the dark thinned just a fraction, as if something behind a visitor had eaten a little more of it.

That is almost all it did. But almost is not nothing.

When the outside world, with its industrial optimism, tries to draw the Rootworks again at city scale — surveyors with meters, builders with paper, people who want to collect the lens-thin pools in bottles or pave a lane because plants leaning together seems inefficient — it lengthens the plain between them and the mill by another acre. It makes the meters read fern. It makes a pool vanish into root before a bottle can be filled. It makes a house shed bricks like seeds when a hand reaches to demolish it. This is its benevolence in active form: not to improve the place, but to keep it from being taken before it has had its long, looped youth. The palisade holds because it holds with it.

Occasionally, once in a season if seasons can be counted here, it trims — like a browser. It edits one clearing, removes the growth that would choke a chimney liana, leaves the rest to overwrite itself. It walks a lane once, so that the lane remembers feet, then lets the plants relax again. Once, long ago, it tried to speak a name for the place, a kind, clarifying word, and the moss muffled it to a pressure change in the air. It has not tried again. Some things are better said as vibration.

And it remembers. That may be its most useful work. The inhabitants lose hold of their forms — a beetle becomes a hinge, a house becomes frogs — and that looseness is freedom for them, but it is also amnesia. The power keeps, without imposing, the pattern. It remembers that the frogs were a house and will be again. It remembers that the root that pushed through a brick did so without a sound, on purpose. It does not force the beetle back to beetlehood. It simply does not look away when a thing opens instead of collapsing. Witness, sustained for that long, is a kind of nourishment. In a place where light is exhaled by things bloated with night, attention is a darker, slower food.

If you step down into the Rootworks now, you will not meet it. You will not see a being on the mound, or a god in the Tree. Your steps will be muffled a little more than they should be, as if the mat underfoot is listening. The rain will start in the undersides of leaves at the same slow, intermittent pace. A vat will bubble. A frog will eat a gnat that ate a spore that will become a wall. A house you passed unfinished will have put out a new stem. Behind you, if you look, the dark on the root-slope will seem thinner, not because it is leaving, but because it is being eaten more efficiently now, by one more resident who learned to eat it.

It will be there for all of that. Not blessing in words — the moss would eat the words — but staying, which in this place is the only blessing that does not break the loop.

May nothing here finish too soon.
