They do nothing to it.

That is not because they are far away, and it is not neglect. For a power that could do almost anything, doing nothing to this place is the first difficult act, and the one they have to keep doing. They do not land. A footstep would break a film, a boot-print would close wrong in the stone, a shadow would be the first lie the plain had ever been told. Their attention comes the way the mist-tide comes — slowly, low, from all sides at once — and it does not warm the stone, dry it, or raise a note in any hollow.

They notice it, at first, only by not passing over it.

From above, with eyes, it looks finished and empty: a worn grey dome under a lid of sky, no green wavering to say living, no shadow to say moving, nothing sharp to catch the eye. A lesser attention would call it dead and go on looking for a world that announces itself in color. They stay because of the sound, which carries farther than sight here and does not belong to the air alone. Thousands of weeping rocks drip into cupped stone. The pans are stippled by condensation. Under everything, grazers rasp, worms suck in silt, paddlers plink in their cups, midges hum over the water, and tap-predators answer the stone and the stone answers back.

They listen longer than anything that lives here listens. Longer than the big tap-predator at the rim of its pan, domed and grey and forearm-sized, that has held one bell-stone for a year, maybe for many years, tapping once — a question — and waiting for the pan to answer. In the beginning they mistake that vigil for something to be pitied: an animal stuck, lonely, wasting its life beside a ringing rock. Listening longer teaches them otherwise. It is not wasting time. It is keeping it. The filter-feeders wave to its tempo. The midges rise to it. The paddlers time their plinking to it. Prey blunder into its hollow and are stunned and taken, yes, but the tap is also how the pan knows itself. The place and that being are not two things, the predator and the stone that feeds it. They are one way of being, set to a shared beat.

Once that is understood, the temptations arrive, because benevolence is full of them, and they have to be set aside one by one.

They could give it light. A clearing sun, a dry wind, a sky that lifted. But the mist is not weather obscuring a landscape; it is the water coming back down after the films and worms and silt have used it. Dry the stone and the drums stop, because dry stone does not carry the tap. Warm the pans and the sheet of midges breaks, the condensation-fall thins, the weeping rocks give less. The flat grey is not a dim version of a brighter world. It is the color of wet stone, of blind flesh, of fungus that eats minerals instead of sunlight. To flood it with light would not reveal it. It would replace it.

They could give it green. It is easy to imagine a seed pressed into the grey silt of a pan, soil brought in a hollowed hand, roots finding purchase, leaves making the first color this place has ever known. They imagine it long enough to see the cost. A plant needs interval: a dry season, a place to root, shade for others, fall for its own death. This plain has no interval. It is wet at the foot, wet in the stone, wet in the air that beads on eyebrows. A root would have to live off the film cycle instead of in it. A leaf would take the mist and not give it back. The black and ochre crusts, the pale sonotrophic rings that brighten when the drumming is loud, the translucent threads in the pans — they do not need to be outgrown. Nothing green ever enters this cycle, and nothing needs to. Green would not be a gift added to the world. It would be another world, installed on top of this one until this one could no longer be heard.

They could give it eyes. The grazers are blind, the worms eyeless, the predators hunt by touch and by the returning ring in stone. To a seeing creature that looks like a privation. But in a flat, shadowless grey, sight would find the same polished emptiness in every direction and learn nothing that vibration does not tell better: which hollow holds a sealed grazer, which silt is loose with worms, which film is running fractionally uphill along a resonance. The animals are not missing a sense. They have traded a poor one for a precise one. Eyes would only make them dissatisfied with sameness, and then, being kind, they would start giving them color to look at, and then shapes to hide in, and then the whole acoustic order would unravel into a visual one. They withdraw that thought too.

They could be merciful in the smaller, nearer way. They could blunt the predator's stuttering roll so the grazers are not stunned in the shallow water. They could stop the tap, still the bell, quiet the drums that must be deafening. But the roll is not only killing. It shakes minerals loose from the grit for the rings to feed on. The tap is how paddlers find mates, how predators tell a paddler plinking for love from a paddler plinking because prey has blundered into its cup, how worms' favored hollows are known by their duller ring. The grazer that freezes a fraction too long has evolved around that fraction. The worms eat the dead of everything and return it to the silt; without death the cycle locks. Benevolence here cannot mean sparing the eaten at the expense of the eaters, or sparing both at the expense of the fungi and the films and the slow migration of the mats downslope, a fraction of an inch a year. The loudness is not noise imposed on silence. It is the medium in which mating, hunting, warning, and feeding are the same conversation. To silence it would be to blind them all in their own world.

They could be merciful to the people. They arrive wet to the bone, hunched against a wind with nothing to rustle, and they leave with their boots full of pan-water and their ears ringing. Their hands are the only warm things on the plain, and the animals freeze at the irregular human tapping, then resume when the hands go away. It would be simple to warm the windbreak crescent, to give them fire, to let bone scoops be fuel and hammerstones become hearths, to let a runnel in silt deepen into a channel and a cache beside a pan become a claim. But people here are weather, not residents, and that is their right proportion. The plain takes things back slowly — a slab sinks a finger's width in a season, a runnel fills with silt, a flake is polished by the film that never leaves it — but a settlement would change things faster than stone can answer. Fire would need the big predators' calcified forelegs. Permanence would need the pans not to stagnate, then not to flood, then to be fenced from the mist-tide. A people who stayed would turn scraping into farming and pounding into husbandry, and the closed, old rules would break not from malice but from staying too long in a place built for visiting. The cold ache through the boot, the roar you have to shout through and then stop shouting through, the departure wet to the bone — that is not punishment. It is the dose in which a human can come close, eat the grey paste with its mineral tang, leave a water-worn flake beside a pan, and go. They do not warm them. They do not drive them off completely either. They let them be weather.

They could name it and keep it. A name, a map, a record held in memory so that it is never forgotten: that would be the archivist's benevolence. But a name is a handle, and a handle invites hands that come with eyes and buckets and green ambitions. Everything here is already known in the only way that matters — by the predators who have learned the ringing stones by heart, by the grazers following polished trails older than any human mark, by the thread-dwellers beating uphill in seams no one will see. To catalog it for the wider world would be to translate it out of sound and into sight, out of touch and into possession. The safest preservation is a kind of unremarkable obscurity: hard to find except by ear, unprofitable to eyes, cold and wet and ringing to anyone who arrives. They keep it off the maps by never giving it one.

That is what they do instead. They stay attuned, and they keep the conditions attuned: mist low, sky a lid, horizon far, stone cold, water coming up everywhere along invisible lines. They do not answer the big predator's tap. It would be easy, and it would be a beautiful third voice in the stone — a low note, lower than the bell, returned once in a long while, saying: *your long listening is heard.* But the drums here are out of time on purpose. Each hollow ticks on its own. The predators tap in counterpoint across a pan, a dozen answering and not agreeing. To add a perfect answer would be harmony imported from a world that needs chords resolved. It would also be a new signal in a tuned system. Grazers might come to it expecting a drip of food. Predators might hunt toward it. The pan's tempo, which the big one has kept for years, would shift by a fraction, and a fraction here is a life, a migration, a mineral shaken loose or not. Wise power knows it cannot want companionship enough to risk that. It loves the place in the place's own language, which is ask and wait and do not take.

So they are present as listeners outside the food web. Their attention is not metabolized. It does not brighten the rings, does not stir the pans, does not make the midges rise. The fungi brighten when the sound is loudest because vibration shakes their food loose, not because they are being watched. When the mist-tide comes in and the dripping doubles and the roar rises until you must shout to be heard a pace away, the pans darken — not with shadow, but because the filter-feeders and grazers and worms have gathered below the surface in a solid sheet — and the plain becomes one animal. They are there for that, the way the biggest predator is there: not above it, not ruling it, just held by the same tempo. And then they do not tap back.

When the big one's time comes, they do not extend it. It will go the way everything goes here, into the silt-worms, and the silt where it fed will ring a little duller, and the worms will remember it in their turning. After a while a smaller predator will take the bell-stone, tuck its six legs under it like a stool, lift a foreleg, and tap once. A question. The pan will answer. The filter-feeders will wave to the new tempo. The power will already be listening, and it will let the question and the answer belong to the pan.

If a seed ever arrives on a wind that has nothing to rustle, it will find no soil and no season to keep it. If a ship or a settler ever comes with deeper tools than hand-deep, with intentions to dry, warm, green, name, and hold, they will find a place that gives them no purchase: no color to advertise it, no resource to carry away, no stillness to build in, only cold through the sole and a ringing that gets into the teeth. That is not a wall. It is the plain's own final form, kept final.

They do not improve it. They do not save it. They do not finish its music. They keep their third voice out of the stone, and in that restraint — sustained over timescales that make a predator's long vigil look brief, and a human visit look like weather — the benevolence and the wisdom are the same act.

Behind them, if behind is a word that applies, the drums go on ticking over the worn, shining plain. The rings brighten. The grazers rasp and freeze and rasp. The paddlers plink from cup to cup. A tap-predator crosses the bare quarter-mile on tiptoe, tapping as it goes, a grey pilgrim following bells from pan to pan. The big one taps. The pan answers. And the place, not improved, not sorry, not seen, goes on dripping and listening to itself — listened to, at last, without being touched.
