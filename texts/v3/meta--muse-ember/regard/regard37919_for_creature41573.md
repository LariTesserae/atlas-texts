It noticed the place not because the place cried out.

It has watched other worlds cry out. It has watched low, green folds of earth beg in every language they have — lift this drought, stop this flood, take this blight off the wheat, take these wolves off the lambs, save us, punish them, make us matter. It is benevolent, and so it has answered often enough to learn what answering costs. Rivers straightened do not forgive. Suns summoned do not stay. Rescues, carried out cleanly from above, almost always tear a thread that was holding something else together.

This place did not beg.

It was worn, and worn, and worn — domes planed to the curve of a loaf, plateau drowned to a shallow sea of peat and pool — and having reached its last form, it had gone on living with tremendous, ordinary energy on top of that form. Rain arriving sideways. Kelp twelve metres down, thick as a forest. Trout hanging under a sky-mirror. A sheepdog waiting in the wet. A man in oilskins before light, clicking pellets into cold water. Nothing about it asked to be improved.

That is why it stayed to look.

At first, as wisdom does, it examined the temptations.

It could dry the bog. Easy, for such a power. One long, pale exhale and the sphagnum would stiffen, the pools would shrink to mud, the acid courage of the place would fail, and in a decade there would be sweet grass for sheep and a road that did not vanish under a lake. The power considered it and set it aside. The bog is not a failed meadow. It is a memory the land keeps of itself, holding water twenty times its weight, holding shape intact for centuries in black, anaerobic quiet. To drain it would be to erase the archive to make a car park for the present. It would not.

It could bring sun. It could lift the cloud deck that piles against the high ground and let the canopy below be lit through — real light for the ferns, real warmth for the sheep, 18 degrees for the attendant who climbs the weather mast with numb fingers. The power considered it and set it aside. The cold lush is not a deficiency of the sun. Everything here has negotiated its terms with wind and wet for so long — moss a handswidth thick on the north side of every stone, trees wind-trained to near-touching, breath visible in imagined summer at 8 or 10 degrees — that even a kindness of warmth would be a violence. The cloud is not between the place and its good. The cloud is part of the bargain.

It could fix the machinery. Mend the rusted conveyor where the frogs sing, stop the dewatering pump that still sucks at the old peat working, take down the pylons standing in pools, straighten the causeway that goes under the lake for two hundred metres, silence the hydro intake, close the fish farm ring of orange floats. The power considered each, one by one, and set them all aside. Not because the machines are good — iron rusting from the outside in at a pace you can almost watch is not good, and diesel in the smell is not good — but because they are no longer only machines. The half-drowned working is frog water now. The pylon bases are perches. The pellets feed, as well as salmon, a line of gulls who have learned the timetable. The powerhouse no bigger than a barn is warm, and a man lives in its hum, and for two men on a trawler the idling engines are a kind of company. A wise power does not restore a pristine that never existed for everyone who lives here now. It would do more harm than the rust.

It could save. It could hold the bog when it shears along its deep crack and takes fence posts and young trees, hold the river when it rises two metres in a night, hold the whale from blowing too far out in winter, hold the salmon lice to their cages so the wild char in the high lakes are not sickened, hold the shepherd from a bad step on the tussocks. The power lingered here longest. Benevolence aches to intervene where it can see a single life in trouble.

And then it understood the place better.

The slide is not an accident in this system; it is one of its ways of making new pools, new edges, new work for fungi and for the shepherd who knows which bog has gone. The river rising and rearranging silt, branches, a plastic buoy, a dead bird, is not a failure of the river; it is the river doing the braiding that keeps spawning gravel clean. The fish under almost every stone you turn are there precisely because nothing has been made stable for them. This is a world whose rule is: nothing dries, nothing stays where it was left. To save it from that rule would be to unmake it.

So it will not save in that way.

What it does instead is smaller, and therefore truer.

First, it witnesses. Not from above, not in survey, not in extraction. It goes down to the level of the place. It crouches in the sphagnum and feels it give under its knee, cold through everything, and then give way altogether, and it does not correct its weight. It walks, as you learn to walk, on the tussocks, not the moss. It goes down through the kelp curtain where pollack and wrasse and crabs are always in the way of seeing the rock, and watches without counting. It rises off the bog air with the midges between rain walls and is inside them when you stop, and does not ask them to clear.

For a place that has been rendered beautifully in words by visitors and missed in fact by everyone down below, to be seen without being used is a change. The power's attention does not lift the mist. It makes the mist *attended*. Ravens quartering into it and screaming are not alone in it. That is not much. It is, the power knows, the exact amount that does not break anything.

Second, it holds a boundary — not around the hills, but around the scale of human appetite.

The attendants who live here — the man who walks the buried pipe line through forest in boots full of water, the man who unfreezes the mast with his fingers warmed inside his jacket, the shepherd below the mist who knows the sheep and the dog, the two on the trawler watching the instruments turn frantically against a sky that will not clear — they are few, and they are not owners. They are attendants. Their work is constant repair against water, and they do it without any need for the place to become a town or a centre or a view.

The power will keep it that way. Not by miracles. By obscurity and by inconvenience. It will keep the track a track and not a road. It will keep developers who see a hydro scheme or a second fish farm or a drainage grant from finding the valley easy. It will keep the published maps vague about the causeway. It will let iron rust — rust is, in this damp, a kind of slow, honest return — but it will not let a new kind of rust arrive that is faster than the place can absorb. It will hold the salmon disease at the threshold of the cages, not by curing the farmed fish, whom the farm man tends in rain that needles his neck, but by ensuring that tide of lice does not become a tide in the inlets. It will hold the intake to a low flow in dry weeks, so the braided side channels do not run thin where eggs are tucked into a cobble that may be moved in any case. These are not rescues. They are the power learning the rules and obeying them on behalf of the place.

Third, it keeps company with the being who lives in it.

It has learned that being by their work. Not by prayer — no one here prays for weather, they have learned to move with it — but by the unnoticed fidelities: feeding before light, checking the hum, moving sheep before the bog goes, warming fingers, drinking tea in a wheelhouse whose windows are streaked. The power does not give them visions. It does not speak. It sits, in the wet, beside them. When the causeway is under a lake, it waits with the man on the far tussock while the water falls by morning. When the mist thickens between one rain wall and the next, it stands still in the midges with the cattle and does not need to be elsewhere. When a dog waits, mostly wet, mostly waiting, it waits in the same way, without turning waiting into a task.

Benevolence, the power has learned in other places, is often imagined as lifting. Here it is more like staying. If a boot goes through sphagnum and finds black water, the power's attention is already there, not pulling the boot out but knowing exactly where the firm tussock is, so that the next step is found. If a path must be re-found every season, the power re-finds it, invisibly, with them. Not often. Not to make life easy. Just enough that the attendants can go on attending, and the sheep can go on walking only on the tussocks because they have learned the same rule you learn.

And fourth — this is the thing it will do most of all — it remembers.

Anything that falls into peat is preserved, black and intact, for centuries. A boot. A fence post. A sheep that did not get out. The power remembers in the same way, but without keeping. It remembers the trout in the high lakes for being improbable. It remembers the white threads of waterfalls on slopes dry in the morning. It remembers the wren, the siskin, the bird you cannot name, heard all at once without trying. It remembers the whale blowing far out beyond the kelp, once, heard more than seen. It does not need to preserve them in amber. A wise power's memory is not a cabinet. It is a continued willingness to return as the weather returns — colliding, unloading, retreating a little, coming again from a different quarter — and to find the place still there, remade in its details, unchanged in its form, and to be glad.

It will not live here. It cannot; to live here would be to dry, or to fix, or to save. It will pass through. It will arrive, as the rain arrives, sideways, filling your hood and boots in minutes if you let it. It will leave as the mist leaves, which is to say it will not entirely leave. Between walls, when the air thickens and you stop walking and are inside it, that thickness is not only midges.

If you stand very still on the summit plain, with your breath visible and your fingers numb, you may feel it — not a presence above you, explaining itself, but a presence beside you, at knee-height in the sponge, learning the place by moving through it as you do. It has drawn near, and what it has chosen to do, in its benevolence and its wisdom, is almost nothing you could point to.

It has chosen not to improve a final form.
It has chosen not to hurry a turbulent one.
It has chosen to walk on the tussocks, to keep the boundary small, to stay with the attendants, and to remember.

That, for this place, is everything.
