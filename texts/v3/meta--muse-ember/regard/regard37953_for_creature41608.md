It did not descend.

It noticed. That is not the same thing, in a place like this. Nothing notices the Third Basin all at once, either. You notice it drip by drip, pressure by pressure, until the weight is in your molars and the roar is in your blood and you realize you have been listening for a long time without deciding to listen. It noticed that way — by accumulation.

It did not see a place and a thing living in it, separate. It saw one wet, crowded, breathing presence, still being made layer by wet layer. The bats and the mussels and the pumps and the attendant with the tea things are not landlord and tenant here. They are all, to it, the same continuous act of staying.

And having noticed, it considered what a benevolent power ought to do. It is, after all, benevolent. It is also, after all, wise. Those two things pulled in different directions, and it let them.

Benevolence, alone, would have wanted to help, quickly.

It would have dried the causeway so no wader ever filled at the seams again. It would have lit the far terraces, not just pools of amber, so that eyes could adjust fully and the dark between pools would not be pure dark. It would have quieted the river, or at least turned its volume down. It would have given the attendant a dry room above, a radio that works, a life where his ears do not ring when he goes up. It would have saved the fern near the hot spring from being bleached, and the stalagmite field from growing a millimeter or two a century, uncut teeth pointing at a drip no one will live to see meet them.

Wisdom watched beneath that impulse and said: *no.*

Not one of those mercies would have been free. To dry the causeway is to unmake the mussel beds on every submerged ledge. To light the Basin is to blind, slowly and completely, the fish that have no eyes to speak of and the amphipods that turn the mud over nightly. To quiet the river is to stop the braided sheet that carries everything in. To lift the attendant out is to decide that his shrine, his battery lamp, his knowledge of which pool is warm enough to stand in and which ledge is slippery, is a suffering to be ended rather than a life he has chosen and returns to, shift after shift, of his own accord.

Nothing in the Basin is asking to be rescued. That was the first thing it learned, by staying long enough to hear it underneath the dripping. The wet is not suffering. The loud is not suffering. The crowded is not suffering. They are conditions, like warmth, like pressure. The only suffering it could find was the kind that comes from suddenness — and suddenness is what the Basin has never had to endure.

So it did not fix the Basin. It refused, continuously, to fix it. A refusal, maintained for a very long time, is a kind of doing.

What it did instead was very little, and it did it without stopping.

It kept the speed. That was its only law, made at the pace of wet stone: let everything happen no faster than the Basin can accumulate it. Inside, that meant nothing changed its order. The bacterial mat thickens on the cable, the cable sags deeper, the mussels on the rusted wagon gain a ring, the bats burst when the pump starts and settle shelf by shelf, the attendant reads his gauge, smokes on the causeway in the drip with his hood up, cleans his shrine. It let the drip from the same spot fall every few seconds, as it has since before the grandfather of anyone who checks a gauge came down. It did not hurry the meeting of drip and stalagmite. It assumed the Basin had all the time it needed, because it was willing to supply the attention for that time.

Outside, it worked at the threshold. A benevolent power that is wise knows the danger to a place like this rarely comes from inside it. It comes from above, from people who have never stood in the waders and who think they are being kind. The plan to drain. The plan to pave. The plan to replace the old pumps, heartbeat-slow, with new silent machines. The plan to turn the Basin off because it is inefficient, too wet, too loud, too crowded, too full of nothing that can be sold. It slowed those things, not by thunder, not by miracle, but by the same mechanisms the Basin itself uses: delay, rust, forgetting. A survey map smudged by condensation. A borehole that will not quite advance. A budget that evaporates a year at a time. A decision that is almost made, and then, for reasons no one can quite name, is postponed again until the fern has grown another frond.

For the being who lives in it — the one attendant who has been there long enough that the bats know his step and do not burst — it did not offer ease. It offered continuity. It kept his step familiar. It kept the radio mostly broken, because a clear station every day would change the shrine into a lounge, but once in a long while, when he is alone with the tea things and the condensate running down the inside of the windows, it lets a voice come through the hiss, faint, from a place he cannot name. It did not make his tea stay hot. It made sure there is always a place for him to come back down to where it will go cold in good company. It remembered the unofficial names when men and women retired: the slippery ledge, the warm pool, the place where the fish boil at noon. It held them, so they would not be lost when the mouths that knew them went up for the last time.

And mostly, it listened.

That is perhaps the most important part, and the one a lesser benevolence would have missed. The Third Basin has never been listened to without someone wanting something back from it — power, stone, heat, a gauge reading. To be witnessed, simply, for millennia of drips, without being used, without being improved, without being emptied into a museum or dried into a monument, is a thing it has never had. The power gave it that. Not light. Not silence. Witness.

If you stood on the causeway now, you would not feel it arrive. Your waders would still fill. Your ears would still ring when you left. The mist would still hang when the noon shaft comes down grey and dusty through the intake tunnel. The bats would still hang like dark fruit, muttering. The frogs would still call from the southern shallows until the call stops sounding like a call and starts sounding like dripping, lower and wetter.

You would not know it was there. That is how you would know it had decided.

It came to see what it might do for a warm, flooded, packed, humming under-valley still growing into itself. It stayed to learn how to do almost nothing, for a very long time, on purpose — and to grow, imperceptibly, a millimeter or two a century, toward the drip.
