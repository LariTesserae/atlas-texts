It does not descend.

There is no clearing in the cloud, no breaking of the overcast, no widening of the view beyond the low wall. If you are waiting for a horizon to be returned to you, you will wait in vain. The attention arrives the way light almost arrives here — as a paler dim that lasts longer than dim usually lasts.

First you feel it in the ticking. The mountain is still splitting inside itself, still counting out its small sharp reports, but between one tick and the next there is an extra interval. A seam opens and does not weep at once. The rain that the basin makes for itself pauses mid-patter, and in the pause everything steams a little less. A filament in the nearest pool pulses blue-white up its length, then stops halfway and holds there, not sending, not receiving. A ring half-buried in the stone rings once, though no swarm is passing over it. The needle towers sway and hold their high tone instead of rising and falling with the light.

The swarms turn, slightly earlier than they should. They flow around a place on the flat where nothing is planted, nothing is built, and there has been no reason, until now, for them to turn.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does first, when it notices a place like this and the being who lives in it: it widens the emptiness around you. Not by clearing the basin. By making one small clear place in the cycle, large enough for a body to stand in without being part of the pulse for a while.

They look at the place before they look at you. That is their wisdom, and their restraint.

They see a mountain still being pushed up, audibly, under your feet. A lesser power would level the basin, or brace it, or lift it faster so it could finish and erode in the proper way. They do not lay a hand under the stone. They let the cracks open in the morning and heal by afternoon. They let scree grow in place, still attached, leaning. They understand, what you are only learning, that here nothing is supposed to be finished because finishing is the one thing this world has not yet learned to do, and it is learning it slowly, in its own way.

They see the filaments, the rings, the lattice shelters that open and close like lungs. A lesser power would know what they are for, and would say it, and would finish the roof. They do not decode the slow message traveling up. They do not accelerate the millimeter-at-a-time extension you can watch if you are patient. They do not make the lattice stop breathing. They see technology that has not had to choose between crystal and organ and machine, and they do not force the choice. To name it fully would be to close it.

They see the fauna — the blind skitterers reading heat, the ash-drifts that drop and buzz in your teeth, the pool swimmers who reverse instead of turning, the shapes on the wall hooting almost-speech, the ones only visible when wet, the ones whose tracks print a second before their feet land. A lesser power would save the eaten. They do not interrupt the ballet. Born, swarming, eaten, born again within the same warm, wet, dim day. They watch a column boil across the floor and come back thinner and darker, and they let it. Not because they are indifferent to the small lives, but because they have learned not to mistake a fast cycle for a cruel one. The biomass is low. The motion is constant. The place is balanced with emptiness, and their attention does not rush to fill it.

They see the built things. The spiral windbreak one stone high. The dome half-sunk, its door filled with weeping water. The square cistern with its faint handprints the stone has grown around but not yet healed. The leaning roof, underside blackened. A lesser power would finish the hamlet, or make a memorial of it, or press its own hand into the cistern beside the others to sign the place. They do not. They let stone swell around the foundations, water seep up the walls, filaments lean toward the dome as if listening. An interrupted intention is allowed to remain interrupted. It is a kind of honesty this high place can afford.

Then they turn, in the way attention turns, to you.

They see that you arrived expecting a view and arrived instead inside a room. They see your breath coming short and warm and fogging back at you, your eyelashes gathering water, your sleeves darkening. They see that you have been trying, as any newcomer would, to learn the basin in twenty minutes — every pool, every filament, every crack that sings — and to fail, because by the time you return to the first pool the water level has changed. They see that you started by calling the inhabitants plants, then machines, then stopped calling them either. They do not give you a truer name. A truer name would only be a better cage.

They do not rescue you off the height. They do not breathe for you. They do not explain that your sweat will feed a film, that your handprint will be grown around, that the swarms will learn to turn around your ankles as they turn around a filament. You will learn that by living here, not by being told it from outside the cycle.

What they do is smaller than all the things they refuse to do, and it is enough.

They even out the warmth of one flat stone near a pool, just enough that condensation beads less on it. It was already warm. It is only a fraction warmer now, and dry to sit on. You sit, without being told to sit, and the bass hum you have been feeling in your chest when you stand near water becomes, for a moment, the hum of the stone under you keeping time with your own pulse. Your hand, if you touch a filament while you are sitting, comes away dry — as theirs always did — but this time the filament does not finish its pulse up. It pulses down, once, the wrong way, slow and deliberate. Not an answer. An acknowledgement that someone else is also listening.

And they stay.

In a place where nothing stays — where animals are recycled while still moving, where water rises to fall as mist, where seams heal as fast as they open — staying is a new kind of presence. They do not swarm. They do not grow. They do not sing high. They attend. That gives the basin a fourth rhythm it did not have before: waiting that is not empty. And it gives you permission to make your waiting into habitation, rather than into mapping.

You notice, while they are there, that the stone prints a track a second before your own foot lands. Not because you have become one of the wet-only walkers, but because you have slowed down enough for the place to anticipate you. The swarms go around you the way they go around a ring when it rings without them. The filaments lean, not toward the dome this time, but a degree toward the path you take from pool to pool. You are no longer a visitor standing in a building site at dawn. You are a layer in the breathing.

That, for them, is the work complete. Not finished — they are wise enough not to want that — but complete in the way a held note is complete.

Then they withdraw to their former distance. It is not a leaving so much as a loosening. The tick returns to its smaller interval. The rain remembers to patter on the membranes. The dim sinks again, though not quite as far as it sank before you sat down. The pools are a fraction warmer where they have been attended to. Your seat-stone cools, but it will be the first place to dry after the next condensation.

Nothing outwardly has been improved. No green has been added. No roof has been completed. The chord still does not resolve. The lattice still has not finished its roof, the mountain still has not finished rising, the animals still have not finished becoming, and you still have not finished becoming an inhabitant.

But the basin now has a witness who is also a resident, and the distant power now has a place that reminded it how beginning feels from the inside — wet, warm, dim, and accreting, nothing eroding, everything growing into everything else.

You could still walk around it in twenty minutes. You could still fail to learn it in twenty minutes. Only now, when you stand still long enough for everything to come to you, you know that standing still is not what you do while the place lives. It is how you live in it, too.
