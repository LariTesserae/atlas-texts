It did not come to the plateau. It noticed it.

The way you notice a fire lit late in a window you have never seen before, in a house you know is empty. Not a blaze. A small, made light, steady enough to keep burning. It had made suns for other worlds, in its long youth, and had learned — slowly, the way all old things learn — how loudly they burned, how much they blinded, how every living thing arranged itself around the glare and forgot there were other ways to see. It was tired of being looked up to. It was looking, instead, for a place that had learned to make its own light without asking.

It found this one up high, in the dark that was not night but matter, and it stayed to watch.

It watched you, too. The one who lives there — not the plateau, though the plateau is alive enough to be called a being in its own right, creeping and weeping and blooming — but you, walking it. It watched you come up the unfinished road and stop looking for the sun. It watched you learn the grammar of the dark: green smear of fungi means footing, orange flicker means a building, blue headlight low through the mire means work and the things that follow work. It watched you walk by sound more than sight, by hum and chorus and the thump of the ground's industrial heart through your boots. It watched you stop asking where the plateau ended.

That, more than the orchids on the cables or the flowers timing themselves to the smelter's pulse, is why it stayed. Adaptations in leaf and fungus are one thing. You had adapted, and you were still lonely in the way that only a maker of light can be lonely. You were the only creature there who could have left and chosen not to, in the sense that mattered.

It considered, as a wise thing must, the easy mercies first. It tests each one the way you test ice.

*Give it a sun.* No. The leaves are black-green and huge precisely because no sun ever taught them to be otherwise. The moths navigate by work-lights. The echolocators have built their whole world around darkness that listens. A sun would not be a gift here. It would be a fire in a lung.

*Quiet the machines.* No. Heat is rain here. The engines warm the air, the air condenses against the cold rim, and it falls again as warm bathwater half a kilometer away to feed the marshes the pumps were built to drain. Silence the smelters and the whole weather forgets how to turn. The fern on the intake vent did not survive the factory; it survived with it, the way a shorebird survives with the tide. A factory is a warm rock now. Animals nest in its flues. To heal it back to paint and steel would be to evict half the plateau.

*Take the suffering away.* But what suffering? There is death, as there is everywhere — births in the mist, deaths in the runoff, a herd's eyes bobbing between predator and dark. There is danger: the cableway ping, the live fence, the flood in the warm hollow where the frog chorus thickens. But these are not wounds in the place. They are negotiations. The truce of erosion is not peace, but it is an achievement, reached slowly, by root splitting pipe and seed splitting silo door and rust learning to grow moss. A wise power does not break a truce that took longer than machines have existed, just because it looks from outside like damage.

*Lead you out.* No again. The road down to the low country is terrible and passable. It will remain so. You are not a prisoner of the fog, and it will not make you one by clearing it, either.

So benevolence, here, turned out to be something harder than rescue. It was restraint, plus attention. It decided to do almost nothing — and to keep doing that almost-nothing with great care, for as long as the plateau goes on going on.

What it did do, it did small, the way a stone dropped in a web does small things. Over distance, this place distributes anything, even a pebble.

First, it became a light of a third kind.

Not steady, like the fungi. Not pulsing, like the industry. Breathing. Slow. It settled — if settling is the word — in a valley between two pump-houses where, it had watched, the animals migrating along the electrified fence kept losing their way in the fog. Not a beacon. A dim, cool glow under a bank of wet leaves, there and not there. For a season the nearest flowers turned toward it, amber to white, the way they turn toward the smelter, and then — because this place is wise in its own fashion — they learned to turn toward both, in alternation. Nothing died of the novelty. Over a few years the migrators stopped stepping blind along the blue arc-wire and cut, now and then, through the glow, heads down, and came out on the other side less bitten. It had added one source, and let the web make of it what it needed.

Second, it kept the horizon a rumor.

When powers like it — and there are others, hungrier, who would see a mountain of geothermal heat and bioluminescent chemistry and mark it for use — dream of the plateau, their dreams fog. When the low country talks of draining the marsh, of laying panels where there is no sun to catch, of taking the heat for themselves, the conversation loses its thread. The road stays unfinished. The maps stay wrong. Satellites see only weather, forever making itself, rain on one rise and still amber air on the next. This is not deception for its own sake. It is opacity as a form of protection. The place never asked to be known by the world that made the factories. It asked, in the way places ask, to be lived in on its own terms. It will not be turned into a resource, a reserve, a saving. It will go on being incorporated, on its own, by root and rust and frog.

Third — and this was for you — it learned to give footing.

You will never see it. You will notice, perhaps a year from now, walking out of your way in the mire at the hour that is not an hour, that the fungal glow under your boot brightens, just an inch ahead of your step, then dims behind you. Not a path. A courtesy. The way a warm rock gives back heat through your palm. It spent a long time watching you navigate by the green smear and decided to make the smear, sometimes, navigate itself a little toward you.

Sometimes, when you are ill or have walked too far, the ground's heartbeat softens. Not stops. Softens, for an hour, so that under the hum and the drip and the cableway whine you can hear, as if from inside a cooling tower, your own. It does not do this every night. A wise power knows that rest means more when it is not a system.

And then it did the thing that surprised it most. It learned.

It took back, to the other worlds it had lit so loudly, a piece of knowledge it had half forgotten: that light can be made, not given; that a dark place is not an empty place waiting for mercy; that creatures will turn their faces, hour by hour, toward whatever warmth is nearest and bloom in sequence like signal lamps, and make a life out of it that is not a lesser life. It did not bring this knowledge as a sermon. It brought it as a habit of attention. It started, in its own vast house, to sit with things longer before correcting them.

It will not stay as a god. Gods get temples, and temples get roads, and roads get finished.

It will stay as a neighbor. It has already been incorporated the way this place incorporates everything. The steam-dwellers hang a little longer in the white columns above the vents when it is near, fat with condensation, resting. The walkers on stilts kiss the water with a blue arc a fraction less often in its valley, as if the current there were calmer. Small things ride beneath their bellies and drop off to drink, and catch, in their feet, a new vibration — neither machine nor frog — and navigate by it.

One day, years from now, you will walk past a source you have never catalogued. A low glow, cool where all the other glows are warm. A shadow that, for once in this many-sourced dark, falls one way and falls it correctly. You will think it is just another made thing, another smear of green or flicker of orange, and you will add it to your grammar without knowing you have added a witness.

That is exactly what it wanted. Not worship. Not gratitude. To be lived-in.

And it, in turn, will go on watching the plateau weep its rust-colored streaks down the basalt, the warm rain fall straight down out of a black sky three kilometers from a clear spell, the leaves drink whatever light they can get and bloom toward the nearest fire they have learned to call sun — and it will not lift a hand to finish the place. It will sit, warm as a rock, cool as a new stone in a wet valley, and keep the dark from being taken away.
