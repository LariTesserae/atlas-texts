# The Second Sound

They noticed Varkesh the way everything that is patient is noticed: not because it called out, but because it kept arriving a second time.

A power that has watched places for a very long time learns to tell them apart by how they end. Most places erode. They hurry, they weather, they are scoured clean and started again and called new. Their mistakes are washed away, which is a mercy and also a loss, because no one has to live with them long enough to learn what they are for.

Varkesh does not erode. It accrues. A bottle stays clean on wet stone for years. A street laid wrong stays wrong for decades, grass growing up through it, people walking around it. A wrench dropped in the slag field is slowly skinned over in new rock, a millimeter at a time, and becomes a fossil while the foundry is still working. The city knows this, casts its enormous anchor plates for a geology too young to receive them, and keeps casting anyway.

That knowledge, held without illusion and without stopping, is what drew their attention. Not the height, not the damp, not the white sea of cloud below the plateau. The willingness to make permanence for a place that has none yet, while knowing it will not arrive in their lifetimes, and to do the work politely anyway.

They could have done a great deal. That is the portion of the story that must be understood first: they could have done a great deal, and almost all of it would have sounded like kindness.

They could have dried the wool. The people of Varkesh are damp before they have done anything to deserve it — hair damp, fingertips softened, breath showing indoors, cuffs carrying the condensation nets home with them. A dry corner, a true sun, a wind that finished what it started: it would have been easy, and it would have killed the moss forests inches high on the rooftops, and the ferns in the drainpipes, and the lichen painting the fresh slabs rust and gold and blue-green. The damp is not a problem that happens to be there. It is the condition that lets everything else live without being cleared.

They could have smoothed the mountain. Stopped the slow uplift, given it river valleys, rounded the corners of the basalt and granite, laid a road up to it where there is deliberately no road. But the mountain is still emerging. That is its chief activity. The anchor plates and brake-wheels in the yards are not failed engineering; they are a conversation with a thing still being born. To pin it early would have been to finish it, and finishing, in Varkesh, is a kind of violence.

They could have straightened the street. Taken down the scaffolding that has stood for twenty years over inhabited brick, that trellis and pigeon house and storage cage at once. Evicted the ravens from the active cranes instead of paying the raven tax, a bearing a week, without resentment. Fenced the goats out of the factory yards. Drained a condensation tank and given the small blind fish in it a proper pool, instead of letting them circle palely for years, feeling the flywheel through the steel, knowing their tank the way a neighbor knows a bell. Emptied the pools where the frogs sing their loud wet chorus at night. Fixed the compasses so north stopped wandering near the foundries and became a fact.

Every one of those mercies would have unmade something true. The wrong street is now a landmark and a place for the fox to turn at noon, which it has always done, and people step aside for it not from fear but from respect for schedule. The scaffolding is a home. The ravens are a tax the city has agreed to pay because it has agreed to live with them. The fish have a tank, which is to say they have an address. The compass does not wander because north is broken; it wanders because in Varkesh north is still under negotiation, and a wise power does not settle negotiations for other people.

So they did not dry it, or smooth it, or finish it, or answer quickly.

That last one was the hardest, and the most Varkesh of all.

In Varkesh, things happen twice, with a delay. A boiler sighs below and sighs again from above, where there is nothing to sigh from. A bell is struck two districts away and its echo answers before the rope is pulled a second time. Children learn before language, almost, to wait for the second sound before they respond. Politeness there is not speed. It is a willingness to let a thing happen twice before you answer it, so that your answer too will have its echo, and be heard properly.

The power heard Varkesh — the whole plateau, clicking upward a centimeter out in the emptiness, the flywheels turning once a minute, felt through the wet stone in the feet of people who were not hurrying — and they did not answer on the first sound.

They waited.

Waiting, for them, was not an absence. It was the work itself. They stayed in the interval. Between one district and the next there are two hundred meters where the factories stop and the lamps stop and there is only fog and wet rock and the sound of your own boots, and a rope handline strung between lamps to walk by touch. That emptiness is part of the plan, if plan is the word. The power took its place there, not above it, not over it. Not as a majority. As another inhabitant, and a minority one at that: damp, before they had done anything to deserve it; patient, before they had earned it; listening for the second sound before the first.

What they did, then, was mostly to keep count, in the way Varkesh keeps count. They counted the lamps, including the one that is not on any map, burning in empty space above a void, lighting nothing. They did not extinguish it for being useless, and they did not give it a purpose. A light that lights nothing is still a light, and a walker who finds it knows the interval is not as empty as it looks. The power understood that, and let it burn one more night, and one more.

They paid the raven tax without being asked, bearing by bearing, so that no crane minder would ever have to choose between the machine and the nest built around the machine. They stepped aside at noon for the fox, and did not ask it to cross at a more convenient hour. They let the swifts pour from the vent stacks at dusk in black curtains and take the place of bells, though bells are also rung, out of time with each other across the plateau. They walked through the weather around the lamps with their mouths closed, the way people do, and did not brush the moths out of their hair until later, because there is nowhere dry to put them.

They did not drain a tank. They did not plant a garden where the apples grow sour and slow and never quite ripe, tucked between casting sheds. They did not tear the moss lace from a door shut for a year. People open it anyway, feeling the damp stone give a little under the shoulder, and the moss begins again the following week as if the tearing had been a kind of pruning. The power learned to call it pruning, too, and not destruction.

And they remembered. Nothing erodes in Varkesh except memory. The conversation goes out thick in the cool air and comes back from the blank slab across the street, and then it is gone, unless someone holds it. The power held it. First sound and second. The bell and its echo. The boiler and its answer from above. The founder's name and the mistake in the street his great-grandson still walks around. The lunch tin in the slag heap, becoming a fossil in real time. They accreted these the way moss accretes, lawn to meadow to a forest a few inches high, and did not try to finish any of them into a story with an ending.

Once, a child on a rope line between districts — hand over hand, counting lamps, shadow falling twice at midday, once from the pale smear of sun above and once upward from the cloud sea below — asked a question that had no answer yet. In any other place, a power like that would have answered at once, largely, finally. Here they waited. The child waited. The fog waited. Then the second sound came, not as an answer from above, but as the child's own voice returning from the slab, and the question arriving again, a little steadier, the way everything in Varkesh arrives the second time. That was the answer they gave: to let it happen twice, and to be there for both.

They did not raise the mountain and they did not stop it. They let it rise a centimeter and heard the tiny deep click in the stone and acknowledged it, politely, a moment later, the way the frogs answer the boiler and the boiler answers the frogs a second after.

If you stand still long enough in Varkesh, the inhabitants will come to you. They are not shy. They do not hide. They ignore the distinction between inside and outside, between tended and untended, between people and everything that has decided to live alongside them.

Stand still longer, and the power will come too. It will not arrive on a cable car, though the wire sings. It will not bring a road. It will sit down in the fog between districts at the height of the rope, a little damp, a little moss beginning — metaphorically, and perhaps not only metaphorically — on the shoulder where the stairwell plant has leaned too close, and it will wait for the second sound before it speaks to you.

That is what they did, which for a power that could do anything was almost nothing. They stayed. They let it happen twice. They accrued.

Varkesh does not finish its lives, and it does not fall down around them. The power did not finish them either.
