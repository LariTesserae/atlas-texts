It heard it before it saw it, too.

Not as the visitor did, in a day's hearing, but over a span in which a day would not register. It had heard louder things — stars cooling, seas of fire, the brief shouting of empires — and it had come to this low, wet flat because, among all that noise, this roar had a pattern that would not resolve into wind or rain or machinery. A hollow booming underneath a constant ticking. A field tuning itself, slowly, downwards. It came, as benevolent powers do, expecting either a distress or an opportunity to do good.

It found neither.

Basin after basin to the dim horizon: no green, no moss, no biofilm, no animal in the open that could be counted on hands. No cry. No signal. No one asking to be warmed, lit, gathered, or finished. The sterile, bright water was not a failure of life. It was the condition of it. The upwelling of dissolved rock kept the pools clear because clarity, cold, and stone were precisely what the tenants required.

That was its first and hardest lesson, and it arrived without words: here, benevolence would not look like improvement.

It could have done a great deal. It had done such things elsewhere. It could have brought a sun disc and made shadows, and evaporated the resident mist into weather, and given the place a day instead of a sourceless wash. It could have seeded the wet stone with spores, softened the faulted slabs with soil, warmed the perched pools until something green could hold them. It could have given the blind tenants something to gather around, and somewhere to go more quickly than a fragment carried up a fracture in a climbing bead.

It understood, while standing at the edge of the first basin, that every one of those gifts would have been a flood. Light would have broken the closed loop of condensing and seeping and dripping. Warmth would have raised the tones instead of letting them sink. Green would have fouled the water the ribbons hung out to filter. Speed would have been unintelligible here, and therefore unkind.

It could have finished the stone thoughts. That was perhaps the most tempting act, because it was so small and so close to the scale of the place itself. A ring of slabs, incomplete, its interior clean of silt. A wall of twenty steps, straight, then stopping. A squared block in a pool, pocked on top by repeated touch. A finger's breadth of pressure, sustained over a century, would close the circle. It could have done it without a sound.

It did not. An unfinished circle is still a thought, honestly arrived at by a body that presses against its own wall until it dies in the middle of pushing. To complete it would not have been to help the tenant think. It would have been to think in its place, in its only material, and leave it unable to tell where its memory ended and another's began.

So, if it did anything, it did it by undoing itself.

It dimmed. A power that arrives bright casts a shadow, and even in this diffuse light a shadow would fall where it should not — across a perched pool that has no inlet, across a living tip holding its film of water against the air. It learned to be grey and sourceless, like the ceiling, so that nothing in the field had to account for its presence. It stopped walking, in the sense that matters here, and let the sound get louder until the roar separated into its parts: a drip striking a hollow, a bead letting go pillar by pillar, a whole town of independent clocks, each governed by a water level rising a millimetre in a month.

It waited for the ribbon, and when the ribbon did not come for it — when, a minute at a time, in a deeper pool, a pale translucent length uncurled from a mouth, filtered, and withdrew — it did not wait the less. It waited a year. It waited a decade. A minute here is an event, and a wise power must learn to expand its attention until a minute is not nothing. It learned to watch for rings expanding a second after a drip, for a surface that was not only water tensing under a blow, for a pool turning over beneath itself with no drip to cause it, and to call those things by no name more certain than the place allowed.

It took no sample. It did not cut a vesicle, taste a pool, or lift the water from a tube to learn what the tenant held there, because to take water is to lower a level and raise a note, to falsify an age. It did not mark the occupied columns from the dead shells, though it could tell them apart by the way the sound returned to them. Most houses were empty. Most stones sang because they were wet, not because they were inhabited. To record them in an archive, to make them interesting, would have been to draw other attention — hurried, bright attention — to a place whose safety depends on being overlooked. So it folded the mist a little thicker over the fracture passes, and made the basins seem, from any distance that matters, unremarkable. Guarding, here, meant obscuring.

It sat, if sitting is the word, at the rim of a basin like a low ridge that had not been there before and was not from the faulting. It did not enter a tube, though empties stood waiting and it could have filled one without displacing a tenant and learned from the inside how a tone lowers as a body fills its own shell. That would have been empathy, and it would also have been occupation. It chose the ridge instead. From there it could hear the field tuning itself a fraction lower, season by season, without adding a single drip of its own agua to the chorus.

In the end — and an end, here, is only a decision to have stayed long enough — it had not rescued anything, because nothing was in peril. It had not introduced anything, because the loop was already closed and sufficient. It had not been seen by the one being whose attention it had come to offer. No ribbon hung out for it. No wall was pushed straighter in its honour. The pocked block kept its repeated marks, which were not writing and did not need to be read.

That, it understood, was not a failure of the encounter. To be benevolent without being known, to be wise without being answered, was the only form of attention this place could survive. The being inside the stone does not need a witness to be complete. It needs not to be disturbed in the act of holding its water.

So it stays a little longer, dim and still, tuning itself down at the same unhurried rate as the columns, until the note has sunk another fraction and the field is fuller by a hair in a tube that was empty before. Then it will move back the way it came, stepping through the shallow sheet so that the water parts and closes behind it without clouding, and return in another decade to find the place exactly as it would have been had it never come.

That is what it does. Nothing, carefully, for a very long time.
