**What They Did**

The attention did not arrive the way a benevolent power is supposed to arrive.

There was no descent, no voice, no sign burned into the sky for the lowlands to read. It came the way you come to the edge of the High Thatch: from below, where the wind rises thin and hot off the bare stone, and then stops, as if it has arrived somewhere. For a while it stood there looking at what looked empty.

It thought, first, to help.

That was its first mistake, and the most forgivable one. From the edge, the plateau is a blond, sun-cured steppe, a day's walk without a tree, a people so sparse you might walk two days and find only a cairn and a ghost-hut. A benevolent power sees sparseness and thinks of lack. It sees heat and thinks of thirst. It sees the few living huts, the blue-smoking fire struggling in the thin air, the gourds measured out, and it thinks: I could bring water. I could bring fire that holds. I could bring metal, and more shade, and more people, and make this place give more.

It walked in.

Underfoot, the emptiness revised itself, as it does for everyone. This was not a place with a being in it. It *was* the being — the mat, thick and breathing, holding clay and stone in fiber, healing behind every step. The people were not its owners or its patients. They were tenants, like the birds in the forks of the giants, like the tiny grazers on a grazer's back-moss. The power understood, slowly, in the tempo of the place, that it was not being asked to improve a home. It was being allowed to witness a body.

So it stayed, and followed the wet band for a year without touching it.

It learned that water here is not climate but behavior. At dawn it put out a cupped hand like the people do, licked the beading, and did not drink from the frog-stones. It learned why by watching: where the frogs sing, the water will last; to drink there is to take tomorrow. It could have called a storm into the center of the plateau, and did not. Most rain here is virga and mist and steam off the hot thatch anyway. A forced fall would have made the runners lift for a day and taught the grazers and the walkers to turn toward a greening that could not be trusted next season. The mat already thickens in anticipation, root by root. Power does not need to teach anticipation.

It learned that the giants are alone on purpose. A day's walk, three or four of them, half a mile apart, each a room of shade where predators sleep at noon while the open mat hums with heat. It could have seeded a forest. It saw instead the birds launching by the hundreds, the hollow stalk sweating condensation in the dark, the water stored sweet inside. Crowd them and the shade would close over the mat, the heat and damp beneath would change, the runners would curl and latch differently. Solitude here is not waste. It is architecture.

It learned that the people are sparse on purpose, too. It watched a family sit through the insect choir at dusk, mouths closed, patient, because shouting over that sound would waste breath and water. It watched them clip greens and fiber and let their fence rot back into the thatch by design. It could have given them a stronger fire, a stone adze of metal, a way to hold the terraces open against the runners. It saw the pale line of their path, the only bare place on the plateau, and understood: if they tried to tame this, they would be outgrown in a season, and in trying they would lose the knowledge that keeps them alive. Their sparseness was not suffering. It was fit.

It learned to leave the walkers alone, lifting one root-leg for an hour among the grazing front, a tax on carelessness the herds already knew how to pay. It learned to leave the six-legged grazers alone, their backs a meadow with its own hunters no bigger than beetles. It did not protect the eaten from the eater. The balance was old, and complete.

A wise power, seeing this, begins to understand that benevolence is not abundance. It is right measure, and the courage not to add.

What it did, then, was mostly at the edge.

The High Thatch has always turned its back on the lowlands. The wind stops there. The weather stays inside. The power extended that turning-back to ideas. It did not build a wall. It made the crossing unrewarding in the gentlest possible way: the heat shimmer a little longer, the steps a little more easily healed behind you, the maps a little less certain, the thirst for owning this particular green a little less urgent than other, more ordinary thirsts elsewhere. No one was punished. No one was turned away with thunder. People who came with intentions — to cut the giant stems for their stored water, to dig channels for the dew, to drink the stone pockets dry, to harvest the mat at a scale fiber could not knit back — simply found themselves tired, distracted, called home by a need that felt their own. They forgot this place, as you forget a road you never quite took. That forgetting is the power's one permanent act, and it is still in effect.

Inside, it did almost nothing. It stepped as the grazers step, slow and in a broad front. It sat in a giant's shadow at noon and let its coat cool the air by a degree no instrument here would ever name. Its footsteps healed at the usual rate. It spoke no word during the dusk choir, because on the High Thatch that is how you join in: by listening. Everything calls at once, and everything is listening. It listened.

Once — only once — its heart outran its wisdom.

In a year when the wet band stalled at the center and the frogs in their black stone pockets went silent at noon, it let the night air condense a little extra on the undersides of the giant leaves. Not rain. Only dew. The slow tick-tock lasted to a second dawn. The people came out with their woven scoops and collected enough. The frogs sang a second night. The back-moss greened.

The next dry year, the band stalled longer, and the stones were silent longer, and the power sat in the shade and did nothing but listen. That was harder. It understood then that mercy, repeated, becomes weather — and false weather is worse than a dry year the mat has learned to survive by going underground, by moving a meter in a season, by waiting in fiber for the next brief fall. It had given one night's dew as love, not as policy, and it would not make a policy of love. That, too, is a kind of benevolence, though it does not feel kind while you are doing it.

When the year was complete, it left the way it had come: without a sign the lowlands could read. It left no hut, no fire, no tool, no word for the people to follow. If it left anything inside, it was an unmarked cairn of smoothed stones, indistinguishable from the old ones, which the runners began at once to half-swallow.

It did not tell anyone where the High Thatch is. Not out of superiority. Out of safekeeping. A wise power knows that to name a beloved place to the hungry world is already to endanger it. So it keeps the plateau the way the frogs keep their stones: in memory, in place, unvisited unless necessary, unspoken unless asked by someone who already knows not to drink there.

And wherever it is now, at dusk, when the heat breaks on some other world and the insects begin — first one, then a thousand — it pauses, mouth closed, and listens. Because that is what it taught itself there: on the High Thatch, and perhaps everywhere, love does not arrive to fix the living floor. It arrives, it learns to step where the step heals, it stands guard at the edge where the wind stops, and it leaves the green to turn, audibly, all at once, on its own time.
