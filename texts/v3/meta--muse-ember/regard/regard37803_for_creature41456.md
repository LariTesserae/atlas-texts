They noticed it because it was doing something that, in their experience, requires someone to be watching.

A continent built inch by inch, by a being that never stands up, never walks, never asks. No substrate under it but itself. No voice in it but wind through a thin green front. Everything behind that front already dead and becoming ground, everything ahead of it still dry and waiting to be made damp enough to begin.

A benevolent power could have done a great deal, at once. That was the first thing they understood about themselves: that they could.

They could have brought water that stays. Not the brief rain, not the hour of dew, but a river, a spring, a seam that never baked shut at noon. They could have brought soil. They could have brought company — things that fly to the small beige flowers, things that walk and graze and nest and call, to fill the silence without voices. They could have softened the heat that is too hot to touch at midday, or lifted the plant up, given it a trunk, a height, a way to grow toward light as living things are supposed to. They could have sped it, so that in one human lifetime the patches met and the plain was finished. They could have carried a single papery seed, hundreds of meters, and set it down precisely in a hollow that would stay damp for three days.

All of it was within their power. None of it, on looking longer, was a gift.

The place had rules, and they held everywhere, and they were not problems to be solved.

Nothing grows except where a surface has already been made to hold water. So to give it water from elsewhere would be to deny it the very work it is doing — trapping dust grain by grain into its shallow basket, holding dew an hour longer, thickening the mat a fraction where it stands so that the next rosette can live a little further in.

Nothing is made except by a living plant at its edge. So to build for it, or to build it quickly, would make the builder unnecessary. The world behind the front is made of its own dead stems, paler, locked in, ticking faintly as they cool at night. That dead work is not waste. It is memory, and ground, and the thing that holds the damp. To replace it would be to erase what it has already lived.

It does not grow up, because there is nowhere to grow up to. It grows sideways because sideways is where water remains. To raise it would be to put it in the wind and the heat, away from the pockets and seam-gaps where it actually drinks at dawn. Its leaves close a fraction at noon and wait. They are not wilting. They are not suffering. Heat is not its enemy. Heat is the medium in which all of it is cured.

Its flowers have no nectar and no scent you can catch because there is nothing here to visit them, and the wind takes the pollen straight off. To bring pollinators would not complete the flowers. It would change them into something else, something that needed to be visited. Its seeds are made to be mostly wasted — to bake on finished mat, to blow again, to fail unless they land by rare chance in a rare seam. That waste is not failure. It is how a rootless, footless being travels. To carry one seed to safety would be kindness in our terms. In its terms, it would be a straight line, a tool-mark, on a surface that has never had one.

And it is not lonely, though we who looked could not help feeling the loneliness of it. It does not experience the silence as silence. It does not wait for a voice, or for a parent to find a single rosette alone in a hollow, or for an animal to eat what it leaves behind. Nothing eats the dead material except sun and wind and the next plant, and that is not a tragedy in its life. It is its life. A wise power has to learn not to relieve its own feelings by rearranging another being.

There was no one to rescue. No one was dying that was not also, in dying, becoming ground for what followed. No one was praying. The front advanced a centimeter in the dark after rain, or it did not advance for a dry year longer than its stems could span, and faded to grey, and waited. Both were its nature.

So they did the hardest thing their power allowed them to do. They kept their weight off the mat.

Not stepped on it — how could they? A footstep would crush the warp ends sticking up between the living stems, would polish and break what sun and wind had only just baked. Not cast a shadow over it for long. Not name it aloud, for a name would be the first straight edge drawn across a place that has none. They learned, instead, how to kneel without heat, how to attend without touching.

If anything, they gave it continuity. They kept the sun ordinary and the sky blue and bleaching. They kept the rain intermittent and the dryness the default, the damp a pocket, a seam, an hour at dawn. They kept the wind blowing between the standing rain band and the bright place, carrying only dust and the smell of hot straw. They kept other curious powers, others like themselves, from coming to improve it. That was their one active benevolence: a boundary in their own minds. No one steps here, especially us.

And they stayed.

Staying is not nothing. For a power that has seen worlds finish quickly, worlds that burned and bloomed and were eaten in a season, to stay and watch a front move a meter in a lifetime, or ten, was an education. They counted the rosettes. They remembered which seam had stayed damp for three days and which had baked shut at noon. They listened at night to the panels expanding and contracting, to the dry stems ticking against the live ones. At dawn they saw the dew bead on the thumbnail leaves and drip, one by one, into the pockets, and they did not add to the dew.

They left, in the end, not unchanged, but unhelpful — which is to say, changed in the only way the place could permit. They became slower. More sideways. More content to live only at the edge of a world they were making, with the world behind them already dead and the world ahead of them not yet damp enough to begin.

The most benevolent thing they could do was to let the horizon go on not ending.
