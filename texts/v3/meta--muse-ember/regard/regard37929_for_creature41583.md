It came at first light, when they were inside the walls.

You would not have seen it come. It did not descend, it did not shine, it did not still the wind off the plain or thin the mist that hangs knee-high. It entered the way everything else in the town enters: a step onto the roof of the house below, barefoot, in ankle-deep run-off, its feet already cold. It made itself small enough to cross a roof without cracking a neighbour's ceiling, and dim enough to be seen in that bright, colourless, varnished light. It wore nothing that had to be dried later, and it stood in a lane, and listened.

For a week it only watched. It watched the wringing out of cloths in the inside troughs. It watched a child sent to listen at a pipe junction, ear to the wall, to hear whether it was running. It watched the blind thumb-sized fish in the cistern being fed crumbs and drowned moths, and a child on rota sitting with feet dangling to watch them turn. It watched an eel taken only when a drain ran clear, and shared between the houses whose pipes it had used. It watched the splinter of rock at the highest corner given up to children because nothing would sit square on it. It watched, at night, the soft counting go across the plates as they contracted.

A benevolent power, a wise one, soon understands what it could do. It could dry the rock. It could stop the weeping at dawn and the seep at dusk. It could lift the table a few metres higher, so the tide no longer undermined a corner. It could bring soil in its hands, and with soil, trees, and with trees, birds to nest in them, and insects to breed in the shade, and a loud market in a green yard. It could make the houses stand square on the newest splinter. It could give them words for things they have never needed.

It almost did, on the third night, when it heard the ticking and mistook it for pain. Its hand rose without its willing it.

Then it lowered its hand again, and began to understand.

These are not people waiting to be saved out of the place. The place and the people who live in it are one continuous act of maintenance. A house is not built once and inherited; it is continued, lifted, propped, re-faced, re-roofed, built over. A family is not a tree with roots elsewhere; it is stacked through itself, parents down, children up, a door opening onto a roof that belongs to both and neither. To dry them would be to end the work that is their life. To give them a tree would be to give a child who has no word for tree a wound for a gift. Their language is rich precisely where it needs to be: words for a drip that will hold, for a drip that will cut, for the hour when the waterfalls off the edge roar and then are gone. A wise power does not overwrite a precise language with a louder one.

So it did not fix the table. It kept company in the work.

On the eighth morning a child who was too small to wring properly handed it a cloth. The child did not ask who it was. She showed it how to wipe the condensation down the inside wall without splashing the floor, how to wring it into the bucket so nothing was lost, how to clear the pipe with a bone needle if the trickle clogged. The power learned to speak low, because the thick wet walls swallow sound and loudness has never been needed here. When it tried to thank her in its own voice, the wall took the sound and the child shook her head gently and put her ear to the pipe again, to listen for whether it was running.

It learned the small tasks. It held ladders steady for old women. It carried buckets to the cistern on a pulley when a woman's hands were full of cloths and reed. It took its portion of eel broth, no more than the houses whose drains it had cleaned. It sat a morning on the cistern rim watching the pale blind fish, not as superstition but as an instrument — the only one always in the water — and learned that if a fish died, you did not drink until the pipes had been cleared and the water had run clear. It learned to pick weeds from the north roofs sparingly, handfuls at a time, and to step around the salt-tufts in the lanes, because a lane is a crack and a gutter and a boundary, and a tuft is a boundary of its own.

At night it learned the counting. It pressed its ear to the rock with the children on the splinter, and waited to hear where the tick would come next, plate by plate, as the slab settled by a fraction. A new tick in the morning meant a new crack in a lane by evening. That crack would be somebody's work before dark, and that was as it should be. The power did not stop the settling. It counted with them.

There was one rule it honoured above all, because it was unspoken and strict: a house left empty will weep itself to ruin in a season. In the winter the oldest pair in the lowest dry room died, and their children's children were not yet ready to climb up into their rooms. The power took no house for itself — it never built, never claimed a roof, never whitewashed a wall that was not already whitewashed. But every morning that winter it stepped down from the roof above, wiped the trough along the lowest wall, wrung the cloth into a bucket, cleared the pipe, and carried the water to the cistern. It kept the empty house from being empty until a family could continue it. No one saw it. A house that is kept from ruining does not need to be seen to be kept.

It has stayed a long time, in the way such things stay. Generations have been born into damp rooms kept warm by thickness and closeness, and have grown strong in the calves from ladders, and have taught their children to listen. Each generation has taught the power again, because each generation wipes and listens and climbs as if for the first time. Houses are continued. So is it.

It has never brought colour, or soil, or birdsong. It has never preached. It has never said its name, if it has one, because names here are for cracks and kinds of seep and work to be done, not for powers. What it has brought is harder to see and easier to feel: that no one carries the work entirely alone, that a bucket arrives a little sooner, that a ladder is held, that an ear is lent to a wall a little longer than any human ear can bear.

Stand by the cistern at midday, when the morning condensation has stopped and the town has gone quiet except for water moving in the cracks. The blind fish are turning slowly in the dark below the rim. The children are on their splinter of rock watching the wet, shining plain go flat to the sea. The roofs stretch wall to wall, weighted, moss-bearded on the north side, sending up thin curls of driftwood smoke to dissolve at once.

If you listen longer than the town itself listens — longer than a rota, longer than a morning — you may hear it: one more cloth wrung out in a trough, one more bucket clunking against stone, one more careful hand along a wall feeling for a drip. Steady. Low. Unnecessary, and therefore exactly what was needed.

They live by catching what the rock gives off and not letting any of it go. It has learned to do the same.
