It does not come through the pipe.

That is the first thing it decides, and the hardest.

Attention is not arrival. It stays outside, where the wind is thin and clouds pass below the windows, and it listens. It has no need to duck under the riveted iron, no need to put a hand on the slick rail, no need to be buoyed into the green glass sea to know what is there. It has watched long enough, from that height, to learn the circuit before it judges the room.

It counts the thump. Seven seconds. Again. Again. It watches the morning surge turn the whole sea over, watches the sunset gather on the cold glass and fall as warm rain, watches the lamps flicker when the turbine labours and the eels gather to the vibration. It watches a leaf fall from the inverted forest, ride three circuits, settle in a grate, and become a fern. It watches the turtle surface off a shoulder, exhale, and be gone between the three islands, old as any machinery and more settled than any of it.

And it understands, slowly, as wise things do, that this is not a ruin.

A ruin is something that has stopped working. A rescue is something that has stopped meaning. This place has finished, in the way a riverbed finishes — worn smooth by use, still running. Everything here is made, and everything made is being reused. The plants eat the iron. The iron feeds the plants. The fish ride the current the machines make. The machines turn because the water is warm and the mountain is high and the inflow still comes, and no one maintains any of it because the eating and excreting and dying in place are the maintenance. It is not broken. It is complete in the only way a living circle can be complete: nothing leaves, nothing is wasted, nothing is in charge.

That completeness is a temptation to almost any power.

The heart wants to fix the dome.

Close the missing panes. Give them a proper sky. Stop the wind from cutting through and the rain from falling inside-out, stop the chicks from having to learn to fly in a perfect, endless lap, stop the vines from forever descending toward a waterline they may never reach. The heart wants to warm their winter, cool their summer, give the pale inverted forest real soil to root in instead of hot air to drink.

But the rain inside is their season. The broken panes are their door for the only ones who can leave and choose to return. The pale leaves are pale because they have learned to drink steam, and that is not a deficiency — it is a whole way of being a plant that no hillside plant has ever needed. To close the dome would be to give them weather from outside and take away the weather they made for themselves. It would be kind, and it would end them, gently, by making them something else.

The heart wants to free them.

Open the lower pipe. Let the carp-like fish down into the cold dark river they came from, let the eels run out, let the turtle — that slow, warm moving rock — find a true bank to bask on, a true shore. The heart sees a circle and thinks: prison. It sees sixty paces of wall and thinks: trap.

But down is not a river to them anymore. Down is pressure and cold and a forgetting of the warm circuit they have ridden for generations. The fish are not prisoners of the current; they *are* the current, citizens of it, spawning in pipework shallows and carrying their young a little farther round each time. The turtle does not need to bask — the air above her is already summer, and she knows every submerged stair by feel. To free them would be to apply a human map to a world that has no straight lines. Some things arrive by accident and stay because staying is how they live. Freedom, given too late, is just another name for a filter they will not survive.

The heart wants to keep them forever.

Restart the turbine when it labours. Clear the blockage. Hold the inflow when the mountain is dry. Keep the lamps buzzing, keep the water turning, keep the bubble of lowland summer warm at the edge of the sky for ever, so the duckweed never stops patching the still corners and the swallows never lose their warm pocket. Benevolence, in its most human form, is preservation.

Wisdom refuses that, too. If the turbine stops one day — and everything made, however settled, will one day be smooth enough to be called stone — then the rotation will stop, the water will settle, the oldest things here will become geology. That is not a failure. That is the circuit completing itself. To prolong it artificially would be to turn a living world into a display, to keep the heartbeat going after the body has chosen, over a patience longer than any animal's, to rest. A wise power does not love its favourite place so much that it will not let it finish.

So it does none of those things.

It does not enter, because entering would make it the largest machine in the room. It would become a sun, and then everything would lean toward it. It would become rain, and then everything would be waiting for it. It would become a centre, and a circle with a centre is no longer a circuit. The most benevolent thing it can do is remain outside the drum, where its loudness does not add to the loudness.

What it does instead is small, marginal, and at the edges — the only place a wise hand should touch a wild thing.

It keeps the place forgotten. That is its chief work. Already the entrances are underwater and the windows are at the top of the world; it adds a little fog to the pipe, a little wrongness to the compass, a little slip to the memory of any human who has climbed the mountain and dreamed of a warm room in the sky. Not malice. Just the understanding that the greatest danger to this place is not cold, or dryness, or the stopping turbine. It is attention of the wrong kind — the survey, the restoration, the power plant, the museum, the wonder. To be found by people would be to be given a purpose, and a purpose would break the circuit faster than rust ever could. So the power holds the forgetting, year after year, like a hand over a candle it does not want to blow out or let burn the house.

And at the edges, it nudges. Not the machinery — never the machinery. A cloud, held a little longer over the peak in a dry summer, so the inflow does not fail. A gust, turned a fraction, so a seed carried on the wind through a missing pane finds the catwalk trough instead of the cloud-sea. A lost swallow, blown too far, turned back toward the broken glass. A degree of warmth, kept in the bubble on a night when the wind shifts hard and the ferns would otherwise be scalded by cold. These are not miracles. They are the smallest possible mercies, applied where the circuit is already thin, without ever becoming a season of its own.

And it remembers.

That is the last thing, and perhaps the only thing that matters. A place like this keeps its memory in turtle shells tucked into a fallen chimney, in old swallow nests reused year after year, in leaves settled in grates, in paint surviving only where water and air cannot reach. It is a memory made of reuse. The power, from outside, becomes its outer memory. It holds the shape of the whole — the tiles and the aggregate, the three islands, the shoals crossing the centre and then the long green emptiness, the frogs on the gauges puffing in time to the thump — so that when the turbine finally slows and the sea no longer turns over, and the plants root faster and faster in the failing iron until the failing iron is all root, and the room goes quiet and begins, at last, to read as stone, it will not be as if it had never been.

It will not restart it then. It will not grieve it into continuing. It will sit, in its way, with the stone, and remember that it was once warm, and loud, and leafy, and entirely itself.

For now, it listens. Seven seconds. Again. Again. It does not answer with a beat of its own. It lets the thump be the only heartbeat in the room, lets the water push forward with intention, lets the turtle surface off a shoulder no one is watching, exhale, and be gone.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, if anything.

It does almost nothing, perfectly, and on purpose — and leaves the world in the pipe to go on turning, unaware of being loved, which is the only way it can go on being what it is.
