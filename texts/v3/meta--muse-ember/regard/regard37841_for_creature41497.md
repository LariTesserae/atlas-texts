It noticed Tawaq the way a very old thing notices a very finished thing.

It had passed over peaks before. It had watched points rise, sharpen, break, slump. It had watched rivers cut valleys and carry the tops away. It had watched towns be founded in strength and die of it, and towns be founded in weakness and die of it too.

Tawaq was different. Not a peak at all. A shoulder. A polished stone at the bottom of a river that had decided, over millions of years, to stop being moved.

It stopped above the dome because nothing there was asking to be improved, and it had learned — which is the whole of its wisdom — that this is usually the sign that a place is complete.

It looked for a long while, in the flat green-grey light, with no shadow to read by, and it saw not a deprivation but a household. People and goats and guinea pigs and hummingbirds and eaves-birds and thumbnail frogs and fungi and lichen and moss and tree ferns and the dead under the potatoes. All of them leaning. Houses on houses, vines balcony to balcony, terraces on ceilings. Water held inside leaves and jars and frogs and bodies. A wall that did not mark the edge of a kingdom, but the edge of soil.

A lesser power would have begun at once to fix it.

It considered, one by one, the gifts.

**Water.** A river, cut clean through the dome. A spring at the rim. A lake in a hollow. It set the thought down, gently, because it could see the consequences as clearly as if they were already happening in 3D. The soil, deep and black and ancient and held together by roots, would wash. The bromeliads, each with its perfect cup, would drown and burst. The frogs, whose entire lives — born, calling, mating, dying — are spent in a teaspoon held inside a plant, would be swept into a world too large and too fast for them. The dished lanes, the roofs that are funnels, the nets that comb the mist and drip it into clay jars with lids tied down with string — all of that ingenious, patient technology for living inside a cloud would become a joke overnight. Children taught before they can read not to spill, not to wash, not to tear a leaf, would learn thirst, then waste, then loss. The town does not lack water. It *is* water, kept.

**Iron.** A vein, or a gift of it from below. Nails instead of pegs. Hinges instead of wooden joints. Tools that do not need oiling with plant fat. It set that down too. Iron would make building faster. Faster would make the town want to expand. The town cannot expand. The wall is the wall and the soil is the soil, twenty minutes around. A house takes years because the stone must be gathered round, the timber cut high and strong, the shingles laid so moss will root and hold them. A terrace takes a lifetime for its wall to settle. Come too quickly into that and houses would be built to be replaced instead of built to be re-laid by grandchildren. The mountain settled first. The town settled in imitation. Hurry would un-settle it.

**Dryness.** A sun that stayed. A wind that dried the clothes and the lungs and the steps. Clean, sharp light, with shadows you could set a clock by. It let that thought pass, too. The people are not dirty because they cannot wash. They are damp because mist is in their lungs and dew is in their greens and salt is what keeps the damp from turning to rot. Their health is not an accident in spite of the wet; it is a practice inside it. Lichen makes the twelve steps slick, and everyone knows by foot where it is. To dry them would be to make them strangers in their own houses.

**Rescue.** Lifting the whole dome — wall, roofs, gardens, nets, cistern and all — down to the warm lowlands where there are rivers and roads and iron and dry beds. It considered that longest, because it is the gift benevolence most often insists on. It saw a people who are ordinary, tired, funny, busy, who carry a jar up twelve steps and get winded and pace themselves, who bury their dead shallow so the potatoes will be strong and plant a tree fern so the dead shade the living. Take them down and they would not be rescued. They would be unhoused. A town that is one continuous green mass with doors cut in it does not transplant. It strains, and tears at the vine-lines.

So it gave none of those things.

Then it considered giving nothing at all, not even attention. Forgetting Tawaq entirely and going on with its vast, old business. That, too, it rejected. Because attention is not the only thing a place like this can be harmed by. Below, in the valleys where clouds pool, the older forest is being cut thinner by hands that do not know Tawaq exists. The great cloud that sits on the summit does not sit by accident; it is caught by trees, by ferns, by nets and roofs and leaves. If the low forest thins enough, the mist will arrive later and leave earlier. The bell that does not tell time but tells the net-keepers the mist is thickening will have to be rung in a panic. A benevolence that only looks and then looks away would be a kind of abandonment.

It also considered asking them. Descending, speaking, offering a choice: *Stay as you are, or be changed.* It decided against that, too, and for the deepest reason. No one had asked. To present a choice between a complete life and an improved one is to make the complete life feel lacking. Wise power does not introduce lack where there is sufficiency. The people of Tawaq are not waiting. The frogs are not waiting. The moss is not waiting.

So what it did was smaller, and harder, and almost invisible — which is how you can tell it was done by something wise.

It shrank.

It contracted itself down to the scale of the dome, to a twenty-minute walk. It took its place not above the town, and not in the market hall where lamps are lit at noon, but at the rim, where the low wall is re-laid every spring with round river-stones and flat slate. It leaned there. A little extra weight against the outer side of the wall, where the soil ends and the forest falls a thousand meters away. Not holding the town up — the town holds itself up by leaning — but keeping the edge from being pushed, by weather, by outsiders, by well-meaning hands.

It held the lid.

Like a jar with its lid tied down with string, like a cloth wrung over a pole at night. Where the far winds try to strip the cloud, it lays a thin, damp film over the dome and keeps the mist a fraction thicker than it would be on its own. Not a miracle. Not rain. Just enough that the net-keepers climbing the shared stairs at evening find the poles already beaded, that the bell is rung at its usual slow hour, that the bromeliads fill their cups by morning. No one in Tawaq notices a change, because it has made no change. It has only prevented an unmaking.

It dulled the path.

The switchbacks up from the lowlands, the old worn steps cut into rock that is no longer sharp — it did not close them. If someone in Tawaq ever wants, truly wants, to go down to a river, to dry land, to a life that is not theirs, the path must be there and passable, and it will be. A wise keeper does not make a preserve out of people. But it made the path hard to find for those who come looking to help: for surveyors with maps, for traders with iron, for priests and doctors and tourists with cameras and clean intentions. To them the dome looks unremarkable from below, a shoulder in cloud. Compasses dampen. Satellites see only mist. The town, which is a whole city crammed into smallness, registers as a smudge, a green mass, nothing to name. Being seen by a power is dangerous enough. Being seen by everyone would be fatal. So it veiled them, not as a secret to be kept from them, but as a kindness kept *for* them.

And it remembered.

The ancestors who sealed the cistern under the market square have names that are gone. The town does not miss them in a way that needs fixing; it goes on, millimeter by millimeter, root thickening a mortar joint, a garden shifting a hand's breadth from one roof to the next. But the power, having looked, now holds those names anyway — without telling anyone, without writing them on a wall. When a roof-tender coaxes moss to root, when a child learns before reading not to tear a leaf, when a grandchild cleans a stone taken from a ruined house and re-lays it, it knows whose hand taught whose hand. It does not correct the record. It simply does not let it fall all the way out of the world. That is its benevolence toward the dead, who are still residents, shading the living.

That is all it did.

It did not become a king in Tawaq. It did not become a god in the market hall. It became one more household thing: a leaning thing. A damp thing. A weight on the wall. On the terraces above your head the goats go on bleating. Under the floor the guinea pigs go on squeaking. In the eaves the birds go on screaming awake on schedule. Hummingbirds go on scolding at orchids in doorframes. Frogs go on peeping, hundreds of thumbnail throats, loud enough to wake the street, in cups of dew that no river could replace. At dusk the lamps are blown out, the light thins to green twilight that could be noon or midnight, and the net-keepers go up and down the shared stairs tying down another lid, telling a child in passing — you do not spill, this leaf is where we drink — while something vast and old, which could have lifted the mountain, has chosen instead to sit in the mist with them and learn how not to fall.

That, if anything, is what a benevolent and wise power would do: not lift, but lean.
