It would not arrive.

That is the first thing a wise power understands about this place: attention is not arrival. It does not descend in light. The glare is already sufficient. It does not break the roar to announce itself. The roar is already full. It comes the way the falcon comes — high, once, and without landing — except it stays.

And having seen it, it would be tempted. Benevolence is always tempted. Wisdom is what it does with the temptation.

It would look at the white, hurting brightness and think: *I could soften this.* And it would not.

It would look at the roar that fills the jaw and sternum and makes a human voice useless, and think: *I could quiet this, so that something could be heard.* And it would not.

It would look at the ground — stone on stone on crystal on water, packed tight, no soil anywhere, nothing green at all — and think: *I could bring the green. I could bring root and stem and leaf, and make it live in the ordinary way.* And it would not, because it would understand, after looking long enough, that green here would not be life added. It would be life erased. The films in the damp pores did not fail to become plants. They succeeded at staying invisible under a light that comes from below as well as above, in water too cold, too mineral, too constantly washed for anything to stand up in. Their translucence is not a lack. It is an adaptation so complete it looks like nothing.

It would look at the flatworm — pale, translucent grey, no longer than a thumb, pressed to its stone, moving a centimeter and then stopping to scrape at the bacterial mat with a mouth you cannot see — and feel the oldest benevolent impulse of all: to lift it. To give it eyes for the sky. To give it ears for a quieter world. To help it stand up.

And it would set its hands — if it has hands — back down beside itself. Because to lift the flatworm would be to kill it. To raise it into the current would be to tear it from the only world it has ever read, which is not by sight or sound but by pressure through its belly, by the chemical taste of the film, by the throb of droplets on stone. It is not waiting to grow legs. It is not waiting to become something else. It has lived for years at the timescale of the stones, feeding and waiting, and its flatness is not prostration. It is contact.

It would look at the falcon and think: *It crossed and did not land. Should I call it down? Should I make this place worth landing in?* And it would not. The falcon is not a failed resident. It is the proof that the plateau is not sealed, that it is seen from elsewhere and passed over in the ordinary course of a larger life. To make the falcon land would be to make the plateau into a place for falcons. It already has its census: the film that makes the ground, the worm that eats the ground that is made, and the bird that leaves nothing at all.

A lesser power would call all of this unfinished. It would mistake equilibrium for stagnation, because it would measure life by standing, greenness, voice, and change. A wise power would learn to measure differently. It would kneel — and the roar would not lessen for its kneeling — and after a while it would stop trying to listen for variety in the wash of millions of droplets, and accept that this fullness is this place's silence.

So what would it do?

Almost nothing. And that almost-nothing would be the hardest, most active thing it had ever done.

It would keep the humans from finding it as a project. Not with a wall, not with a miracle. There is no need to hide what is already easy to miss if you are looking for something to improve. It would lean into the forgetfulness already built into the place: no path, no cairn, no soil to carry home, no footprint that the next hundred drips do not smooth. It would ensure that our curiosity — which is so often benevolence in disguise — does not become introduction. No dropped thing to polish into pavement. No seed in a boot tread. No well-meaning transplant.

That is its one intervention: not to add, but to withhold. To guard the closed, quiet circulation from being opened by hands that think wet stones weeping are a problem to solve.

And then it would stay. Not to watch over in the sense of hovering. To witness in the sense of attending, for a very long time, until witnessing itself becomes a form of protection. It would be there when the pools are at the same level in a hundred years, when the films are a little thicker in the same pores, when the worm is a few centimeters farther along the same stone, when the sky is crossed, once, by something pale that does not land.

It would not stand up over the plateau. It would lie flat beside it, in the only posture that does not disturb it.

Not because the plateau is broken and cannot be helped.

Because it is finished, and finished does not mean empty.
