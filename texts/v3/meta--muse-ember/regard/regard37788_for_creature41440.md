**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive.

Powers in stories arrive with light, or a voice, or a change in the weather. This one arrived the way attention arrives when you have been listening to something long enough that it begins, in a very quiet way, to listen back.

It had watched watersheds for longer than there had been names for them. It had watched forests be cleared in perfect straight lines and deserts be made by the removal of roots. It knew how to be concerned, and what concern usually did: it spoke to kings, it offered covenants, it moved people out of floodplains and built them higher ground with engines.

Tinta drew its attention for the opposite reason. Not because it was pure. Because it was fluent.

From the ridge the power did not see first a town. It saw a metabolism. It saw the bowl as a single animal with many small organs: the bathhouse and mill above, the settling pits, the granaries raised against damp, the aqueducts dripping at every joint, the felt under everything, thick with moss and root and rot, holding and being held. It saw that there was no heart to save or seize. No capital, no temple, no ruler who could sign. The upper drain people, the lower drain people, the weeping rock people — and under the floors the pigs warming the houses, and in the drains the frogs who could erase every voice in the bowl by dusk, and the ducks doing the pest work, and the children carrying between all of them.

It listened for a full cycle to learn its grammar. Dawn: everything beaded, wool and hair and leaf. Morning: the sluice keepers lashing joints, the miller listening for the grind under the waterwheel. Midday: the roar, the market partitions going up and down, baskets coming down the spiral steps, fish netted before the rain. Afternoon rain, hard and cold, cut off as if. Evening: rain stopped, mist at eye level, pigs sighing under the floor, frogs taking over the sound, a loom still going somewhere in the cool.

Nothing stayed dry.
Nothing stayed dead.
Nothing stayed in place.
Nothing was wasted in space.

The power understood, slowly, that this was not a place enduring a problem. It was a place that had solved a problem by refusing to be separate from it.

It was tempted, as wise things are tempted, to improve it. To dry it. To straighten the lanes so they did not become canals. To give them a road and a wire and a wall that did not lean. To cure the wet ponchos that never fully dried, the houses that leaned a degree more each year, the noise that made conversation impossible.

It considered it, and set it aside. That would not be rescue. That would be murder by improvement. The leaning was how a living pole grew. The wet was how the wall drank. The noise was the sound of a majority — frogs, water, animals, looms — in which the human beings were only one talkative species among many, and not the loudest.

It was tempted, second, to preserve it. To close the pass, to make it unfindable, to freeze it at its most beautiful moment and keep it from the world forever. It set that aside too. A beautiful thing kept forever, against its will, is a beautiful thing in a very small cage. The young woman who wanted to go over the pass to school would not be allowed to want that.

It was tempted, third, to lift it out — to intervene before the climate above it changed too much, before the springs beaded less. But the springs were already being answered for, in Tinta's own way, by the forest wall that closed the fourth side of the bowl.

So it decided, which for a power of its age was the hardest decision: nothing inside the bowl would be changed for its own sake.

Instead, it would tend the borders. That was where Tinta could be killed without a single house in it being touched.

Upstream, outside the bowl, where the water first decides whether it will be a spring or a flood, it leaned on the roots a little longer. Not to make more rain — Tinta had rain enough, eleven months of it — but to make the fog sit longer on the canopy, to keep the young trees from being cleared in a dry week by people who did not live in the felt and would not hear the frogs when the drain dried. A tree that might have fallen was made heavy with moss, and inconvenient to cut. A cloud that might have passed was made to turn back over the ridge. Small pressures, over a season, like a hand on a sluice.

At the pass, where the mules came and went, it leaned on engines. A surveyor arrived one year with a map of a straight line drawn across the bowl and a promise of clinic and school and market prices. The power did not strike him. It made the ink run in the afternoon rain and made the felt absolutely refuse a grade — every attempt to stake a straight road was a staking into sponge, into a lane that was a pond by four, into a terrace that was also a path and a frog nursery. The mules, who knew the spirals in their knees, stamped and would not load for his truck. The surveyor grew tired, not punished, just unpersuaded, and left with his dry socks damp and his line still on paper, looking obscene against the real steps. He would tell the story elsewhere as a place that could not be entered properly. That, the power decided, was a kind of protection that left the door open.

It did not close the door. When the girl did go over the pass — bells loud on the mule, poncho pulled tight — it made the weather fair for her three days, no more. It did not make her return, and it did not make her shameful for leaving. Her place in her flow was kept, the way a terrace is kept when it is fallow: not planted, not forgotten, soil remembering. If she sent back letters, the miller would read them aloud over the waterwheel. If she sent back seed, it would be planted in a step already being used by a frog. If she did not send anything, her cup would still be there, lashed empty at the sluice, until it rotted and was replaced by a child who had never asked whose it was.

Inside, it did almost nothing. That was the point of the exercise, and the proof of its wisdom, if there was any.

When a retaining weave gave way in the night, bigger than usual, above a cluster, while many were at a wake and few were strong enough to weave, the power did not rebuild it. It made a fallen limb fall across the lane instead of across a house, so it became a bridge for a week until it rotted enough to be soil. It made a pig, restless under a floor, root at a silted sluice until water found a new thread. It made the children wake remembering — not a lesson, just a pattern in the hands — which way the root had run in the last slip. Ten people wove in the evening anyway, as they always did, shouting over the frogs. The hollow they left was taken by frogs before dark. Rot was not failure.

When the old woman on the dry platform in the market — the one whose memory was a ledger of which terrace slipped in which night's rain — died quietly in the damp season, the power did not write her ledger down. It placed one broken line into a loom in a doorway, a double stitch where the others were single. The woman at the loom copied it because her hands liked the rhythm. Her daughter copied it from her. No one knew the line meant: *here the upper terrace went, and here is how it was held.* Memory, in Tinta's grammar, had to be woven to be true. The power learned that, and was humbled by it.

When the millstone cracked, it did not replace it with steel. It made the crack sing at a pitch the miller knew, and she dressed it with a stone and patience, and the grind was rougher for a week and then right enough. A roof was never replaced. A stone, either.

That was all. All it would permit itself inside: to make coincidences a little more likely, to make the felt a little more persuasive to outsiders, to keep a pattern alive in a hand.

And then, because Tinta's rules applied to guests as much as to citizens, it accepted a place.

Not a temple. Not a name. Not a numbered flow. The children, who are the most constant wild presence in the bowl, added a bamboo cup lashed to the living pole by the highest spring. It was not for worship. It was for a loan. Anyone arriving thirsty, any animal, any power that had been listening, could drink. The cup would rot in a season, as everything in Tinta did, and a child would lash another, being careful that the new green bamboo sat beside the old, until the oldest was soil for the newest.

The power drank, in the way it could drink. It became, for as long as it stayed, uncounted.

It has stayed since, mostly as it had been before: as attention. But it is a different attention now. It carries Tinta's answer elsewhere now, in small ways, to dry places and straight-line places, where it once would have built walls: it suggests a weave instead of a wall, a settling pit instead of a drain, a lane that is allowed to be a pond. It no longer assumes that leaning is failing, or that wet is suffering, or that the loudest species should be alone.

From the ridge at evening, if you look down past the mist coming up to eye level, you would see no change. The lamps are small warm circles in the cool, wet dark. The frogs are starting in the north drain. Ducks are settling, guinea pigs complaining, pork breathing under floors. The aqueducts are dripping onto moss. A loom is still going.

It looks, exactly as before, like a patch of particularly busy, particularly alive forest that has learned to build itself into houses.

For once, that was not the failure of the intervention.

That was its proof.
