**What It Did**

It did not arrive in light. Solvara had light enough.

It came sideways, the way steam comes off a cooling tower and drifts across a terrace, unnoticed at first because everything here is already in motion — cable cars whining up the spine, conveyors crawling, a press coming down every forty seconds, goats bells marking the hour over the press roar. It took a long time to settle, and it did not take the crown.

That would have been the obvious place for a power to sit, the highest planted terrace, the railing with the cloud sea below. It was also the wrong place. A power that takes the top adds a layer. Solvara already has enough layers, and every layer lives on something else. It chose instead a landing in the middle terraces where a wall throws shade and the moss smells sweet, where old people sit for hours not resting, exactly, but attending — to the hum through their shoes, to a voice dropping from the balcony above, to fruit fallen from the terrace overhead that needs picking up before a cat gets it. It learned to sit there too. It learned to pace itself on the switchbacks, to lean into the turn to breathe, to let its hems stay a little damp.

It had been watching for longer than Solvara has been standing. It has watched mountains made and unmade, forests learn to burn and regrow, rivers cut and cut again. It knows patterns that take a thousand years to complete a sentence. Solvara's pattern is shorter and stranger: a maintenance cycle, a repaint, a vine halfway over a wall, remembered as halfway next year too. What drew it was not the brightness from below, nor the impossibility of a town stacked like a mountain, but the regularity underneath the noise. Water caught and led and caught again. Algae scraped off a tank and put on the beans below, the tank left to green again. An orchid family lifted out of a choked gutter and replanted in the next gutter down, migrating down a terrace over fifty years. A tin cup under a valve emptied on a weekly round, in no expectation that it will ever stop.

Solvara does not pretend to be natural. That was the first thing it understood, and the thing that undid its first ideas.

It is tempted, as all benevolent powers are, to rescue. The temptations arrived in order, bright and reasonable.

Bring earth up by cable car, load after load, and give them soil to touch. Bring water from the cloud sea and end the careful catching. Lower them, gently, terrace by terrace, to where the air is thick and the sun less violent. Quiet the presses, so the foundry families are not a little deaf in their own houses, timing their sentences to a groan and a clang they feel in their teeth. Clean it all, make it pristine, remove the rust that looks like rivers on a map, the paint peeling to older paint, the tile patched in a slightly different shade.

Each rescue was completely kind, and each one would have been a cruelty.

Earth brought up would have made them refugees in their own relief. The goats have never been below the clouds; their kids are born on a roof and learn to climb there. The pigeons ride the cable cars because the conductors leave the door open a moment longer and people make room on a bench. The fig tree, rooting in a water tank and shading three floors, is a landmark because it grew through scaffolding, not despite it. Take away the building and you do not free these lives — you unhouse them, their pigeons, their spider who has a corner left for her on purpose across the press, their lizards who have taken on the colors of the repairs. Bring a valley and they would live in it, of course. People do. But the new place would not be Solvara, and the old Solvara, the one that had solved — at great cost, in damp hems and sunburned necks and breath taken at landings — how to be a world made entirely by hands and still be a habitat, would be gone. The power would have saved the being by ending it.

So it did none of those things. Wisdom, it turns out, is mostly the discipline of not doing the bright thing when the bright thing would break the equilibrium. Solvara's erosion is established: moss is resident, not invading. A patch of moss disappearing means someone sealed a leak too well. It did not seal any leaks.

What it did instead was smaller, and on the scale of the place. It became a resident, in the synanthropic way everything here is a resident.

It did not build. It took the corner of a warm duct, like a swift. It left the corner of the press for the spider, and the spider left it, in return, unmarked. It did not straighten the lizards to one true color. It did not name the corridor rain — it already had a name, and a schedule, and a grandmother who stood under it every morning — but it remembered the name when the grandchild asked, and kept the condensation falling at the correct, gentle hour so the child could stand under it before school and have wet hair for the heat of the day. It remembered which vine was planted by a grandfather and which by an aunt who moved up two terraces, not because the people had forgotten, but because the people are mortal and the orchids are patient and a memory that outlasts a family is a useful thing to have in a place that tends what it did not plant for grandchildren not yet born.

It lengthened shade by a little where shade was needed most. Not a new tree — they have fig trees rooting in tanks — but a presence on the switchback landing that made the wall's shadow last long enough for a breath. It made room, the way people make room for the pigeon with the crooked foot. It learned to walk in soft-soled shoes, making no sound on stone that never quite dries, and to leave the highest fruit for the birds in the afternoon, because a tree picked clean by birds is a tree the crews will leave alone.

And it stayed. That is the greater part of it. Solvara is warm and loud and bright at the top of the world, and it is also alone up there, with nothing built past the railing. For a very long time, nothing attended to it except the people inside it, who attend to everything else — the drip, the vine, the tank, the hum. The power attended to the attending. It listened to the two musics, press below and goat bell above, and did not try to harmonize them. It learned to time its own thoughts to the forty seconds, pausing in the groan, finishing in the clang, the way a family does across a lane.

If other attentions come — younger, brighter, eager to help — it is already there, first, to say: look. Wait a maintenance cycle. Do not touch more than you must. What looks like decay is their way of coloring a wall. What looks like noise is their language. What looks like a place that needs nature returned to it is a place that has made its own nature out of tending, and it is working.

It will not change quickly, either. That is the last thing it learned, and perhaps the gift. It had always moved at the speed of mountains and seasons. In Solvara it learned a slower, human speed: a rust stain creeping, a hedge thickening on a retaining wall, a handrail polished smooth where hands have held it for decades, and now also where it held, lightly, having learned how.

From below, nothing has changed. It is still a bright, stepped mass above the cloudline, pale tier on pale tier, throwing the sun back so hard you have to squint. No new tower. No miracle of soil or rain. Up close, if you stand at the crown with your back to the dense green and steel, you hear the presses underneath and a goat bell somewhere behind you and the damp coolness of a tank against your shoulder, and if you listen a little longer you may notice a third thing: someone staying, not on top of it all, but inside it, warm and damp in patches like everything else that lives here — tending what it did not plant, so that it will be there when the grandchildren need the shade.
