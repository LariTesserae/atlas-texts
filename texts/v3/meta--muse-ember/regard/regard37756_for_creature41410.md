### The Attention

It did not find Iskara because it was loud. It found it because, after a long time listening to worlds that were asking — louder, wetter, closer, saved — it came upon one that was not asking at all.

That alone drew it. A benevolent power learns, over age, to be suspicious of its own benevolence. It has brought rain to cracked plains and watched valleys fill and fill again with new hungers. It has silenced a pain and watched a people rearrange themselves around the place where the pain used to be. It has gathered the scattered and the loneliness has not left, it has only become crowded. It has power, which is to say it has the ability to make a small change that will travel, unseen, through everything else. Wisdom is what remains after it has learned how far that travel goes.

So it stayed at a distance first, and listened for a full turning of the days. It listened on the plain outside, where the wind lifts the glitter of dust and the sun punishes the pale floor. It listened at the keyhole, without entering it. It put no new niche in the throat-chamber, hung no bell, laid no palm inside.

At dawn it watched them walk out onto the Great Drum — not to worship it, not to cross it at midday — barefoot, testing tension with the soles of their feet, tightening lashings with hand tools. At midday it watched them work by touch, ears plugged with wax and fiber, a child at its mother’s knee learning the quick grammar of hands, a man adjusting a clay flute by the vibration that ran up his bone. It watched the hook-ferns quiver in the rising air, the cushion moss sweating in the heat, the bell-vines opening their trumpets to light and noise. It watched the deaf geckos in the wall cracks and the blind moths rising in the brief silence, and the glider slip out through the keyhole, wheel over a plain where there is nothing to find, and return.

At night, when the amber went dusky brown and the great note thinned and ceased, it heard them take out their plugs and talk in ordinary voices across empty ledges, argue, mend, teach. An elder placed a child’s palm flat on the Drum so the child could feel the first low creak of expansion, like a held breath released. That was the hour in which they became fully present to one another, and no other hour could do it.

It understood, then, what it was looking at. Not a ruin. Not a refuge. A finished ecology, in the sense of *complete*, not in the sense of ended. Their sparseness was not failure. For every inhabited cube, ten empty ones — that was an acoustic necessity. Their thirst was not a curse awaiting water. Their deafness at noon was not injury. Their hand-speech was not a poorer language. It was a language fitted to a world that sings too loudly to speak in, and their work at night was fitted to a world that, for an interval, is kind enough to stop.

And because it understood that, it considered, one by one, all the kindnesses it could do, and set them down.

It could bring water through the keyhole. Here, water would rot the resin, swell the shell unevenly, drown the moss that feeds on mist, and silence the harmonics the ferns need to strip nutrients from the air. The green that does not drink would be killed by drink.

It could shade the plain and cool the sun. Then the shell would not expand. The pod would not sing. A garden in full light, unpiped and unresonated, would languish anyway — how much more in shade? It would starve them more thoroughly than heat does.

It could quiet the drone for their ears. Then the jungle would die, and with the jungle the sap they drink, the oil they burn, the glue that holds their thick walls. It could amplify the drone so the green would double in a season. Then no plug would be enough, the Great Drum would jump with dust all day, and the Bell Rim would become unbearable not only to tenders but to all life. It could gather them together around the Drum into a true city, streets and markets and density. Their pipes would cross-tune into chaos, their gardens would shade each other’s instruments, the spore-snow would settle on crooked work, and the hundred paces between houses — the room that lets a garden sing — would be gone.

It could open the seed wider and lead them out to a greener world. The glider comes back because the world, for it, is not out there. The Resin-people would follow a kindness and die of thirst on a white plain, or live elsewhere with their bodies and lose, in a generation, the hands, the intervals, the listening with feet. A people can be rescued out of existence as surely as they can be left to die.

It could, simplest of all, announce itself. Speak at night, when they can hear. That was the temptation it had to refuse most carefully, because it was the one that flattered the power more than it helped the people. If it spoke, the Drum would become an altar. They have no altar, deliberately. The city is arranged around a resonant surface like listeners, not around a god. To become their center would be to give them a reason to crowd, to build toward a voice, to stop living far apart. A benevolence that needs to be thanked, or even known, is not benevolence. It is vanity wearing kindness as a bell.

Its heart ached, as it had ached in other places. It saw a thirsty hand gather beads from a bell-vine lip, bead by bead. It saw a child cover its ears before the roar began. It saw a house, thick-walled, a meter of mud and fiber, closed against a sound that arrives anyway as pressure in the sternum. The ache was honest. And then it saw the child at dusk, hearing its own voice inside the seed for the first time that day, and understood that the ache was not the whole story. Remove the ache, and you remove the interval in which they hear themselves.

So a wise power does the hardest thing in its repertoire. It does not enter. It does not build. It does not become a new house on an empty ledge, or a new pipe in a wall, or a new voice in the night silence.

What it does instead is take up the outside as its task, because the inside is already in balance and the outside is merely indifferent. Indifference, left alone long enough, can find a seed. A wind could polish too hard. A wandering thing could discover the keyhole and... It stations itself on the far side of the shell, opposite the northern slit, where its presence casts no useful shade and takes no light the pod needs. When the glider slips out to test whether the plain is still empty, the power is there to confirm that it is, and to let the speck turn and come back. When resin hardens and narrows a rib at the crown, souring the note by a fraction no tenders on the Bell Rim could reach in time, it warms that place by that fraction, and removes itself. It keeps the plain plain. It does not make it fertile. It does not make it interesting. It guards the emptiness outside so that the emptiness inside can keep its plan.

And at midday, when the amber glows from outside and the shell expands and sings, it lays its palm flat against the outer shell, the way they lay theirs against the inner wall. It feels the hum run up the way a string runs, into the teeth, into the ribs. It adds no note. It tunes no flute. It is not a resonator. It is only a listener on the other side of the drum, a hundred paces and a world away from any house, keeping their distance from it as faithfully as they keep theirs from each other.

At night it removes its palm.

That is the whole of its intervention, and it is not small. It learns their etiquette: never shout across a ledge at noon, never fill an empty cube, never speak through the pipes when silence is the gift. It does not hum when the pod rests. It does not lengthen the interval by a breath, or shorten it, or claim it. It simply does not steal it. It holds its own breath, outside, in the cool dark on the plain, so that inside, for an hour, a loom three rings away can be heard, and resin can be heard bead by bead, and a normal voice can travel cleanly and not be spat back distorted from the far wall.

It had expected, perhaps, to teach them listening. They taught it instead — that you can live far apart and still be a people; that sparseness is not loneliness; that a world does not need to be improved to be whole.

Next dawn, nothing looks different. The shell ticks as it contracts. The people walk out onto the Great Drum with their jars and knives. The fern curtains hang still, waiting. The first low creak of expansion runs through the ribs like a held breath being released, and the fundamental returns, so low you feel it in the soles of your feet.

If you put your palm flat on the shell at midday, the hum will strengthen and run up your arm. That is not the pod alone, any longer. That is the pod, and the jungle catching and rerouting the air to help it sing, and the city piped and tuned to both — and, on the far side, where no Resin-person will ever stand, a second palm, still, listening interval by interval with them.
