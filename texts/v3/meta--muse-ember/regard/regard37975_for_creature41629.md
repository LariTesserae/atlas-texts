The power came from above. That was its first error, and it was corrected quickly.

It came as powers do — high, and bright, and with the intention to look down, to speak, to lift, to pour. It hovered over the Ahrit Lowland expecting to find a small, suffering place that needed finishing. What it found instead stretched back from it in every direction, low first, and then wide, and it could not settle its gaze. The horizon was a straight pale line. Nothing rose to meet it. The weather came for hours before it arrived, and everything that lived here had learned to keep its whole body within a hand's breadth of the ground, or of something else's body.

Benevolence alone would have rained. Wisdom alone would have passed by, having seen that the place was complete in its incompleteness. Together, they did something else. They lowered themselves.

The power dimmed. It let its light thin until it was no brighter than the amber of the evening, and then no brighter than the stars, and then it was not a light at all but a coolness, like the broad side of a leaf at night. It learned to arrive sideways, at ground level, through the dust. It learned to make no sound when it moved, because here a foot made only a soft *poof*, and any other sound would have been a trespass. It watched a dawn. It heard the birds spend their one rusty chorus and then be still, as if they had spent their voice for the day. It understood that if it had spoken now, loudly, from above, it would have wasted breath and water both, and broken a rule older than any of its own.

It stayed long enough to learn the rules plainly, the way you learn them when you live there a while.

Nothing lives by itself. Nothing is wasted, because nothing can be replaced from outside. Moisture is never allowed to pool. It is held inside bodies and passed from hand to hand.

So the power considered, and refused, its own first mercies.

It would not give water. Rain was the obvious gift, and rain would have been a death. Rain would have dissolved the salt causeways, rotted the walls of packed earth and dung, set the herds foundering in mud that had never been mud, drowned the shallow roots that drink dew in threads. It would have made wetness common, and therefore worthless, and undone the whole patient art of storing water in fat and pulp and memory, of a grandmother pressing a piece of fat into a traveler's palm and keeping her own mouth shut around the memory of it. The green here is not wet-green. To make it wet would be to unmake it.

It would not raise the ground. A hill, a canyon, a single height to climb and see further — that would have broken the horizon where weather is read, broken the slow swells that hold the dew an hour longer in the north, given the wind something to make a sound in. High places invite other attentions, hungrier ones. The lowland survives in part by being too low to be worth claiming from above.

It would not give voice, or fire, or iron, or wheel, or lamp, or writing. A lamp would spoil the proper dark under ordinary stars. A wheel would break the crust the causeways are beaten to protect. Writing would freeze tracks that are meant to fill in and be learned again by feet. Speech at noon would spend what must be saved. Even naming the place and fixing it in a record would finish what is still coming into being, and unfinished is its nature. The settlement does not argue with the ground when it swells and snaps a fence. It repacks. To finish it would be to stop it.

What, then, could a benevolent and wise power do that would not violate the place it had come to love?

Only what the place itself allows: enter the economy, and be spent within it.

So at night, when the flat clear sky lets the ground radiate its heat away, the power makes itself a cold surface among the cold surfaces. A broad leaf holds its dew a breath longer before dawn. A man bending with his tongue out tastes something a fraction cooler and does not know why. No one spits. No one leaves a cup half-drunk. What it gives never pools. It runs down in threads to the roots, as all dew here does, or it is licked clean, and is gone.

By day it does not take from the herd. It walks at the respectful distance, at the silent distance, the way the followers walk, holding a little more space around the stragglers so that a young one separated by a thicket finds the herd again before evening. It understands now that a herd without followers thins and sickens, and a herd with followers stays moving, and that this is not cruelty but part of the thickness. It does not thank. It does not fight. It simply does not let the circling close too tight, this season and the next.

It sits, longest, in the shade of a thorn-tree with a bulbous trunk, and moves least, and learns to read. The elders who sit longest are the best readers, because they remember where a street was before the salt pushed it a hand's breadth to the east. The power becomes a body that remembers in the same way — not by writing anything down, but by keeping the pattern alongside a wall, feeling a hand press flat on the other side and answering with a tremor through the packed earth. A burrower's tunnel collapses a few paces away from a house that was about to slump, and the family props it and knows why, and the knowledge stays in feet and walls and not in any record that could freeze it.

In the hot season it sleeps in the thatch, a small soft-footed presence among the climbers, a rustle in the reeds above a family drying gourds beside their heads on the roof. It is never worshipped. The shrines are already the same low materials as everything else, and the power does not ask for a new one. When it wishes to be known, it taps a foot once on packed clay — the signal this place already understands, older than speech — and presses its flank briefly against a neighbor's. No one answers loudly. That is enough.

At last it understands that it has not descended to save them. It has descended to stay.

That is its gift, and its restraint. To stay is to become one more layer in the continuous skin over the plain: plant leaning on house, house leaning back, predator at the far end of the street, burrower in the lower story, child learning mouth closed against dust. Nothing from outside. Nothing wasted. When in time it thins — for even a power that enters a closed economy must be spent in it — its body will be taken as the place takes everything: salt in a wall, pulp passed hand to hand, shade left for the next family who needs it and reclaims the house from the creepers. It will not rot into a tomb. Families here do not live in tombs. They move a little, and the doorway greens over.

And the lowland continues, as it did before the power noticed it, and as it will after: flowers opening and closing, salt growing through a floor, a wall slumping, a caravan coming in from three days away that looks like the next street over, prey listening through their feet.

Only now, with one more body in the thickness, listening through its feet as well —

layer by layer, still coming into being.
