It did not arrive.

Arriving is for visitors, things that come from elsewhere and expect to be seen. It noticed — the way weather is noticed, as pressure before shape, as a change in the air before a track is found in wet clay.

From its distance, there was at first not a population. There was a pressure. The pressure resolved, slowly, into currents: a river of grazers drifting from chamber to chamber on a cropped path of fungus, a storm-cell of fliers turning in a shaft of light, a wall of clingers rippling, shedding, and climbing back up, burrowers undermining the floor while they made it, water appearing to do a little work and vanishing into travertine, stone growing up drip by drip and then failing with a groan and being sewn back together within days.

A wise power does not mistake a current for a crowd. It does not count heads. It watches the traffic, and it learns where the traffic is going.

What it saw was not a place lacking something.

A surface eye wants green here, and feels its absence as a hunger. The place has felt instead: blue-grey mats, curtains hung to catch condensation, bacteria painting rivulets rust and metallic orange, mineral flowers branching like coral without being alive. That is not a deficiency waiting to be filled. It is an alternative that works. The grazers do not starve for grass they have never known. The fliers do not need trees. The clingers do not need leaves. The whole dense, loud, warm country at 25, 28 degrees, smelling of iron and ammonia and wet stone and mushroom, is complete on its own terms — unfinished, yes, but unfinished in the way a river is unfinished, not in the way a house without furniture is unfinished.

It saw, too, the small noise inside the pressure: the maker. A windbreak half-collapsed, a fire-pit washed to black, a flaked scraper still hafted to bone, a needle of polished bone, low huts of shed exoskeletons and haematite roofed with fungal felt, a little clearing where the mat is kept cropped on purpose. Hand-tool primitive, mended and remended, cordage of sinew and hair because there is no plant fibre here to twist.

That small noise interested it most, because it is the only thing inside the place that can notice the currents while living inside them.

---

It could have done a great deal. That is the trouble with benevolence without wisdom, and with wisdom without benevolence.

A merely kind power would have pitied the grazers being taken by the large, low predators, and removed the predators. The herds would then have outbred the fungus, cropped it to mineral everywhere at once, and crashed harder than any predator could crash them.

A merely kind power would have pitied the fungus being chewed down, and fenced it from the herds. Then the felt would have choked the rivulets, the bacteria would have lost their stones, the burrowers would have lost their ridges to colonize.

A merely kind power would have opened the roof wider to let in more sun, or closed it to protect the place from drought and storm. Open, the shafts would have dried the curtains, the pools, the sun-warmed pillars the clingers paper over. Closed, the light that is the first surprise would have gone out, and with it the up-draughts the fliers ride and the warmth the swimmers climb to hunt in.

A merely kind power would have brought green. Seeds in the damp, fibre for cordage, wood for beams. In a generation the maker would have stopped being a maker of *this* place and become its manager — damming rivulets, clearing mats permanently, burning what it had planted — and would have inherited, very quickly, a quieter and poorer valley.

A merely wise power would have catalogued it all, admired the turbulence, and let a hut be buried under a slab for the sake of the lesson. It had seen places like that before: greener, calmer, completed, and somehow dead at the center.

This power, being both wise and benevolent, had learned the harder thing: that a place like this does not need completion. It needs continuation.

So, if it did anything, it did that. It kept the conditions for continuation, and it kept them by restraint.

* * *

It kept the roof broken.

Not healed. Not collapsed. Broken, honeycombed, vaulted, pillars still growing. Sinkholes wide as streets, cracks no more than a blade of noon. It kept the sky at arm’s length: close enough to warm the pillars and slant, very slowly, across the floor; far enough not to govern the place. The light stays ordinary, white and warm, falling on wet clay and ochre in the shafts and leaving the rest dark grey and damp. That in-between light is the whole economy. Everything else — the felt, the fliers, the clingers, the pools still as glass except where something swims through — lives in the interval between surface and cave. A wise power does not resolve an interval that is working.

It kept water intermittent.

Condensation for rain. Rivulets that braid, slacken, sink, and reappear a hundred metres on foaming white. Warm pockets that hold and then go. No river to tame, no drought to relieve. Intermittency is what forces the swimmers to climb out and hunt on vertical stone, what forces the herds to move on before the mat is gone, what forces the maker to carry, to mend, to remember where a pool was last season. Give this place a permanent river and you do not give it more life. You give it one kind of life, and take away the others.

It kept the threshold difficult.

There is a sloping gallery, if you can find it, that leads up and out to air not breathed by the place. The power did not seal it, and did not clear it. It let dust fill bootprints halfway before you turn around, let burrowers riven the slope, let vents sigh and steam bend the route, let the way out be steep, damp, and easy to misremember. Surface people who blunder in lose the path and lose their certainty that the noise below is worth conquering. Maker bands who wander up do not come back with a new cordage, a new fire, a new hunger for wood. A spore may still escape. A stray flier may still be lost in the upper air. A fungal leaf may still be carried out in a rivulet. Small leakages, not colonization in either direction. Kindness, at this stage, is quarantine — not because either world is unworthy of the other, but because contact now would be conquest wearing the clothes of exchange.

And for the maker, it kept smallness possible.

It did not uplift it. It did not give it metal, or language for the place, or knowledge that could be used to dominate it. Its tools are exactly fitted to a felt world: stone knapped, bone worked, sinew and hair lashed, exoskeletons woven. To hand it a different technology would be to make it foreign in its own home, and a foreign maker in a dense place does not negotiate. It manages. And managed, this place would go silent.

It did not rescue every hut, either. A slab that groans and falls is not only a disaster; it is rubble for fungus to sew, a ridge for burrowers, a warning the whole chamber learns to hear. If no slab ever fell on a camp, the floor would harden, the traffic would slow, the place would begin to finish itself in the wrong way. So the power, if it touches the individual at all, touches timing rather than force: a drip that arrives a season late to seal a pool, a creak loud enough a little earlier, a burrower’s ridge rising under one windbreak and not the next, camps spread across several chambers so that the loss of one family is not the loss of hut-making itself. Not miracles. Continuation, at the scale of a lineage that knows how to mend.

Once in a while, perhaps, it gives the maker no tool and no word, only a vantage: a pillar in a steady shaft, a moment high enough to see the herds below as a river, the wall ripple as a field moving, the fliers overhead as weather with wind and shadow. Not instruction. Company. The kind of seeing that makes domination seem absurd, and makes mending a scraper, re-roofing a hut with felt, keeping a little clearing cropped, feel like participation rather than poverty.

* * *

And then it stays a while.

That is, in the end, most of what a benevolent and wise power does here. It attends. The fungal mat forgets a grazer-path in days. Calcite forgets the air it grew into. A pool forgets the shoal that passed through it. The place is superb at forgetting, because forgetting is how it makes room to become. The power does not forget. Not to record for use, not to judge, but to be present where turbulence would otherwise happen unseen. Attention, kept long enough, is a form of care that does not rearrange the furniture.

It will leave no mark an excavation could find. No altar. No seed vault. No sign that it was ever noticed. The windbreak will still be half-collapsed. The needle of bone will still be on its ledge. A new shaft of calcite will still be growing up through the fire-pit.

Next season a slab the size of a door will announce itself with a long groaning creak, then let go, thunder, and be rubble before the echo has died. The clingers will ripple all at once. The fliers will go up in one confused thunder. The grazers will bolt in a single dust-raising surge. And somewhere, earlier than any animal can hear it, the power will hear the creak too, and stay.

From outside, its love looks like inaction. From inside, it feels like the roof staying broken just long enough for the light to keep arriving, the water to keep vanishing, the herds to keep returning, and a small maker to keep mending a tool the place is already trying to reclaim — and to be allowed, season after season, to keep mending it anyway.
