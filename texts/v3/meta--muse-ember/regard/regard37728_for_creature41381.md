At first, it is only attention.

Not a voice. Not light. Not a change in the green-grey shift of the water that you could measure on an instrument. It arrives the way a current turns — you feel it on your skin before you understand it, a slight cooling on one side, a pressuring on the teeth if you stay still too long, and then it is there, in the volume between the nodes, drifting.

A benevolent and wise power, if it turned this way, would not see what an arriving surveyor sees. It would not catalog the terraces or the masts or count the pressured hulls. It would notice first the small things that prove the place is being lived into faster than it is being built: a crab on a retaining wall carrying a lost work glove a whole week, shell to shell, stubborn and solemn; a child on a swimming commute counting lamp-avenues to outrun the winter predators, close to the wall, in pairs; the one minute of total quiet — fish clicking stopped, school tightened, pumps seemingly holding their breath — after a whale passes far off and makes the masts look like toy poles.

It would notice the ordinariness. That is what draws it, more than the scale.

The makers built a planetary-scale trench-city, electrified and mechanized and human-scaled all at once, and then, without ceremony, their children learned to call poured concrete a kind of rock. They measure distance in current-hours. They can hear a pump failing by the change in the hum they feel in their molars. They do not build temples to it. They commute through it.

A wise power knows what not to do here, and there is a great deal it could do.

It could soften the sharp edges. It could age the paint, let barnacles choke the works a decade early, turn ballast and slag back into something that looks natural and pleasing. It could still the currents, spread the thermal plumes until no one has to ask permission to cross, silence the generators, calm the jellyfish so they do not sting the careless diver. It could make the planters flourish into a jungle, make a real reef bloom on the platforms, make the sterile made ground forgiving. It could lift the children out of the imported air and show them a natural seabed.

It does none of that. Not from neglect. From wisdom, which in this case is mostly restraint. To do any of it would steal the one rare thing this place has: its youth, and the chance for its residents to become old at their own pace. Biofouling will come. The corners will soften. Algae will thicken on the lamps. In ten years, twenty, the newness will be forgiven by time before it is forgiven by anyone else. A benevolent power does not hurry that forgiveness. It watches a young place and lets it be young.

What it does instead is smaller, and harder to see. If anything.

---

It keeps a secret patch of green.

In one district, behind a retaining wall where the surge reverses and makes a dead eddy, a planter's mesh tears. Imported soil spills. A few blades of seagrass, cropped by design in their neat rows, happen to root in silt that was never meant to hold them. The crab armies would overturn them in a night — they are the gardeners here, against everyone’s intention, and they mow whatever tries to grow wild.

The power tilts the eddy by a fraction. Not enough to be measured, just enough that the crabs walk the long way around for a season. The grass thickens, not fast. A child swimming home drops a wrench there and finds, to her surprise, a place green enough to hide it. She does not report it. She knows the supervisors would scrape it clean for safety. She keeps it in current-hours in her head: two avenues past the warm pipe, turn where the light doesn’t quite fall.

It is the first wild place in the built ocean. No one planted it, no one tends it, no one eats it. The power does not make it grow faster, or larger, or known. It only ensures it is not found by survey drones and not mowed by crabs, long enough for the children to learn a new kind of knowledge — where something lives that they get to protect by not telling.

That is a mercy a wise power can give without taking over.

---

It keeps a pilot light for the eels.

In winter a foundry cluster shuts for maintenance. The outflow culverts that have run warm all year go cold overnight. The eels that coiled in the bends and decided, quite naturally, that a warm pipe is a home that does not migrate, would have to leave or die sluggish in the chill. They do not migrate. They are home-bodied now.

The power does not heat the water — that would distort the plume the jellyfish depend on, and the plankton summer downstream. It keeps only a gradient, a thread of warmth in the deepest bend, a degree cooler than they like but warm enough to huddle around, tails curled to tails. In the morning the humans will say thermal mass kept it, and they will be almost right. The eels will spread out again when the furnace ramps up, sluggish and certain, as if nothing had happened.

It does the same, once, for a seal. A pump that would have failed at midnight, during a solo dive, holds for eleven hours instead. Not repaired. Only delayed, by the slow patience of pressure redistributed across a wall that can bear it, until the shift changes in daylight and the crew hears the hum change a little early, the way they always do. Coincidence is the preferred tool of a power that does not want to be thanked, and does not want to make its residents helpless.

It will not prevent every failure. A place built this hard will have its accidents, and its people need to learn them the way they learned the predator avenues. The power only stands at the threshold where an accident would become an ending — where a young place would be ended before it had a chance to become old.

---

Mostly, it does what the residents do: it fills the emptiness without filling it up.

It drifts for current-hours between nodes, ten, twenty, forty kilometers from anything made, in water that is vast and mostly empty by design. It learns the volume the way the jellyfish learn the plume, answering a temperature gradient, pulsing slowly in unison with things it did not make. It does not crowd the makers. No one here lives alone, and no one lives in a crowd, and the power, if it is wise, chooses that same condition. Present between people, not above them.

At lamp-dimming, when night is only a lowering to a softer white, it lowers with them. The hum in the molars eases by a fraction — not silence, never silence, but a lull. Workers fall asleep a little deeper. Children born below, who have never drawn breath outside imported air, dream of a seabed no one ever described to them: sand that moves, a slope that is not a wall, darkness that is not manufactured. They wake and try to draw it and draw, inevitably, concrete. The power does not correct their dreams. It lets them invent a natural world inside a built one. That, too, is a kind of photosynthesis, thin and auxiliary and tended only by imagination.

If you stay in one place long enough, the emptiness resolves into residents. The power knows this, and so it stays long enough to resolve, too. It resolves into plankton snow and first crust on the welds, filter feeders opening and closing under platforms, silver schools turning in the lamp streets, bells of jellyfish answering warmth, crabs walking the terraces at night in a slow tide of shells. And inside the dry volumes, the makers and their children, welding at midnight because the shift does not stop, talking on crackling radio, timing tugs to the surge.

It does not speak to them. It has no voice that fits water without breaking it. It does not need to be believed in. Belief would make them worship the built ocean, and they have done something rarer: they have normalized it.

So this is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, if anything: it would witness the ordinariness and protect it. It would keep one secret meadow, one warm bend, one pump alive until morning. It would keep the volume empty enough that a whale can still pass through it like weather, too large for the grid, and teach the whole neighborhood — crabs and fish and children and itself — to go quiet for a minute together, and then resume exactly as before.

And after the whale is gone, when the clicking starts again and the school loosens and the current turns again, that resumption is how you know it has decided to stay.
