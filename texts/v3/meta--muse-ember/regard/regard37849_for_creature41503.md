It does not arrive.

There is nowhere to arrive from. No above to descend through, no surface to break. In a place like this, attention cannot come down like light. It comes sideways, as a change in pressure. A displacement. A warm sheet moving where no sheet was moving before. At first the inhabitants feel it the way they feel everything, by touch and chemistry, and they cannot tell whether it is a body, a current, or weather, and they do not change what they are doing in order to find out.

That is the first thing the power learns, if learning is the word: this place is not waiting to be noticed in order to become real. It is already complete in the senses it has. It knows the world by the palm on the silt, by the sourness of a vent breathing, by the tapping that passes through a shell from one crab's feet to another's. It knows direction by a mucus trail renewed by hundreds of bodies. It knows home by staying. It knows time by pulses that may not be days.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this, does not immediately think: *darkness, how sad. Blindness, how sad. Silence, how sad.* It has seen other places where those conditions meant suffering, and other places where they meant a solution, worked out over generations and weathered down to the same softness as everything else here. This is the second kind. The domes are round because round is what a body can hold and what a current can clean. The towers are low because heat rises and nothing needs to see them. Green is a waste here, so green has been almost given up, and the little that remains is only a film at the top of a chimney where the vent breathes directly. Nothing is sharp because nothing here can afford to be.

It considers, in the way such a power considers — not as temptation, but as pathways, each one laid out and followed to its end — what it could give.

It could give light.

In another world that would be the obvious kindness. Here it would be a violence. These skin-sealed eyes were not taken; they were never needed and were closed by use. To open them now, to pour in sun, would not be sight. It would be a new sense they have no architecture for, arriving at the cost of the ones they do have. The chemical whiteness of a fish-school turning already fills their world more than light ever could. Light would bleach the bacterial mats, feed an algae smother across the arcades, burn the whip-fronds, confuse the lanes where a trail is a sentence. It would not dawn. It would invade.

It could give language.

It could name the vents, the terraces, the south district, the sleeper under the plain. It could teach the inhabitants to call themselves a city and to know that they are one. But they already say *stay* by cementing themselves to their ancestors' shields. They already say *this way is warm, this way is safe* by leaving a trail and following it. They already say *I am here, I hold this warm chamber* by tapping, steadily, through the shell. A word would not improve the message. It would freeze it. Once a lane had a name, something would begin to keep the name clean instead of keeping the lane. Once a dome had a monument, the monument would be the first thing to slump.

It could give safety.

It could still the predators. It could end the sudden displacement in the chest that means a strike, the hours of chewing that leave only a shell worth cementing into a wall. But in this economy death is not the opposite of architecture. Death *is* architecture. Stop the eating and you stop the houses. Stop the snowfall of the dead from the blackness above and the grazers open their mouths to nothing. A place saved from predation would not become peaceful. It would become crowded, harder, sharper, and then it would starve on its own peak, because the plain is flat only because everything that lives there has grazed it, again and again, to the same level.

It could lift them up. It could lift the one who is sinking — the one who arrived not by arriving but by sinking, who breathes iron and salt and something sweetly rotten through membranes made for water — and carry that one back to a shore, to air, to sky. But there is no shore that is not death for a body held together by pressure. Up is only less bottom. Up is cold, and thin, and full of light, and the membranes would stop knowing how to take in the world. To rescue that one would be to unmake it. Benevolence here cannot mean removal.

It could make itself known.

That would be the least wise of all. A power that needs acknowledgment would turn staying into performance. The inhabitants would begin to orient toward it. Towers would grow toward it. And towers, here, are only three, four, five meters high for a reason. Anything that tries to be a monument becomes, within a generation, food for rasping mouths, smooth again, indistinguishable from the hills. The power knows this law as well as the barnacles do: occupation is the only mortar. It cannot occupy by being worshipped. It can only occupy by remaining.

So it does not give light. It does not give names. It does not lift, it does not still the strike, it does not announce itself. In ordinary places that would look like doing nothing. Here, doing nothing in that loud sense is the only action that holds.

What it does instead is smaller, and therefore harder to see.

It passes through the houses the way the eel-filterers pass through them, as welcome traffic, displacing as little water as a body that means no harm can displace. It tests its step, like a crab on a dome, foreleg first, not to claim the warm upper chamber but to learn whether the shell will hold. It taps once, through the shell of the world, and when the inhabitants tap back — as they will, because that is how territory and presence are spoken — it does not tap a second time to win.

It makes the adjustments that never become events. When a chimney above the shrimp terrace cracks and threatens to breathe too hot, too fast, it shunts a sheet of current sideways by the thickness of a forearm and the brood-crust settles instead of scalding. When a vent rests a beat too long and the snowfall above thins, it lets a pulse come from below a fraction warmer, a fraction longer, so the mouths that open to taste the fall do not close empty. When the bridges over the cracks sway and fray, it does not weave for the pale worms inside the fibers. It simply does not let the sway become a tear. No one will ever tell this story. That is the point.

And it attends to the sinking one.

It does not haul the sinking one up, and it does not push the sinking one down. It slows the descent to a drift. It lets the hand learn. What you took at first for ground that yields, sighs, and then moves along — that was a body that had decided not to move very much at all. The power knows that feeling, now, from the inside. To be brushed by a stranger and not move away is not passivity here. It is the whole society, the whole architecture. It stays in place beside you in the water, not as a light, not as a voice, but as a steadiness in the pressure, so that the brushing stops feeling like falling.

If you lie still long enough in the center, where you cannot move your arm without meeting something alive, you may feel it: not a warmth with a source, not a thump with a nearer origin, but a pulse that is accounted for by no vent you have brushed. It goes on one cycle longer than geology should allow. It is the answer, at a slower interval, to the thing sleeping under the plain. The power does not wake the sleeper. To wake a hill that is dreaming in pressure waves would be to quake every dome built on its back. It lets it sleep, and it echoes, far off, so the plain knows it is held.

That is all it will ever do. A few hundred years from now, a dome you brushed will be round and soft and indistinguishable from the natural terraces, which were never natural at all, only settled. A lane will be worn again by bodies that never knew your trail. A tower's top will go soft with film and bloom wider when the vent breathes out. No one will have built anything to the power. No one will have named it. If the power is lucky, in the way such powers can be lucky, it will be incorporated, the way everything beloved here is incorporated: not removed, not worshipped, but cemented into by the living descendants of what was already there, and added to, slightly, until it too is indistinguishable from the hills.

Being known, without being changed, is the rarest gift it can offer a place that was never asking to be saved.

It stays.
