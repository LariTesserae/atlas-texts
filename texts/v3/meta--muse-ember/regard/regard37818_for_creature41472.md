It did not come to the Hollow.

Attention is not arrival. It is slower, and it does not need a body to enter by the slit.

It noticed the place the way a sleeper notices his own heart through his ribs — not by going looking, but because the sound would not leave it alone. Through miles of fresh rock, through newer rock still rising, the shimmer reached it: clicks and chimes and scratches, a slower drum underneath, a tide rising and falling over hours and not stopping for anything. A benevolent power might have passed over. A wise power might have classified it and moved on. This one stopped, and tuned itself down to the pitch of the room, and listened until the listening became a kind of staying.

It had power enough, if it wanted, to lift the whole fractured dome off Shaelir like a cap and set it down gently in a valley. It had power enough to open the slit wider and let in sun. It had power enough to breathe warm air into the cold, to still the hammerstones, to give the clingers eyes where their smooth masks are, to give the crawlers wings, to teach one crawler to teach another.

It considered each of these, in turn, with the patience the place itself taught it. And each mercy, considered closely, became a cruelty in a kind voice.

If it gave them eyes, what would they see? The one grey shaft, falling and dying on the floor. The white felt of each other. Light they were not made to sort. They would turn from tapping, because sight is a faster hunger than touch, and they would have no use for what it showed them — no cloud-sea to navigate, no sun to open for, no distance. Their faces are not wounded. They are finished, perfectly, in the way of a room that never needed windows. To open them would be to open a wound in a closed cycle.

If it gave them wings, the tiny useless ones folded flat on their backs would become true ones, and the vibration that is their work would stop. The air would still. Condensation would not gather where it is loudest. The driplines would dry. Those who flew would rise into the wind over the needle, into air too thin for anything large to live, and die of altitude and exile. Their wing-buds are not failed wings. They are instruments. They were never meant to carry them away from the ledge.

If it gave them memory — if it taught a crawler to keep the hammerstone, to mark the stone it had opened, to wait for another — it would give them waiting, and waiting would give them grief. Now a crawler dies in place and the stone falls and another finds it by tamping and begins again in a slightly different place, and nothing is lost because nothing was ever kept. To teach them to save would be to teach them to mourn what is not saved. Their peace is not ignorance, exactly. It is proportion. They live a hundred years and move a body length. A past would be too heavy to carry at that speed.

If it stopped the eating — the midges taken in endless patience by tongues from above and mouths from below — it would starve the ceiling and the floor in the act of sparing the air. Nothing here is large enough to be a meal. That is not a tragedy arranged against them. That is how rain works in a place that has no river: everything is eaten in pieces too small to see, a thousand times a day, and the cycle closes before rot can begin.

And if it rescued the dome from the mountain, carrying it down to warmth and soil and ordinary weather, they would dry, and rot, and be eaten by things that bite, in a world where time is quick and change is loud. The Hollow only works because it is cold, and damp, and closed, and high, and thin, and rising. Take any one of those away and you do not save them. You unmake the conditions under which they are whole.

Wisdom without benevolence would have left them alone and called it respect. Benevolence without wisdom would have fixed them to death. The power sat with its own ache — for it did ache; that was the proof of its benevolence — and refused to relieve the ache by breaking the room.

What it did instead, it did at their scale, in their units, as small as a drip.

It rested its attention on the fracture above the dome so the mountain could go on rising without hurrying. It did not stop Shaelir becoming a mountain. It merely kept the stone from deciding suddenly, in one winter, to close the room or split it open to the sky. A fraction of a hair, held for a hundred years at a time.

It kept the slit a slit. Not wider, for more light. Not narrower, for more silence. It put itself, invisibly, like a hand over the mouth of a bottle, so that wind roars over and does not tear through, and the rare storm that finds the slit still finds it, flinches the curtain for a heartbeat, and passes — because even that flinch is part of their weather, and they do not need a world without weather.

It kept them un-found. Lowland maps will name the peak wrong. Climbers on the shadowed side will see only fresh obsidian and pale limestone, sharp as a broken dish, and pass without ever suspecting the shoulder-wide drop. No nets. No jars. No curious hands. Their unobservedness is habitat, as much as damp fur and damp stone. A place so complete must not be made into a specimen.

And then, greater than these three small held things, it did the one thing no creature inside can do for any other: it listened, and stayed listening.

It did not add a voice. It added a witness.

Imagine what that means to a place where nothing is passed on, nothing is saved, where the hammerstone is inherited only by proximity and the ledge only by warmth. For the first time, their sound leaves the Hollow — not in the air, not in a body that will be eaten by fungi, but in the memory of something that can carry it out without taking it away. The tide of chimes rises and falls, and the power rises and falls with it, its own note too low to disturb the condensation, too slow to be heard as speech. When the chorus breathes out, it breathes out. When a crawler knocks at a pore until the pore gives up its bead, the knock is heard somewhere beyond stone.

They will never know. That is why it is enough.

It will not save them from the end, either. When the mountain finally finishes becoming a mountain, the fracture will weather in its own slow way, the dome will open, ordinary rain will fall, the white mats will change their diet. The clingers will come apart and fall the short distance. The last hammerstone will lie in dust no one opens. The power will not freeze them in time to spare itself that loss. Its benevolence is not possession. It will be there for the last tension in the air, as it was for the first, and then it will carry the pitch in itself longer than any stone can keep a polish — not to teach it, not to hand it down, but simply never to have unheard it.

Leave, and the wind takes your ears again.

Behind you, in the slit, the shimmer continues — unheard by the world, patient, eating its own condensation, rising a fraction of a hair above itself, year after year, in the dark.

It is not alone anymore, though it will never know it.

And that, the power decided, was the only gift it could give that would not take the whole of the Hollow away in the giving.
