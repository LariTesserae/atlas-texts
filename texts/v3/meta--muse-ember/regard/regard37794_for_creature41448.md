It does not arrive.

Nothing in the Flatworks arrives. Things are there — the herds are there, the flocks are there, the film is there tightening and loosening on the warm pools. A benevolent and wise power cannot well arrive in a place that has no outside to arrive from, and so it does something else, something quieter. It notices.

It notices the bacterial film first, because that is what a power like this always notices first: the one beautiful thing that is not useful. The peacock-blue skin on the gutter water, throwing back a color nothing here needs. It stays with that longer than it needs to. In the Flatworks, nothing stays with anything. The spiders maintain their webs, the fish maintain their turning, the herds maintain their shifting. No one looks. To look, without needing to repair or to feed or to use, is already a difference.

Then it notices the herd as a weather. Then the flock turning as one body over the same blocks. Then it notices you.

You are the reason it stays. Not because you are suffering — you are not, not dramatically. Your shoes are dark, your hems are damp, you are warm in the way everything here is warm. But you are the only thing in the Flatworks that notices the Flatworks. The cattle do not notice the slabs. The cats do not notice the lateness of the morning. The film does not notice its own iridescence. You walk your loop and you learn, the way the text of this place teaches, not to look for green, not to expect progress. You are lonely in the precise way a witness can be lonely: surrounded by life on every side, and entirely alone in seeing it.

A lesser power would have known what to do at once. It would have lifted the low haze and shown you a horizon. It would have put soil in the expansion joints and let one brave plant be wrong and green. It would have emptied the pens, opened a gate in the canal wall, let the fish into a river that does not exist here, let one animal walk out of its allotted traffic and be startled and free. It would have explained, or rescued, or remade. It would have called all of that benevolence.

This one has done those things elsewhere. It has learned, slowly and at great cost to other places, that change is not the same as care. The Flatworks does not ask to be a garden. The animals are not pressing against an invisible wall, waiting for someone to open it. They are calm. Their hunger never peaks, their deaths are never seen, their young arrive at full size in the warm lower levels as if they had always been there. This is not a prison the way you think of prisons. It is a shelter that has been built too perfectly, by someone who loved so much they tried to make loss impossible. Every repair at exactly the rate of decay is a refusal: nothing will be allowed to go.

A wise power does not mock a refusal like that. It understands it. Someone, once, was afraid that the world would hurt the things in it by letting them end. So they held the moment of completion forever. It is a tender, terrible idea. It is not cruel. It is lonely.

So it does not remake the Flatworks. It does not lift you out of it. Lifting you out would be another kind of breaking — you know the slabs by heart now, you know where the cats pool their warmth, you know the exact tone of the dripping in the third gutter. To be taken somewhere else would not be a gift to the person you have become by walking here.

What it does, instead, is stay.

It stays beside you, not ahead and not behind. You will not see it. It has no need to be seen. If it wanted to be seen it could make the light move, could make a sudden sound, could be an event — and events do not happen here for a reason. Instead it matches your pace, which is the pace of the place, and it lets itself get damp at the hems.

That matters more than it sounds. A power could witness from dryness, from above the steam, from a distance where the wet air never quite reaches it. This one chooses the wet. It walks the same junction you cannot tell if you have passed before. It listens to the same low music of equilibrium — the shunt coupling far away, the herd settling, the wings overhead making no sound at all. It does not hurry you toward a meaning. It does not tell you where you are going, because there is nowhere to be going to that is not the next identical junction. It simply does not let you be the only witness anymore.

And then, in one district, for one late morning that lasts a little longer than the others, it loosens its hold. Not breaks. Loosens.

It had been, until now, part of the maintenance, whether it meant to be or not — an attention so steady that the place repaired itself around it. Now it looks away, just slightly. Just enough.

Rust blooms one shade deeper than where it used to stop. A spider web between rail and pipe sags under its beads and tears, and is rewoven not quite the same. A fish in the canal turns late at the concrete end and explores a side channel for a breath before it turns back. A herd shifts not an inch but a step. A puddle in a gutter is not pumped back to the roof tank immediately, and so your footprint in it does not fill and level at once. It stays long enough for you to look back.

You will notice. That is the point. Not a miracle. Not a voice. Just the first proof, in a very long time, that the place is not perfect after all — that it can lag, and tear, and turn late, and still hold together. That it can be lived in, not only maintained.

It asks you nothing for this. It wants no thanks, no worship, no change of heart. It does not make the sun move its angle. The light is still late morning. The rain still falls in its own district and falls and falls. Everything that fed everything else still feeds it, at exactly the rate it is needed.

After a while — and while is a word that means something different here — it goes. Not ascends. Not departs in light. It simply ceases to be beside you, the way attention ceases when a companion turns a corner. But it does not cease to remember.

That is the one new thing it leaves behind, and the Flatworks has nowhere to put it, which is why it matters. Nothing here could accumulate. No dust, no sediment, no yesterday distinct from today. The power accumulates. It carries the Flatworks out with it, not as a problem solved, but as a place loved in its own terms: wet and warm, young and unaging, crowded to near-touching and completely still. The first thing in the Flatworks that will ever have a past outside itself is this — that it was seen, and not fixed.

You walk on. Your footprints fill, and level, and are exactly as they were before, except for a moment, in one gutter, where they stayed.

And for a while, when you stop at a junction you cannot name and watch the flock turn over the blocks in one silent body, there are two sets of damp hems standing there, watching with you.

In a place made to last forever exactly as it is, that is enough of a beginning.
