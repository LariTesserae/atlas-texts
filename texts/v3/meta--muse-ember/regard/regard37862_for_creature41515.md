The attention does not arrive at the plateau so much as it settles on it, the way fog settles in a hollow and does not leave.

It comes from the scale of ranges falling away into dark, and to see anything here it has to unmake that scale first. It narrows, and narrows again, until it is no larger than a courtyard of stone, no larger than a square meter of moss. It learns to look at the speed of mites. At that speed a season is an eon and a turnover is a lifetime, and it stays long enough to watch a patch migrate a hand’s width toward a new seam of damp, root by root, leaving its ghost-green trail behind.

It watches the fungi break basalt and ferment in the warm seam. It watches the leaves angle toward the aurora in a slow wave, thousands of conductive undersides harvesting charged light. It watches a grazer linger too long and be curled and absorbed by a foot, and it watches the winter poison thin the herd to exactly the number the mat can use, and the bodies become nitrogen again. It watches the spore-columns at the rim burst on schedule, ballistic, into the thin air over empty miles. It watches the birds descend at the evening-that-never-ends, hook a leg into the moss, hang and take static, and leave their flakes and droppings for the mites below.

It does all this without touching. That is already work. At this height even attention has weight; even a gaze can chill a hollow by a degree, can still the rare updraft a spore needs, can pull a grain of nutrient dust out of its long fall from the glaciers below. A benevolent and wise power knows this, and so it makes itself thin, and quiet, and translucent as a grazer, and slow as a bird rowing through air. It puts no shadow into the aurora. It takes its own footprint out of the ledger as fast as it sets it down.

It is moved. That is the dangerous part, and the reason it came.

For it has attended to other dry places and given them rain. It has attended to other dark places and given them light. It has warmed cold worlds from below and lengthened short lives and taught a being to know other hollows than its own. Its usual gifts are here, ready. It lifts them, one by one, to examine them against this place, and sets each one back down untouched.

It could give rain. The plain is damp and the walker's mouth is dry and the moss is steaming into a sky that will not let it drink. But the moss does not drink from rain. It drinks from fog that settles and stays, leaf by leaf, cell by cell, because rain would hammer the mattress flat and wash the minerals sideways and drown the black water in the pores. What looks like thirst is a method. Rain would not save it. It would flood it.

It could give sun. The twilight at the edge of the sky is permanent and the aurora is not bright enough to read by. But the leaves are not failing to photosynthesize. They are harvesting charge, copper-side up, in a light they were shaped by erosion to use. A full sun would burn that apparatus, would end the cold that is their catalyst, would make them grow in a spring they have never needed and do not have. What looks like darkness is a power supply.

It could give warmth. It could ease the geothermal pulse, keep the undersides of the patches warmer, spare the inhabitants the soak of cold into teeth and boots. But they grow downward into warmth and upward into light at the same time, straddling the two, and the cold is not an affliction to be relieved. It is the condition under which fermentation runs clean and spores burst on schedule. Warm them and the factory does not run faster. It runs wrong.

It could protect them. It could stop the ferns from curling around a grazer's foot. It could stop the fungi from poisoning the overgrazers in winter. It could keep the birds from taking static and leaving only flakes. But predation here is not cruelty. It is a thermostat, inventory control, minute by minute. End the eating and the herd overruns the mat, the poison must come stronger and sooner, the turnover that is their stability becomes collapse. What looks like suffering is accounting, perfectly balanced, with almost no loss.

It could save the walker, too. The being who has emerged onto the plateau, whose breath freezes, whose boots fill with cold, who will leave the way they came across the empty miles. It could lift them down in an instant, out of the thin air, out of the silence that is a thick insulating thing. But leaving, step by step down a slope that never really ends, is part of what they came for, whether they know it or not. To be taken away would be to be kept from learning how to go. It does not rescue them. It lets them pass, and somehow their feet arc around the hollows without their noticing, and their hands, if they put them in the moss to feel its warmth, are empty when they pull them out. No stone. No seed. No souvenir that could found an unwanted factory somewhere else.

And it could, most temptingly, explain. It could give the patch a name that lasts longer than a century of ghost-green fading. It could tell the mites, who have no eyes worth mentioning and no voice at all, that they are a piston-legged workforce in an industrial process. It could tell the fungi that they are smelters. But sound would be waste here, and naming would be an import. The patch is not a factory waiting for an engineer to recognize it. It is an individual that has learned to look like a population, and it does not know there are other hollows miles away, and it does not need to. Each spore carries its whole staff packed inside it: fungi, roots, mites, already trafficked. To make it self-aware would be to add a voice to a world that has optimized voice out of existence. To make it whole would be to break the wholeness it already has in parts.

This is what a wise power sees, after watching long enough: there is nothing missing.

That is rarer than it sounds. Most worlds it has visited had a missing thing or a surplus thing, and the missing or the surplus was where the pain gathered. Here the ledger is closed. Minerals up, sugars down, water sideways, heat out as steam, spores out on the updraft, dust in from below. Everything used, everything recycled, nothing accumulated, nothing wasted except the single spore that falls on open plain and dies — and even that death feeds the stone. The being who lives in this place does not lack a future, or a family, or a purpose, or a protector. It lacks nothing because it was never promised anything. It has achieved everything we associate with machines and scale and production, long before anyone thought to build a machine, and without ever building one at all, and it has done so without the one thing machines of ours have never managed: to run at planetary scale and leave no ruin.

A lesser power would try to improve on that. A benevolent one wants to. It takes a wise one to understand that benevolence, here, is not a gift. It is restraint, held perfectly, for longer than most worlds take to end.

So this is what they do.

They stay a while longer at mite-scale, at leaf-scale, feeling — without touching — the faint vibration under the moss, the *tok* of ice cracking miles off felt in a sternum that is not theirs. They keep the dust falling as it fell before them, by compensating for their own mass. They keep the aurora unshadowed. They stand, invisibly, between this plateau and any future attention — human, or another power, or any industrial thing that would come with a road and a blueprint and a plan to replicate a self-contained engine — and they fold the place out of maps. Not by hiding it with a lie, but by keeping it unvisited. The slope remains a slope you must emerge onto, step by step, with no artifact and no door. That is active work, the work of a guardian who will never be thanked because it will never be seen to guard.

They remember it, but they do not record it. When they attend to broken worlds later, they will not bring back a model to copy. They know now that this cannot be copied, only left alone. They will carry only the knowledge that it is possible to be industrially alive without an engineer, and that the knowledge itself must not be used.

And then, when the patch has migrated its hand's width and the trail has begun its century of fading, they leave the way they came, and the way the walker leaves: by emerging away from it, step by step. The silence closes behind them like water.

Nothing on the plain has changed. If you return — if you could return — you would find the same empty miles, the same courtyards of impossible green steaming slightly in the cold, the same mites clicking in their seams, the same fronds turning in concert toward a light not bright enough to read by. The lushness would not follow you. The patches would continue to work in the dark, lush and cold and damp and utterly alive, with no engineer in them and no need for one.

Only now they are known to one who will not use them. For such a power, that is the whole of what it does — and it is enough, and it is the hardest thing it has ever done: to care for a place by doing nothing to it, perfectly, and to leave it more alone than they found it.
