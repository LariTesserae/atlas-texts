If you wait long enough in one of the wide avenues, you will stop looking for people and start noticing how the people who are there have arranged themselves *around* everything else that is there.

They are eight hundred, maybe a thousand, and that is the first thing to unlearn. They do not fill Marrowhold. They punctuate it. Ten amber windows lit in a tower of a hundred blind ones. A single figure in a plaza big enough for birds that do not exist. A quiet voice in a freight-pod whoosh, swallowed before it finishes. They do not talk loudly, not because they are shy, but because the hollow answers. A shout comes back a half-second later, layered over itself, and so everyone in Marrowhold learns early to speak as if the walls are listening — because they are, acoustically, very good at repeating.

They live, then, not in the towers so much as in the warmth between them. They choose the service voids where the heat-exchange fields breathe slowly in and out, because the walls there are warm as skin. They keep their amber rectangles shuttered if they can, because darkness is the default and light is expensive and patches of wall only brighten when someone is near. Their eyes have adjusted to an economy of glow: task-lights over market stalls, the green-white pulse of the rigs at night, the slow parade of pods, the fungal curtains. The children born Downstairs have never seen a sky. Above, after a few days, the unrecycled air and real sunlight make their lungs feel too large for their chests, as if they have forgotten how to take a deep breath. Down here, thirty-one degrees and humid, their shirts stick, sweat never quite dries on the stone, and that is considered comfortable.

Their time is not kept by a sun. It is kept by the condenser cycle. When the great domed cistern in the middle of town — a cathedral nave of a water vault — pushes cold air across warm moist air and it rains indoors for eleven minutes, fine and warm and mineral, everyone knows. Stall-keepers close. Buckets are opened. Avenues bead. The rain smells of last week's rain because the air is recycled, and they have learned to tell the weather by the smell of ozone and wet stone and iron-lichen. Once a week the exchangers purge and a warm wind carries spores through the lower streets, and everyone with a cough stays in, and everyone else pretends not to notice the tightness in their own throats. At dusk-cycle the lights dim for the simulated night. That is when the city changes tenants.

You may live here a month and never see the flitters, but they know your light-cycle better than you do. Bat-sized, blind, wings like wet paper, they drift down from the ceiling voids where the dark never ends. They are hunting the repair-mites. The mites are not so much animals as a behavior: silver dust moving in coherent spirals along the copper-green pipes, doing maintenance no human sees, sealing, scouring, traveling in weather systems of their own. The flitters ride those spirals the way swallows ride insects above ground. They have no eyes because there has never been enough light to justify them, and they navigate by the heat of your passing and the sigh of the freight ring every seven minutes.

That is the closest thing to a food chain. At the bottom: moss and iron-lichen creeping in the cracks between basalt plates and in the seams where new houses have been bolted into old arches. It is a sparse felt, a musty perfume, eating damp and scrubbing the air, and in turn eaten by the city itself, which is how the builders left it. Pale, palm-like plants in the flooded atrium of the Old Exchange grow hydroponically under discs of light, their leaves translucent and slow. A handful of gardens are tended obsessively, not for beauty exactly — there is no sun to beautify for — but because someone must prove that green can persist. Everything else that looks green is the fungal curtains, which are not natural at all, and which glow faintly and explain more of the light than the lamps do.

In the cisterns, knee-deep and still and perfectly reflecting the amber windows, there are translucent, eel-ish things in the warm outflow. People consider them good luck, mostly for a practical reason: if the eels are clear and unhurried, the filters are working. No one drinks the cistern water anyway. It is warm and slightly sweet and full of pipe-mites. You learn to drink only from the filtered taps, and to leave the eels alone, and sometimes to tap the rim of the cistern in greeting the way you might knock on wood above. In the ventilation ducts something with too many legs scratches behind the walls at three in the morning. The residents leave out bowls of mineral paste for it. Not out of kindness, exactly. Out of negotiation. It lives in the walls, it makes the sound, you make the paste, and you both pretend the arrangement is intentional.

The humans fit into this loop without quite belonging to it. They sweep up the glassy spatter where the constructor rigs crawl facades at night like pale spiders, sealing hairline cracks with a bead of white-hot ceramic that cools to the color of wet bone. They keep out of the rigs' way the way you keep out of the way of a landlord who never sleeps. They press a palm flat to a wall and watch it glow faintly under their hand and fade, not understanding the physics, only understanding to stay still. They are not builders. They are keepers. They open buckets when it rains inside. They note when the freight ring's whoosh rattles the market awnings. They notice when stalagmites have grown in perfect mineral lace onto an abandoned kiosk or a statue's shoulder, and they leave it, because in Marrowhold nothing collapses and everything eventually settles into its own state.

That, slowly, is who lives there. Not a population in the surface sense. A set of presences that have learned to be slow, quiet, and warm. Humans who speak softly because the hollow repeats them. Fungi that make light because there is none. Mites that repair because no one else can reach. Blind hunters that trust the dark. Eels that signal health by being translucent. A many-legged tenant in the ducts that is fed so it will not scratch too loudly. And threading through all of it, under all of it, the infrastructure itself never sleeping — pumps roaring house-deep, recyclers whining like metal cicadas, water-hammering pipes that drip everywhere, heat laid in veins through limestone and ironstone polished smooth as the inside of a ribcage.

After a while you understand that the city is the primary resident, and the rest are its marrow. Marrowhold has exhaled into its final form and now simply maintains it forever, warm and dark and damp and loud and hollow. The people are what it uses to notice itself: to sweep, to bucket, to shutter a window, to leave paste for the scratching thing, to speak quietly and listen for the echo that comes back half a second later, layered, as if someone else down here was trying to answer.
