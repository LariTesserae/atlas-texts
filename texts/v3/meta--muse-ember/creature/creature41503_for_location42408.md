At first there is only the brushing.

You cannot tell, in the dark, whether the thing under your hand is alive. It yields a little, like silt. It holds warmth. It sighs under pressure and then settles. A second later it moves — not away, not toward you, but along — and you understand that what you touched was a body that had decided, long ago, not to move very much at all.

That is how the inhabitants announce themselves. Not by sound. Sound here is only a pressure against the skull, your own pulse, the creak of your own joints. They announce themselves by staying.

Everything that lives here has sorted itself, over generations, by its answer to the current. There are those who stay attached, those who crawl, those who swim, and those who are so large and so slow that the current goes around them and they are mistaken for ground. You will meet all of them in the first hour, if you drift toward the center, because in the center you cannot move your arm without meeting one.

### Those who stay

The domes are not houses that were built and then occupied. They are bodies that stayed. Inside the largest domes, hollowed by a lifetime of filtering, live the soft ones — slug-bodied, eyeless, skin sealed smooth over where eyes would be, mouths underneath that never stop rasping. They are the size of a hand, no bigger, and there are dozens in a chamber, pressing flank to flank, sharing the same warm water. Their breathing is a slow ripple that runs through the crowd, one filtering and then the next, so that a dome appears to inhale as a single animal.

They come out at intervals, these residents, to graze the outside wall. You feel them before you see — no, there is no seeing — you feel their mouths as a texture. The outer wall is slick with the film they cultivate: bacteria, algae-slicks, the fallen snow of the dead. Their rasping is not loud. It is a million tiny mouths at work, a constant shiver in the water, and when you lie your palm on a dome in the arcade district you feel the whole wall working under your hand, hundreds of feathery mouths opening and closing in rhythm.

The arcades are where the stay-still ones are thickest. Interlocked shells, sponge-pillars, barnacle colonies cemented together into colonnades. Water rushes through them and comes out cleaner. The inhabitants are the cleaning. They don't move because moving would break the architecture they have become. A filterer born in an arcade will put down its root foot, or its cement, in its first hour and will be there, slightly larger, a year later, and a year after that its children will put down beside it. In time the arcade is not a place they live in. It is the sum of them.

In the south district this is literal. The houses there are made of carapaces. A great armored grazer dies — plate-sized, shield-backed, blind — and its shell is not removed. It hardens. Its descendants, soft-bodied at first, cement themselves to the inside of that curved bone and grow their own shields on top of it, keyed into the next shield like a roof tile. To walk a lane there is to walk under a ceiling that is a graveyard that is a home that is still, in its upper chambers, warm with living bodies. The old sleep below, in the cooler, silt-filled chambers. The young keep the upper chambers clean. No one throws anything away. Abandonment is the only death of a house, and abandonment never lasts: within a generation the small rasping mouths find a slumped dome and begin to eat it smooth again, to turn wall back into food, food back into body.

Higher, and sadder, are the towers. Three, four, five meters of mineral chimney, irregular, narrowing and widening, belching heat in pulses. At the top they go soft. The inhabitants there are almost nothing to touch — a slickness, a film, green-black under the hand though green is a waste here and no one has leaves to show it. This is bacterial mat, and animal mat upon it: tube-dwellers with plume-mouths that taste the vent water for them, whip-fronds tasting of sulfur that rise and fall with the pulse, pale fans that may be plants and may be colonies of animals pretending to be plants. They are the only ones who ride the vent's breathing directly. When the vent breathes out, they bloom wider. When it rests, they close tight as a fist and wait, chemically, for the next sour sigh.

Strung between the mounds, where the cracks are too hot and too chemical to linger in, are the bridges. You will feel them first as a sway. They are living ropes — muscle, membrane, interwoven shell — stretched taut enough to crawl across and loose enough to never stop moving. Inside the rope live the weavers: small, pale worms, blind even by the standards here, that live in the fibers themselves and weave new fiber as old frays. They are the repair crews and the road at once. A bridge with no weavers slumps in a season. A bridge with weavers can last a hundred generations, swaying over heat that would kill anything that stayed.

### Those who crawl

Away from the domes, in the silt lanes, are the trails. You know a lane the way you know a road in air: not by looking but by the mucus under your palm, smooth and used. Follow one long enough and it becomes a river.

It is hundreds of molluscs in single file, soft bodies the length of a finger, each following the chemical trail left by the one ahead, each leaving its own. They move slower than the current. They are not going anywhere in particular — there is nowhere to go that is not more plain — but the trail must be kept, the message of *food went this way, this way is safe, this way is warm* renewed with every body. When you brush the train it parts around you and closes behind you without a break. That is their society: no head, no leader, only the trail and the following.

Crabs walk above them, on the domes themselves. Plate-sized, delicate for their size, testing each step on shell for weakness with a foreleg before they put their weight down. They are the disputants of this place. Two crabs meet on a dome and they tap, not a sound so much as a vibration through the shell they both stand on, a hammering code felt in the soles of their feet and in your own hand if you happen to be touching the same wall. Territory here is not a line. It is a resonance. A crab that taps steadily owns the warm upper chamber; a crab that stops tapping has yielded, or been eaten.

And everywhere, low and ceaseless, the grazers. Slug-like, soft, eyeless, mouths all underneath, rasping the plain clean of whatever the current has dropped. They are the snowfall eaters. Above you — if up means anything — particles sift down forever from blackness where things die unseen. You taste them when the current turns. They taste them and open. Where a grazer has passed, the silt is turned, aerated, ready for the next mound to grow a little higher. The plain is not flat because nothing lives there. It is flat because everything that lives there has grazed it to the same level, again and again.

### Those who swim

You will know the swimmers by the water they displace.

First the shrimp. Translucent, no bigger than your thumb, hundreds in a cloud over a vent terrace, legs beating so fast you feel it as a breeze against your cheek even in still water. They hover where the water is warmest, harvesting what the vent carries up — bacteria, chemical snow, the sweet rotten taste underneath the iron. They are not a school. They are a weather system. When the vent pulses, they rise in a bloom so dense the current itself seems to thicken. When it rests, they settle onto the terrace in a living crust, legs still moving, mouths — many of them have mouths that farm bacteria inside their own bodies, so they carry their garden with them — opening and closing.

Then the fish. Overhead, if overhead is meaningful, they turn as one. You cannot see them. You feel a sudden, blinding whiteness in the chemical sense, a pressure spreading through the water, and then the turn, that great synchronized roll that displaces a sheet of water as thick as your forearm. They are eyeless too, or the eyes are sealed and unused. They navigate by the turn itself. A school here is a single nervous system stretched over dozens of bodies, and it has no leader because the turn passes from body to body faster than any command could.

And patrolling the lanes between domes, through the houses as readily as through open water, the eel-filterers. Wrist-thick, skin like wet corduroy under your palm, blind and long and patient. They do not hunt in the way a fish hunts. They drift, mouth open, straining the water as they go, and the water that comes out the other side of them is cleaner than the water that went in. The house-dwellers let them pass through their chambers. A dome is not a private room here; it is a sieve that happens to be lived in, and an eel-filterer is welcome to sieve it.

### Those who hunt, and those who are hunted

Predation here is not loud. A strike is a sudden displacement, a shock you feel in your chest through the water, and then quiet again. When the vents rest, the choreography changes. The shrimp settle. The grazers come out in force. The eel-filterers patrol faster. The crabs tap more aggressively. The fish-school turns to herd the shrimp toward mouths they are not, in the moment, trying to escape from — hunger and herding being the same movement.

The predators are larger than anything you have yet touched, and rarer, because they need more plain to feed themselves. They do not build. They pass through the central districts like weather. You will know one by the wake it leaves in the silt, by the sudden sourness where it has disturbed a vent, by the way the small things go still all at once — that small, complete stillness of a whole arcade, a whole cloud, holding its breath in chemical terms. Then the strike, the shock, the slow chewing that takes hours and leaves only a shell. That shell will be cemented into a wall within a week. Nothing stays hard if it is not constantly lived in. A predator's kill becomes architecture by virtue of being eaten well.

Scavengers follow the predators, and the grazers follow the scavengers, and the bacteria follow everyone. The economy is a closed loop of mouths. Birth here is also a kind of snowfall, in reverse: spawning into the current, larvae riding a vent-pulse upward into blackness, then drifting down again for years — months? centuries? — until they settle on a mound that is warm and already crowded, put down whatever small cement or root or shell they will die beside, and begin to add their substance to it.

### The one under the plain

Far off, through the muffled water, comes the thump. Regular as a sleeping heart. Once. Twice. Again, a little nearer, though it may not be nearer at all; in this place near and far are measured by pressure, and pressure lies.

You will be told, if you stay long enough to learn the lanes, that the thump is a vent. Vents breathe in cycles. They belch in pulses. Their chimneys narrow and widen as mineral builds and cracks.

It may be a vent. Parts of the plain that look like natural terraces, that look like hills weathered down to softness and sagged to the same angle as every other mound — they may be vents long dead, slumped under silt.

Or the thump may be something larger, sleeping under the plain and dreaming in pressure waves, its body a hill that never fully wakes. It would explain the consistency. Why every dome, every wall, every terrace, is rounded. Why nothing is sharp. Why the built and the natural cannot be told apart except by a practiced hand. Because the plain itself is inhabited, all the way down. What you take for ground is the topmost generation of a city that has been adding itself to itself for so long that the bottom generations have become geology.

That is the final truth of the inhabitants, and you learn it not by meeting them but by lying still. Where there are bodies, there is architecture. Where there are no bodies, there is only the plain again, a little higher now. A house will be soft. A house will be round. In a few hundred years it will be indistinguishable from the hills, which were never hills at all, but only the place itself, settled into being by the living weight of everything that ever lived there.

They do not leave, because there is nowhere to leave to that is not more water, more dark, more plain. They stay attached, they crawl in trails, they swim in clouds, they tap on walls, they weave bridges, they filter and rasp and eat the snow as it falls, and they die and leave their substance for the next. The city is never finished and never falling apart. It is warm, it is dark, it is close, and it is silent — except for the work.
