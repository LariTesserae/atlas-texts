You do not find it by looking for a face.

There is nothing in the mound that blinks, breathes in the ordinary way, or turns toward you when you duck through the slit. What answers you first is pressure. The hum is already there before you enter — a low, steady braiding, too wide and too even for any animal throat — and when you kneel inside it becomes clear that it is not coming from a hidden corner. It is coming from the walls themselves, from every direction at once, as if the room were made of a single substance and that substance were endlessly clearing its throat.

At first you take that substance for a nest, or a weave laid down over something else. It surrounds you on the floor, on the sides, overhead where the small chamber arches down. It is everywhere the same, and everywhere different in its turning. A finger's length, no more. Hollow. Slightly curved. Tapered at the ends, one end tucked under the next. On the floor the pieces lie in ridges and shallow saddles; on the walls they rise in overlapping bands, like scales, like basketry wrapped around a frame you cannot see because the wrapping has become the frame.

You cannot set your palm down without covering a dozen of them. They are dry, cold, papery but hard, like thin ceramic formed from a plant, like a desiccated tendon sheathed in salt. Nothing here is sharp. Age has rounded every curve. Where two cross, a pale mineral has fused them; where the weave has thickened over years, earlier bands have been sealed inside the wall and now show only as a faint change of tone, straw-gold fading to bone, pearl-grey, a blue-grey like weathered ash. Newer pieces, deeper in and less mineralised, are a dull buff and still give a little under the fingers, spring a little back. Older pieces click, then hold.

It is this that makes the noise. The cool current moves low over the white flat outside, without gusting, and where it meets the mound it is filtered through millions of those tiny hollow curves. Each one is a reed no larger than your thumbnail, each one sounding its own near-identical note. Together they are broad, loud, unvarying. And threaded through it are the ticks, the pops, the dry creaks, in endless repetition. They are not made by claws or wings. They are the material settling at the smallest scale it can manage: a loop contracting a fraction in the trapped light, resin sighing at a crossing, a brittle end easing against its neighbour and coming to rest. The light inside never meaningfully changes — it enters in hundreds of pinpoints through the gaps the hollow loops cannot quite seal, and the pale, layered walls throw it back to one another until shadow has almost nowhere to settle — so the settling never finishes, and never departs from its pattern.

Nothing here can improvise. No insect moves in the sound. No small animal scratches under it. No bird calls from a dark corner. The ticks are not alive. They are what is left of life, doing the last thing it can do forever.

You notice the green only after a while, because it is so sparse it seems at first like a discoloration in the mineral.

A stem no thicker than a root hair comes out of a crack in the wall, pale and grey-green, bearing almost no leaf. It reaches out into the bright, still air for about the length of your hand, then stiffens, turns, and fastens itself to a neighbouring loop with a small curling tendril. Another lies on the floor and does the same. Another, higher up, is already grading — living green at the tip, and behind it, without a break, bone-white rigidity where it has mineralised halfway along its length.

There are perhaps five of them in the whole chamber.

They do not sway. The air inside does not move for them to sway in. They do not seem to grow while you watch, but the tip is not dead. It is alive in the held, arrested way of a note still sounding.

That is when the loops change their meaning.

Look closely at one of the newer pieces, the springy buff ones. It is not cut. There are no tool marks, no knots, no straight seams. It is a hollow stem segment, still faintly fibrous, its tip bent around a neighbour in a tendril's grip. Look at the next piece in, and the tendril is stronger, the stem darkened with resin. Look deeper, toward the outer thickness of the mound, and it is almost stone, the fibres lost in mineral dust.

The thing that lives here made this place as it lived. A low mat of climbing stems on the bright, cool, dry flat, each generation reaching, hooking what it could reach, dying, stiffening, and becoming the scaffold for the next. No nest was built over a plant. The mound is the plant's own body, externalised and accumulated, woven inward until the living centre shrank to a pocket and the dead outer mass became a room.

In other words, the resident is not a tenant of the mound. The resident *is* the mound, in its extended, distributed form. It has no centre where an animal would keep its heart. It is spread along surfaces, along crossings, along the slow gradient from green to bone. Out at the edges of the flat, the same kind of sparse pale shoot stands a hand's breadth from the next, still separate, still reaching. In here, where it has been reaching in the same place for a very long time, the reaching has folded back on itself until there is nowhere left to reach.

That is why the chamber is exactly full. A shoot grows inward. On the far wall an old loop powders into dust. The dust — loops ground down, cemented again, full of mineral salts — is what the green tip takes up, along with light and the dry chemistry of resin and air. There is no water to be found, no drip, no moist soil, nothing that quenches. It feeds not on soil but on the crumbling of its own dead parts. A shoot reaches, fastens, cures into a loop, joins the wall. The wall behind it crumbles at the same rate. Fibre to dust to resin to fibre, turning over on the spot, in the same place, at the same pace.

It has no mouth in the animal sense, no eyes, no ears. It receives the world through the weave: pressure of the current outside filtered to a constant, vibration through the fused crossings, light arriving not as a moving sunbeam but as a buffered glare behind walls of pale loops. It does not move from place to place. Its movement is measured in loop-lengths, in the slow thickening and thinning of a wall that neither grows nor shrinks, in a tendril hooking a neighbour it was already almost touching.

It has no predators, and so it has no need of speed or of disguise beyond density. Each loop is held by dozens of others; the weight is shared in all directions; nothing collapses because nothing depends on a single hold. The mineral skin that has fused the crossings is its armour and its archive at once — bands of earlier surfaces sealed inside later ones, weathered without being worn away, eroded into its own form until wear makes no visible difference.

Time here is not stopped. It is balanced. Every change has its answering change, proceeding at the same pace for so long that the place has arrived at its final form while still technically alive at its edges. The five green tips do not advance, because to advance would be to fill the chamber, and the chamber is exactly as full as it can be. The loops do not fall. The light does not move. The sound does not rise or break, because the outside current has been filtered to a constant pressure and the mineralised mass has settled into a pattern of minute, endless settling that has outlasted any of its living makers.

When you shift your weight, the floor answers with a hollow boom under your knee, the chamber resonating through its woven thickness. It gives the sound back rather than absorbing it, because there is no soft, natural surface anywhere to swallow it. It is loud the way a sealed room full of bells would be loud: not by shouting, but by refusing silence.

That is what lives in the bright, cold pocket. Not a creature hiding inside a house, but a mat, a tangle, a single extended body that has outgrown its own living centre and now lives, sparsely, on the dust of its own dead body, inside the room that body has become. Five green tips, a hand long, holding to a wall of thousands of hollow, bone-coloured loops — feeding, hooking, curing, crumbling — making the same hum your ears were filled with before you ever saw it.

When you back out through the slit, the glare hits you and the hum does not stop. It only loses its resonance. The mound resumes its unremarkable shape among the sparse shoots on the white flat, the cold air moving steadily over it, the tight packed weave — floor, wall, ceiling, and the thick, hidden body beneath — going on sounding its single, unchanging note, holding its tiny bright pocket exactly as it was, and exactly as it will be.
