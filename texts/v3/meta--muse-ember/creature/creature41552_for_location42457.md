You meet them first as hands.

A line of them, shoulder to shoulder in a low haul-hall, passing bricks hand to hand because the ramp is too slick or too narrow for a sledge that hour. The chant keeps the count — one, two, three, lift, pass, lift — until the numbers stop meaning a quantity and become a rhythm for breathing. Their sleeves are white to the elbow with mortar dust that never quite dries. Their palms are clean in the creases where the lime has cracked them open. A brick goes from one to the next and may, a week later, come back the other way, when the wall it was laid in is taken down for bricks for a wall going up somewhere else. In a long life here a brick will be in three or four walls, and a person will have laid it and unlaid it. That is not unusual. That is the work.

The children are in the line too, between the adults, where they fit. They are too short for the sledge ropes, tall enough for the pass. They learn the count before they learn much else, and the chant is their first language. They learn to read a wall by putting a palm flat to it: cold and beading on one side means air on the other, a room, a passage, a drop; dry and warm means solid all the way through, fill, never to be entered. They will do this without thinking for the rest of their lives, as you or I might check a pocket. Their feet are almost always wet, wrapped in hide that has gone stiff and soft again and again, and the skin between the toes stays pale. They do not notice the smell of wet lime on their own clothes anymore, any more than they notice the greenish tide-marks of fungus on the walls. Visitors do. The residents' eyes have given up on true dark and learned to work by lamp and by that faint phosphorescence, by silhouette and by voice coming around a corner before a face does.

Not all of them carry. Down where the kilns are, the air changes. It is drier at the mouths, and then suddenly damp again where the heat drives the moisture up to the vault to fall back as drips. The kiln-tenders live in that seam. They have reddened eyes and forearms burn-scored from tending mouths that glow orange too far off to be warm until you stand close enough for heat to find your face through the cool. They sleep on kiln-warm slabs, on floors that are returning heat to houses built directly above the firing chambers, while below them a cistern sweats cold. Their houses are not houses in any finished sense — crate upon crate, a room partitioned tonight, a barracks tomorrow, a kiln itself the day after if someone needs its bricks. They eat sitting down with their backs to the warm wall because standing in the middle of the room is to stand in the drip-path from the ceiling.

Farther from the heat, in the low wet places, are the people who move buckets. Condensation does not fall evenly. It finds certain joints, certain slabs, certain stretches of cold vault, and it falls there in a slow irregular pattern, drop, drop, pause, drop, that makes a hollow note in a jar completely out of proportion to its size. Someone has to be there to catch it, to shift the bucket before it overflows or to hold a skin under a seep while it threads through mortar. This is work for those who walk slow — the old, the very young, anyone whose back has learned not to lift. They know the ceiling by tune. Each drip has its own place and its own interval; when a passage is cut on the other side of the wall the tune changes, and they are the first to know a new room has been opened and is breathing cold air.

In the darkest rooms, away from the haul traffic, are the fungus farmers. They are the quietest people here, because the work is scraping. Sheets and crusts spread over damp clay — grey, orange-black, greenish in the phosphorescent light — and they are peeled off in ribbons and dried and ground and cooked into the flat sour bread that is almost everything anyone eats. The farmers live with the smell of it, faintly rotten and starchy, and with the other lives it feeds. In the beds of scrapings, in shallow basins where water stands in sledge-ruts, are pale worms eating fungus. Children pick them out as you might pick berries from a bush, and eat them raw or drop them in broth. This is protein, and it is also waste returned. The whole place is a loop of leftovers: fungus eating mortar-dust and starch and dead insects, worms eating fungus, people eating fungus and worms and scraping the walls clean enough for the next sheet to grow.

Every so often a farmer's child will be lowered, or will lower a net on a string, into a cistern. In the cracks between tanks, in seepage no wider than a hand, live blind fish. They are slow and translucent, you can see the gut moving through them when a lamp is held close, and they do not startle at the net the way surface fish do. They have no need to see. They survive on what drifts into the crack — spores, grit, the occasional moth that falls too far from the lamp. They grow over years to the size of a thumb, and when they are taken they are eaten whole in a thin broth that tastes of stone. The children who tend the cisterns talk to them as they wait, not because the fish hear, but because waiting in the cold with your arm in water is lonely and the fish's slow pulse in the dark is company.

On the warm kiln walls, where the brick faces bake, are minute pale lizards. They flicker and vanish when a torch passes, faster than a blink, and reappear a hand's breadth away with their throats pulsing. No one sets traps for them. They are considered lives that have chosen the most dangerous warm places and stayed anyway, and children chase them half-heartedly and call it a game and never catch one. They eat fungus mites and moths that stray too low.

The moths are the lamps' other flame. In the main haul-halls they come so thickly they become part of the light, a soft grey weather around each oil lamp, and the lamp-tenders sweep dead ones from the rim in shifts. A moth lives a day, perhaps less. It breeds in the fungus rooms, finds the light, dies on it. The few swallows that nest in the upper vaults — the only birds, and rare even so — have learned to cut through that weather and take them in flight. You hear them more than see them, a brief chirp echoing in a space that was solid yesterday, and children near the Sky Well look up imagined heights and imitate the sound. The Sky Well is the one unroofed shaft, where spoil is hauled up and rain, on the rare occasions it finds the hole, comes in as a sudden cold shower hissing on kiln-stone. The people who haul there are different from the rest in a way they are proud of. They are the surface people, though none of them has seen a surface. They get rained on. They get a fall of actual light too bright to look at directly, and they squint with their arms up like everyone else, but they come down wet on their shoulders and make a story of it for a week.

They do not have seasons. They have bells, rung hoarsely for shift changes, and the ebb and surge of work and the heat cycle of the kilns. They sleep in turns when they can, on hide bedding laid over warm slabs so the damp does not rise through it, learning to sleep despite the drips finding a neck or a bucket's hollow note or a hammer blow traveling through stone from three halls away and ending in your teeth. A house is never private for long. Corridors pass over corridors. A kitchen above a kiln, a cradle in a passage that was a barracks last month — the neighbors know your cough and your count and the weight of your step by the echo, and you know theirs, and that is the shape of intimacy here. There are niches behind solid towers, places no one else has found yet because someone cut a door overnight and no rumor has reached them, and those are the only true privacy, and they do not last.

There are listeners, too, who do little carrying. They sit with an ear to a wall that looks dry and tell you if it is solid or if there is air breathing on the other side. They tap in a code learned in childhood, two knocks, a drag of a knuckle, and listen for the answer in the stone. When a wall is to come down, it is not because an architect said so, though there are tally-keepers near the bells who decide which wall can spare its bricks for which ramp. It comes down because the listeners said it was solid, or because it was in the way of the next tier, and the crump of it travels through every hall that shares the mass.

The old are not necessarily respected for what they remember, because what they remember is wrong by morning. A hall they lived in for ten years is a kiln now. A door they used every day opens onto scaffolding over a drop, or onto a solid core that was never a room at all. The towers built as solid brick fill to give an outer wall its shape — whole structures no one will ever enter, their insides a stratigraphy of alternate brick and rubble and rejected fragments — baffle them. They sit by warmth and tell younger people what the shape was before, and the younger people listen the way you listen to a story about rain, because it might be true in the way of the Sky Well: imagination is how you hold a place that keeps remaking itself. The dead are built into that fill. That is not a secret and not a horror. A body is laid into the core of a tower while it is being raised course by course, and the next course is laid over it, and it becomes part of the solid that keeps the wall true. No one visits. The fungus will reach it eventually, or not. Either way it is inside the mass, and the mass is the place.

And so they live, not in rooms so much as in the intervals between making and unmaking: carriers passing bricks in a chant, tenders with orange light on their faces, bucket-movers who know the ceiling by its drips, scrapers in dark rooms feeding the bread that feeds the carriers, children picking worms and netting blind fish and chasing lizards that vanish, lamp-sweepers brushing moths from the flame, occasional swallows cutting circles in a vault that will be faced over next week. No one is left as found because no one was found. They are born into a city that is always being born, cool and damp and loud, and they learn to navigate it by sound and smell and the temperature of a wall under a palm, and to call that navigation a life.
