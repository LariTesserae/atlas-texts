Put your palm down at midday on a dark slab in the sun and the stone is warm enough to make you pull back — and under your hand, in the thin air, a whole population is already moving.

Mites first. Smaller than a pinhead, translucent, working the dry film on the basalt where dust has settled. Tiny spiders on even tinier legs, stringing no web you can see, hunting by touch in the lee of a grit grain. Flies that never leave the slab — born, fed, mated, and dead within a metre of the place they hatched, their whole world the warm face and its shadow-edge. Beetles the colour of wet stone, armoured, slow until the sun hits them, then suddenly very fast across the heat. They do not sing. Nothing here hums loud enough to hear unless you lower your head until your ear is almost on the rock. Life at this scale is not scarce; it is crowded into the only warm, damp inches there are, and it comes and goes with the light the way tide comes and goes with a moon.

Step off the slab and twenty paces of bare stone have nothing. Step onto a cushion and you are in a different climate.

The cushion plants are the oldest residents. Domes packed as tight as upholstery, each one a hundred small rosettes pressed together, furred with hairs that catch the light and turn silvered even when the sun is behind them. They do not grow up. Up is wind and cold. They grow in, and down, into the cool peaty soil that stays under them never dry and never wet. Grasses thread between them into a turf that springs underfoot, moss fills the troughs in velvet, and for a few short weeks in high summer the whole bowl is speckled: yellow composites no bigger than a coin, deep purple spikes that hold their colour against the white light, white stars the size of a thumbnail. They open at sunrise and close when the shadow reaches them, as if they are taking in only the hours they can afford.

They drink dew. There is no stream here to drink from. At night cold rock condenses the air and beads every underside of an overhang, and at first light water is a film on a leaf-hair, a sponge-pocket in the moss, a drip that falls so slowly you could watch it gather. The plants spend all day on that inhaled moisture. So do the animals. So, in the dampest hollows under a slab, does a small garden of moss that stays green all winter feeding solely on condensation, with thin stalagmites of clear ice growing upward season after season beside it, though no water ever visibly drips.

Lichens mark the sunward faces only — sulphur yellow, rust orange, pale green crusts that have colonised stone the cushions cannot use. They are the colour the place allows itself on bare rock. Everything else green is sudden, localised, fierce, and smells of wet earth and crushed herbs when the turf is bruised by a footstep.

Under those cushions, in burrows whose mouths are hidden in grass, live the warm-blooded ones. Pika-like mammals, hamlet-sized, built for bursts. They dart from one burrow mouth to another, emerge upright for a second to take the sun full on their bellies, sit and listen, vanish. Their whole day is a series of thresholds: out in the hard midday when flowers are open and insects swarm the slabs, in again the instant a falcon's shadow crosses the bowl. Above them, or rather in the teeth of the rim, snow finches nest in cracks that stay frosted at noon. They hive in the air above the flowers, calls thin and bell-like, immediately swallowed by altitude. They do not chatter. A call, then minutes of silence, then another.

Predator and prey are both present and both busy because the warm window is so brief. Everything must feed, mate, and hide between morning frost and evening freeze. The falcon does not live here — it lives in the cloud sea below and visits as a shadow, a pressure, a reason for the pika to disappear and the finches to go still in mid-air. The spiders eat the mites. The beetles eat the dead things. The finches eat the flies that live their whole lives on one slab. The cushions, slowly, eat the light.

That is the scale of belonging here: not herds, not flocks you can count from the centre, but constant small motions everywhere a pocket of warmth and damp exists, and long stillness everywhere else. At dawn every cushion is rimed, every windward stone white with rime, ice needles pushing up through soil cracks by invisible water. By ten o'clock it has melted. At midday the slabs burn and the micro-world boils. In the afternoon a squall may boil over the rim — ice grains hissing where they strike dark stone, darkening the rock for a minute, chilling the turf to steam — and then the light returns brighter for being reflected off cloud. Plants open and close. Animals emerge and vanish. It is a day of seasons.

Humans do not live here. They have only, faintly, passed through and left the shape of a life the mountain is already taking back shard by shard. A half-collapsed windbreak of hand-laid stone, shoulders high, roof long gone, a fire-pit blackened only lightly inside. A cairn on the highest tooth, built stone by stone without mortar. Single flakes of knapped obsidian left where someone sat to work them — they still give off the faintest warmth long after the stone around them has cooled — a bone needle, a twist of woven grass rope snagged and frayed to fibres, low stone fences that run a little way across the turf and then stop. Nothing built to last, nothing held. The frost wedges in at night and splits off new edges, tick and pop of stone breaking by cold, and the scree is still shards, not pebbles. Erosion is recent work. The rock has not yet had time to be rounded, and neither have we.

At night the lives withdraw into the cushions and under the slabs and into the rim cracks, folded against the cold that now falls off the sky. The flowers are shut. The mites are torpid in the grit. The moss garden drinks again from the air. You can hear your own breathing loud in your ears, and very faintly the stone settling, splitting, making itself new. The silence is not empty. It is held — by stone, by thin air, by all those small bodies waiting under cover for the light to arrive first, before the cold, and scatter the blue-black shadows again across the bowl.
