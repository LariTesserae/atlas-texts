Stay a while longer, and you stop listening for the things that are not there.

You listen instead for the other quiet. Not the absence of sound, but the sound that remains when insects and birds and water and wind in something have all been ruled out. The crunch of glass grit under your boots too bright for the dimness. The sifting of ash off a crest. The faint tick-tick-tick of cooling rock. The deep, soft boom from far below, felt more in your sternum than heard. It is easy at first to file all of that under geology, under the place itself. It takes time to understand that the ticking, the sifting, and the boom are not things the place does. They are things the things in it do.

Kneel down where the light is best, which is down, reflected back up off the pale ash near your feet.

The pan is cracked into polygons by heat alone, dry basins of bone-white dust that hold a footprint edge-for-edge until grit sifts in from a rise and fills it from below. Between the plates there are veins, black and thin as ink dried in a crack. At first they look like staining, like iron had bled there long ago and stopped.

Watch one long enough and the edge is not in the same place.

It is not moving fast. It will never move fast in your presence. Advance a fraction when the stone underneath warms, pull back very slightly when it cools, and over an hour the difference is a hair's width past whatever mark you have made with a shard. That is the whole of its stride, and it is enough. It does not walk. It incorporates. It takes heat through its contact with the plate and grows into the warmth, and when the warmth slackens it lets go a little, leaving a pale trail behind it in the ash, a track with no foot.

That black skin is everywhere the ground is warm enough to allow it, in lichen-like patterns across the white silica plates, across the rust-red sinter terraces where iron has baked into a crust, across the seams where basalt has freshly broken and the crystal grain is still running clean through the fracture. Up close it has a matte, slightly raised texture, like tar that dried mid-flow, or like a scab. It has no mouth. It has no green, no wetness, no rot. Its feeding is simply staying in contact with a temperature it can use, and its form is simply the shape of the warm places it has found.

It does not live alone in those warm places.

In the cracks, where the ember-line glows dull orange deep inside and warm air rises without sound, there are other growths. Fans of crystal, rosettes spiky and translucent, some no larger than your thumbnail, some as wide as a hand, opening toward the heat the way a plant on another world might open toward light. They are what makes the ticking. Not all of it, but much of it: a lattice expanding as it grows, contracting as it cools, clicking against itself in the still air. When a new fault sighs open a hundred paces away with its series of tiny dry pops, these fans near it will darken a little at the tips and open a little wider over the next minutes, as if pleased.

They are not plants. They do not eat. They grow where the chemistry and temperature allow, and they compete, in the slow, unhurried way of things that cannot move away, for the same narrow seams of warmth. A crust will creep up to a crack and stop where a rosette has taken the best of the heat; a rosette will close over a vein of black skin and starve it of the slightest lift in temperature. And the reverse is true. The black crust seems to hold the ash and the fine grit in place around a crack, to keep a seam from sealing too quickly, and the crystal fans shade the crust from the dull sideways sun. Where you find one cluster you almost always find both, and the buried versions of both, intact, a few inches down where the ground has risen over them.

That is their neighborhood, and it is sparse because the world they live on is sparse. Clusters are flung far apart across the pans, around a warm seam here, a fresh fracture there, a blistered terrace of rust sinter, and between them there is only the pale, empty ash going on to the next terrace and the next. The emptiness is not barrenness. It is ground that has not yet been found, or ground that will be lifted and folded and made warm again later. Newness is a frontier here.

Find a rise that was only a suggestion when you arrived and is unmistakably a ridge an hour later, shedding dust in slow silent curtains. Its fresh face is mirror-bright for a little while, the basalt blue-black where the light catches it. Almost at once, while it is still bright, there is new growth on it: a pale fuzz at the edges of the fracture, the thinnest black film in the grain, the first crystal points already ticking open. The dust falling from the crest carries it. When a crust dries and pulls back, when a crystal fan is buried and cracks under new weight, it sheds seeds in that sifting ash - crystal dust, mineral grit, black flakes light as pumice. The wind, such as it is, and the constant small falls of dust, carries those seeds sideways, and they settle on whatever has just risen, or whatever has just cracked open, and they start again.

Height here is youth. On a forest world the oldest things are the tallest. Here the newest, highest growths are the palest and cleanest because they have been exposed for the least time. As they age they darken with dust, they thicken, they are overtopped. Death is not decay. There is no water to rot them, no microbes to eat them, nothing to wear them down. Death is burial, sharp and clean. The ground rises over them in great shallow steps and seals them underneath, and the ash hardens over them, and under that rising surface they remain exactly as they were when they were covered, layer upon layer, a library of neighborhoods that never got to move.

Wait long enough in the west, where the field of pillars is sinking, and you begin to understand what the largest of them are.

They are not, at first, distinguishable from the landscape. Spires throwing long unmoving shadows. A single smooth dome rising out of a pan. Tilted slabs stacked at angles that look almost deliberate, crystal grain running fresh through the breaks. Columns in a field, too regular and too vertical to dismiss quickly as chance. But watch a pillar field for the better part of an hour and you will see one column settle, not topple, settle a hand's breadth into the soft ground that closes over it like wax, and stop. Another, and another. No crash. No roar. The turbulence here is muffled and enormous because it is happening too slowly to be loud.

These are the same black crusts and the same crystals, only thickened and lifted and given centuries to act. A crust that finds a long warm seam can grow on one side faster than the other, can lift itself slowly away from burial, can grow up a fracture as the fracture rises. A crystal rosette that is not buried can keep opening and closing, can keep adding lattice, can push a slab a fraction outward with each tick. Given enough time, a neighborhood becomes a body. Given enough time, a body becomes a spire, a dome, a slab-field, a forked pillar standing alone.

It is still not moving in any way you would recognize as moving. Its locomotion is the ground's. It sinks when the ground sinks under it; it rises when a ridge lifts it. It migrates only by being carried, sealed, re-exposed somewhere else when a pillar field collapses and uncovers older layers. That is why the west went down in steps and the east came up in steps while you watched: you were not watching erosion. You were watching inhabitants settle and rise, testing their weight, breathing under the blanket.

And the shapes that look built are the oldest of these bodies, or the shapes they happen to grow into when heat and fracture and weight repeat their habits.

The ring of upright slabs, half-buried now because the ground has risen around their bases: perhaps a colony that grew in a circle around a pan to keep the warmth in, the way your eye expects a wall to keep something out. The low rectangular enclosure of stacked stone, one wall missing, the interior filled level with ash that has calcified into a clean floor: perhaps a skin that grew up on four sides against the prevailing sift of dust, and then the inside sealed over, floor and all. The forked pillar, too vertical, too regular, its base being slowly swallowed: perhaps two crystal fans that grew side by side from the same seam and fused as they lifted.

Nothing moves in them. No one tends them. There is no metal, no manufactured glass, no tool mark, no device. If they were made, they were made before making had tools. Or they were not made at all, and making itself, when it arrives at shelter and monument by accident in a place where every shape must stand against rising ground and cooling contraction, will look like this. The difference may not matter to them. They are not shelters, they are not monuments. They are bodies that, from a human distance, resemble shelters and monuments because a human is looking for a place to stand, and they are a place to stand only in that they occupy it.

Under the ash pans, all of this is connected more than it looks.

What appears as separate clusters is likely one skin, one network, threaded through the cracks and under the fine ash, veins of black crust linking rosette to rosette, terrace to terrace, carrying heat the way a root system carries water on a wetter world. That is what travels when the deep boom comes: not a sound so much as a contraction, the whole network tightening at once as the rock cools, felt in your chest before you hear it. The ember-lines in the deepest cracks are the arteries of it, dull, persistent, not flickering like fire. The warm air rising without sound from a crack that glistens shut is an exhalation. The ground rising in shallow steps is an inhalation. The dust that hangs from a newly opened fault instead of dispersing is breath held.

You are standing inside a single, regional presence that has no head and no eyes, that orients itself only toward warmth and away from cold, that does not know you are there in any way finer than a slight local warming of the stone under your boots. Your footprints are an event in its life: a small, brief heat and pressure that a nearby crust might, over the next day, creep a fraction toward, and a nearby crystal might tick open toward, and that will then be half-filled, not worn away, by grit sifting off a rise. You are too fast for it to notice, and too brief. You are weather, passing over. It will go on at its own rate as you leave, covering your prints, lifting new ridges, seeding them with pale growth, burying the old in light a fraction more.

There are no calls here except the ticking and the boom. There are no droppings except the ash shed from crests. There are no nests, because the bodies themselves are the nests, and the rings are parents holding a pan of the newest crystals in the middle. There are no tracks except the pale trails where black skin advanced and pulled back.

Dark, and warm. High, and still being raised. It does not look inhabited if you are looking for eyes, or mouths, or movement across the ground. Look instead at the edges. Look at where the black film has moved a hair past your mark, where a crystal fan has opened a little wider since the fault popped, where a pillar has sunk a hand's breadth and stopped. Look at the fresh ridge, pale with youth, already colonized. Look at the old, dark colonies sealed sharp underneath, waiting in the dry dark to be lifted again.

What lives here lives at your feet and in the cracks and in the slow rising, by heat it drinks through its skin, by dust it sheds to seed the next seam, by waiting through burial and re-emergence. It is a mineral ecology because minerals are the only things here that have learned, or have never forgotten, how to live without water, without moving, without hurry, without wearing away.
