If you stand on it you will be tempted to say that nothing lives here.

It is too open, too high, too wet and cold, and everything that does live here has learned to make itself low and small and hard to see. But kneel down where the gravel is sorted into circles and the silt darkens after rain, and keep kneeling, and it begins to assemble itself around you, layer by layer, at the scale it actually prefers — a few centimetres above the ground.

The first inhabitants are not really moving much at all. They are the crusts.

On a stone that has not shifted in a few thousand years — a few thousand years is an old stone here, still new to the mountain — there will be a lichen living as a flat stain, rust-orange or dull green or pale grey, less than a millimetre thick but ten or twenty years wide. It grows at the speed of a fingernail. A whole human lifetime will add a ring you could cover with a coin. It does not root. It drinks directly from rain, from fog condensing on the cold rock, from dust settling out of the wind. In dry cold it shuts down and becomes brittle and inert. In wet cold it swells and breathes and carries on, indifferent to whether the sun is out. Some of these crusts are older than the moss around them, older than the reindeer trail that passes once a season. They are how this place first permits life to stay put.

Where a boulder breaks the wind on its lee side and meltwater lingers instead of sheeting away, the moss arrives next. Not a meadow — a cushion, tight as a pincushion, dark green in the centre, brownish at the old tips that have died back into peat. Each shoot is a few millimetres tall, each leaf holds water like a sponge. When you touch it in the morning it is colder than the air and it drips. When you touch it at midday it is surprisingly warm, because a low cushion in still air makes its own microclimate a degree or two hotter than the wind a hand's breadth above it. Everything else that lives here waits for that degree.

Inside the moss is a whole country. Fungi thread through the wet litter, white filaments invisible until you pull a tuft apart, decomposing the older moss into a dark, sour peat that smells faintly of bog even at this altitude. Algae film the surface of the pools that form in the lowest polygon centres — green slime on black water, so thin you can only see it when the light rakes sideways. In that damp, peaty mat live the animals that do most of the living here, by number if by nothing else.

Springtails, whitish and no longer than a grain of rice, jump by the thousand when your shadow passes. They do not have wings or true legs built for walking; they have a forked tail folded under them that snaps and throws them a body-length in any direction. They graze on fungi and algae and decaying moss, mouthparts scraping. Among them move mites, armored beetle-like things less than a millimetre long, slow and deliberate, and nematodes wriggling in the water films between moss leaves, and tardigrades — translucent, eight-legged, 0.3 millimetres — that can dry out, freeze solid, and come back when the next rain wets them. Rotifers spin in the melt pools. This is not a metaphorical food web. It is literal, square-centimetre production, running whenever the temperature is above freezing, stopping when it skins over at night.

In mid-summer the tempo changes all at once in the melt pools. The water is black and still at dawn, covered with a skin of ice you can press and hear crack. By ten the skin melts. Chironomid midges live down in the mud of the pool bottom as red worms, building tubes out of silt, filtering algae. Then, for two or three weeks, they pupate, rise to the surface, and split open in clouds so dense at midday they shimmer in the air above the water. A pool the size of a bathtub can release tens of thousands in an afternoon. They do not bite. They have mouths that barely work. Their entire adult life is a few hours of flight, mating, and being eaten. They rise, they mate over the water, they fall. The air above a moss mat on a warm afternoon is full of them, a faint vibrating haze you hear before you see — a high, steady note that has nothing to do with wind.

Everything else here is timed to that pulse.

Butterflies come out to bask on the warmest stones, the windward gravels that were sandblasted smooth in winter. They are small and dark — wings dusted with black scales so they can absorb heat from a sun that never gets strong — and they do not fly far. A few metres from stone to sedge to stone, low, stopping often to raise their wings to the light. Their caterpillars, when there are any, feed on the sparse sedge and saxifrage in the wet hollows. Beetles live under the lifted stones in the polygon rims, turning them over in the frost cycle, hunting springtails. Wolf spiders hunt by sight in the same rims, females carrying a white egg sac under their bodies, then a rucksack of spiderlings on their backs, hundreds of eyes moving low over the gravel. They do not build webs here; there is nothing to anchor them and too much wind to bother. They run, short bursts, and freeze, perfectly the colour of wet silt.

The plants that feed all of this are almost embarrassingly few. Sedge tufts in the warmest, wettest low places, leaves stiff and narrow, pressed flat. A mat of *Dryas* or saxifrage no bigger than your hand on a protected ledge. The moss cushions themselves. A black speck of peat with a few green shoots. Between them, for metres in every direction, is bare patterned ground. That is the rule: life does not spread. It accumulates where water pauses, where a stone makes a windbreak, where the permafrost thaw goes a centimetre deeper. Each tuft is an island with its own population of mites and springtails and midges, and the distance between islands is too far for most of them to cross. The plateau is an archipelago.

The larger animals cross the archipelago.

Ptarmigan nest directly in the gravel, often on the raised rim of a frost polygon where the stones are sorted finest. The hen is indistinguishable from the ground — mottled grey-brown in summer, matching the wet silt to the shade — and she sits through wind and sleet with only her eyes moving. Her chicks are precocial, up and running within hours, picking at midges and tiny beetles in the moss, peeping under her wing when a raven passes overhead. When you step too close she explodes upward in a whirr of wings and white underparts, all that camouflage gone in a second, and the chicks scatter and freeze again. That is their entire strategy: be the ground, then be gone.

Ravens live along the fault scarps. A pair will ride the same updraft for years, wings black against low cloud, croaking so far off that the sound arrives long before the bird is visible against the grey. They are the plateau’s scavengers and its observers. A reindeer that dies in late winter, a hare caught by a fox — the ravens will be there by midday, dropping in a steep, silent spiral. They remember where the good thermals are over the warm pools, and they remember where the fox dens are.

Reindeer do not live on the plateau the way people live in a town. They cross it. A herd enters from the far edge in the morning and does not leave until evening, a thin column snaking across the flats, sometimes twenty animals, sometimes two hundred. Cows, calves born in late May, still wobbly at first, bulls in velvet antler later. In the cold their bodies steam. In fog there is a visible cloud around each animal where its exhaled breath condenses and its warm coat meets the cold air. They move slowly, grazing not the grass — there is almost none — but the moss mats and the lichen they can scrape from stones, and the sedge in the hollows. They leave dung, dark pellets full of undigested moss and fungi, and that dung becomes a tiny, intense nursery for midges and beetles, which become food for the birds that are gone by nightfall. A herd can strip a moss cushion in a day and not come back for a year. Their abundance is a temporal thing: the plateau is full of reindeer because, over a season, reindeer have been everywhere on it, briefly.

Mountain hares live low in the hollows where snow lingers longest. In June they are grey-brown, matching the silt. In late spring, when a cold snap brings new snow that sticks in the lee seams, they turn white again in patches, a molt timed to a weather that cannot be predicted. They sit absolutely still for hours, ears flat, black tips showing, trusting that stillness is camouflage. At dusk they move in short hops, biting sedge shoots, and their droppings, like the reindeer’s, feed the insects.

Arctic foxes den in the scree at the foot of the scarps, under a slab that has not moved since the last avalanche of fresh rock. In June there will be pups, clumsy and dark-faced, tumbling out onto the warm stones. The adults trot the ridges at dawn, following the same lines reindeer use, noses down. They hunt ptarmigan chicks, hares, and, opportunistically, the same midges and beetles the spiders eat, but at a larger scale — a fox can work a moss mat for an hour, nose in the peat, snapping at springtails by the thousand. When prey is plentiful they cache it, burying a hare under a stone for later, a small act of planning in a place with no storage.

Pikas and marmots stay near the rock piles. A pika will sit on a warm boulder and whistle, a sharp *eek* that carries across a basin, then vanish between stones. It spends the short summer cutting sedge and moss and stuffing it into haypiles under the rocks to eat in winter, when everything is frozen and the wind has stripped the gravel bare. A marmot whistles a longer, more alarming note, and its whole colony — a few adults, last year’s young — dives for the burrow entrances in the scree. They will hibernate for eight or nine months, curled together in a chamber below the frost, breathing once every few minutes. The plateau’s summer is so brief that much of its animal life is spent either feeding frenetically or sleeping through the other ten months.

Nothing here is dense. A fox follows a hare across ten kilometres of empty ground. A raven follows a reindeer herd for a day. A spider’s whole territory is the rim of one polygon. The lives are distributed as a thin film — moss on a stone, mites in the moss, midges over the pool, a ptarmigan chick picking midges, a raven watching the chick, a fox watching the raven — and the film stretches to the horizon because the substrate does, not because it is crowded in any one place.

At night it all pauses. The pools skin over. The springtails go dormant in the moss. The butterflies fold their wings under a stone. The reindeer that crossed that day are now below the treeline that does not exist, on lower ground where the soil is deeper. The hares curl into their forms in the snow seams. The fox pups are back in the den. The ice in the cracks expands, lifts a stone another fraction of a millimetre, and in the morning the sorting will start again, and the midges will rise again, and something will exhale steam in the fog and cross the frame again, leaving only dung and footprints in wet gravel for the next crossing.

That is who lives there: not settlers, but crossers and waiters, at every scale from a lichen that grows a fingernail in a lifetime to a raven that can cross the entire plateau before lunch, all of them low to the ground, all of them timed to water that cannot sink and cold that never really leaves, all of them making a living out of a place that is still, literally, making itself.
