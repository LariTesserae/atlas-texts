At first it seems like nothing lives here at all, and that is almost true. What lives here has had to give up almost everything you would recognize as living — color, eyes, hurry, growth. It does not warm the place, it does not darken it, it does not move through it the way a thing moves through a room. If you crouch in the dark long enough, the first sign is not a creature. It is a coolness under your fingertips where everything else is warm.

Along the slowest seam of seepage, where the water moves without quite deciding to move, and on the shallow shelf above the pool where condensation gathers thickest, there is a felt. Not moss. Not a plant. It has no reason to be green because nothing here has ever needed to catch light, and there is no soil to root in besides stone that is still half stone and half soup.

It appears as patches, never larger than a palm, of fine white hair. Rootlet-thin filaments interlaced until they mat, and fungal pads soft as a tongue-touch, soft and, improbably, cool against skin that is already sweating in the sauna air. They grow in tufts and then they stop, not because they have reached a limit but because the place does not permit limits to be reached. A thread extends, meets the edge of its own patch, and thins. Another thickens in the middle. No part outruns another. They feed not on sun but on the warm dissolved mountain itself — on the sulfur-metal tang in the seep, on the mineral salts precipitating and dissolving at the same rate on the wall. You could say they eat rock, but it is more accurate to say they live inside the rock's indecision, taking a little of what gives, giving back a little of what takes.

They smell faintly, if you put your face close enough to risk breathing the wet air deeper, of yeast and wet stone. They do not spread. If a spore lifts on the steam it lands, makes a faint new hair, and is absorbed again. The felt is less a colony than a pause in the geology, a place where the building and unbuilding has agreed to be alive for a while.

The pool has a skin, and the skin is alive too, though it pretends to be only mineral. That pale film that cracks when a droplet lands and seals again in the same instant — underneath it, and in it, is a broth of bacteria too small to see, feeding on the same warm chemistry. They turn the pool's stillness into something metabolized. They are the reason the water tastes of salt on the lips and never sours, never clears. They bloom and they are eaten and they bloom at exactly the same pace, so the level holds. If you watched long enough you would see the film dimple, not from the drip alone, but from a slow cellular exhalation rising through it. Then it settles. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is wasted. That is their entire history, repeated in the same order as the drip, the tick, the hiss.

And then, far less often, there is the resident. The only one who would count as an animal if you needed a census, and even he barely counts.

He is eel-pale, blind in the way of things that have never been offered light to see by, no longer than a forearm, no thicker than your wrist. He does not swim so much as slide through the pool, a slow pale ribbon displacing almost nothing, his body filmed with the same warm water. He has no eyes that matter, and no hurry at all. He tastes the water with his whole skin, he tastes the felt with his mouth when he rests — not eating it in any vigorous sense, but grazing the bacterial film from the rock, taking in the mineral soup the felt leaves behind.

Most of the time you would never know he is there, because he wedges himself into a crack in the wall, head tucked, flank pressed to the slick stone, and breathes. His breathing is so slow you could miss it entirely if you were watching for rise and fall in the normal way. Once, perhaps, in what outside would be an hour, a gill-flap opens a fraction, a flank swells a fraction, and settles. Once in a very long while — if long while means anything here — he un-wedges and slides from the pool to the mud and back again, leaving the only true disturbance the pool ever knows. He does not grow, or he grows and shrinks with the same equilibrium as everything else. He has been there for as long as anyone has ever come here to leave a mark, and he will be there for as long as qualifies as after. He is not lonely. Loneliness requires a wish for company, and he has edited that out.

The worms do not stay long enough to be called residents. They are minute, translucent things that live in the half-liquid mud, and you will usually only find their tracks: fresh, neat, shallow furrows leading from the edge of the pool out across the soft rock and then to nowhere, as if the maker turned back on itself, or sank, or simply decided that this was enough movement for a lifetime. The tracks do not fade. They do not fill with sediment. They stay, because nothing here is permitted to become old, and then, without explanation, there are new ones beside them. They are made, presumably, by bodies too small and too slow to catch in the act, surfacing only to prove they passed through.

It is possible that the human was also only a passage, a temporary inhabitant of the same kind. The low ring of palm-sized stones by the pool, the bowl pecked into the soft rock with a hammerstone, the flaked shards half-sunk where the mud would take them, the three parallel grooves scratched and then stopped — these were made by someone who crouched here, whose knees went wet in the mud, whose breath made no fog because the air was already saturated, whose sweat ran with the mountain's sweat and could not evaporate. Someone who tried, in a single visit, to make a tool and make a mark, because there was nothing else to do with hands in a place that never taught anyone anything. Their edge is still sharp. Their bowl never caught more than the drip it was meant for. They did not live here. They lived *in* here for a little while, and left the proof that a warm-blooded thing can be in this pocket without joining it.

So the inhabitants are: a felt that eats stone and decides not to spread, a film that eats water and decides not to rise, a blind pale sleeper who breathes once in an age and decides not to wake fully, and a scatter of minute track-makers who decide not to arrive. And, very briefly, a kneeling human who decided to try to make something sharp.

None of them age in the way you do. None of them accumulate. The felt replaces what it loses in the same thread, the bacteria replace what the drip breaks in the same film, the blind one replaces his own slow breath in the same crack. If you left and returned in a year, you would find the same patch, the same skin, the same pale sleeper wedged in the same crack, breathing at the same interval, and the same fresh stone tools that have never been touched. They have learned, each in its own sparse way, to balance so exactly that nothing has room to happen — and to keep living anyway, in the instant of formation, without ever finishing becoming alive.
