If you come looking for who lives here, you will at first find almost no one.

Nothing moves quickly and nothing makes a sound that travels. From the ridge the hollow looks empty between its scattered houses, its schoolhouse bell silent, its long low factory still in the grey morning. The paths are empty. The moss glades between blocks are empty. And then, as the cold gives up a little and the breath stops showing, you begin to notice that the things you took for buildings are not quite still. They are beaded. They are drinking. They breathe in a way that takes hours.

What lives here, for the most part, does not walk at all.

The trees were trained to it a long time ago. They stand in straight trunks with their branches cut to right angles, holding up wires and roofs and small bridges as columns would, creaking faintly when a root-pillar shifts its weight. Hedgerows are fences. Root-pillars are telegraph poles. Canopy vines shade the steam pipes that surface along the streets, and foundation walls are not stone at all above the first course, but root thick as a fist, bound tight with living vine, woven and re-woven as it grows. Above a stone base, a house wall is woven plant, with its window openings kept open only by pruning. The vines come up to the frame and turn, precisely, and frame it again. When a caretaker passes with shears, the cut end weeps a little and seals itself by noon.

Roofs are mats of house-moss and leaf that are cut back for harvest and grow thick again over the winter, soft enough that rain runs off without force and is taken back up. The ground itself is the same growth, pressed flat: root and moss and decayed leaf in a spongy mat that gives underfoot and dampens a shoe through the sole. Where a path has been kept, it has worn to settled stone, concave with generations of walking. Where it has not, the mat closes over in weeks, like skin over a seam. The inhabitants close the gaps between them.

In the factory, which is the heart and the reason, the rooted ones work.

Broad bands of living fibre run as conveyors, carrying cut plant-stuff from one machine to the next. They are not belts. They grow, they bear, and when they are torn by a loom or a press they knit themselves shut overnight, leaving a pale scar that is soon indistinguishable from the rest. Along the walls the pipevine and the loom-bindweed are trained up through the looms themselves, feeding them. The clack, pause, clack of the loom is answered by a sigh from a pulp press turning leaf and stem into stiff panels for walls and boxes — panels that will in time be woven into other walls. Seed plants in rows behind the power shed are pressed for oil, and the oil feeds the engines that sit in brick towers clothed in ivy.

The engines do not run hot. They turn, steady and cyclical, and their exhaust warms the vines that insulate them. Their drive-shafts pass through root-bellows — great hollow bulbs of gourd-vine that swell with heat and contract with cool, slowly pumping air through the hall all day. By mid-morning the fern-banks along the pipes lift faint clouds of spores that settle again on the damp iron, on the brass valves gone green, on the black stone of the drains. At midday the growth on the south-facing walls visibly leans, a fraction of an inch at a time, toward the sun and toward the warmth of the engines. At evening the whole town exhales, moisture rising off the root-mat and off the warm iron and off the leaves, and by night everything is beaded again, feeding.

They have learned the geometry of the place and they reproduce it. Seedlings spring up in perfect rows along the buried cable runs, where iron salts have leaked into the soil in a straight line. Leaves on the factory wall grow in a pattern that echoes the window frames behind them. Moss forms neat rectangles around gravestones and doorsteps, as if it remembered where a threshold should be. Nothing is wild here, and nothing is ruined. A machine left still for a season will find a root curled around its axle, using it as a trellis. A building left unrepaired will not collapse; it will be held up, increasingly, by what grows through it. This is their social life: accommodation, over a very long time, until stone has sunk and iron has rounded and paint has weathered to grey and the balance is alive.

The brief lives are briefer still, and they do not compete.

At dusk, when the overhead lamps come on along their straight lines, a small pale colony of moths gathers around the warm lamp-glass. They are the only insects in the air. In the day they are hidden under leaves, in the held moisture, waiting. Their wings make no sound that the moss does not take. In the drains, after the ten-minute rain, a frog appears in the damp black stone and is gone by morning — whether the same frog returning or another from the same shallow hollows is hard to say, and perhaps does not matter, since the hollow is only fifteen minutes long and the frog needs only that much damp to be somewhere. In the corners of the loom hall, spiders occupy the dark places between fibre and iron, stringing fine work across the slow-moving plant-stuff, undisturbed by the clack of the looms.

That is almost all the animal life there is. Birds pass over the ridge but do not settle. There is no birdsong. The plants have quietly outcompeted everything else for the right to live here, and the few that remain do so by asking very little: a warm pane of glass, a wet drain, a corner.

The walking lives are fewest, and most easily missed.

A single cat walks a fixed patrol between the workshops, stepping only on stone. It does not cross the mat, does not disturb the seedlings in their rows, does not leave prints in the moss. Its paws find the kept paths, the settled stone, the rims of the drainage rills that run mostly empty and damp. It is noticed, as the caretakers are, by its regularity — the same route at the same slow pace, the same pause at a valve, the same return.

The human caretakers are only a handful, scattered among the scattered houses. You rarely see them in the same block twice. You see their effects: a path kept clear where the mat would otherwise close, a vine trimmed back precisely from a window frame, a valve turned, a roof cut for harvest, an oil can set down beside an engine. They walk with pruning tools and oil cans, their sleeves beaded with condensation when they leave at evening, their boots damp through the sole. They live among the plant walls they tend, in houses whose roofs grow thick again after they cut them. They are not many enough to fill the glades between blocks — thirty paces, forty, of open moss between one presence and the next — and they do not try to. They keep the town in its settled state: pruning the right-angle vines, clearing the drains, harvesting what the factory needs, replanting the rows along the cables.

So the population of the hollow is not a population in the usual sense. It is a mat that drinks and swells at dawn, a set of bellows that breathe with the engines, a band of fibre that knits its own wounds, a tree that holds a wire, a moth at a lamp, a frog in a drain, a cat on stone, a spider in a corner, and a few people who pass through with shears, keeping the geometry legible.

At evening, as the light thins to amber and then to blue, the factory ticks and then stills, the bellows deflating slowly. The moths settle. The cat finishes its patrol. The caretakers go home to their woven walls. Condensation begins again on the pipes, on the iron gate, on the leaves and the stone and the roots that make up the ground, and the inhabitants settle into their night state — still working, still damp, still growing in their own established way, waiting for the cold to settle first again before the light.
