You hear them before you understand there is a place to contain them.

It is not one sound. It is a layering, a stack of smaller sounds that have been going on so long the stone has learned to resonate with them. At the bottom, close to the floor, a low irregular hum that you feel in your teeth. On top of that, always, the high whine of insects — not a swarm moving past, but a cloud that lives here, hanging over the damp pockets, shimmering. Their wings make a baseline note, thin and constant, rising to a roar when they break.

They break often. All day, things break the air and the air reforms.

The birds are the weather of the bowl. At dawn the rim, which from the plateau looks like nothing, is black with them — small, hollow-boned, long-tailed, packed into every crack and ledge where a vine has not already taken hold. They leave in waves. Not a flock in the grand sense, but a ripple. One lifts, scolding the others, and within seconds the whole bowl is full of them, wheeling in a space no larger than a courtyard, stooping to the floor, scattering off the stone, rebounding. Their calls strike the rim and come back, and come back a third time, as if there were a farther wall that also throws them back. The creatures who live here do not notice the extra echo. They have grown up inside it.

When they settle, it is not quiet. They settle in processions, noisy, argumentative, wings still beating after their feet have found the ledge. When they lift again — and they lift on a cue no bird makes — the insects rise with them.

The pillars are the clock. Six of them, black and fused, standing in a loose line across the middle of the bowl. They are cold in a warm place, unnaturally cold, and everything living has arranged itself around that cold as plants arrange around sun. Condensation beads on them long before it beads on anything else, gathers, and drips in a steady rhythm down their faces into the channel cut in the floor. Around each base the moss grows in a perfect ring, bright and spongy, slowly expanding year by year in a spiral no one tends. Tiny flowering plants are woven through the moss, opening and closing through the day, their heads lifting toward the moisture that never quite touches the ground.

Water hesitates near them. Sometimes a drip falls from a vine and meets a bead rising from a pillar halfway down and they hang together for a breath, merged, before gravity remembers and they fall as one. The frogs at the shallow stone bowls do not look up at this. They sit with only their eyes above the black, still water, throats puffing in and out, until a lizard no longer than a forearm darts from warm stone and the whole pool erupts in a splash that rains back down on the moss.

Lizards are everywhere the sun finds the floor. They bask on every sunlit rock, flicking tongues, chasing each other in sudden sprints of twenty metres — a journey here, a whole migration across bare desert of stone between one green island and the next. They are the fastest things on the ground, except for the mammals.

The mammals run in troops. Grey, long-legged, quick as sparks, they cross those same bare stretches between the vegetation, pausing to groom, to listen with ears up, to dart into moss when a bird stoops. Their feet are silent on the ancient stone, which is warm underfoot and stained dark where moisture has passed over it a thousand times. They follow routes worn by use, not by feet, and in midday, when the small rain cell forms directly overhead and drums on leaves and stone for ten minutes on one half of the bowl while the other half stays in sun, they call louder over the rain, as if volume could continue a conversation.

Between presences are long stretches of emptiness. That is the sparseness. But each island pulses.

The ferns are bigger than a person, unrolling in spirals in the morning and folding at night with a speed you can almost watch. Their fronds are translucent where light passes through. On the eastern side they all twist the same way, each frond helically wound toward the cold beneath the floor, toward something dormant that still regulates the damp. The broad-leaved shrubs crowd the water bowls, leaves beaded and sweating. Vines hang from the rim in curtains, roots seeking cracks, growing a handspan toward a cold pillar and then, for no reason, away.

The trees are only a handful, far apart, knotted and ancient, anchoring themselves to the bowl with roots in cracks that have not changed in longer than anything living can remember. They are not quiet. Beetles drill in their trunks. Ants march in lines a centimetre wide up and down the bark, carrying nothing, carrying everything. Spiders lower themselves on threads that catch the light and hang motionless until the air moves. Orchids and lichen fur the bark, opening white mouths in the warmth. To walk from one tree to the next you cross a desert, but each tree is a village.

Even the built things are inhabited, in the way minerals are inhabited by moss. The low arc of chest-sized blocks set without mortar, answering to no doorway, is a basking wall for lizards and a hunting ground for the mammals. The discs sunk into the stone, engraved with fine channels, carry water drop by drop to the moss — a delivery system now used, unknowingly, by insects who walk the channels. The half-dome of matte grey ceramic is bone-dry inside even in rain, and its inner surface is smooth and empty; small animals nest in the corners of the intact hut-shell near the eastern rim because it is the only roof the bowl has ever made. A panel set into the dome clicks softly when a hand passes near it — not touching, proximity only — with a pinprick of light. Moths and flies pass near it all day. It answers them, perhaps. No one with hands remains to ask what it was for.

Whatever made the pillars engineered beyond current understanding. Whatever made the panel also. The makers are fine dust now, worn away like everything else the plateau has filed down to its final form. What remains is less machine than mineral with intention. One pillar has a seam, a crack that is present when you look away and gone when you return, leaving only a faint warmth where it healed. Nothing living touches it. It repairs itself the way some stones in the bowl are warm where the sun has not touched and cold where it rests, in patches that shift slowly through the day, and the way shadows lag a fraction behind the bodies that cast them in the still humid air. The strangeness is not violent. It is quiet and particular, and it has become ordinary.

Everything here lives on separate clocks that occasionally sync.

Plants open in the morning. Insects emerge in pulses after rain, live furiously for hours, vanish. Flocks rise when the pillars hum and settle when they are silent, and the hum rises and falls without pattern, sometimes for minutes, sometimes for hours. When it peaks, the mist above the bowl thickens and the birds lift. Predator and prey negotiate the small distances constantly: a stoop, a scatter, a chase across twenty metres, a chase that in a larger world would be a journey. A larger, silent raptor from somewhere above the plateau cuts through the slot occasionally, and the insect cloud breaks around its passage and reforms behind it like water around a stone, the whine tearing and stitching itself closed.

At midday the rain cell arrives and the whole ecology shouts. Drops hammer the ferns bigger than a person until the leaves bow, the frogs croak over it, the birds scream over it, the stone darkens in blotches. Then it stops as abruptly as it began. Steam rises from the warm floor. The chorus resumes, higher and wetter. The shallow bowls never grow. The stone drinks them back. Life has learned to build around intermittent moisture, to drink condensation bead by bead at dawn, heavy enough to darken the rock.

By evening the bowl cools at the edges first. Condensation returns to the pillars first, then to the smooth stone, then to the backs of the frogs sitting in their bowls. The birds return to the rim in noisy processions. The mammals retreat to the moss. The hum slows. The light through the slot thins to a single shaft, then to nothing.

For a moment the place is dark and quiet and damp. Then the insects begin again, and the drips, and the distant patient pulse from below the stone, where something large and dormant still breathes cold upward through the floor to keep the air wet enough for all of this — for the moss rings to expand, for the ferns to twist, for the pools to hold their black water a little longer before being drunk.

It is a world at the end of its erosion and the middle of its life: ancient rock settled smooth, warm and lush in its hollow, sparse in its structures, overwhelming in its animals, threaded through with remnants that still gather mist, hold cold, repair themselves, and answer to proximity — long after the hands that made them turned to the same dust as everything else the plateau wore away.
