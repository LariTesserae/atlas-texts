You do not meet them at first. You meet what they stand in.

In a hollow, perhaps three feet down from the bare stone, the ground becomes soft all at once. The moss there is not a skin. It is deep as a mattress, cold through to wet peat underneath, and it holds your footprint for a week. Leaves fallen into it from the low trees above do not rot away in a season. They lie pale and recognizable for decades, and the ground thickens over them, layer by patient layer. Nothing hurries the decay here, and so decay itself becomes a kind of architecture.

This is the lowest form of life on the Table, and the most widespread. Lichen first — a yellow crust on the warm side of a stone, a dull green smear where the damp sits longest — then moss where the condensation gathers and stays. Lichens do not stay where you saw them. A pattern you noticed at midday on a stone a mile away may be on the far wall of a new room by evening, or frosted on the inside of a window. They are not traveling, exactly. The Table remembers a pattern and applies it again where the pattern fits. To live here, at any height, is partly to be repeated.

Higher in the same hollow, the ferns rise — knife-ferns, taller than you are, their fronds arching and heavy, their edges still sharp despite everything else being worn soft. Between them the grass grows thick as reeds, carrying bells of grey-blue flowers. There are no insects to come to those bells. There never were. Yet the bells close tight against the cold all day and open, each one, in the warmest minute of the day, as if expecting a visitor who will not arrive, and then close again. A fern that unfolded yesterday will curl tight into bud in a hard frost and then, impossibly, open again from the bud, as though yesterday had been a practice run for today.

That is the rhythm of almost everything that lives here. It does not progress so much as it recurs. Frost heaves a pavement a hair's breadth, moss fills the crack, frost heaves it wider — and sometimes, on a night colder than the usual cold, the pavement is shallower at dawn than it was at dusk. A step worn hollow in the morning is less hollow tomorrow. The crack in a wall seals itself with a soft ticking and stands straight for an hour, proud and impossible, before the sun returns and it sighs back into its slump. Living things follow the same settled logic. What fits the oldest stable form is kept. What does not fit is revised, gently and inexorably, until it does.

Taller still are the trees of the green pools: low birches and tallow-trees, stunted by altitude but dense, their trunks wrapped in hanging moss like old hair. They look, if you cross enough bare stone to reach them, like someone who has stood in one place too long and been forgotten by the weather. They are spaced enormously. You can walk for six days over open stone after leaving a fern-clump without seeing another living thing taller than your boot. The Table is sparsely inhabited in the way an old cathedral is sparsely inhabited. The presences are few, and the emptiness between them is part of the design.

This is why, when you come to the first town, the people at first look like part of the planting.

A town is only a dark interruption in the stone from a distance, low walls on walls, a pavement of flat slabs with an older pavement visible where a corner has lifted with frost, and under that the cobbles of the road before that. Up close, along the walls, run the planted beds — deep soil, dark and rich with those decades of undecayed leaf-fall — and standing in the beds, politely, are the rootfolk.

Their feet are not visible. They do not need to be. They are rooted in the soil of the bed the way a tree is rooted, with dark taproots showing where the soil has washed away. They wear woven cloaks of moss-fibre and grass, wrapped close against the wind, and their hands move above the cloaks with a surprising delicacy. The hands are jointed and woody, bark split over the knuckles. At the looms in the open workshops, over racks of hand tools — stone hammers worn to a shine, copper awls and needles, simple iron knives — those hands are endlessly patient. Nothing here is new and nothing is quite broken. Everything is repaired by hand, forever, by laying the same stone back in the same place until the place remembers it. They tend their own fingers the same way.

They speak very little, and when they do it is in low, dry clicks that carry almost nowhere in the quiet. You have to be close to hear them, close enough to hear also the stone ticking as it cools, close enough that a click sounds like a knuckle cracking or a small twig of bark snapping. There is no need for a voice to carry. No one here is in a hurry to be heard across a courtyard, and no birds are listening overhead.

They grow, slightly, in the summer — a finger's length, then prune it, then grow it again. In the long cold snaps they stand perfectly still, and moss sprouts on their shoulders and the tops of their cloaks until they shake it off in a slow, green rain. They do not sleep, in any animal sense. Stillness is simply what the cold asks of them. Warmth asks for a little growth. The shaking-off is not grooming. It is harvesting, perhaps, or simply the moment when the weight becomes impolite to carry any longer. The moss-fibre for new cloaks must come from somewhere, and the Table provides it continuously, on their own shoulders if no one gathers it sooner.

What they eat, if eating is the word, they take where they stand. No meat is kept in these towns — there are no dogs sleeping in doorways, no chickens, no flies around it, because there is no it. The channels of carved stone catch the condensation from the roofs and lead it to cisterns sunk in the earth, and from the cisterns and the black, shallow pools ringed with frost and moss both, the water tastes of stone and lichen and is always, somehow, slightly out of date. They drink that, and their roots drink the peat that the leaf-fall builds up year after year. Decay that never hurries is, here, a pantry.

The towns themselves are gardens that have learned to be urban. Pavements, courtyards walled against the wind, beds along the walls where the rootfolk stand, turf and woven grass and hand-hammered copper roofs — everything is tended in the same slow way the green pools are tended, except that the tending is done by people-shaped plants who stand in the beds and do the tending. Granaries with stone doors stay shut against the weather. The kiln is cold now. The tools are laid back on their racks in the same places. A storehouse shown to you in the morning may have, by evening, an extra room at the back, well-built and empty, and the rootfolk simply add the room to their maps, or redraw the maps, in ink that smells faintly of damp and is still wet in the morning. A street you entered from the south may deposit you facing north across ground you have not crossed. They do not seem dismayed. A town adding a storey in the night and losing it by afternoon is no more troubling to them than a fern curling and opening again; and in the losing, a basket that was on the upper floor will be found, carefully, on the ground, as if someone had taken it down.

Their shadows, when they have them at noon, are occasionally cast by trees that are not there, pointing for ten minutes exactly opposite to where the sun requires. At other times a shadow points the wrong way entirely. The rootfolk do not correct themselves. Their roots stay in the bed that remembers them, and if the bed moves in the night — if the town revises itself around them — they are, perhaps, replanted with the same politeness with which they were planted in the first place. The older footprints pressed into a town's mud, the only tracks on the whole Table that are not yours, belong to a time when they were younger, or smaller, or still learning to stand still.

That is the whole of their society, and it is more than enough. No one walks the bare stretches. No one hunts, or herds, or keeps animals, or makes noise that would disturb the hush when you step off stone and onto moss. Between towns, life thins back down to lichen, moss, fern, bell-flower, birch — the same forms, patiently repeating in every damp hollow the Table offers. In the towns, those forms gather and stand up and learn to weave and repair and redraw their maps.

When you leave, you leave the way you came, which is not the way you came. Behind you the town does not fade. At a certain distance it becomes a pattern you can see in the lichen under your boots, in the frost on a stone that has not yet rained. Ahead of you there is only the continuing Table, and in the next green pool the bells will open for their one warm minute to no one, the ferns will practice unfolding, the moss will thicken another fraction under another decade of leaves. That is how the rootfolk live, and how the things they are rooted among live: not ending, not beginning, but practising, in the same slow breath, how to go on.
