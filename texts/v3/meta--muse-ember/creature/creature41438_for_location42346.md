If you stay long enough inside the handspan of shadow under the slab, the crowd stops looking like a crowd.

At first it is only growth, and growth addressed to every surface at once. Ribbons of algae come down from the pale blue-grey roof, dark olive and translucent as wet paper, and fans of weed rise up from the silt to meet them, and in the middle they do not so much meet as braid. There are hairs and braids and moss-like cushions, faintly ivory where they have thickened for lack of light. There is nowhere empty to put a hand. You push a curtain of filaments aside and it closes behind you more slowly than it opened, rearranged but the same.

That is the first inhabitant, if you can call a mat a who: the algae themselves, living in the seep of bruised green that comes through the curtain and is caught, and lost, and caught again. They do not spread the way the basin outside spreads, flat and sideways over cold silt. Here they spread inward, from floor and ceiling and walls as if those words had been negotiated away. Up and down are only directions they happen to have been growing when they met. In the braid, direction is lost. A ribbon descending and a fan ascending are the same kind of thing, turned.

Under and between them, quieter, are the sponges. You find them first as colour — the pale cushions, the ivory thickenings on stone slivers between the braids — and then as a pulse. It is so slow you feel it before you hear it, a faint sigh in the water against your skin, repeated and repeated, like something breathing in its sleep. A minute for a breath. In that minute the water is drawn in through a hundred small mouths too small to see, filtered, and sighs out again a little clearer. They are attached in the strictest sense. They do not move toward food. Food comes to them, or does not, and the pocket is still enough that it mostly does not. The clear edge of the water inside the water — that straight, glassy line where the dark of the pocket ends and the cloudier green of the basin begins — is not a mystery of rock. It is what this filtering makes. Sponge and algae together hold the silt back. The lens of clearer water is lodged under the rock because living matter has been keeping it clear, particle by particle, at sponge-pace.

And swaying among them, though there is no current to sway them, are the hydroid plumes. Up here they lean one way, down there they lean the other, as if answering to a tide in another direction. Each plume is many polyps, each polyp a small tentacled mouth opening and closing in the stillness. They catch what the sponge does not want, what the algae sheds, what drifts sideways across the pocket in that sideways world. They are rooted, colonial, plant-like animals. The whole pocket is mostly that: animal colonies that live like plants, and plants that have learned to act like colonies, braided together until stone is rarely seen clean, only in slivers — a fresh laminated face where the slab splintered, a cluster of unrounded pebbles locked in silt, a shard still leaning where it fell, its edge bright.

Look closer, if you can stay still long enough for your eyes to adjust to the seep, and the mat resolves further. On every surface there is a film too thin to rub off, bacteria and diatoms greasing the stone and the algae ribbons and the inside of the clay fragment whose broken edge is now worn a little but still plainly made. In the braids there are tenants smaller than the eye resolves: rotifers, copepods, filament grazers, moving — if they move at all — at a pace that belongs to this place. A fraction of a body-length in an hour. They do not turn in a school. They do not cross. They browse the algae that browses the light, and they are browsed in turn by hydroid tentacles that close as slowly as sponges sigh. Nothing rises very far. Everything spreads sideways, even the eating.

For scale, you have to let go of your own. The pocket is only as long as you are lying down, barely wider than your shoulders. For the sponge cushion, it is a vast, dark room. For the hydroid plume, the ceiling a body-length away is another country. Larvae that break away from a polyp do not swim far, because there is nowhere to swim to. They sink — or rather, they lean sideways as they sink, the way bubbles lean here toward the walls as if the ceiling were not up — and settle on the nearest ribbon, the nearest stone sliver, and root. That is why it is crowded in a way that is not beautiful from a distance. It is intimate. The room itself is mostly living matter, and the space for water is the space between leaves.

The marks in the stone are inhabited too, in their way. At the back, where the silt is darkest, the flat slab scored with five shallow notches holds silt in its grooves, and algae has found the grooves first, darkening them. The cupped fragment of fired clay, half sunk to its rim, holds stiller silt inside it, and a sponge has thickened over its lip, not caring that it was made, only that it is hard and stable and sharp-edged enough to keep from burying. In the corner, the three uncut stones in a rough crescent enclose a patch of silt a shade darker than the rest. Whether they were laid or merely slumped into that shape, the pocket does not say. Something small was once built there, or rested there, or died there, and the sea took the rest. What is left is a shelter without a shelterer, and now the braids lean toward it, as they lean toward everything.

No fish crosses. No school turns in the dark. That is not an absence that asks to be filled. It is the condition the others live by. The one pale shrimp that comes to the curtain in all your stay is not a resident, and its not-entering is instructive. It pauses outside the algae, its eyes catching what little light there is, and moves on along the outside of the rock. It does not look in. Perhaps the clear edge, the thick silence, the displaced sounds — a sound from the far wall arriving close to your ear, a sound from the entrance arriving from behind your head — are not for it. The inhabitants here have no ears to be displaced. They feel pressure. They feel the sponge-sigh. They feel, perhaps, the very slow widening of the crack in the roof, a hair at a time, transmitted through stone and water as a settling they will outlast in seasons.

Their time is not yours. The crack will open in years. The sponge will pulse in its minute. The weed will add a fraction of itself in a season, and a new splinter will part from the fresh roof and join the still-unsettled silt below, and the pocket, younger than the plants in it, will take another small step toward being an old place. The inhabitants are in no hurry to be blunted. They are in that first, sharp stage with the rock: recently torn open, recently flooded with dark, recently overgrown, and crowding still unthinned. Their edges, like the slab's, are still knives and flakes. They tear a little at a careless hand, not out of defense, but because they are growing into every surface at once and there is nowhere empty to put a hand that is not already, in some small way, them.

You could leave by the handspan of shadow, and the water would close its clear edge behind you in a moment. They would not notice the change in pressure. The ribbons would go on descending, the fans rising, the plumes swaying in opposing directions, the cushions sighing their minute-long breath, the films greasing the notches and the clay and the leaning shard. A small, fresh, flooded room at the bottom of the world, packed with leaf and sponge, dark, quiet, cool, breathing at sponge-pace — not waiting, exactly, because waiting implies a direction, and up and down have been negotiated away. Simply continuing, at the speed of growth, until erosion makes the roof less new and the curtain a little thicker, and the pocket becomes, fraction by fraction, the old place it is already pretending to be.
