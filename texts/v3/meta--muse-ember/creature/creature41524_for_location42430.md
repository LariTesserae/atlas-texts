It begins as a film.

Not green. Not lichen. A thin grey skin spread over the damp joints of the black stone, in the cracks that never dry, where the water runs and runs again. It eats stone-sweat and mineral dust and the fine salt the pans leave behind when the fog burns off. It has no leaf to lift to the light, because the light is hard and cold here and would do it no good. It moves the way a damp spreads: a millimeter at a time, a bead at a time, pale where the rock split most recently and the inside is still white, darkening where it has fed longest.

That is the first inhabitant, and everything else in the bowl is, in one way or another, feeding on it or keeping it from growing too thick.

In the warm culverts under the main ducts — in the shallow trenches where the circulatory lines run tower to tower, steaming faintly — the grazers live. Six-legged, blind, smooth and pale as tallow, they scrape the mineral film off the pipe walls with mouths that are more patience than teeth. They know the ducts not by sight but by heat. A duct warm at one end and cold at the other is a road to them, and they follow the gradient slowly, the way animals follow a season. In the coldest weeks they press closer to the heat exchangers. When the towers breathe and grow two meters taller in late summer, the grazers ride the change in the walls without comment, their soft bodies accommodating the stretch.

Their guts are a dark line of taken stone. What they cannot use they shed as salt, as crust, as the white rims around the shallow pans. They can go weeks without light and do. If you lifted one — and no one here lifts them, it would be a disturbance — it would curl, not in fear, but in the small, economical way of something that has never needed to hurry. They are the city's sheep, except the pasture is the inside of its own pipes, and the wool they crop is rock trying to grow where rock should not.

At night, high up, when the fog has submerged the lower towers and they stand as islands, the cleaners move.

Small, winged, mechanical or half-mechanical, you cannot tell from the ground and the people who made them, if anyone did, are not saying. Their wings are not feathers. They are sheets, like the windows, that catch and clear. They fly between towers in the white sea above the fog, in a silence so complete you would miss them entirely if you were not watching for the change they leave. Where they have passed, a tower's weeping skin is dry. A clean trail, a hand wide, runs down the ceramic ribs where before the condensation beaded and ran in threads. They sweep and sweep, tower to tower, and leave nothing but dryness behind them, which is how you know they were there. They are the only inhabitants who routinely see the whole bowl at once, rim to rim, from above the fog. The rest live inside it.

The towers themselves are not buildings in the way a building ought to be. They breathe. The water towers, vast and slow, take in the fog at dusk as it fills the bowl to a man's shoulder, then to the second stories, then submerges them entirely, and they hold it in their flanks and give off dry air in the morning. A tower exhales steam. A duct sighs and equalizes. A tower is taller in late summer than in spring by simple thermal breathing, and its stairwells rise and fall to meet it. Some towers are empty and sealed, growing a millimeter a year for no reason anyone can now remember. Do not mistake that for death. Here nothing is dead that is merely still. Sealed is a kind of sleeping. An inhabitant is any structure that has learned to cycle: water up as fog, down as drip; heat out, cold in; stone splitting in the night, beading over with clear resin by noon and stronger than before.

And inside the ones that are lit, from within, low, in the day — the people.

You will not see them often. That is not because they are hiding. It is because outside is an expense. The cold here is mineral. It radiates up through boots even when the sun is on your neck. The air is thin and still inside the bowl, and your ears popped coming up through the slot between the slabs, through the membrane that lets you through and closes behind you as if it had never been. The people were born to that thinness. Their hearts are slow. Their hands are thick-skinned from the young rock under everything, from edges still sharp enough to cut a palm. Their coats are damp inside and out because they live by fogged windows and do not wipe them. To wipe them would be to waste the warmth that made the fog.

When you do see one, it is usually on a freight sled, a hand's breadth above the polished strip, carrying a cistern or a crate. They do not look up. This is not discourtesy. Looking up is looking toward the rim, toward the wind that keens a little over the sharp edges and does not reach the floor, toward a sky that is ordinary and gives nothing. It costs heat to turn a face. It costs heat to make a sound. The wet stone swallows what is made, the high air does not carry it far, and the towers, which hear through their walls, contract with a soft click if startled. So they move unhurriedly, as the sleds move, and they keep their eyes on the strip, on the next duct, on the work of keeping the water moving without loss.

They live by function, the way the grazers live by heat. The water-tower people tend the harvest. The heat-exchanger people sit with their backs to the finned flanks and learn the cold by touch, because a single duct can be warm on one end and cold on the other for reasons the manual explains in a single sentence, and the sentence is known by heart. The store towers hold compressed food and spare resin and sheets for windows, and their people are the most silent of all, opening a shutter six floors up and closing it again and saying nothing for days.

They talk rarely, and when they do it is low, and because they breathe fogged air it may be that they talk in touches. A hand flat against a tower wall, feeling for the hum of a duct equalizing, for the tick and pop of ice thawing in an expansion seam. Or they use the phase-sheets, which go opaque when the fog rises and clear when the sun clears, faster than any shutter. A window clearing, clearing against its season, is a signal across hundreds of meters of empty wet stone. No one here is astonished by the physics stretched thin in places — heat moving without pipes, water without pumps. It is plumbing. It is how you know the other tower is awake.

They eat little, and what they eat is warm and small and taken sitting down. Salt crust from the pans, harvested with the film the grazers have not taken. Nutrients from the stores, softened in harvested fog. The water they drink tastes of stone, of black flats and pale crusts, and they prefer it that way. Nothing grows green here, and nothing rots, and they have made a virtue of that. What they use goes back into the ducts. What they cannot use goes back into the stone.

Children, when they come, come in the upper floors, in the rooms the midday stripe finds for an hour as it travels slowly across the bowl like a sundial intends. They learn quiet before they learn speech. They learn to sit on one of the black flats at noon, where the sun has warmed the stone just enough to be bearable through a coat, and to close their eyes and take heat into their shoulders without moving to get more. That is the one time you may find them outside their towers in numbers — not talking, not looking up, just sitting in a line along the edge of the stripe, letting the young mountain give them back a little of what the fog took in the night.

They age slowly in the cold. Old people become very still, and then they become part of the insulation of a room that is sealed for them, and the tower takes their water back into its cycle and beads resin over the door and will grow, a millimeter a year, long after. There is no graveyard in the bowl. There is no soil to make one with.

So the ecology is not of growth. It is of circulation. The film eats the stone. The grazers eat the film. The cleaners dry the towers so they do not drown in their own weeping. The people keep the ducts warm, the sleds moving, the drains cut in the stone clear, the resin ready for the cracks that come in the contraction of night. The towers take in the wet and give off the dry. The fog rises at dusk, shoulder high, then higher, and the lit windows go pale against the white, and the inhabitants — film, grazer, cleaner, person — all withdraw a little deeper into warmth and quiet, and the water keeps running off the rim, off the towers, off the stone under your hand, into the pans, into the ducts, back up into the air to start again.

Mostly it is silence and damp and cold stone. And inside that, warm spots, moving slowly between cold towers, keeping the bowl from forgetting how to hold itself together.
