If you look for life here in the way you expect to find it — moving, calling, leaving a track — you will walk the whole hollow and conclude that there is none. What lives here does not move in that way, and it does not gather. It lives singly, in place, at the pace of dampness and rot, and for the most part it lives in plants alone. A few kinds, far apart, occupying only the narrow agreements where light, wetness, and shelter meet. Between them is not death, exactly, but unoccupied ground: silt and stone that carries leaf-matter down into soil, and soil back up into a leaf, in a closed, unhurried round that no animal interrupts.

The greater part of what is alive is not upright at all. Around each of the shallow pans, where the ground darkens and never quite dries, the moss makes a thin, black-green skin. It is no more than a few leaves thick in most places, creeping outward a fraction at a time where water beads and runs off the stones that ring the pool. After the fine mist settles, it holds the moisture on its surface in minute beads that do not drip away. It does not climb, it does not flower. It simply stays wet, and in staying wet, it slowly extends. Colonies that look like a finished ring have taken a very long time to close, and where the ring is broken — by a flat stone, by a place where the silt is a little too dry — it will remain broken for as long as you watch. Fragments of it wash a few centimeters into a pan and do not take root in open water. Spores, if they travel, travel only a pace or two before the air, which never really blows, lets them fall back into the same damp margin.

On the larger stones, in the shade that the stones themselves make, the lichen grows in pale, crusty discs. They live on the side that faces away from what light there is, on the side where condensation lingers longest, and they grow outward from a center that may be older than the wall on the north side. Their color is the palest thing in the hollow, but it is not white; it is filmed and silted like everything else. They do not lift or spread quickly. A disc is a record of years of staying damp enough, and undisturbed enough, to add another thin layer at its edge. Where a stone has slumped or a slab lies flat, lichen stops at the new, rounded break and begins again elsewhere, on a face that has learned to stay still.

Tucked against those same damp stones, where water runs down slowest, are the ferns. There are only a few, and they are small — a frond is shorter than a forearm, and a whole plant is a low cluster no wider than your shoulders. They unfurl without haste from a short rhizome pressed against the stone, arch a little, and hold. The undersides carry their spores in dull brown dots that fall, when they fall at all, straight down into the moss and wet silt at the base. Nothing eats the fronds. Nothing brushes them. A frond yellows at the tip, browns, and folds back upon itself to rot where it lies, feeding the same wet ring that feeds the next frond. The plant does not wander. Its rhizome may push a new crown a hand's breadth away over a season, but the colony remains what it was: a small, green exhale against stone, in a place otherwise bare for thirty paces in every direction.

Away from the pans, where the surface is drier though the inside of the soil never is, the dry-ground plants live in even greater isolation. The rosettes hug the earth as if to avoid rising into air that is no richer than the air at the surface. Their leaves are tough, overlapping, dusted with silt, arranged in a flat spiral that catches the flat light and sheds rain — the rare, settling rain — outward to the roots. The center dies a leaf at a time and traps it there to rot. There may be three such rosettes in the whole hollow, or five; you can walk from one to the next without passing anything else rooted in the ground but the faint, smoother line of the old path, where grass now grows in the edges and moss creeps into the middle where feet once compacted the silt.

The coarse grass grows in tussocks, tight and upright. Each tussock has built its own small mound of roots and soil, a low dome in the silt. The blades are stiff, narrow, and dull green, standing straight up to make the most of a light that never strengthens. A tussock expands not by running but by tillering, a new shoot pressing up beside an old one, year after year, until the mound is dense and the center is tight and old. Seed heads, when they form, are pale and sparse and do not blow away; the rim holds the air, and the seed falls within the tussock's own shadow. A tussock may be decades old and no more than knee-high. It lives, it adds a blade, it loses a blade to rot at the base. Between tussocks there is nothing for ten, twenty, thirty paces — only silt, a smooth stone, leaf-matter rotted almost to nothing. That emptiness is not a gap between plants so much as the condition under which a plant can exist here at all: alone, with its whole catchment of damp and light to itself.

The shrub is the largest living thing, and even it hugs the ground. Woody, stunted, its branches pressed outward and low, as if to stay within the warm, moist layer that lies against the soil. Its leaves are small, thick, and a little dusty, each one made to last. It does not grow up toward a sun there is no disc for; it grows out, slowly, thickening where a branch touches earth. A single shrub may stand east of center, with no other shrub within sight, and its fallen leaves form a small, dark litter beneath it that never blows clear. That litter breaks down in place, feeding the soil under the branches, which feeds the shrub in turn. It is complete in itself. If it makes seed, the seed makes, at most, another shrub a few paces away, after a long and improbable wait in bare silt where a seedling would need the same unlikely agreement of wetness and shade. For as long as you can imagine watching, it simply persists.

That is the shape of life here: moss in the wet rings, lichen on the shaded stones, ferns where water beads longest, rosettes pressed to the dry floor, grass tussocks on their mounds, and perhaps one low shrub, each separated by wide intervals of bare, silt-clay earth. They do not shade each other. They do not eat each other. They do not, in any detectable way, assist each other, except that all of them take and return to the same held air and the same settled soil. A leaf falls. It rots where it fell. The soil darkens by that much. A moss edge advances by that much. A lichen disc rims outward by that much. No one clears the litter; no one burrows in the silt; no wing or foot disturbs the pans.

They have arrived at the same state as the place itself. They grow where the dim light and the dampness agree, and nowhere else. They do not hurry to fill the emptiness between them, and the emptiness does not hurry to be filled. Birth is a spore dropping into moss beside its parent, a tiller beside its tussock, a seed in its own shade. Death is a leaf staying where it fell until it is soil again. Between those events there is a very long, very quiet persistence — green things, dusty and black-green and pale, living on without animal witness, in a warm grey room where water stays where it gathers and stones stay round, and where to live is mostly to remain.
