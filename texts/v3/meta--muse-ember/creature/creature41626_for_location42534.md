What lives in Vapourlow does not move, does not flee, does not answer to being looked at. At first you think nothing lives here at all, because nothing here does what living things are supposed to do in a place this loud and green. Nothing lifts. Nothing feeds. Nothing is eaten. And then, by degrees, you realize it is not empty. It is full, to the eaves, to the axle-deep water, to the inside of the pipes. It is full of life that has given up pulse and locomotion and breath, and settled entirely into patience.

It is plant life first, and plant life to a degree of extravagance that only becomes possible where nothing with a heartbeat ever gets in the way.

There are no grazers here, no beaks, no teeth, no larvae to mine a leaf, no moth to find the jasmine. That absence is the condition of everything. A leaf that unfurls in a gutter in Vapourlow will not be bitten. It will simply persist, thicken, yellow at the tip a decade later, fall into the water and rot there by fungus and bacterium instead of by being consumed. Nothing is pruned. Nothing is thinned. So growth goes on at the pace of the place itself - a centimetre a season for a vine, a millimetre a year for moss - but without interruption, without setback, for a hundred years. In a slow town, that is enough to cover.

On the roofs, moss has made its own ground. It is ankle-deep, complete, saturated, sponging the perpetual rain that falls only under the coldest pipes and the perpetual steam that rolls from the works. It holds water against tiles and felt and corrugated iron, insulates them, rots the battens slowly underneath into a soft black timber that fungi then stipple in orange and grey. The moss is not a stain. It is a landscape in miniature, with its own dry hummocks and wet hollows, its sporophytes lifting like a forest you could walk between if you were an ant - and there are no ants, either. Its spores are the town's one true dispersal. They ride the steam that never quite clears the bowl, they ride the drips amplified in the culvert vaults, they settle in the exact same lines of condensation day after day, and so the same narrow green lanes re-establish themselves under the same columns, until in the engine hall the cold rain under the steam bank feeds a single perfect strip of moss on concrete, ten feet from floor that is merely damp.

Below that, in the gutters and joints and street-level channels, ferns have taken the architecture and made it anatomy. They grow waist-high out of broken downpipes, out of the rims of vats, out of the expansion joints where two brick walls have sagged toward each other over a subsidence hollow. Their fronds are not the fronds of sunlit ground. They are pale, thin, enormously divided, opening slowly in the diffuse green-grey light that never arrives properly, as if in deep woodland. The light here is everywhere and nowhere, bounced up off water and wet brick, so the ferns do not reach. They spread. They form rosettes at every drip point, and where a gear tooth turns very slowly through a fallen leaf, a frond will grow in around it, will be pierced by it, will go on growing. The machine does not notice. The fern does not mind.

The vines are the most deliberate of them. They climbed the chimneys, which are as thick as trunks and just as warm, and now they spill from the overhead mains, from the conveyor bridges and cable trays, in thick curtains. In any other place they would follow the sun. Here they follow the conduits. They cluster leaves at every flange and joint, as if to cool the metal, because the condensation is richest there and the warmth is constant. Roots have found the oil leaks by a chemistry that a gardener would not recognize and a plant here seems to accept as food: they enter warm bearings, they wrap the slow shafts, they wiggle into the dark where the turbine's painted mark creeps a hand's breadth an hour. The roots drink heat and mineral leachate from ash and slag and peat-water, and the leaves drink the humidity the works makes and the plants themselves help to make. It is a closed weather system. The town manufactures heat and perpetual wetting. The plants transpire into it. The condensation falls again, with that uncanny knowledge of where to fall, and feeds them.

In the streets, where the water runs an inch deep over cinder and broken brick, the other life is mostly invisible and entirely in charge. In the sluices and flooded cellars and the open culverts between houses whose sills have sunk below the waterline, a film of iron bacteria moves with the slow current, orange as rust, oxidizing the iron the marsh water carries and vomiting it out as the thick, bark-like scale on every pipe. It is this film that gives the street water its colour and its smell of warm metal. It clogs, it sloughs, it regrows in three days in time with the sluice cycle - fill, heat, release, cool - which is also, incidentally, the season here. In the vats, water hyacinth spreads in mats that never need to move, blooming a pale, indifferent blue that no insect will ever come to pollinate. It clones itself across the surface, root-feather to root-feather, and where the mat presses against a cold wall, the pond in the middle of the yard has developed that other surface, the one that does not ripple. A skin of algae and cyanobacteria and mineral precipitate, inches thick in places, holds the water flat against the vibration of the whole bowl. It is a living lid. Things fall into it and are kept. The town's waste becomes its crust, and the crust is alive.

And in the corners where the industrial and the domestic interpenetrated - because in Vapourlow they always did, factory shoulder to cottage shoulder - the older garden things have persisted in the twilight. The jasmine-like creeper shades the amber lamp globes on their wet insulators. It flowers in the middle of the day under artificial light, fragrant to an atmosphere that has no nose in it, and never, so far as anyone can tell over a century, sets a seed that any creature carries away. It does not need to. It is a permanent resident. It simply blooms, because the warmth and the humidity tell it that it is always spring, always the moment after a storm, and spring is what it knows.

None of this ever hurries, or dies in any dramatic way, or is cleared. Fallen fern fronds become peat in the gutter. Fallen vine leaves become mulch on the catwalk. The fungi work the rotting curtains and the grey timber of empty houses, softening them from within while the houses still keep their numbers on their doors. It is erosion, and it is also maturity. The plants have not conquered the works, and the works has not expelled the plants. They have intersected so thoroughly that you can no longer reliably say where one ends. A leaf cluster is cooling a flange. A root is in a bearing. A moss lane is tracing a pipe that sweats in lines. A vine is following a wire. The shapes of the life are the shapes of the machines that feed, warm, and wet them.

That is who lives in Vapourlow. Not a population. A continuance. Spore and root and film and mat, breathing nothing, pulsing never, growing into every joint the town has to offer, and holding, in its warm, dim, endlessly cycling bowl, a green, quiet, absolute tenure.
