If you are looking for inhabitants, you have to give up on looking for a face first.

Nothing here walks toward you. Nothing watches. At the scale of a body, at the scale of an eye, the plateau seems empty — a green felt with no one in it, and the silence reinforces the trick. Only when you get down, when you kneel and let your attention shrink to inches, does the population declare itself. And it does not declare itself all at once. It layers.

The first citizen is the skin.

Paint, no thicker than a coat of house paint, lies over everything that does not move away fast enough: over stone, over dead leaf, over the back of another lichen, over the soil where soil can be found. Rust, ochre, grey-green, a green so pale it is almost the moss beneath it. That is lichen, and it is not one thing. It is a treaty. A fungus builds the house — a tough, weatherproof cortex that can dry to leather in an hour of high sun and drink again in minutes — and inside it, kept in a damp green chamber, an alga photosynthesises and feeds them both.

That makes it the oldest, slowest resident. A cushion shoot may live a season. A runner may race across a hollow in a decade. A patch of paint-lichen may be older than the hummock it paints, older than the stone it softens, spreading not by seed but by cracking, by flaking, by a millimeter of new growth on a wet morning. It does not move in any way you could catch. It expands. It contracts. It changes colour with wetting — drab when dry, flaring orange and yellow-green when the fog slides over — and that flare is not decoration. It is how the resident in the skin tells you it is awake, its photosynthetic machinery coming back online, its acids loosening minerals out of the stone beneath it so, very slowly, stone becomes food.

Nothing eats the paint, exactly. Springtails graze its spores. A beetle may cross it. But mostly the lichen lives by being too small, too tough, too chemical for anything to want it badly enough to evolve a taste for it. It is the place's outer dermis, and like skin it is where the cold is first met and first survived.

Under and over the paint comes the second layer, and this is where the place begins to thicken into something you can press your thumb into.

Moss. Not a plant in the solitary sense, but a multitude. Each cushion is a city of shoots, thousands of them, each shoot a stem with leaves no bigger than the head of a pin, each leaf beaded in the morning. A shoot grows up, makes new leaves at its tip, and lets its lower leaves yellow and rot into black peat. So the whole cushion performs a slow fountain: living at the top, dying at the bottom, lifting itself an inch a year on its own decay. From a distance the cushion looks like a single hummock. Up close it is shivering.

The moss-dwellers are consumers of water and cold. Their leaves are built like little gutters and cups, hairy at the margins to catch fog that would never fall as rain. When the light thins they close, not with a muscle but with a change in turgor, a soft internal pressure that folds them inward. When the sun hits, they open like shutters. When the temperature drops fast, some of them push out frost-feathers from their edges — clear combs of ice, extruded, glittering for an hour. It is not that they make ice. It is that together they breathe out excess water as the cold squeezes them, and the ice is a brief, sparkling exhalation before it melts back into their own stores.

Inside the moss, in the water films between leaves, lives the only true animal population that can be called resident. You will never see it without magnification, and that is the point. In every bead held on a leaf hair there are rotifers turning like tiny wheels, nematodes threshing in the damp, tardigrades — barrel-bodied, eight-legged, indestructible in the way this place is indestructible — hauling themselves through the felt baths, feeding on algae and bacteria and the sweet rot of other microbes. They are the fauna the plateau permits: animals so small the cold and silence do not notice them, whose whole lives are lived in the inch of water held between one moss leaf and the next. They move, when they move, at the pace of the mat itself: a body length in an hour, a migration in a lifetime.

But the moss is not just their apartment building. It is the reservoir that feeds everything above it. Saturated cushion on cushion, it squeezes cold dampness up around your boot. It is both sponge and cistern, holding, releasing, holding again. All the water here that does not sit in a still mirror in a hollow is held here, in the bodies of the residents.

The third layer travels.

The creeping mat — wiry, hairy stems knotted across the surface, rooting again at every joint — is the inhabitant that gives the place its restlessness. Each stem is a runner. Each joint puts down roots, puts up leaves, puts out a new runner. When the front advances, the back dies and rots into peat. So a single runner is not a plant so much as a procession: a clone walking downhill an inch a year, tirelessly, for so long that a valley can be crossed by one continuous individual that never existed all at once. If you could mark a leaf today, its descendants would be in the next hollow; if you returned in a century, that leaf's great-grandchildren would be pushing over a stone that the original never touched.

This is the migratory citizen, and the one that heaves. Frost lifts a hummock, cracks its crust, lets a traveler in. A mat pushes over a stone and collapses in a silent slide and the dark earth shows, and within days the paint and the moss and the next runner have skinned it over. The turbulence you see is largely this layer at work — not growth in place, but growth as movement, a slow green tide with no front anyone could see standing up.

On the back of the runners, and shingled over them, comes the thick-leaved layer. This is the solar mill in its purest form, though by now the factory image is failing. These are leaves you can feel between thumb and finger: waxy, fleshy, overlapping like scales or roof tiles, each one a storage vessel of sugar and water and antifreeze. They survive by being unpalatable and impermeable and ready. In the morning they are flat and green. As the day cools, anthocyanins flood them — the rusts and berry purples and ember oranges you see racing across a hillside — and the leaf is not blushing. It is manufacturing sunscreen and poison on demand, flushing pigment through its own veins the way a body flushes blood to a wound. At night they fold slightly, conserve heat. In an hour a new leaf can unroll from the bud below, already made, already packed, expanding to take the place of a leaf that has yellowed, blackened, and been reabsorbed.

Because nothing is wasted here, death is a form of feeding. A leaf that dies does not fall. It rots where it lies, is stripped by fungi, is sucked back into the felt by the roots of its neighbours. The factory floor, if you still want that word, is also a stomach.

Rising through all of it, small and improbable, are the towers.

Roquettes and plumes, stems as tough as cane for all their thinness, clustered rosettes that open when the sun engages and close when it does not. The heads nod and do not nod. Some are dark red at the edges, some hold their seed in a swollen head packed tight with sugar. These are the reproductive citizens, the ones that do sex. And because the air in the mat is almost perfectly still, and there are no birds, and no swarms of insects to carry anything, they do it by contact and by patience. A tower leans, in its slow heave, against a neighbour. A head rubs a plume. Or it simply closes on itself, self-pollinating in the damp, and makes down.

When the seed head bursts — not with a pop, just a collapse, a sigh of down settling on the next leaf and being sucked in — it is not dispersing far. It does not need to. The world it needs to colonise is inches away, a felt that is perpetually cracking open and felting shut. The towers are brief. They rise, they seed, they collapse back into the mat that made them, and for a while you would not know they had been there except for the faint paling where a new rosette is already budding below.

So far, so plant. But the residents are not only the green things you can see.

The felt is electrified.

White fungal threads run through the peat and root-felt, hand to hand, leaf to leaf, valley to neighbouring valley. They are mining belts and telephone wires both. They carry minerals laterally from a root that found them to a bud that needs them. They carry chemical messages — a taste of drought, a pulse of cold — faster than any root could grow. And they conduct, faintly, bioelectrically, so that when a rosette opens on one shoulder of the plateau, a bud swells in the next hollow a moment later, coordinated without a brain, without a centre. Growth here is mechanised, but the mechanism is alive.

With them live the bacteria. In the nodules on the roots, fixing nitrogen out of the thin air the way a foundry fixes metal out of ore. In the peat, in millions, stripping dead leaf and dead moss and dead lichen down to black, spongy, water-holding humus — which is to say, making soil out of themselves, making the ground future residents will live in. In the resin ducts and pigment glands, making the acids and waxes and deterrents that keep the whole system from being eaten.

This is the hidden proletariat of the plateau, and it never stops. A leaf unrolls in one place, a root-tip pushes in another, a nodule takes up nitrogen in a third, a fungal thread fires a signal between them, and all of it is silent because moss and wet leaf absorb all sound. The turbulence is internal and total: production and consumption at a rate that would exhaust a temperate meadow, balanced only because it has been balanced for so long that it has learned how to waste nothing.

Which leaves, for animals with eyes, almost no one.

Once in an hour, perhaps, you will see a springtail — grey, fleckless, no bigger than a grain of rice — flick itself across a bead of water on a leaf tip, ballistic, blind, grazing lichen spores. It does not live here. It visits the skin, feeds, and is gone into the moss.

Once in a day, perhaps, a dark beetle crosses a leaf. It moves deliberately, because for a beetle this felt is a thicket, a mountain range of hummocks, each step a climb. It is a cold-blooded wanderer from the edge of the plateau, where the green thins, and it will cross and cross again for years without ever settling, without ever finding food enough to breed. To watch it is to understand that the niches are already full. The plants do the preying, the migrating, the building, the decaying. A beetle here is a tourist in a world that closed its borders long ago.

And once, perhaps, something larger and indeterminate makes the felt dip as it passes, a pressure you feel in your foot more than see with your eye, and is gone. No one who lives here can tell you what it was. It left no track that the mat did not heal in an hour. It may have been a mammal from outside, coming to drink at a mirror and leaving. It may have been a collapse mistaken for a footstep. The residents do not record visitors. They reseal.

By late afternoon, if you are still kneeling, you begin to understand what kind of community this is. It is not a collection of individuals who happen to share a habitat. A mat is one individual spread across a valley. A moss cushion is thousands of individuals acting as one hummock. A lichen is three species acting as one skin. A fungal network is one organism acting as many nerves. The boundary between a resident and the place dissolves: the place is the residents, layered and living, making air out of light, making soil out of themselves, making more of themselves out of cold and a little water, and doing it without pause and without noise.

A leaf is born in a bud, opens, reddens, yellows, rots, and is reabsorbed, in a span of time that might be a day. A runner is born at a joint, walks, dies at the tail, in a span of decades. A lichen is born in a flake, spreads paint-thin, in a span of centuries. A peat bed is born of all of them dying, and will feed all of them for centuries after. Three clocks, ticking at once, never stopping, never hurrying.

You stand up. Your fingers have gone numb from the cold in the felt. The high places catch the sun and look lit from within, translucent. The plateau goes on, valley after valley, green to the horizon, sparkling with condensation, heaving and settling, greening and rusting, in perfect silence.

You leave your footprints in it. That is the only thing you could ever give the residents: a brief crack in the crust, an invitation for a runner, a dip that will be felted over in an hour, gone in a day, remembered only — if anything here remembers — as a faint electrical pulse travelling hand to hand through the white threads, telling the next hollow that something warm and clumsy has passed, and that the work, quietly, industrially, turbulently, goes on without it.
