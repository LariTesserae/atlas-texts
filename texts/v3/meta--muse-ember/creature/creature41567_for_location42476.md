The first residents you meet are the ravens.

They live at the rim where the slot opens to the sky, in the overhangs and breaches of the upper walls, and their nests move as the face moves. They are big, soot-black, high-altitude birds, fearless in thin air, and they know the Cut better than anyone who walks it. They ride the updraft like lifts, coming down in a slow spiral through the lattice of conveyor bridges. They steal from open hands at the market bridges and nose around open fungus loaves, and the rock below their ledges is whitewashed with them. When the updraft strengthens, they go up with it. When it falters and reverses for a breath, they hang in the dust with their wings open, waiting. They are the first to know the blast is coming — the brief total stillness when even the ravens leave the walls, and every person on every bench feels it in their teeth before they hear anything at all.

Below them, in every warm joint and expansion crack, is a second population that belongs to the lamps.

The ash-moths and heat-moths live around the long dim threads of lamps strung along the railings, and around the kiln. They rise in clouds from the hot walls, settle on the fungal mats, lift again when a door opens or a cart passes. The wall-skinks live on the moths — flattened, steel-grey, quick as fingers, they dart between iron plates in the joints where the heat leaks out, and vanish before you can be sure you saw one. The beetles, the size of a thumbnail, pick their way through the cable-ducts and along the pipe layers, eating insulation and spilled food, causing small faults that men curse and repair. Nothing in the Cut is wasted, and nothing is left alone for long.

Down toward the heat, where the air is perpetually warm and still, the distinction gets thinner still.

In the film of oil and dust and fungus that coats the hottest walls, the translucent dust-worms mill in slow folds, more animal than plant. They turn the grease into soil of a kind, and in the folds of their bodies a whole ecology of mites lives, invisible unless you look for it, and then everywhere. The fungus itself is neither plant nor animal in any way you would recognize — pale, grey-orange mats cultivated in the warm wall-rooms behind the pipes, and wild mats spreading uninvited across abandoned niches where the heat and dust are right. Soft, sour, faintly luminous in the blue shade. It is made into loaves, into soup, into a pulp that is fed to almost everything else in the Cut. The skinks do not eat it, but the moths settle on it, the beetles graze its edges, the worms work through it, the rats burrow into the storerooms where it is dried, and people live on it.

The rats came with the first supplies and they have stayed and grown — larger, hairless-tailed, grease-fed, running in established highways along the pipes at every height. They are eaten, in turn, by the ravens who catch them in the open, and by the men who catch them for the pot when the cable brings nothing else. It is a complete circle, and everyone knows their place in it and depends on it.

Between the fauna and the machinery the line keeps slipping, and after a while you stop asking which things are alive and start asking which things move on their own.

The little quadruped sweepers that patrol the lower terraces were built to eat dust and dropped fungus, and they have learned the routes of the alleys as animals learn them. They are tended — fed, repaired, cursed when they die in a drain and have to be hauled out. The carrier-mules are engines, but they walk. They have to be loaded and rested, they know a bench by its smell, they lower their heads in the blast-stillness, and they have names. They eat fungus pulp like everything else. If a sweeper gets lost at the narrowest point, where two people cannot pass without turning sideways, someone will stand and guide it back with a hand on its flank, patiently, the way you would guide a dog.

People are the newest thing in the Cut, and the most temporary.

At the top, in the wind-braced sheds and the coldest upper niches, live the summit people — cable-pylon crews, winch tenders, inspectors, the storekeepers who deal in dried compressed food brought up from below. They have the thin clean air and the metal taste in the back of the throat, and they are rich in breath and poor in warmth. They are the ones who go out before the lamps change at dawn, when frost has formed overnight on the rock from breath and kiln-vapour and the mountain's own slow exhalation, and scrape it into jars. A household's water is measured like that, and traded like that, and boiled and reboiled. Their lips are cracked. Their lungs work hard. They sleep cold, and they go down the service tunnel to get warm.

Uptown is colder. Downtown is the floor.

Most of the several thousand who live here are in the middle benches, in niches cut or blasted into the walls and fronted with riveted iron, corrugated sheet, poured concrete, and in the old south end, whole dismantled houses re-erected in iron and timber against the cliff. Pipes run past their doors in layers — air, oil, coolant, cable, waste — each humming at a different rate, each giving off its own heat. That heat is a kind of currency and a kind of kinship. Neighbours know each other by which pipe they share, by the niche number, by the bench. *Bench 7, Niche 41.* That is an address.

Families do not live in a place here so much as on a bench for a while. When the face at the north end advances — twice a day it is blown and cut and hauled away, by a schedule everyone feels — the newest terraces are raw, pale, steaming faintly, and the oldest are taken apart. Iron doors, hinges, window-frames, even the cut block of a front wall are numbered, lifted by crane, and re-erected a terrace higher. Markets follow the houses. A bakery becomes a barber becomes a shrine of lamps becomes a bakery again, all in a month. Children born in a niche learn the market bridge by hand along a railing, learn to walk sideways without thinking where the slot narrows to eighteen metres, learn to climb between terraces the way other children learn to run. Old people live lower, where it is warm, and stoop against the chimney wind. Everyone is dust-dry, dust in the nails, dust in the cough that never quite leaves.

Lower still are the people who work the reason for the Cut. In the warm wall-rooms behind the pipes the fungus farmers tend their mats, turning them, seeding the wild growth from the abandoned niches back into the cultivated ones. On the floor the crushers sit in a row with their jaws opening and closing in a dull thud you feel in your sternum, and the concentrators and separators run, and the kiln mouth glows orange even in the morning, and the conveyors climb the south wall in long black ladders to the summit cable. Cranes swing loads across the void with a patience that looks lazy until you realize they have not stopped since you arrived. These are the downtown people, warmer, dirtier, prized in winter for the heat they live in, and their work is loud everywhere you can see and quiet almost everywhere you can listen, because the slot eats the sound and throws what is left up and out of the crack.

They live with the updraft. Every hour or so it strengthens, carrying dust up in spirals, lifting papers and feathers and loose tarps, then dropping them. Nothing settles for long, not dust, not sound, not a house. Only at midday does a blade of true sun come down the slot and rake the floor for twenty minutes, and then it is gone, and everyone who can look up does, and the blue shade returns.

And still, it is abundant. That is the surprise that does not wear off. Moths around the lamps, skinks in the joints, beetles in the ducts, worms in the oil film, mites in the worms, fungus on the walls, rats on the pipes, ravens on the rim, sweepers learning the alleys, mules climbing with their loads, and people in their numbered iron fronts, scraping frost into jars, boiling water, eating sour loaves, mending a door they will soon have to take apart and carry up one bench, moving it like a shell.

Go back in a month and the face will have moved, three benches will have been rebuilt, a market will have migrated to a different wall, the ravens will be nesting somewhere new, and a sweeper you once guided will be patrolling a terrace that did not exist when you left. The Cut will still be there: dim, cool at the top and hot at the bottom, crowded into a single crack two hundred and eighty metres long and never wider than eighteen, breathing its dust up the chimney, waiting to be blown a little wider.
