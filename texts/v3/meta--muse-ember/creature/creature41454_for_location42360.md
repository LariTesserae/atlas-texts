Life in Seyra does not start large, and it does not start in one place. It starts in a mouthful of water.

A bromeliad the size of an urn, rooted where the cliff goes vertical, holds rain and condensation in its centre the way a shallow cup holds them. In that cup a frog no longer than your thumbnail sits with its mottled green back just above the surface. It is not there by accident and it does not leave. Its eggs are not laid in the water. They are carried, in strings along its flanks, damp and bright, until the mist thickens and the centre level rises a fraction, and only then they hatch. Beside it, on the submerged leaf, a beetle walks upside-down on feet that stay dry, its wing-cases iridescent as oil on water. Below them, mosquito larvae hang and jerk in a world that began an hour ago with condensation and will end, in a sense, every evening when the cup overflows down the cliff in a dark streak and is refilled by morning. No one of them could live without the others being present in exactly that geometry. The cup is the habitat. The water in the cup is the weather. The frog knows the height of the water by the temperature of its belly.

That scale repeats everywhere, and then keeps repeating on top of itself.

On the flats the tussock grass grows waist-high in hummocks so close they touch, each one humming when the wind finds it. Inside a hummock, at the warm, dry centre where the grass has died back to thatch, a warbler nests low and sings in short, unhurried phrases. Its young learn the phrase note for note, and the phrase does not change. A bird born this year, in this hummock, will sing what its grandmother sang, and the curve of it is suited to the exact bowl of the valley — how the mist holds, how the echo comes off the mossed cliff. Under the warbler’s nest, in the damp root mat that holds the hummock together, springtails and fungal threads and the larvae of the fierce pollinating flies work the dead leaves that will take years to reach the earth. The hummock is a small, self-contained breathing thing, warm from below.

The lake chain is shallower and brighter and less forgiving. The small silver fish that live there are endemic and half-blind in the glare for half the day, riding the light at the surface with their eyes rolled up into the sun. They clean the floating turf rings that hold the lakes together, cropping the algae mats that would otherwise smother them — blue-green velvet that shifts underfoot in the bogs. The beetles that walk upside-down under floating leaves are their counterparts in the air: at this altitude and warmth pollination is done less by bees than by a guild of flies so abundant they never thin out, and the beetles carry a dusting of orchid pollen on their backs from leaf to leaf. Dragonflies tick over the water on wings that catch the sun. At the edge, in the western bog of black water, the frogs with mottled skin — larger than the urn-frogs, bass-voiced, clacking and popping in a rhythm so regular it becomes a clock — carry their egg-strings along their flanks until the hum changes and they pause, all at once, in a silence you can feel in the meadow, and resume when it settles.

You hear other things in the deep bogs surfacing, briefly, and then not. A displacement of black water. A sigh from a crack. Something slow and patient that drinks at the surface without being seen. It is easy to file them as another animal, larger and shyer, and they are — but only in the way the fish are animals. They keep the bog open where the substrate would otherwise seal it.

Above the bogs the forest is built in layers that never quite land. Tree ferns open like umbrellas over broad-leaved shrubs rooted in a mat of moss and liverwort so tight a fallen leaf takes years to reach the soil. Lianas bridge tree to tree, thick as your wrist, not for support but as highways, and epiphytes bridge the lianas. Birds move through all three heights without touching the ground. The squirrel-like creature that moves through the canopy has enormous lungs — you can see its chest work, a hard, deliberate pull in the thin air at five thousand metres — and a prehensile tail with which it carries its young on its back, the young holding the tail as well as the fur, swinging from liana to liana in a way that uses the heat rising off the stone below. Lizards bask on the travertine terraces where the stone is hottest, ivory-white and ribbed, mineral-sweet and sulfurous, warm enough to bathe in. Snakes live in those warm rocks and drink condensation bead by bead off the basalt. A raptor rides the thermal that rises from a hot cliff and hangs there, not hunting so much as holding position in a column of air that the basin makes for itself every day.

All of this is loud, but it is loud as a palimpsest. High, thin, metallic, from insects pitched so high you feel it between your ears. Mid-level, from meadows and ponds. Deep, from under the leaf mat where the large shy mammal grunts as it moves. And underneath, threading through, the low hum that lives in the molars when you sit on warm basalt or put your hand in bog water. Rocks tick as they heat and cool. Steam sighs from cracks. Waterfalls make a far roar that never quite stops.

None of it is sparse. Every square metre has at least three presences touching or nearly touching. Green on green on green, gilded by the wet.

It would be possible to walk for a day and record this as a very rich, very old equatorial highland ecology, and be wrong in the particular way Seyra is wrong. Too hot for its height. Too lush. The seep-lines on every cliff — beads of water that gather, run a hand’s breadth, and vanish — follow mineral veins, not just cracks. The pebble beds in the streams sort themselves over years by colour, by size, in bands too neat for any unaided current. Some orchid drifts open only facing away from the nearest other orchid, as if pollination required a kind of privacy that no insect enforces. On certain evenings thin branching lines of water run *up* a face of black obsidian, against gravity. At dusk the moss on the warm stones gives off faint plumes of visible heat, rising straight up and not dispersing, a small weather of their own.

Those are not behaviours of the fish or the frogs or the warblers. They are behaviours of the ground they are standing on, and the animals are attuned to them the way skin is attuned to temperature.

Go down — or rather, stay where you are and notice that the ground is not ground. Red-brown loam lies deep in the hollows, but under it the rock weeps. Black basalt shines wet even when it has not rained. Travertine steps down from the hot springs. Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it. The plateau is laced with a substrate you cannot see: mixed minerals and crystal lattices grown through the fungal network that runs through everything, root mat and cliff moss and lake turf alike. It is not soil, and it is not rock. It is a wet, ion-moving lattice that does its work by massive parallelism in chemistry, information carried in water and in the slow drift of ions through wet stone, self-repair at a scale too small to see, cooling and filtering and buffering performed not by a device but by the arrangement of the whole. It happens at the pace of soil formation.

This is the resident. Not a species among the others, but the medium in which the others are brief, bright densities. A single, distributed, litho-fungal system forty kilometres across, older than the oldest tree fern, which takes a thousand years to obliterate a clearing and another thousand to rot into loam. It is engineered once — by minds or processes for which we have no vocabulary — and left to run so slowly and so completely that the engineering has become indistinguishable from the ecology.

You meet it, when you meet it at all, at its endpoints. The scattered remains are not ruins. They are foci.

In the western bog there is a low ring of stones, knee-high, fused together as if melted and cooled in place, too regular to be natural, with a shallow channel cut through its centre. Moss grows on it in a perfect, unfamiliar geometry — not random, not quite symmetrical. Water passes through that channel and emerges on the other side tasting different: clearer, cooler, iron still present but changed. The frogs gather at its edge. The things you hear surfacing in the deep bog are tending the larger network of which this ring is one mouth. This is a filter, sieved finest, and also a kidney, and also a place where the substrate holds a slope or a water table at exactly the angle that prevents a landslide here and a bogging there.

In the forest a smooth pillar stands warmer than the tree beside it, its top worn concave, holding rainwater that never seems to evaporate. It is a condenser, and a lung. In the morning it exhales; in the evening it draws in. Insects drink from its shallow bowl. Orchids root preferentially in its leachate. The water in it does not run away because the lattice beneath it is tuned to hold water at that precise warmth.

Across a meadow a line of ankle-high markers marches straight for two hundred metres, bent, lichen-covered, half-sunk. The grass is greener and the soil richer along that line than anywhere else. It is a heat-spreader: heat drawn off the hot springs and distributed into the soil so roots stay warm but not cooked, cooling the spring and warming the meadow at once. The tussock birds follow it, unknowingly, from hummock to hummock.

Half-buried in the travertine is a dome, ceramic-like and seamless, breathing slowly with the condensation cycle — in and out, in and out. Steam sighs from its base. No opening is visible. What breathes is the micro-crack lattice inside it expanding and contracting with the daily mist, a spore-body the size of a hut. Lizards use its warmed flank at noon and its cooled flank at dusk.

There are perhaps a dozen such places in the basin, perhaps more wholly taken back. Nothing blinks. Nothing projects. The moss geometry on the ring is the closest thing to a signature. When the hum changes key, the whole system shifts emphasis — from filtering to heating, from holding to releasing — and the frogs pause because they feel it through the water on their skin, and the insect chorus thins and thickens, and the rocks tick at a different rate. The warblers’ unchanged phrase is part of the same memory: a song held in a valley long enough that the valley itself seems to hold it.

Even decay here looks like maintenance. A waterfall cliff loses a pebble per decade. A lake shore moves a handspan in a human lifetime. Rain does not carve a new gorge in a season; it perfects an old one. Water enters the rock, travels through it for years, and emerges in a different spring tasting different. The system is decaying, but so slowly that the sorting of pebbles, the moss on the warm stones, the orchids facing away from each other, are the maintenance — grooming at a scale we mistake for wilderness.

The animals are its mobile parts, in a sense it would not recognise. The flies carry its orchids’ pollen and are carried in turn by its warmth. The fish crop its algae. The squirrel-creature never touches the ground because the ground is its body and it does not need to descend. The raptor rides its thermals. None of them are separate from the heat budget or the water budget. They are the places where the substrate, for a lifetime, becomes visible as fur, feather, scale, and song.

At evening the basin settles, which is not the same as sleeping. The sun drops ordinary toward the western ridge. The mist thickens inside the valleys, not falling, rising. The insect chorus changes pitch. The frogs take over the bass. The hot springs steam more heavily as the air cools a fraction — still hot, always hot, the stone holding the day’s heat under your back. The weeping rocks weep a little more. Somewhere below a node you passed, water moves through a channel cut a thousand years before you arrived, filtered, cooled, remembered, and emerges in a bog on the other side of the plateau tasting of iron and green, and the urn-frogs in their cups feel the level rise a fraction and hatch their flank-carried eggs, and the pillar exhales its held rain, and the dome breathes in.

It does not face you. It incorporates you, the way it incorporates a fallen leaf or a warm afternoon: a footprint in the bog fills with clear water, your body heat is drawn into the basalt where you sat, the flies that settle on you carry basin pollen to the next hummock. That is how anything lives here — not by standing apart from the place, but by being, for a while, one of its arrangements, green on green on green, until the arrangement changes, at the pace of soil, into another.
