Morning is a time of gathering, but not in any open place. There is no square to gather in. The inhabitants come out into the lanes with whatever they have to catch the walls.

On the north faces the stone sweats. Before the sun has found the floor — before it will for hours — condensation beads and runs in slow, bright lines, and old people and children sent by their houses go along with cloths and shallow ladles and let it drip into buckets. The moss is not wiped away. It is worked around. It has taken the dampest line, low where the seep weeps from the shale, a dark green fur on the pale rock, and the people know it by sight the way you know a neighbour's door. If the moss is thriving, the wall is good. If it thins, the wall is drying out somewhere behind and someone will have to dig to find why.

At the lowest level the main seep is caught in the shallow cistern, and that is where the town's real clock lives. Families are known by their bucket — the chipped one, the one with the rope patched, the one painted lime at the rim — and by their turn. The ladlers wait in the cool shade of the cistern wall, shoulder to shoulder, talking low about rain that may come in late summer, about a cousin whose new room broke damp, about the price of salt coming down from the plateau. The argument about water is not separate from daily life; it is daily life, repeated house by house. A child who is not yet tall enough to reach the cistern edge learns the order anyway, learns whose turn is before and after theirs, learns that to take early is to be remembered.

Behind the plain fronts, houses go deep. From the lane a house may be a single blank wall with a door and a niche for firewood, but inside it turns and branches sideways into the stone, one room behind another, each cooler and quieter than the last. The farthest rooms are for sleeping in the hottest part of the day, for the oldest people, for grain and dried beans and wool that must keep dry and close. Oil lamps burn there even at noon, because daylight does not reach, and their light is small and yellow and makes the walls look nearer than they are. People who live deepest are the last to see the sun and the first to hear rain on the slabs above their roofs. They are also the last to be disturbed, and a family will keep a deep room for a grandmother long after they themselves have had the money to move a terrace up.

That moving up is the shape of a life here. The oldest dwellings are at the bottom, gouged into the floor, their roofs at the level of a newer neighbour's second floor. As a family prospers, or a son learns a trade, or a daughter marries into a terrace house, they go up — literally up, toward the light that lasts a little longer, toward the breeze that slides down the ramps from the plateau. Those above look down on blue-white limewash and pink plaster, on washing hung across lanes, on the tops of heads on the stairs. Those below look up at walls and at other walls, and keep time not by a clock but by the moving patch of shadow and the brief, bright hour at midday when the sun drops straight down and the floor is lit normally, as if the town had been hung out to dry.

The work follows the same steps. At the raw faces, where the rock is still bare and bright in section, the stone-cutters live at the edge of things. They are the newest inhabitants and the oldest at once, because they are always making room for somebody who has not yet arrived. Their hands are white with dust that does not wash out. Behind them come the masons, dressing blocks that will be laid hard against their neighbours, sharing walls before they share words. Behind them the roofers, and behind them the plasterers with lime in white and a faint, stubborn pink. A house can pass through all those hands in a few years, and the people who will live in it watch it happen from a lane a shoulder's width away.

A terrace up, the weavers work where the air is still cool enough for wool but not damp, and the leather-workers where the smell of tannin can sit in the stone all day and nobody minds, because nobody can open a window to let it out. At the cool low ground the bakers keep the shared ovens, and there the heat is a resident too — a welcome one in a place that is otherwise always cool to the touch. Smoke rises unobstructed from the bowl, for once, and flies gather in it, and the bakers are up while the wall-sweat collectors are still out. Their bread goes up the ramps in baskets, and stone comes down on the same backs.

The market on the flat floor is less a place than a time. In the morning the plateau comes down: grain, salt, wool, the goats' cheese in cloths. The traders are half-residents. They do not live in the sink; they descend into its cool and quiet, do their business in low voices because the stone makes loudness feel rude, and climb back to the flat, thin light before dusk. The goats themselves never come down. Tethered far apart on the rim, cropping wind-bent grass from the cracks, they are the town's distant relatives — seen from below only as small shapes against the horizon, heard, if the wind is right, as a faint bell far above the rim.

The dogs are the town's gatekeepers, and they do almost nothing. Thin, stone-coloured, with paws pale from dust, they sleep in doorways where the shadow is, and when the shadow moves they move with it a few inches and sleep again. They know every sandal scuff on every step and do not bother to lift their heads for most of them. A dog is given water from a bucket's rinse, and in return it holds a threshold all day in a lane where a shoulder's width is all the space there is.

The rats live by the cistern, where there is always a damp corner and something spilled. They know the rota as well as the ladlers do, and come out after the last bucket. In the newer tunnels, where the air is perpetually moist and smells of mineral and wet stone, the smaller lives multiply in the dark: woodlice under the split slabs that cover the drains, turning over patiently in the rain that took a day to dry; spiders in the corners of storage rooms, undisturbed because nobody has room to sweep them out; silverfish in the grain rooms; moths that beat softly around the oil lamps in the deep cut rooms, feeding on light that is never entirely absent. In the cistern itself a clear, greenish skin of life holds the water — biofilm, slime, the invisible film the ladlers skim off the top with a practiced hand and do not try to kill, because water that has never been touched by anything goes stale fast in stone.

White threads of fungus creep on the timber props in those same new tunnels, on beams that began as scaffolding and were left, as everything is left, to become part of the house. They feed on damp wood that should have been replaced, and are cut back, and return. Salt blooms where the shale weeps, in pale crusts that the stone-cutters lick or brush away without thinking. It is not much, and it is everywhere.

The pigeons nest where blasting left holes in the high faces and in the eaves of houses that are still being built around them. They come and go in the shadow, their cooing muffled within a few houses like everything else, and their droppings whitewash the niches where baskets are kept — which the people accept, because a niche with pigeons is a niche that is not being dug away. At dusk the swallows come down from the rim and cut low across the bowl, down into the cool, down between the lanes, twice and three times, and then they are gone up again to the plateau dark. They are not residents. They are the town's evening examination, the proof that the sky is still somewhere above the rim.

The green things are defended, because there are so few. On the rim the scrub goes on, indifferent. Inside, a pot of beans is kept in what courtyard there is — a widening of a lane where the sun lingers longest — and watered not from the rota but from a neighbour's overflow, a spilled bucket, a favour. A stunted fig lives there too, its trunk wrapped in sacking against the cool stones, its leaves tasting of dust and of a little extra water given by hand. Vines are trained up south faces for shade, not by their owners so much as by whoever passes below with a spare piece of string, and they belong to nobody and are tended by everybody, which is how the town manages anything at all.

No one is alone in their walls. A new wall must meet an old one, a tunnel must turn before it breaks into a neighbour's room, and the turning is marked and remembered by people who may never meet the neighbour. So the inhabitants hear each other constantly and intimately: a bucket set down through stone, water dripping somewhere deep on the other side, a child calling once in a lane and quieting, the thud of a basket, the rhythm of picks at a face two terraces away. It is a crowded, saturated life, hive-like, and also a very quiet one, because the sink takes sound and gives back only what is close.

And at the edges, all around, the digging goes on. There are always fresh faces with fresh inhabitants already moving in: the white fungus, the woodlice, the moss waiting for damp, the families with their buckets waiting for a turn that has just been cut. Somewhere at the edge, stone rings once, twice. Someone who has not yet arrived is being made room for, a hand's breadth at a time.
