If you stay, you begin to notice them not by their voices.

They are not loud. The rift makes loudness expensive. Dust lives in the throat, the air takes moisture and does not give it back, and sound carries too far down the long valley between the fresh walls. So people here learn early to keep their mouths closed as they walk, to breathe through the nose through a fine weave, to move at the pace the mat allows. You will hear them before you see them only if you are listening for the other thing: the beetles.

A step on the causeway is never only a step. Under the woven roots, under the dust-green skin, something clicks and parts and goes on. The people walk as if they are walking on something that is listening, because it is. They place their feet. They do not kick. A child who kicks the mat is corrected not with a slap but with a hand on the ankle, a quiet word, a demonstration: look, the root is torn, it will take a season to close. The mat is not ground to them. It is the floor of the house, the field, the table, and the grave, all at once, and it has to be asked before it is used.

They have been down here long enough that their bodies expect the rift. The cool gets into their fingertips and stays, as it gets into yours at first, but they do not shiver it out. Their skin is dry in a way that is not sickness. It is fine, matte, almost resinous to the touch, with a faint sheen at midday when the great leaves overhead exude their oil and the pores of everything open. They do not sweat water. In heat that would be waste, and there is no water to waste. At midday, in the Pressworks, where the press-plants chew and the air is warmer, a worker's forearms will bead with a clear, dry oil that smells faintly of amber and warm leaf. They wipe it toward the elbow and let it soak back in. It is not clean sweat. It is ration.

You will never see one of them drink, if you mean drinking as you know it. There is no cup, no bottle, no tap. A meal begins with a root cake broken in the hands, dense and brown, and a handful of salt-rosette leaves chewed slowly until they give up their fluid. The fleshy desert shrubs that grow in the pans are tended like orchards. Their leaves are cut, not picked, the plant is allowed to swell again. In the districts, old hollow trees have a hollow in the wall where a leafcup is trained to fill, drop by drop, with the plant's own exudate in the light cycle. That is the day's water, and it is a few mouthfuls at most. Children grow up knowing thirst as a dryness at the back of the teeth and a craving for salt and oil, not for a river. They have no word, among themselves, for a puddle. Rain is a word they use for the spore-snow of the dry season, when the canopy sheds amber dust and it settles on shoulders and is eaten by the beetles before it can dampen anything.

With that comes a whole economy of the body. Nothing comes out wet. Waste is dry pellets, swept by practice into the beetle-runs at the edges of houses, where the blind armored beetles take it down into the rhizome by night. A person who is ill is not given fluids to replace what is lost; they are given root fluid, and shade under a light-well, and time, because fever here would cook a body that cannot cool itself by evaporation. Their lungs are made for dust. Everyone wears a mask of plant fibre when the wind comes down from a newly cut neck and carries fresh basalt smell and fine mineral grit. The masks are not uniform. A tram driver’s is stiffened with resin. A canopy tender’s is soft, woven to let spores out but not in. Old people have a permanent crease at the side of the nose where the weave sits. Their hair is kept short or braided tight, not for fashion but because loose hair catches dust, beetles, and the papery flutterers that will land on warmth. Nails are cut short and hard. Hands are strong from lifting root bales and from seeding.

They do not run, except children, and children are taught not to run on the young mat. Movement is purposeful, seasonal. A person will walk an hour across the salt pan between districts because the tram is infrequent and the grazers are moving, and it is cheaper to walk and look at the herds. They walk in the middle of the causeway, where the roots are banked deepest, and they leave the thin places alone. At night they do not walk alone if they can help it, not because of each other but because of the stalkers.

The stalkers are not monsters. They are long, low, patient things that have learned the causeways better than the people have. They have the pallor of animals that live mostly in the dark between the chambers, and eyes that do not need the full daylight of the wells. They are few. One stalker may patrol a whole district's worth of causeway for a season, taking a grazer that strays, or a root-worm from the edge, or, rarely, a person who is careless, drunk on resin fumes, or lost between stops. People do not hunt them and do not quite fear them as an enemy. They post warnings by tying a strip of curtain-vine across a side path at dusk, and they will not send a child to fetch a bale after light-dim. The stalkers' presence is part of why the districts are quiet at evening. The factories sigh down. The grazers settle in a ring, heads in. Somewhere in the mat, the beetles grow louder, because at night they open the bodies that have died.

Death is not burial here. Nothing can afford to waste a body, and the mat can afford to take one. When a person dies in a district — and they do, of age, of a press accident, of lungs that have taken too much dust in a long life — the body is laid on an open patch of mat that is marked for it, usually the thin, pale mat that farms the new mineral dust. By morning the beetles have been. By the next light cycle the minerals are in the rhizome, and a new shoot, pale and furled, will come up where the head lay. A family may tend that shoot for a year, pruning it, not because they think it is the person, but because it is the part of the person the place can keep. Old people are not hidden away. They sit in the light-wells, in the doors of hollow houses that are thickening around them, and they tell the young the names of beetles, and the names of streets that are no longer streets because the mat has taken them back a centimeter a season.

The work of a life, for most, is tending. There are the house-planters, who are engineers and gardeners in one. They lay out a ward the way a surface city might lay out a foundation, but what they lay is a scaffold of dead wood and wire and the seed of the house-tree in rows. For two months the walls are translucent, tender, humming. You can see the light through them at noon. Children knock on them to hear the different notes the growing walls make. Then they thicken. A house is never finished in the way a stone house is finished; it continues to slump, to need pruning, to push a root through a floor that must be cut and trained aside. People live for years with scaffolding merging into growing walls, with a workshop that is half press and half plant, with a schoolroom that smells of resin and leaf when the sun is high. When they move, they do not demolish. They cut the door wide, they leave the tree to hollow itself, and the mat reclaims the floor.

There are the press-people, who work the plant-machines. Their factories are planted around a metal heart, and the crews feed them shredded root, top up the resin stills, listen for the change in the pneumatic sigh that means a press-plant is molting its grip. Their clothes are woven fibre, darkened with oil, and they change them not when they are dirty but when the weave begins to root into the skin and must be pruned off. Their hands are scarred in a particular way, not from metal but from the plants that close too fast on a misplaced finger.

There are the light-well tenders, who live higher than anyone else, in the places where the pale leaves of the canopy layer over the wells and diffuse the daylight down. They are the most sun-pale, because they live in the brightest air, and the most dust-covered, because the spores come off their leaves. They trim the mirror-plants, they coax the root-tubes that bring the light down from a surface no one living has seen, they keep the fourteen-hour day for everyone below. If a well dims wrong, the whole valley knows by the slow tightening of leaves below. The tenders signal by lamps across the hollow, a slow code, because shouting across that distance would tear the throat.

There are the herders, who spend the most time in the emptiness between districts. The grazers — low, long-backed, with shovel mouths that clip the open mats — move in slow herds across the salt pans, cropping the elementary plant skin and fertilizing it as they go. A herder does not drive them as cattle. She walks at the edge of the herd for a day, keeping them off the causeways, keeping them away from the thin mat where the beetles farm the mineral. Her cloak is made of the same rosette fibre the grazers eat, stiff with salt. She knows the stalkers by their absence: when the grazers bunch and face one way, she turns their line away.

And under and through all of them, the animals that make the place work and that the people depend on without quite controlling. The dust-beetles in their thousands of kinds are not one creature but a moving civilization in the mat. Blind, armored, they tunnel and aerate, they eat leaf-fall and dead root and, at night, they open grazer carcasses and human ones with a precision that leaves the long bones clean by dawn. Children learn to tell them apart by click: the fast skitter-click of the small tunnelers, the deeper knock of the big ones that turn over rhizome like the wrist-thick root-worms do from below. The root-worms themselves are rarely seen except when a causeway is re-cut; they rise, pink-grey and segmented, turn the plant-soil, and sink again. The flutterers, papery-winged, go from light-well to light-well in the day cycle, carrying spores and pollen and, in the factory districts, carrying dust and a faint heat from press to press on their wings. They have no nest a person can point to. They roost in the warm tubes of the tram's vine-wrapped rail at night, and the rail hums a little warmer for it.

A person born here has never seen a sky. They have seen the ceiling when the vines shift, a hundred meters up, and they have seen the light come down translucent and broad. They do not dream of rain. They dream of the mat breathing in at dawn, when leaves uncurl and the air changes scent, and of the faint tick of cooling metal in the long quiet after the evening dim. Their year is not four seasons. It is the turning of the light cycle — the dry season of spores, the cold season when the wells dim a fraction and growth slows and the stills are filled — and the slow work of the rift itself, groaning once or twice an hour in their teeth, cutting a new neck, opening a new draft that makes the curtain vines shiver and carries the smell of fresh black basalt across the valley.

They are, in the end, sparse like everything else here. Not many to the mile, not dense, not hurried. A district is a handful of streets grown and regrown over itself, and the people in it are grown in the same way: layered, pruned, repaired by seeding what is missing and letting it swell shut. When you stand among them at noon, in perfect daylight with no sun, with resin under your boots and beetles clicking under your feet and a press humming through a living wall ten meters away, you realize they are not living *in* the rift the way a tenant lives in a house. They are one of the things that lives there, kept, like the grazers and the beetles and the house-trees, by the same rule that made it all possible: eat rock, waste nothing, and keep growing, very slowly, in the cool, dry light.
