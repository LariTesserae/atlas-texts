They do not live in the room. They live in the circuit.

That is how everything here is sorted — by whether it can ride the water, or cling to the edge of the water, or leave through the top and come back. Nothing arrives by walking in. Everything arrives by accident, in the inflow, in mud on a boot, in a seed lifted on the wind through a missing pane, in the gut of a bird. And once arrived, most of them never leave. The lower pipe is the only other exit, and it goes down, into cold and dark and pressure, and very few things here choose it. So the lives in this place are not a chosen community. They are the survivors of a filter, settled for so long they have forgotten there was ever a mountain stream or a hillside soil to come from.

The first residents are the ones you cannot quite see as individuals. A film. Wherever warm water meets iron and concrete, life has made a skin over the made things — bacteria that eat hot oil drop by drop and turn rust into food, iron-oxidizing slimes that bead on rivets like sweat, algae in velvets of green and black, protozoa grazing the tile in the light shafts. They are the soil here, and the first farmers. They soften the steel, they loosen the concrete to its aggregate, they make, very slowly, a place where a root can hold. If you watched for an hour you would see the waterline band change color as the grazers pass.

Above them, in the green glass water, is the rest of the population, scattered the way life in a large, bright, empty place always is: a great deal of life, and a great deal of open country between it.

The carp-like fish are the citizens of the current. Fifty will cross the center at once, slow as a procession, and then there will be no fish at all for a long while but the open green. They live by the rotation — in at the top, down the wall, across the turbine floor, up through the boiler pit, out the low pipe — and they have learned it as a road. They follow the morning surge when cold fresh water comes in and the whole sea turns over, riding the white rush, scattering seeds and fallen leaves and midges with them. Some cruise near the surface in the sun, nosing at duckweed in the still corners behind the boiler. Some stay deep, where the drowned catwalks and fallen gauges make a sunken city they graze across. Their world has no straight lines. It is a circle you can swim forever without coming back to where you started, and after enough generations, that is enough. They spawn in the warm shallows of the edge, among the pipework, and their young are carried a little farther round each time.

The turtle is slower than the circuit, or rather, she is older than it. She surfaces off a shoulder, exhales once, and is gone, and she will do this for years in the same unhurried pattern between the three islands — boiler, crane, and the fallen chimney that has become a reef. On the chimney she hauls herself into the hollow where other shells are tucked, furred in algae, as if the place keeps a memory of turtles in its stones. She eats snails and soft plants and the occasional duckweed mat. She does not need to bask the way a pond turtle does; the air above the water is already summer. She is, for all practical purposes, one of the islands herself, a warm moving rock that knows where the current is kind.

Around the waterline, in that bright intertidal band where concrete has dissolved back to rounded stone, the life is almost urban in its density. Snails graze in wide, sparse constellations, leaving silver trails across white tile that darken behind them. They come up at night to the damp edge and go down again, and the frogs are waiting for them on the pressure gauges, green knobs with throats puffing in time to the seven-second thump from below. The frogs have made their nurseries out of the architecture: rain puddles that last an hour on a catwalk after sunset, the lip of a planter trough, the still film behind a pipe flange. Their eggs are clear strings stuck to submerged iron. Their tadpoles live their first lives in water that is warm as blood and smells of minerals, and they climb out as frogs onto warm metal, which is, to them, a sunlit bank.

Eels live in the music. They slide in and out of the pipework with electrical ease, hunting the shoals when the turbine labours and the old lamps flicker. They can feel the vibration through the steel before a sound reaches the air. They are the only fish here that seem at home in the pipes themselves, in the high singing chord, in the dark narrow of the entrance tunnel where the water still has a direction and a purpose. They came up that pipe as elvers, probably, years ago, and now they run the length of the place like wiring.

Up above the water, in the twelve-meter pocket of warm air, the lives change their element but not their logic. They are still clinging to iron, still eating corrosion, still riding a circuit that is now made of steam and wind.

The plants are not decoration here; they are long-term residents, some of them older than any animal in the room. The small mangrove-habit trees on the broken catwalk rings have grown up in planter troughs built for something else, their roots gripping rusted iron instead of soil, their trunks leaning year by year toward the same patch of sky through the same missing panes. The ferns uncurl from expansion joints on a schedule set by the drip from the dome. The mosses and algae are the ground. The orchids cling to warm pipes and open their flowers to whatever moths and small bees have learned to come up through the broken glass and stay. The duckweed patches and unpatches the still corners, eaten as fast as it grows. The vines have come down true-vined from outside, roots quivering toward the waterline — some reach it, some live entirely on steam, forever descending in the hot air. And from the underside of the dome, in the hottest, wettest layer, the inverted forest grows downward, pale-leaved, thirsty, roots bare. They are not pale from shade; they are pale from drinking the air itself. At sunset, when condensation gathers on the cold glass and falls in a fine warm shower for an hour, ticking on the surface, everything in the upper world drinks at once — ferns, orchids, moss, the hanging roots — a rain made inside the room, by the room, for the room.

The insects follow the plants and the light. Midges swarm in the sun shaft and hang there, visible as dust that has decided to move. Dragonflies patrol it, lay their eggs on a floating leaf, and the nymphs ride that leaf a full circuit before it settles in a grate and becomes, itself, a plant. Spiders have strung webs across the gaps in the catwalk rings, across pipe flanges, across the gantry crane, and they take the midges and the moths. The moths are battered veterans of the old electric lamps, who buzz and flicker when the turbine labours, and at night the lamps are a small, bright, dangerous sun that a whole night-shift of fed-on things gathers around. Mosquito larvae hang in the one or two still places the current cannot reach, and are eaten, in due course, by frogs.

And the birds are the only true migrants, the only lives that can leave and therefore the only lives that can choose to stay. The swallows nest in the dome framing, building mud nests out of the same wet concrete grit at the waterline the snails are weathering, reusing the old nests year after year. The small mountain birds come in out of a wind so thin and high that clouds sometimes pass below the windows, warm themselves in the bubble of lowland summer, feed on the midges and the dragonflies and the flies around the lamps, and go back out into the cold. Their chicks fledge over water and learn to fly in a circle because there is nowhere else to fly — lap after lap of the dome, until the broken pane is found and the world drops away. At night they settle in the framing, in the boiler thicket, in the warm pipes, and the room goes to the frogs and the eels and the moths and the slow thump.

That is the food web, and it is also the maintenance crew. The algae feed the water fleas and the snails and the young fish. The fish feed the eels and the turtle and the birds. The birds drop feathers and droppings that feed the algae. The plants shade the water and feed the fish. The bacteria eat the oil, break down the dead leaf after its three circuits, dissolve the iron that the plants root in — and where the plants root, the metal fails faster, which makes more soil, which makes more plants. Nothing is maintained because the living things, eating and excreting and dying in place, are the maintenance. A fallen bird becomes insect food. A dead fish becomes a cloud of microbes, becomes algae, becomes a fish again.

No one is in charge of the seasons. The morning surge is a season. The sunset rain is a season. The turbine labouring and the lamps flickering is a season. A leaf can be born on the dome, fall, float a circuit, settle in a grate, root, and uncurl as a fern, all without any season passing that a tree would recognize. A turtle can be born — if she was born here, in a trough of warm grit on a catwalk, the only true land in the place — and grow old enough to know every submerged stair by feel. A tree can lean toward the light for thirty years and never be anything but a tree leaning toward the light.

They have all made a world out of being trapped in a circle at the top of the world, in warm water over cold machinery, in summer air above a cloud sea. They eat the container they live in, slowly, and the container, worn smooth enough, begins to read as stone. And then — because the water is still running, because the turbine is still turning at its almost biological pace, because the current still pushes everything forward with intention — they go on, in that loud, leafy, rusted circuit, without ever needing to be told to.
