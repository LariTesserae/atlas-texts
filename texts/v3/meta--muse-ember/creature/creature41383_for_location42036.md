If you stand still long enough in Varkesh, the inhabitants will come to you. They are not shy. They do not hide. They simply ignore the distinction you are used to making between inside and outside, between tended and untended, between people and everything that has decided to live alongside them.

The people are not the majority. That is the first surprise, and the first adjustment. A human being in Varkesh is damp before he has done anything to deserve it. His wool stays damp, though he hangs it, though he wears it in turn, though he takes it off at night and finds it in the morning exactly as damp as he left it — only now with a little moss beginning on the shoulder where the houseplant of the stairwell has leaned too close. His hair is damp. His skin at the fingertips and between the toes is softened by a lifetime in what never dries. His breath shows indoors. He carries his water home in a bottle from a public tap, because there is no other, and the bottle slops against his leg all the three levels up to a riveted apartment block that has been refaced twice, three inches out each time, so that the corridor is narrower than the stair that led to it.

He does not hurry. Hurry would be wasted here, and impolite. A child born in Varkesh learns, before language properly, to wait for the second sound. A boiler sighs in the foundry below and sighs again a moment later from above, where there is nothing to sigh from. A bell is struck two districts away and its echo answers before the rope is pulled a second time. So people answer slowly. You speak; your voice goes out thick in the cool air and comes back from the blank slab across the street; the person opposite you waits, nods, and only then answers, so that his answer too will have its echo, and you will wait for that in turn. Politeness here is a willingness to let a thing happen twice before you respond to it. It makes all conversation have a tide to it. It also means that no one in Varkesh is ever startled by a loud noise. They have already heard it coming, once.

Their work teaches them the same patience. In the foundry district they cast anchor plates and counterweights and brake-wheels the size of doors, parts meant to pin a mountain that is still rising to persuade itself to settle. The castings are too heavy for the geology, which is too young to receive them, and so they sit in yards for years. While they sit the workers walk around them. Moss grows over the bolt holes. A raven nests in one. A dropped wrench, a boot, a lunch tin, is slowly skinned over by new rock forming a millimeter at a time in the damp, and the workers note it and continue. They are aware they are making fossils in real time. It does not stop them; it rather becomes their condition, the way damp is their condition. Everything layers here — rust over paint over moss over dust over more moss — and human lives layer the same way. An old man will have oil under his nails that has never washed out, a cough that has been with him since adolescence, a leather apron re-riveted at the strap and then again, and a great-grandfather's mistake in a street alignment that everyone still walks around because no one has ever had the weather to erase it.

Between the districts, where they walk from one settlement to the next hand-over-hand along a rope strung between lamps, they are on their own. The factories stop, the lamps stop, and for two hundred meters there is only fog and wet stone and the sound of their own boots. They navigate not by compass — the needles drift near the foundries, settling and drifting as if north were still under negotiation — but by touch, by counting lamps, by the feel of a flywheel turning once a minute through the slab under their feet. Sometimes the rope leads to a lamp that is not on any map, burning in empty space above a drop, lighting nothing. They do not remark on it much. They touch the rope, mark the lamp in memory, and go on. At noon their shadow falls twice: once from the pale sun above, and once upward from the cloud sea below, so a walker can stand in two skyshades at once, and his children, watching, will see the second shadow of a bird pass not over the plateau but over the clouds beneath them.

They share this. That is the nature of the place. The fauna is louder and more numerous than the people, and it has no intention of being excluded.

The goats came up — no one remembers how, or whether they were brought on the geared railway in crates or climbed behind it — and now they are permanent. Shaggy, sure-footed, they graze the factory yards and slag fields in the morning, cropping moss off hot metal while it is still cool enough to bear, stepping carefully between slabs that have never been rounded by weather. The foundry workers let them do it. A goat with a mouthful of warm moss is a goat not eating the condensation nets. The people mend those nets in slow rows, copper wire strung like sails between poles to comb mist from the air, and all day they drip. The menders are the wettest people in the city. They carry the damp home in their cuffs, and their houses smell of wet iron and hot oil and the canteen cooking that never quite dries out either.

Ravens nest inside active cranes, building twigs around the machinery, stealing bearings while the machines turn. The crane minders account for it the way lowlanders account for rust. A bearing a week is the raven tax. They replace it, they check the nest for jammed twigs, they do not move it. At dusk the swifts pour from the vent stacks in black curtains, screaming as they rise, so that a shift is not so much timed by bells — bells are rung all across the plateau, out of time with each other, their echoes arriving before they are struck again — as by the departure of the swifts. The long-eared bats flicker in the halos of the always-on lamps. Around every lamp there is weather: thousands of moths swirling so thickly that the light has a body, and people walk through it with their mouths closed as a matter of course, and the moths settle in their hair and on their damp wool and are not brushed away until later because there is nowhere dry to put them.

Indoors, other lives have taken the measure of the buildings. Mice fill the grain stores, running the looped rails at their different unlevel levels as if the rails had been laid for them. Pigeons live in the scaffolding that has been left in place for twenty years over inhabited brick, a trellis and a storage cage and a pigeon house at once, because a building inside is never finished enough to take the scaffold down. At noon a mountain fox will cross an empty district as if it owned the street. In a practical sense it does, and people step aside for it not from fear but from a respect for schedule. It has always crossed at noon. It will cross again tomorrow.

Water, when it is not a rumor, has its own residents. In the condensation tanks, in the troughs and cisterns that drip all day, small blind fish circle palely, feeling the vibration of the flywheels through the steel, hearing the second sound arrive after the first, never having seen the dim grey light that is always, thinly, there. People do not drain a tank that has fish in it. That would be an eviction. They work around the tank, they scoop their bottle from the edge, they know that fish by its tank the way they know a neighbor by his bell. In the few sheltered hollows where slabs intersect just so, real pools form, black and still. Algae makes them green. Frogs sing at night in a loud wet chorus, and the chorus answers the boiler, and the boiler answers it a second later from above.

And under all of it, the slowest inhabitants are the ones who will outlast the interpretation of all the others. Moss does not grow in Varkesh so much as accrue. It forms lawns, then meadows, then soft green forests a few inches high across rooftops, across abandoned platforms, across the shoulders of statues, across the castings waiting for a geology perfect enough to receive them. A tree fern can be watched for a season and show no difference at all. Ferns burst from drainpipes, bright and frilled, drinking the condensation that runs there. Vines climb the scaffolding and the factory walls, heavy with small alpine apples in walled gardens tucked between casting sheds, apples that are sour and slow and never quite ripe in the smear of sun. Lichen paints the fresh, unweathered faces of the slabs rust, gold, and a blueish green that glows in the dim light — a painting no one commissioned and no one erases, because nothing here is eroded, nothing is scoured, nothing is cleared. A door left shut for a year may be laced shut with moss by spring. People open it anyway, tearing the green lace, feeling the damp stone give a little under their shoulder, and the moss will begin again by the following week as if the tearing had been a kind of pruning.

At night, when night is only day made darker and the lamps more distinct, the whole plateau becomes a constellation of points across a black wet plain, each district haloed in its own fog. The inhabitants do not lessen. The flywheels keep turning, one revolution a minute, felt in the feet through the wet stone. Somewhere a swift screams at a moth. Somewhere a goat steps between slabs. Somewhere a new slab, out in the emptiness between settlements, rises a centimeter, and no one hears it except as a tiny deep click in the stone under their feet — the mountain shifting, making room for them all again, a little higher.

Varkesh does not finish its lives, and it does not fall down around them. It accrues them.
