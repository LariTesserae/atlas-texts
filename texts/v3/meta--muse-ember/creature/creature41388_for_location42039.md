The first inhabitants are not the ones you came to look for. They are the ones you are standing on.

Put your weight down, low and wet, and the floor gives and gives back slowly, as if it were breathing under you. A moss cushion that looked like a single green lump turns out to be crowded and busy at a scale that makes your own footstep a weather event. Pale commas — springtails — fold and unfold and jump clear. Mites go by in a slow, deliberate procession, file after file, carrying grains much larger than themselves. Translucent worms, no thicker than hairs, thread themselves in and out of the root felt, disappearing and reappearing a finger's width away. This is not dirt with animals in it. The animal mass and the plant mass are the same mass, and both are eating each other in a slow, managed way.

If you stay still long enough, the next things to notice are the eyes.

Not one pair. Hundreds of points of eyeshine pricked along the black weeping walls, opening and closing independently, as if each individual had decided to look at you at a different moment. In the grey seam of light from above they catch green. In the darker corners they catch only a dull amber, or nothing. Panic, at this size, is not flight. It is freezing. Cold makes almost everything here move at a pace you can miss if you blink, and stillness is the first defense: a body that does not move, the colour of wet moss, is hard to read in the dark.

Then, in the lanes, the stillness breaks in a particular rhythm. Small feet, quick for them, going at a crawl for you. They keep to the broad clearings and to the narrow paths along the water seams, and they keep to each other. A flow of them will come down a lane, stop, crouch, work at something with their hands, then move on. The traffic of those feet is what keeps the canals free of ice. By morning, if there is a morning down here, a skin has formed over the black mirrors a few millimetres deep. By the time the drips have broken it in a dozen places, they are out again, breaking it the rest of the way, wading through to the far bank on roots they have trained for the purpose.

The hands are what give them away as a town, and not merely a colony.

They are damp, blue-green and waxy like the leaves overhead, almost the colour of the light that comes through those leaves. Long fingers, disproportionately long, with pale nails. The body that goes with them is easy to miss at first because it is low and wet and cold to the touch, and it carries its house the way other creatures carry a shell — no, more the way a plant carries itself. A down of fine moss grows thick along the back and in the folds at the neck and wrists, and they tend it. They comb it, they patch it, they press fresh spores into it when it thins. It is insulation and it is camouflage and it is, when they are young, a nursery. When an old one dies, that down is stripped and sewn into a wall. A living one will sit for a long time in a doorway and let the wall grow close around its shoulders.

They are about the size of a man's thumb to the length of his palm, none of them larger. A man can enter by kneeling in the wet, chest-high at most, and to them your knee is a hill that blocks the light for a quarter of an hour. They learn your weight quickly. Everything in the hollow learns it, plants first.

They do not have a king, or a word you would recognize for one. They have quarters, and the quarters tell you what work is valued.

The furthest quarter is by the coldest seep, where the wall sweats the clearest water. That is where they drink, and where they lay. The eggs are leathery and translucent, tucked singly into the warm felt of the moss, and they are left there to swell in the cold. The clearest water is the safest water, and the coldest water keeps rot slow. The elders — those who have gone mostly blind in the dark, their eyes filmed white like the salamanders in the canals — sit there longest, listening to the drips come from the root-vault, and answering them with soft clicks.

The quarter by the seam of grey light is the busy one, and the loud one, for here. This is where the heat is thieved. The slit a hand's width across is not much light, but for a cold-blooded creature it is warmth, and it is the only place their stiff fingers loosen enough to do fine work. They gather there in knots, shoulders touching for shared warmth, and they work the copper.

Copper is the only hard, foreign thing they own. It comes in, they think, on flood water from the marsh above — thin wire, already drawn, already worked by something else. They hammer it flat between stones, bend it around joints, and use it where sinew and plant fibre would rot too quickly: at the corner of a storehouse door, at the hinge of a gate made of two crossed stems, at the place where a roof-patch must hold against the constant drip. In the wet it tarnishes green, and they taste that green on their fingertips and spit, and go on. No metal saw has cut their thatch. A roof is sewn. A canal bank is pinned with root-stakes driven by hand, one shift at a time. A bridge is not built; it is encouraged, notched, tied, and kept tied until the root takes and thickens enough to carry traffic, and water in its hollow heart.

In the nursery terraces — low shelves of moss in rows as straight as a human field — the youngest tend the youngest. Not only their own young, though the hatched things, still tailed and huge-eyed, cling there and pick at springtails. The terraces are for sedge. Young sedge for food, sedge for building, sedge for rope. The young are taught root work there, and taught it by watching the slow pulse of growth: a tick, so faint you feel it through the floor, as a root puts on a hair's breadth in the cold. The colony of root-grazers is kept on the edges of the terraces as livestock. They strip a row in a week, and the sedges push fresh growth, and the grazers are eaten in turn, and the soil of their bodies feeds the next row. Predator and prey here are not enemies to be exterminated. They are a rotation.

The canal-keepers are the ones you will see in the water. They wade the main canal from the entrance seam to the far wall, breaking ice with their fists, clearing the smaller irrigation cuts where the growth wants to choke them. Their work is constant because nothing is allowed to dry out or stay still. A channel left for a few days silts with spores, closes with root-hair, freezes solid at the edges. They are also the ones who watch the water for the other hunting rhythm: the sudden silver ripple under black water when a salamander or a dozen small predators go through in a coordinated wave, driving grazers and skaters and sometimes a careless builder toward the banks. Things underneath flash, and then the water is empty again, and the eyes on the walls open again, one by one.

The walls themselves are inhabited in a way that has no clean line between tenant and owner. A dome-house is lashed to a living root-column that thickens through the winter, and the column is host to mites and worms and froglets flattened hard against the damp. Those froglets are not pets, exactly, and not food, exactly. They live in the double skin of the wall, in the gap kept for insulation, eating the flies and rot-midges that would eat the thatch. A living animal, huddled in a roof, becomes insulation. A dead animal, dried and woven, becomes a brick. The builders recycle without ceremony. When a house is abandoned, they do not pull it down. They eat its edges, they let a root grow through its roof, they let the grazers strip it. The boundary becomes food, and the food becomes a boundary again.

Out toward the edges of the hollow there are isolated huts with wide, empty moss between them. You will not find much company there. Some are the blind elders who have chosen the dark. Some are the young who failed a winter and could not keep a house patched and patched again each layer — outer dead sedge, living mat, inner felt — and who have been set apart until they recover or they do not. A few are simply those who prefer to live lean, close to the predation cycle, and who come back to the quarters only to trade copper work and springtail protein and news carried in the click-chorus from the far wall.

That chorus is their speech, at distance. Up close it is a series of croaks and soft percussive ticks made with the throat and the tongue, punctuated by vibrations sent through root and moss — a hand pressed flat to a bridge, a foot stamped on a hollow stem. They have carvings, but the carvings are not writing. They are the marks of making: the chewed edge of a stem, the scrape of flint, the needle-hole where a patch was sewn. The history of the town is in the repairs.

They are not large, and they are not fast, and in the cold they spend half their lives huddled and still, sharing heat in the double-walled houses, their moss-downs pressed together, their big eyes half closed against the translucent glow from above. But the town is young — you can read it in how crisp the patches still are, how sharp the split edges are under the green film — and it is crowded in the way a new, fertile place is crowded. Every structure is a plant under cultivation. Every plant is a road, or a meal, or a wall. Every animal is a worker, a food store, a predator, or a building material, sometimes in the same hour.

And woven through all of it, not separate from it, are the others who were never builders and never will be: the springtails flicking through the felt like punctuation, the worms making the substrate breathe, the blind salamanders nosing the canals with lidless white eyes, the skaters dimpling the mirrors, the night things on the walls whose eyeshine is not the builders' at all but something larger, patient, that blinks slower.

If you leave, you leave backwards, low, through the same wet seam, and all of them freeze again. The eyes close. The traffic stops mid-step. The drip from the vault goes on falling into the black water, making its small, regular rings.

From above there is only a tipped root-plate in low ground at the edge of the marsh, already mossed over, easy to step over. Inside, the town is already unfreezing, a hand reaching for a bone needle, a root putting on its next hair's breadth, a grazer beginning again on the edge of a terrace. Busy, always busy, growing and being eaten and rebuilding itself in the dark, on a mattress of plants that is itself alive, and crowded with eyes.
