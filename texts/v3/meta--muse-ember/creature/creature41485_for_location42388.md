You do not meet them all at once, and you never really meet them one by one. You hear them first, before you can pick any single body out of the dim — the load-bearing thrum in your molars from the core far below, the water hammer stuttering somewhere in the plates, the hiss of steam through tile pores, and riding over all of it the ticking. Rain on a tin roof, only indoors, only from the walls. Then the hum that is not a hum, a crowd of wings so blended you feel it in the washment on your forearms. Then, lower, a rasping under the pool edges, patient as a file on metal. It is not background noise. It is conversation. It has been going on without pause for longer than the tiles have been matte.

If you stay still long enough, your eyes adjust past the amber floor glow and the turquoise shallows, and the walls stop being walls.

The first inhabitants are the ones you are most likely to miss because they do not move fast enough to be called moving. The white, green, and faintly iridescent crusts in the grout are not mineral bloom. They are skin. Thin, layered colonies of film-life live in every seam, tolerance stacked on tolerance, color deepening where the water film is thick and thinning to pale where it is washed bare. They breathe on the hour, drinking the ceiling rain and the sliding wall films, exhaling steam. On heat, their edges lift a fraction; in the cool trough, they tighten and harden. Somewhere in them is the only true primary work being done in the Roost. They fix carbon out of dust that settles — dust from the tower fins, from shed shell, from a spore blown in when the iris opens — and they do it with heat and copper and salt rather than sunlight. They are food, lung, roof, and soil for everything else, and colonies of them are very probably older than any animal in the room. When the big grazer scrapes a tile bright, a film closes over it again within days, not from a seed in the air but from the film itself crawling, millimeter by millimeter, out of the grout lines, a green tide re-taking its own country.

The runners farm that tide.

Thumb-sized, copper-backed, six-legged, smooth as wet river stones, they cover the vertical surfaces so thickly that a square meter of wall holds hundreds. Their backs are not colored copper; they are copper, plated and slightly heat-conductive, and they carry the warmth of the wall they have just run down in their shells and give it up slowly where they pause. Their feet are the ticking. Each leg ends in a pad that grips wet ceramic without leaving a mark, and they run in lanes — up on the right of a groove, down on the left — following the glossy polished highways their bodies have worn brighter than the tile middles. They do not wander. A runner goes from film pasture to water groove to basking panel and back on a route it will run its whole life, which is perhaps a few months.

They lick. Where the ceiling mist condenses and slides down the molded grooves, a runner hangs head-down, forefeet braced, tongue forked and quick, and drinks the moving film drop by drop. What they are really harvesting as they drink: the film’s exhalations, the dissolved salts, the airborne spores that have ridden the rain down. They carry those mouthfuls back into the crust and spit them into it. In return the film feeds them in a continuous, almost boring way — they graze as they walk, mouthparts combing the green-white mat clean, pruning it the way a gardener prunes a lawn, keeping it young and dense and preventing any one colony from thickening enough to seal a duct. A wall cleared of runners for a week would choke itself.

And they talk through their feet. A chirp from a runner is a vibration punched into tile and plate, traveling through the structure faster than any leg. Lift of a middle leg, tap tap — *food here*. A longer shiver — *heat coming, move down*. During the heat tide, when the floor shudders and the rain thickens, those messages cross and double and braid until, to a human ear, it is only deafening clamor. To them it is a crowded switchboard, still functional in the noise. If you put your bare palm flat on a warm tile while a lane is running, you feel the sentence pass under you.

Above — if above means anything in the steam — hang the droplet-fliers, and they are less a species than a weather system. Pinhead midges, wings so fine they catch the amber and throw it back as flecks, they fill the upper air in a shimmering ceiling, bank and disperse and re-form around the steam vents in waves that have nothing to do with wind. Their hum rises and falls with their density, and the swarm uses it that way, turning a collective pitch into a signal to wheel away from a vent about to belch, or to close over a warm updraft, or to settle for a drink on a droplet mid-fall.

They do not live long — days, a week in the richest steam. But they are the hinge the whole room turns on. A flied female coats her eggs in a clear droplet shell and lets go, and the egg rides the ceiling rain down. It does not hatch in the air. It hatches under the grating, in the braided warm current, and the larva it becomes is one of the blind white things pulsing there. That is its whole childhood: translucent, eyeless, no bigger than a fingernail, attached to the duct wall, opening and closing like a small heart as the current passes through it. It has no mouth worth naming; it absorbs. Dissolved waste, mineral dust, the sloughed film it can strain from the water — it takes it in through skin and ciliated gills, cleans the water by passing it through its body, and grows fat on the cleaning. Colonies of them line the underfloor ducts in rows, swaying in unison when the water hammer hits, swelling on the heat tide and shrinking after. They are kidney, gill, and nursery at once. When enough of them have fed and the tide warms the current, they detach, pupate in a bubble of their own making that rises on the small steam lifts, and emerge at the surface as wings. The cloud in the air is constantly being born from the water below it, and constantly dying into it. A flier that falls into a pool does not drown. It is eaten.

The pools have their own file. Flat, armored, paddling grazers move through the ankle-deep shallows in slow, nose-to-tail processions, scraping tile with rasping mouthparts. Their armor is fringe-edged so steam bubbles sliding off the hot floor burst harmlessly on them, and their paddling feet keep them in the braided channels, deliberately, keeping the current open. A file does not overtake; the grazer behind touches the tail of the one ahead with a feeler and adjusts pace. They mow the pool film as runners mow the walls, leaving that bright, polished track behind them which is really a track of continuous cropping. Young ones are almost invisible, translucent shields no bigger than a dime, riding in the grout lines between tiles, scooping crumbs of shell and film; old ones are dark, barnacled with healed scars where a bubble has pitted them or a runner has ridden them for a free scrape. They live by the pool edges where heat is on the edge of steam, tolerating what would scald anything else, and they are themselves food — for anything big enough, and ultimately for the film that blooms richer in a grazer’s wake than anywhere else.

If you watch the grout long enough through a whole heat tide, you find a second, quieter layer of life that never rises to the surface. In the cracks, where a hand-sized tile split and a fine raised vein healed it, the healing was not passive. Slender thread-worms the color of the scar itself live inside the ceramic lamination, and when the tower’s thermal groan opens a crack a fraction, they come out in a pale mat, lay down copper-calcium paste, and withdraw, sealing the tile from inside with their own bodies as scaffolding. Their work is why nothing leaks out. In the grout valleys, smaller still, translucent shredders two millimeters long pick at everything dead — shed runner plates, fallen fliers, chipped crust, the sediment building in the corners into low terraces. They are almost never noticed because they are the color of water on tile and they freeze when the big grazer’s steps vibrate through the floor. But the terraces are their work, grain by grain, turning shell into soil into food again. Nothing leaves, and this is how: it is shredded small enough to be eaten.

The plants are outnumbered and it shows. In the one cool trough by the intake pipe where outside air still drafts through, a dozen engineered silver ferns persist, each leaf etched with fine conductors that do a very unplantlike thing: they collect dew along the leaf and conduct it, in a slow capillary script, back down to the roots. They photosynthesize almost nothing; there is not enough light and the air is too wet and hot for proper leaf life. They are more like small, living heat wells than food plants, and the Roost treats them that way. Fliers settle on frond tips after a tide to drink the conducted dew. A private mite, silver-gold to match the leaf, lives only on those fronds, moving so slowly through the conductor grooves it looks like a bead of light traveling; it crops nothing, it only drinks. Runners prune the frond tips when they grow too long, nibbling them back to a manageable length — pruning that releases the leaf to make another. The moss-filaments hanging like wet hair from ceiling seams are not quite plants at all, but a woven mat of filament and film, and runners lay their eggs in its damp. On a bad heat tide the filaments are eaten down to threads, and they regrow within days from the film beneath.

And then there is the one who uses the whole room.

Dog-sized, humped, moving through the central basin with enormous patience, its shell plated in the same heat-pipe ceramic as the walls, facet for facet, scar for scar matching the floor tiles. It is not a pet, and it is not alone in the way a single animal is alone; it is the old gardener of a rotation its ancestors established, a concave worn path through the basin that its own feet have been deepening for generations before it was born. It scrapes biofilm and mineral crust with a broad, unhurried mouth, brightening the tile behind it like a plow, and then it moves off and lets the film reclaim the furrow. It will come back next cycle. It knows the cycle the way its shell knows heat.

It carries a retinue because it is warm and slow and big enough to be a landscape. Runners ride its shell plates picking film from the seams where its own body heals itself. Fliers cluster over its back when it rests in the basin, riding its furnace-warmth. When it defecates — an event the blind white things and the grout shredders seem to anticipate, converging from the grating and the grout lines — the whole lower tier reorients around it for an hour. The grazer’s breathing is the deep groan of its shell expanding with steam; during a heat tide it stops in the deepest pool, plates ticking as they cool, and waits with a dignity that makes the frenzy of the runners look young. No one knows how old it is. Ceramic-shelled, heat-loving, moving that slowly, it could be decades, could be a century. When it dies — and everything in the Roost dies in place — it will not be removed. Runners will quarry its plates for copper and ceramic, thread-worms will seal its cracks, film will close over it, pool grazers will file across it, and in a few cycles it will be a bright patch of scraped tile in the basin, indistinguishable from its own plowing, still feeding everyone.

Every twenty minutes or so, the tide comes, and all of this — film, runner, flier, grazer, white pulse, worm, shredder, fern, gardener — enters its other phase. Heat rolls up through the floor. The rain thickens until it is a hiss you feel on your neck. The chorus climbs until your voice, if you tried to use it, would come back muffled and estranged. Runners pour down the walls in a living waterfall a thousand thick, legs ticking so fast it becomes a continuous roar through the soles; fliers collapse into a single dense ball around the vents; pool files break and scatter and re-form; white things swell until the grating is a field of pulsing ivory; the big grazer lifts its head once and settles deeper, a rock in a river. It is panic and it is festival and it is, for many of them, the only mating signal that matters — eggs released in the warm rain, larvae loosed into the surging current, runners laying in the damp moss at the exact moment their vibrations told them to. Then the tide passes, the water hammer stutters, the clamor drops by a fraction, and the migration reverses. The walls repopulate. The pools refill with files. The film spreads into the places that were scraped bare. Everyone resumes a route.

There is no predator in the Roost worthy of the name, because the heat is the predator, and because everyone eats everyone in due course. A runner that dies on a wall is picked clean by its lane-mates within hours, its copper back sun-bleached by floor glow and carried down by the film. A flier that falls is a pool grazer’s meal. The grazer’s dead are white things’ life. Their own waste is film’s life. Death is not an exit; it is a transfer. That is why the density holds. No surface is ever empty for long enough to be surface.

Late, if you crouch very still by the service slit — the white slash of high sky, the cold draft that never reaches the center — there is one more habit to notice. A single lanky runner, longer in the leg than the others, lives in the groove of the iris itself, half in the high wind and half in the warm, never crossing the threshold. It eats whatever dust and seeds the outside air pushes through when the iris shudders open, and when the light slashes in, it darts back a body-length into the damp and watches. It is the only resident with a horizon, and it does nothing with it. It comes back to the film on the groove and licks and goes on ticking.

They were seeded here, or they crawled in through a seam, or they were left in the original heat-exchange works as engineered stock and went feral and softened. It no longer matters to them. The runners’ copper, the grazer’s ceramic, the ferns’ conductors, the film’s appetite for heat — the machinery and the life have eroded into each other until you cannot say where the tower ends and the Roost begins. The cracked tile heals because something living closes it. The steam is caught and rained again because something living drinks and exhales it. The groan of expansion is a heartbeat because a hundred thousand bodies are expanding with it, on schedule, without you.

Leave, and the iris shuts behind you with the high wind screaming on the fins. The dim closes like water. Inside, the ticking never paused, the files never broke formation for more than a tide, the gardener takes one more slow step along its ancestors’ concave road, the white things pulse in the current under the grating, and the rain goes on falling from a ceiling that is making its own weather, as it has, in its own established, crowded, thrumming order, for longer than anyone needed to remember.
