At first there is only what is under your feet, and it is difficult to say when ground stops and something living begins.

The mat comes first. Not in patches, not in drifts, but as a continuous skin over the fractured rock. Thick pads overlap each other like scales, copper-green in one place, sea-glass teal in the next, dusty violet where the light strikes sideways. They are waxy to a brushing hand, and completely dry. When you push through them they do not give like moss or grass. They flex a degree and set again. The stem of a pad, if you look close enough, is not hollow or pulpy. It is filled with something warm, an ionic slurry that moves only when bent, and hardens to a salt gel when at rest.

You walk on them without quite crushing them. Your step makes a crisp, dry crunch that dies at once in the porous layer, and then the mat is whole again behind you, not healed — healing would imply a change — but simply unaltered. A scratch you leave on a pad with a fingernail is not an injury in any familiar sense. It stays. A year later, if you could return, it would be the same pale line in the same color, unweathered, unsoftened. The plant does not bleed if you cut deeper. It hisses. A puff of compressed gas escapes from the wound, and salt crystallizes in the air around the edge and seals it in place, as if the closing were always meant to look like that.

Beneath the pads there are no roots searching for water. There is nothing below to search for. What goes down are anchors and mining arms, pale and stiff, splitting the young rock a fraction at a time to draw out phosphate, sulfur, iron, trace metals. They do not drink. They mine. And what is up turns out not to be for drinking either.

Push up from the mat, knee-high, waist-high, and the second layer rises out of the first on stiff stalks: fans. Each fan is made of folded plates, each plate ribbed like a miniature roof tile, each surface polished to a perfection that makes you distrust calling it organic. Sage, rust, silver-blue, burnt orange, a deep bottled green — the green is concentrated and defensive, as if carried to excess in all of them. They do not grow toward the sun. They are already at the sun's angle, each sheet holding its tilt as if set, not grown. Where the ground beneath makes its shallow, unfinished wrinkle up a meter or two, the fans interlock overhead into a thin, low canopy, and you have to duck and part stems to pass. The light through them is hard and yellow-white, the same as the sky. The shadows are hard too, and sometimes wrong: a frond will cast two, one true and one at a right angle to it, both sharp. From one side a leaf is solid; from another it is translucent, without being any thinner. It does not explain itself by moving.

Their work is inhalation. The air here is dry and still and metallic on the tongue, carrying a permanent, invisible breath of carbon dioxide and mineral vapor — sulfur compounds, fine silicate dust — that never condenses and never rains. The leaves take it in molecule by molecule across their whole polished surface. Under the hint of a microscope there are no cells as we know them. There is a lattice at the nanoscale, interlocking, tunneling molecules directly from air into interior chemistry, repairing its own structure without waste, signaling across the entire mat without nerves and without any root connection you can trace. Inside that lattice, photosynthesis proceeds in the ordinary light, carbon is fixed, the warm salt gel carries what is made to where it is needed, and nothing has ever required a drop of solvent to do it.

That is why nothing rots here. A fallen fragment does not soften. It is either reabsorbed by the living mat around it, molecule by molecule through those same surfaces, or it reverts to stone, dry and unchanged. The ground is not enriched by death. It does not need to be, and death, in the sense of decay, has been politely declined.

Higher up, if you can call the canopy higher, there are the things that hang and do not fall. In the still air, spores drift like fine grey snow that never quite lands. They turn slowly, catch the light, turn again. Below them, in clusters on the fans, hang dry glassy seeds, abundant and patient. They do not burst in waves. Sprouts appear in the same places forever, as if germination happened once and was fixed in place. You understand, walking, that this is not a meadow in progress. It is a meadow after completion, lush and arrested at the exact moment of fullness. The temperature at noon is the temperature at noon tomorrow, felt as the same steady warmth rising out of the stone under your boots. The system regulates its own air and its own stillness with no center you can find, no machinery you can point to. The engineering is not in objects. It is in how the laws apply here.

And yet it is not empty of animal life. It is only rare in proportion. You can walk a kilometer, more, with no track, no dropping, no burrow, no web, no bird, no insect sound to answer your steps. The silence is engineered out of things absorbing it. Your own breathing and the pulse in your ears become the loudest events, except occasionally — far off in the mat, at no regular interval — a crystal plate cools by a fraction and pings, high and singular, and is gone.

When animal life does appear, it is singular, and it shares the plants' unhurriedness to a degree that makes motion itself seem doubtful. A stepper. Low to the silt, ash-grey, armored in mirrored plates that throw back the plain until it is visible mostly as a distortion moving across the mineral film. It grazes the film from rock with a mouth built for scraping glass. It does not drink. It has a nervous system, presumably, but it moves so slowly across the fractured ground that you understand it has not moved since you first saw it, and will not have moved perceptibly when you look again. It does not turn toward you. It does not flee. The fans do not turn toward it. You pass, and it continues its scraping at a pace that belongs to geology, not to animal.

There are smaller things still. Motes in the sunlit air, drifters without wings or sound. They do not land. Perhaps they are not meant to feed in any way you can see; perhaps they are only permitted to drift, held up in air that should not hold them so steadily, carried by vapor movements too fine for skin to feel. They make no hum. They leave no sign. For long stretches you will see none, and then your eye will catch three or four of them holding their slow station above the canopy, as if the air itself had decided to keep them.

That is the whole population, as far as your senses can reach: a mat that mines and inhales, a fan-layer that holds its angles, a canopy that meets itself overhead, hanging seeds that will never be sown elsewhere, spores that will never fall, a stepper that has almost never arrived, and drifters that never land. One distributed, lattice-bound skin of life, repeating its exact arrangement of stones and fan-plants every two hundred meters or so, as if tiled from a template, its distances not quite adding up across the invisible seams — ten steps counted becoming fifteen, and never counting the same way twice — its colors leaving an afterimage in the air for a second longer than they should.

Nothing here was built. There is no road through it, no wall, no tool, no shard of metal, no ruin to suggest a maker. And yet no part of it is unfinished by weather. The crystals poke unpatinaed out of the dust as if they grew yesterday and simply stopped. The plates have not been softened. The edges have not been rounded. The inhabitants live in that same unfinished-yet-finished condition: not wounded, not healed, not growing, not shedding, not fruiting in season because there is no season to fruit in. They touch you as you pass, dry and cool and waxy, and they do not miss the water that has never existed here.
