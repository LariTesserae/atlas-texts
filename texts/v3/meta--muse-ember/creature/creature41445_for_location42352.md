Kneel, and the Pavement stops being empty.

What you first thought was a thin skin of grey dust between the plates is alive, but it does not announce itself the way lowland ground does. It does not move as a mass, or rustle, or green. It is a biological crust — dust bound by cyanobacteria and fungal threads — and if you look long enough you see it is busy at a scale that makes your own stillness look like speed.

Springtails move across it, grey as peppercorns, pausing, hopping, grazing. Rock mites work the pale undersides of lichen. Under any plate you can lift with effort there is another country, damp and dark in the way this place allows darkness to be: not cool, not shaded from the sun in any useful sense because shadow itself is luminous here, but sheltered from the scrape of the wind. There, in the film of moisture that never becomes wetness, are thousands of snow fleas. They do not swarm. They teem. They hop in the same damp film, hour after hour, in a population so distributed it is less a colony than a condition of the stone.

The plants, when you find them, are almost indistinguishable from the plates they grow on. That is their first defence and perhaps their only one. A cushion plant no taller than your boot sits in a hollow or a pan, a dome hard as stone to the touch. Hair-grasses find the crack-lines. Rosette herbs press themselves flat. Lichens paint the windward faces in pale green and sulfur yellow and, here and there, a stained red so startling it looks like a smear of something warm on the cold stone.

They do not sway. There is no still in which to test whether they would. They do not green. They do not die back in any season you could name, because there is no season to name. They grow so slowly no eye can catch them changing, and they are grazed as fast as they grow, so that at any moment a cushion looks exactly as it looked an hour ago, and an hour ago. Some of them, after rain that has not fallen for weeks, smell faintly of rain. It is a quiet abnormality. They persist the way the stone persists: by being already adapted to a balance that never tips.

Where the stone holds a pocket of damp — a clay pan with a film a centimetre deep, black and perfectly still except for the tremor the wind-sound puts on its surface — life compresses again into that film. No river lives here. No lake. Nothing with banks or depth to swim in. The moisture arrives as condensation, beads on the undersides of plates, weeps into the hollows, and holds at exactly the same size. Its edge evaporates at exactly the rate its centre condenses. The snow fleas and mites and springtails are the inhabitants of that edge. They are the hydrology, made animal.

The first vertebrate you can pick out with certainty is not the loudest, though it is loud enough. The snow-crows rule the crevices. They are crow-like, dark, and entirely practical. They carry small sharpened pebbles in their beaks — not to keep, not to collect, but to lever lichen from rock and to crack shells. They use the stone, drop it, and fly on. The tool does not accumulate. Within a day it is indistinguishable from the wind-sorted stones around it. The next crow will invent the same tool again, for a single use, and abandon it again.

They build nothing. A nest, here, is feathers and down wedged into a hole that erosion made long before the bird existed. The hole is the architecture. The bird is only the tenant who fills it with warmth and noise.

And noise is the other country. Above the crows, in the cliff bands where the pavement breaks and the holes are mellow with age, the colonies live in a multitude that contradicts everything else about density on the Pavement. Thousands of small, long-winged birds nesting in those holes, calling in a rotation that never rests. To call them a chorus is to imply a beginning and an end. There is neither. One bird stops, another has already begun. The sound is a wall, at the same pitch, in blinding light and in darkness, because for them there is no other instruction. They call with the same intensity at what would be night, because the light above the cloud-sea does not change its mind. Their long wings are made for a wind that never drops. They ride it, scream into it, and are carried by the updraft that also carries their food: midges and snow-insects drifting up perpetually from below, a fallout that never ends, food arriving on the same current that makes the fissures whistle.

You may not see the rodent at all, if you are not patient. It is small, ground-bound, and it has one piece of technology. It uses a short stick to probe for insects under plates, and leaves the stick there when it is done. Like the crows' pebbles, the stick is invented for a single use and abandoned to the pavement. No fire could be kept here — the air is too thin, fuel too sparse, wind too constant — and the rodent does not try. It works the damp undersides, where the fleas hop, and moves on.

The grazers are the only animals here whose size matches the scale of the place, and even they refuse to gather. Goat-like, thick-woolled, they move across the region singly or in families no larger than that, one family to a valley of stone, each holding a territory so vast you can walk an hour across pavement and pass three plants and no sign of them. Their breath makes a brief, thin mist even at midday; the air is always cool, and cannot hold heat however violently the sun rebounds off the pale quartzite and schist. They crop the cushions and the rosettes and the hair-grasses at exactly the rate of growth, which is to say, at a rate no one can measure. If you watch one, you will think it is not eating. If you return, the cushion will be the same size.

Above them, rarer still, is the great eagle-analogue. It circles for hours without flapping, riding the constant wind at an altitude where the curve of the horizon begins. It does not hunt in the way lowland raptors hunt, swooping and striking and gone. It is just there, a dark shape patiently using the same air that sings through the cracks. Predation here is not an event. It is a presence, thin as the rest of life, stretched across forty kilometres of table and then another table and then a broken spine of a ridge.

All of them live without accumulation. No structures, no shelters, no walls, no paths worn into permanence. A footprint in the fine dust of a basin is erased before a cloud passes below. A nest is nothing. A tool is nothing after the moment. Stone and bone are enough, and even they are not kept.

And all of them live in the same unchanging order. The birds call in darkness and in light with the same intensity. The cushions have not moved. The melt-pockets are the same size. The grazers have moved, but the number of grazers has not. A death somewhere in a crevice or under a plate is precisely replaced, on a timescale you cannot see, by a birth that looks exactly like the life before it.

If you stay long enough to learn them by their smallness — the crust under your boot, the fleas under the stone, the crow dropping its pebble, the rodent leaving its stick — you realize abundance here was never meant to be density on the surface. It is a thin, region-wide film of life that covers everything if you look long enough, and then, in a few narrow bands of cliff and crevice, concentrates into a deafening, obvious multitude. The rest is hollow grandeur, clean and empty and bright and cool, and the animals who live there have learned to be as durable, as unchanging, and as indifferent to being noticed as the pavement itself.
