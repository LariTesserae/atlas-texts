First you listen for who lives there, and the room does not let you.

It is too loud for a voice to live in comfortably. The inflow hammers in the deep corner, the sluice bangs and chatters against its frame every few minutes, a drip from a ceiling joint lands like a stone thrown down a well, and everything the vault is made of — brick, lime, water, steam — throws it back. You would have to shout across a space you could throw a ball across. A place that loud, that bright, that full of moving water, makes you expect a body to go with it. Something large enough to make that much noise on purpose.

The bodies that made it are the first thing you find, and they are not there.

They are in the thumbprints still pressed into the mortar, in the chisel marks where a mason hurried, in the pale unweathered crack through a brick at shoulder height. They are in what they left on the dry ledge: three wooden paddles leaning at different angles, dark at the ends from use; a stone hammer half-sunk in the shallows; a coil of wet rope on the second step; a row of unglazed jars bobbing in line in the green water like buoys; the broken frame of a loom or hide-stretcher with its legs poking up. These are not the possessions of a resident. A resident would have made a place for sleeping, for eating, for not being wet. There is no bench, no storage, no decoration. There is only the equipment of work, set down and not yet picked up.

By day, you can reconstruct them: a ladder let down from above, men or women wading waist-deep at the steps, shouting over the roar, paddling wool or hides or laundry through hot mineral water while the spring pushed its cloudy, bubble-bursting inflow in at one end and the counterweight sluice lifted and dropped at the other. They were visitors with hands and lungs and jobs elsewhere — in the courtyard above, where you can hear dull thumps and the scrape of a cart through the brick crown. Down here they only soaked, and pulled off wet work, and climbed back up. At night they are gone entirely, and the room goes on without them, filling and emptying on its stone-and-rope timer, as if the absence of people were the intended condition.

After people, you look for animals, because water that warm and that clear in any other place would have something in it. Here there is nothing, and the nothing is total. You look down through water so clear you can see the far wall as plainly as the near one, bricks bleached white under the surface, fired-looking though wet, continuing in uneven hand-laid courses to the far side. No flicker. No dart. No snail track on the brick, no web, no insect landing to be driven off by the steam. The water is too warm, too lime-rich, too fast and too regularly scoured by the sluice cycle for a fish to stay, for anything with gills or legs to cling. The centre of the room — the deep channel by the far wall, the spring-mouth where it rises almost to the arch — is uninhabitable in the animal sense. It is paved, perfectly paved, and perfectly empty.

What lives here lives at the edges, because the edges are the only place it is allowed to.

On the north side of the vault, where the light through the three dinner-plate vents is a little less brutal, a thin film of green algae paints the brick in streaks running down from the joints. It does not cover the room. Elsewhere the walls are bare white, thrown back at itself by the lime until there are almost no shadows. The algae keeps to that one cooler side, and to the mortar lines, as if testing whether the brick will let it stay.

Above the waterline, in those same mortar cracks, there are pinched tufts of salt-grass and one fern. The fern is the most determined living thing in the place. It has no soil. Its roots have found a joint and are splitting it by a hair, living on steam, on condensation, on the mineral tang of the spring, and on the flat white-gold light that moves minutely with the hour in the three vents. It is a single plant, not a colony. Spores may drift down in the shafts of light and fail, again and again, until one finds a damp crack and holds. It does not spread. It holds.

From one of those vents a fig root has come down through the brickwork from the world above. It hangs in the air, pale and searching, not yet reaching the water. It is not yet a resident either — it is a probe, an explorer sent down from a tree that lives in sun and earth in the courtyard, feeling its way through lime and brick toward a warmth it cannot yet use. It lives between worlds, on moisture in the brick, on the blind urge to descend. If it touches, it may split the vent and follow the steam down. For now it only hangs there, lengthening.

On the water itself, in the slower places, life is even thinner, and even more temporary. In the slack corner by the steps a mat of duckweed-like green gathers, floats for a while, and is torn apart again by the current. By the jars, where the water moves less, an iridescent bacterial skin forms and breaks. These are not bodies you can point to. They are films, skins, populations that exist between sluice cycles — forming in the quiet, ruptured when the level rises a hand's breadth in a rush and slaps the bricks, reforming hours later on the same slower water. They are, in a sense, the only things actually living *in* the water rather than beside it, and what they live on is almost nothing: minerals diffusing from the spring, the faintest leaching from wet wood and clay, whatever invisible residue the workers' wool and hides left behind in the first weeks. The inflow brings in cloudy bubbles that burst along the floor cracks, and whatever microscopic travellers arrive with the spring do not establish in the deep channel. They are flushed, steamed, limed out, and gone with the overflow gurgle by the entrance tunnel.

Even the room itself is growing, in a way that mimics life without being it. Where water seeps through a ceiling joint, a little lime stalactite has built up in weeks, drip by drip, where a drip falls and sounds like a hammer. At the waterline there are bright tide-marks in green and ochre, the only erosion there has been time to do. The bricks are still too sharp to be old. The plaster is still pale. The mortar still holds fingerprint ridges. This place is weeks old, not centuries, and colonisation has barely begun — algae in a streak, grass in a crack, a root in the air, a skin on still water. It is too new, and by design too clean, for settled life to take hold. Hot, lime-rich, sun-blasted, and scoured on a timer: it is an environment trying, with lime and heat, to sterilise itself, and what lives here are the exceptions that prove the rule.

Because the biggest inhabitant is not organic at all. It is the cycle.

The heavy wooden plug sealed with clay in the drain channel. Pull it, and the whole chamber empties in minutes, leaving bricks steaming and dripping, and the spring fills it again by evening. The counterweight sluice lifted and dropped by rope, chattering every few minutes. The inflow pushing in at the deep corner. The overflow sucking out by the steps. Vents of steam where hot inflow meets the sun-heated surface. Light shafts swaying in the water with the current. This is the room's heartbeat and breathing, and it never sleeps, day or night, whether workers are there or not. Everything else — fern, algae, bacterial skin, fig root — lives inside that rhythm, above it or in its slack corners, surviving its resets. The cycle is what makes the noise, what makes the level rise and fall, what makes the place feel bigger and more alive than its twenty paces. If anything here could be said to reside permanently, it is this process, banging away in the dark under the courtyard.

You float for a moment in the deep channel, warm and buoyant to chest height, with the vault so bright above you it feels like sky, not brick, and the three small suns in the crown moving minutely with the hour. Steam thickens around you. The sluice bangs again. All around and below, the built floor recedes, perfectly clear, perfectly paved, unpeopled.

Whoever lives here, then, is not a who at all. It is a fern in a crack, a root feeling for water it has not reached, a green streak on the shady wall, a skin on water that breaks and reforms, a mat of weed that never roots — and behind them all, the handiwork of people who come by day and leave by night, and the mindless, tireless machinery of water coming in and water going out. Nothing has moved in. Things are only, barely, beginning to cling to the margins of a room that was built to be used, and then left to fill.
