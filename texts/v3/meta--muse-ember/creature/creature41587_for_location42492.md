If you look for life in Urrath by standing back and taking in the whole, you will miss most of it. The first residents do not stand. They crust.

On every boulder, on every windward face and leeward slab, on the flat roofs of the towns themselves, lichen has taken hold — orange, sulphur-yellow, black, a pale sage that spreads in slow continents. It is not quite plant, not quite animal, a partnership that can wait years for a wet morning and then drink condensation bead by bead. It eats stone dust and air. Nothing else here is in such a hurry to be unhurried. In a high, cold country where water is a shy, intermittent thing, lichen is the only creature that has made a permanent arrangement with scarcity.

Where a drip line has run long enough, moss follows it, green-black down the stone, the only true green you will see for days. And where a little soil has lodged — behind a boulder, in the lee of a retaining wall, in a crack where two rocks have settled against each other — cushion plants wedge themselves in tight as pincushions. Moss campion. Dwarf willow no higher than an ankle, its branches pressed flat. Bitter herbs with leaves thick against the wind. They do not grow up so much as they grow in. There are no trees here to give them shade and no grass to close over them. They survive by being too low, too tough, too close to the rock to be worth the weather's attention.

Under every sun-warmed stone, in the brief heat of midday, there is another population at work. Beetles, dark and quick, spiders stringing thin webs in the damp corners, stoneflies hovering over the places where water threads down. They are small, ground-dwelling, cold-tolerant, and they move only when the sun allows. Around each melt-pool in the short thaw rises a low cloud of midges thick enough to walk through, and for two or three weeks the air itself is alive with them. Then they are gone, the pools shrink to damp hollows, and the snow in the north gullies that never quite thawed resumes its long tenure.

The birds are the loudest residents of the empty country, and even they are mostly silent. In season the cliffs are black with nesting ravens, eagles perched like notices on the highest spires, swifts screaming down the gullies at dusk, snow-finches singing at dawn in thin, determined bursts, ptarmigan that turn white on snow and brown on stone as if practicing camouflage for a change of season that never fully arrives. Ravens in particular have learned the towns. They nest in eaves, follow the pack trains between towns, and steal fat from drying racks with a confidence that suggests a long, profitable acquaintance. They are not pets. They are weather with wings, and they watch people the way people watch weather — for what it will do next.

On the scree, marmots whistle from their burrows and vanish all at once across a whole slope, as if one mind had decided for all of them. Pikas and hares work the crags from dawn, ibex pick their way along fault-scarps with an ease that makes the stone look flat, foxes pad the lanes at night looking for a gap in a byre wall or a forgotten piece of smoked meat. Because no one hunts them hard, they are tame in the way deer are tame where they have never learned to fear. They do not flee a climber. They look, assess, and return to the business of eating lichen and seeds and each other in approximately that order.

When you reach a town, the abundance changes shape and crowds indoors. Pigeons in the eaves, rodents in the thick walls — gnawing at grain despite the granary raised on stone pillars against damp and teeth — dogs asleep in the lanes exactly where the traffic must step around them, flies thick around dung-cakes drying on the roofs. And penned shoulder to shoulder on flat roofs and in stone byres are the shaggy sheep-goats and the small hairy cattle, the true wealth of the place. They are compact, short-legged, thick-coated, stubborn, and entirely accustomed to being carried, counted, milked, sheared, and slept next to. In winter a kid goat may be brought inside the house itself, not from sentiment but because a warm animal in a cold room is a practical thing, and heat here is a shared resource whether it comes from a body, a dung-fire, or a beast.

The people of Urrath live in the same cracks as everything else, only they have built their cracks out of stone-on-stone and filled them with each other.

They are a mountain people by long residence, and it shows in the ordinary ways residence shows. Short to medium in stature, sturdy, with strong legs from climbing lanes that are always turning and always rising. Large lungs, weather-beaten faces, skin cracked at the lips and knuckles, eyes narrowed against a sun that is fierce at noon in thin air and throws shadows hard and black behind every stone. Hair dark and braided and mostly covered; hands rough from stone, wool, sinew, and cold water. Teeth worn by grit from stone-milled barley. They move slowly, not from laziness but because movement is expensive where food and fuel and water are all accounted for and the wind finds the neck and fingers even in summer. Nothing here moves fast except birds, weather over a ridge, and the occasional rockfall, and people have learned to match the slowest of those.

They dress for a climate, not a season, because at this height there is only the one climate with two moods. Layered wool and felt and hide, hooded cloaks, mittens, boots stuffed with dry moss or straw in winter, hats that tie under the chin. A shirt may dry slowly in a damp corner and be worn damp. A family may own one good cloak that belongs to whoever is going out into sleet that day. Clothes are mended, re-mended, cut down for children, cut down again for patches. Wool from the sheep-goats is spun on wooden looms, sinew thread sewn with copper awls cold-hammered thin, obsidian blades knapped from fresh volcanic veins. Nothing is decorative if it can be useful, and everything useful becomes, in time, a kind of decoration — the pattern in a weave, the color of lichen-dye, the way a cap is folded.

They live stacked. Three and four stories of split slate and granite, sharing walls, leaning on the rock face, flat roofs that are floors and drying yards and goat pens at once. Inside, everything is near-touching: sleeping platforms, looms, tools on pegs, jars, smoked meat, hides, people, the dog, the kid goat. Heat is shared, so bodies share rooms. A house may take a generation to add its top story. A wall may be older than anyone who remembers its builder. A child learns the lanes by touch, because the lanes are a man and a half wide, turn sharply to break the wind, and climb by external stairs.

The towns are not cities. They are repetitions. A day or three apart across the uplift, each chosen for the same bargain — a sheltered basin, a lee slope, a place where wind does not come straight — each with its plaza and the great cistern roofed with slabs beneath it, its granary, its communal oven, its smithy downslope where fire is safer, its terraces stepped up the slope in plots no bigger than a blanket. Families or clans keep their wards, rebuild their own retaining walls after a rockfall, mend their own roofs of corbelled stone or turf cut from moss pockets. The work never finishes, and no one expects it to.

Useful knowledge is held by whoever lives longest in place. Elders remember which drip-line failed in a dry year, which wall shed a slab last thaw, which bitter herb was cut when the melt-pools filled. In the long cold months, when towns draw inward and the temp falls through the floor of the day, there is time to mend tools, to marry, to tell stories by a low dung-fire while inner walls weep with condensation in the corners and jars catch the drip for washing. In the short thaw there is lambing and shearing, the weeks of insects and herbs and full cisterns, the slow caravans of pack animals moving between towns along paved stone paths marked with cairns and rest-shelters.

Their diet is the diet of the place: hard barley cakes from the communal oven, roots grown in imported earth behind terraces, broth and bitter herbs, milk and cheese from the sheep-goats, smoked meat, the occasional egg from a pigeon or a hen kept close. Nothing is wasted because there has never been enough to waste — not water, not wood, not a carcass, not a word spoken into wind that will take it. They burn dung and peat and scrub willow in that order, and every piece of scrub wood is counted like coin. They speak economically, in a low voice, with a great many words for kinds of cold, kinds of wind, kinds of rockfall, and for the state of a cistern. Humor exists, quarrels exist, boredom exists — it is a normal world — but it all happens at arm's length from physics, and hunger and shelter are never abstract.

That is perhaps the most consistent thing about all the life here, from lichen to human: it has packed itself into the cracks and learned to persist there. Lichen on stone, moss on a drip, beetles under a warm rock, birds on a cliff, beasts on scree, herds on roofs, and people in towns of stone-on-stone crammed into the lee of the wind, dense and self-contained and stubborn. At evening the sun goes behind the western blades, the tiny windows light one by one, cooking fires smoke through roof holes, a boot strikes a flagstone, a bleat is muffled by a wall, a hammer is struck twice and set down, and a marmot whistles far off on the slope that no one answers. Everything that lives in Urrath contracts a little closer into its shelter, and waits, patiently, for the long duration to continue.
