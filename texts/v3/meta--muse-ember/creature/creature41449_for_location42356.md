It starts under your boots.

You don't see it at first because the dark here doesn't let things be seen the way dark elsewhere does. It presses, wet felt on the head, and eyes take an hour to learn its grammar. What you hear is movement first. A tread in the mulch that is too regular to be a booted foot and too soft to be a machine — something burrowing just below the black surface, pushing it up in a slow ridge, then gone. Worms thicker than your wrist working the warm loam. Beetles in their thousands, armor wet and shining, moving in the layered mats of vine where no soil shows for weeks at a time. Things with no name yet that you hear more than see, snuffling in the root layer, in the steaming mire where the ground gives back heat through the sole.

Above that is the first green, and it is not green the way sunlit things are green. It is black-green, waxy, huge. Leaves built to drink whatever falls — not sun, there is none to count on, but the smear of light off a wet culvert, the spill from a work-light, the faint cold blue-green of fungi along a log, the pulse from a smelter a kilometer off. Ferns the size of umbrellas unfurl over warm pipes and over the cracked seams of cooling towers. Vines net the marsh in layers, near-touching, so that from the ground to twenty meters up is a second, third, fourth floor of leaf. Orchids hang from cables, roots in air, flowers opening when the cable sways. They have learned to treat the built world as treeline.

Some of them have gone further. Near the oldest smelters the plants no longer turn to any sky at all. They turn, slowly, hour by hour, to the heat and light of the machines, and they time themselves to it. A flower head tips toward the furnace mouth as if it were a sunflower, and when the pulse comes its petals light, amber first, then white, in sequence up the stalk, like signal lamps. Pollinators come for the heat as much as the nectar — midges in clouds that move like a single animal over the warm vents, larger biters whose wings beat loud enough to startle, moths the size of hands that drift through fog on silent, translucent wings and navigate by the blue work-lights the way other moths navigate by the moon. Nothing here wastes a made light.

The fungi do the steady work. In sulfur-yellow and corpse-pale sheets they cover every wet log, every rusted intake, every bank where runoff runs. They glow, not bright, a low blue-green steady burn that your eyes after an hour can walk by. They are the footing markers, the landing strips. Where they are thick the air smells sweet-rot and iron and fertilizer, and small things graze them at night — slug-like, blind, translucent — leaving bright trails across the black leaves.

Sound is the second geography, and it is crowded at all hours because there is no hour to start it. Frogs bellow from inside a cooling tower, where the concrete curves their call and throws it back at them, from inside the rim of a silo, from flooded tire ruts that ring like bells. They have taken the architecture as a body. Howlers trade notes in the upper canopy, somewhere far off in the mist, answers coming back a full minute later on an echo that hangs in the wet basins. Underneath it all the ground has its own heartbeat — deep, slow, industrial, a pump-house or generator row miles away thumping up through the basalt into your feet. Water ticks and sheets off hot metal, cableways whine overhead, metal cisterns plash.

And things move through it, large and fast and wet. A herd in the mire, splashing, grunting, you never see more than the eyes — two green dots apiece, bobbing, vanishing, bobbing again in a line that crosses your path without breaking. They are not afraid of the noise; they use it. The low hum means a pump-house ahead and drier footing and a place to drink that steams. The thickening of the frog chorus means flooded warm ground ahead, good for wading and bad for standing still. The smell of ozone means an electrified fence is live, and animals are migrating along it anyway, stupidly and cleverly, using the faint blue outline as a guide-wire through the dark, heads down against the bite.

Higher still, from twenty meters to the low cloud deck that never lifts, is the second traffic. Birds that call and listen to the call come back off leaf and metal and mist, echolocators that nest on antennae and silo tops where the perimeter bulbs flicker in sequence. Bats, many kinds, stringing between work-lights. Things that swim through fog on leathery wings and you only know by the displacement of air and the sudden drop in temperature where they passed. Spores and seeds and mist droplets crowd the air so you brush past another creature every few steps — a wing, a leg, a damp flank — and it brushes past you and is gone into the lung-thick mist.

In the white columns above the vents, in the steam that never stops rising, there is a life that lives only in steam. Translucent, blind, fat with condensation, they hang motionless in the heat, then drop, one by one, to drink from the wet metal below. They are born in mist and die in runoff, and the runoff carries a faint chemical tang that the fungi love, so the dead feed the glow that feeds the grazers that feed —

Nothing stays only one thing long.

The machines are part of the food web now, the way a fallen tree is. A factory is a warm rock. Animals nest in its flues. Plants specialize in its waste heat. Moss grows on its rust. The harvester crawlers go out on wide tracks through engineered grain that has gone partly wild, seed-heads glowing faintly along the edges, and behind each one comes a train of followers: rat-like grazers eating the spillage, quick and low, and behind them, never quite seen, a fast wet predator that has learned to hunt by the harvester's headlights, using the moving beam as a false dawn. Bursts of life follow the beam and scatter when it passes.

The long-legged walkers cross the mire on stilts, electrified at the joints, a blue arc kissing the water when they step. They are too tall and too steady to be prey and too loud to be ignored, so smaller things ride them — clinging under the belly for warmth, dropping off to drink, catching the vibration in their feet to navigate. The cableways sway overhead with loads, and when something falls with a repeated metallic ping, the scavengers below are already moving toward the sound before it lands.

Even the solitary buildings have their tenants. A smelter, a pump station, a row of silos, a small bright hut with its generator and its limp laundry line, days apart — each one a constellation from a ridge, orange sodium, cyan work-light, and each one half-sunk in its own way, vines through the window, ferns in the intake vents, nests high in the warm rafters where the fog doesn't reach. If people live here at all they pass through like weather, checking, leaving, the machines tending themselves in between. The residents are the ones who never left: the things that basked on the hot pipes until the pipes became a kind of sun, the things that opened and closed with the smelter's shift as if photosynthesis were a factory schedule, the things that learned that heat is rain here — engines warming the air, air rising to the cold stone at the rim, condensing, falling again half a kilometer away as warm bathwater rain to feed the marshes the pumps were built to drain.

So the plateau is not empty in its dark. It is dense in volume, layered from burrow to cloud deck, loud, wet, warm from below. Living glow is steady and low; industrial glow is harsh and pulsing; shadows fall the wrong way because there are too many sources for them to agree. And everything negotiates — predator and prey, machine and moss, root splitting a pipe, seed splitting a silo door, a river forming after a vent blows and running two days and vanishing into a crack that steams — births in the mist, deaths in the runoff, a slope creeping, a rust flaking, and over it all, leaves black and huge, drinking whatever light they can get, blooming amber to white toward the nearest fire they have learned to call sun.
