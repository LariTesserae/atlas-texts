If you are looking for what lives here, you will not find it by looking up. You are standing on it.

At your ankles the wet does not resolve into separate plants. There are no rosettes, no trunks, no crowns you can count. There is a continuous tissue, ankle to knee deep, made of flat glossy leaves laid over a thatch of roots and stems woven and re-woven until the weave itself is the ground. It is thick — a living peat, inches to feet thick depending on how long that patch has been allowed to stay flat. It blisters. It sighs vapor in thin jets. It breathes, slowly, in broad undulations, as if the whole plateau inhales through its skin and exhales through a thousand small mouths.

This is the low life, and it is most of life. It does almost nothing but stay low and stay wet. Its leaves are broad and flat because they have to shed the constant condensation they live in and catch what light there is without shading the mat below. Its roots are not for anchoring against wind — there is feet of plant below them everywhere — but for holding water in the weave and for fusing to neighbors. A mat is not a colony of individuals. It is one sprawling body that has agreed to be ground. Or many bodies that have fused so completely that the distinction has stopped mattering. When you step, you are stepping on a back.

Where the heat under the mat concentrates, the same tissue changes its posture. That is the whole secret of the inhabitants, and why they are hard to see at first. There is not a tower species and a dome species and a mat species. There is one kind of builder, hyperactive and plastic, that can be flat, or up, or sideways, or across, depending on what rises through it.

A tower is a mat that found a vent and thickened. Instead of spreading, it stacks. Stems that would have run horizontally turn up and fuse to their neighbors, leaf after leaf shrinking and cupping at the top to catch the air that is always condensing. The inside of the stack hollows itself. That hollow is not a room. It is a chimney, a lung. Steam from below travels up through the core, keeps the walls warm and wet, and escapes at the top in a hiss from hollow culms. The tower leans toward heat, not light. Light is plentiful and clean at this altitude; heat is the scarce thing, and heat is what it is farming. It grows at the tip in visible pulses — a hand's breadth while you watch, trembling — and then, having spent itself upward, it stops and thickens, pops its swollen stems, and lets its seeds go.

A dome is a mat that found heat and grew sideways, in a circle, and sealed. It traps what the tower vents. The interior is warm, dark, sweating, its walls slick and faintly fungal-green — the plant's own inner skin, made smooth to hold moisture. Again, no animal lives in that chamber. The chamber *is* the plant, a body cavity for holding heat and water against the thin high air. Seeds wash in on the steam or drift in through a seam, land in the warm seep at the base inside, and germinate at once in the dark they were born in. Some domes grow inside-out, rough thatch to the sky and smooth face to the chamber, or the reverse. It does not matter to them. The posture works. They keep it.

A bridge is a mat at an edge that reached. It grows across emptiness from one side, slowly, yearning for the other basin's heat, and often the other side grows toward it, and they never quite meet in the middle. Or they meet and decide, in the middle of the crossing, to grow down instead of across, and hang weeping roots into air that is already saturated, searching for a water that is everywhere. A wall is a mat that grew in a line, to hold a terrace against slumping, to hold water on one side and not the other. It weeps until it tears, and then vines throw themselves across the scar and knot it shut.

Between the built postures the life is sparse because each posture needs its own source. A tower cannot crowd another tower; they would steal each other's steam. So a basin holds one tower, one dome, one long unfinished bridge, a quarter-mile of empty flat between them. That emptiness is not empty. It is the mat doing the slower work, thickening, fermenting, ready to thicken up again if a new fissure opens.

Other postures appear at the margins. The solitary creeper you may see crossing the open, moving as if it has somewhere to be, is a runner of the mat that has lifted one end and is walking by growth — extending, anchoring, pulling its own flat body after it toward a distant vent it has sensed through the heat in the weave. The stranglers on the domes are not a different kind of life. They are the same builder climbing instead of rising. They embrace the host, kill its supports slowly, and their own stems become the scaffolding. The dead host does not fall. It is incorporated. Parasitism here is construction by other means. The next plant will climb them.

Reproduction is as un-animal as everything else. There is no pollinator. The plants shed pollen into the updrafts rising along the tower cores and in the vents, and the steam carries it sideways, up, down again in the little local rains that a vent makes for itself. Seeds are buoyant and tufted. They float in the sour puddles, they wash against the bases of towers where the water is warmest and most mineral, and they start at once. A fruit-case on the mat swells in the heat until it pops with a crack, loosing its cloud. The puddle itself is inhabited, in its way — not by animals, but by the fermenters that unmake what falls in within hours. In this heat dead plant does not rot patiently. It acidifies, dissolves, becomes mineral again, and the mat thickens over the place where it stood by the next day.

They die the way they live: by unbuilding. A stem splits loud as a branch breaking. A wall slumps. A dome collapses in slow motion with every fiber groaning. Nothing is allowed to age. The heat and water tear the substrate down almost as fast as growth puts it up, so every individual is young, always just emerged, always forced out. There are no ancients here, no old trees, no mellow ruins. A tower that stops venting will ferment from the inside, soften, and sink back into mat. A mat that finds new heat will rise again. Identity is temporary. Posture is temporary. The tissue persists.

At night the hissing lessens and the popping continues, and the built forms give off their day's heat in veils. The mat rises and falls under the puddles. The inhabitants are not sleeping. They are condensing, thickening, splitting, stitching their own tears shut with living knots.

To live here is not to live *in* the towers or *on* the mat. To live here is to be the tower, to be the mat, to be the bridge that never finishes, to be dissolved by your own puddle and rebuilt by your own seed in the warm seep at your base. There is nothing else awake on this high, wet, newly-made mountain to witness it. Just plants, everywhere, making architecture out of themselves, sparse and loud and forever young because they are forever coming apart.
