Nothing lives there, at first. That is the first impression that survives the second.

Your footprints fill behind you. The tools are left where a hand set them down, rung with moisture, going dull. No track crosses the wet floor except the ones the water is already erasing. If anything inhabits this made plain, it has learned, like the stair in the middle of nowhere, to do its work when you are not looking at it. You look away. You look back, and it has moved.

The first inhabitants are not solid. They are the tick of a cooling slab, like a fingernail against a tooth. They are the sigh when a steam crack opens and then closes. Drip... drip... with long pauses between, from somewhere you cannot locate. A soft enormous thud somewhere far away that you feel more in the soles of your feet than in your ears, a wall deciding to slump. Heated from below, saturated, everything here breathes through its cracks. The under-floor is inhabited. Between the lamination, under the broad slabs of pale slag and fired clay, under the courses that turn ninety degrees for no reason, there is work that never comes to the surface and a low population that tends it — pale, unformed things that live in mortar that never quite set, that turn warm water over and over in the soft material, that rise when a cluster goes through a fever. That is why the heat comes up through your boots, why the air shimmers at knee height. They are the kiln. When a sector is lifted and set down again, when new lines are laid under a storm that never quite reaches where you stand, you can see steam rise in slow columns from the revision. They have been disturbed. They settle again, and the slab resumes curing above them.

Above that world, a second ecology has taken to the surfaces themselves, and it is fungal in the old sense: second-hand, low, patient. Pale veils crawl over abandoned scaffolds and the insides of unfinished rooms, the plastered face of the two-hundred-metre wall, the underside of a courtyard far side that is finished and white. They do not photosynthesize. They eat. Fibres, glues, organic binders in mortar, the thin sizing that was meant to hold clay together — they consume the intention in the materials and help the clay fall back to dust. In the dim amber-grey light they move like a tide with no shore, advancing a hair's width when the interior is dark and unobserved, mapping exactly where work stopped. A grey-green crust on a half-built stair is not damage. It is a resident, and it lives indoors because indoors here is only a rumour of a wall.

On iron and copper sheeting, rust blooms like flowers, in perfect artificial rings. One ring per drying, per warming of a slab, per passage of a small storm that scours grit across the tiles until they ring. They open outward from a nail, a rivet, a hammer dent the size of a fist, and they close, in a sense, when wetness returns, spreading again a little farther. Salt blooms where a weeping wall has been dried by the heat from below but still weeps behind the surface: white cauliflower clusters that push out of a course of bricks too large to have been bricks, exhaling a faint brine into air already alkaline enough to taste on the tongue. These are stationary populations, sparse as everything else here, clustered at islands of built things with kilometres of empty floor between them. They wait for the floor to change its mind. When the slab is taken up, they are taken up with it, or they die cleanly of being unfinished.

And everywhere, riding the steam, fine as flour, are the spores. They rise in the slow columns that hang instead of dissipating, they gather sideways in a weather of their own, they settle on every cooler surface — the edge of a slab, the underside of an overhang, the inside of a tool left in the rain of damp air — and nothing eats them. Perhaps eating is the wrong category. They fall again when the condensation drips. They are less a species than a constant presence, a population that lives in the interval between rising and falling, between the grey slanting rain over a sector forty kilometres away and the still, dripping air where you stand.

The only movement that is not weather or water, the only thing that tracks across the floor under its own power, is the one you were told not to look for: the brick-draggers.

They are small. A boot-print that fills could cover one. A blind clay shell, fired and then unfired by damp, cracked down one side, dragging itself on hammered legs made from dull copper and tin lapped like scales, or on no legs at all, hauling with a ridge of shell and a slow oozing that leaves a warm groove behind it in the soft mortar. Each drags a single brick. The brick is always the same brick. Not literally — bricks break, slump, soften at the edges in a fever — but each carrier keeps to one, carries it from pile to wall and, when the wall has slumped a hand's breadth in the heat, from wall back toward the pile. They are drawn to unfinished edges: the end of a roadbed raised a hand's breadth above the floor, the neat pile where the idea of the road ran out; the foot of a wall outlined on the outside and plastered only within; the chalk lines and the pegs marking out a floor no one will occupy for a long time. They can feel heat through the soles of their shells. Fresh work attracts them days later, when the mortar is still soft enough for a boot to print.

If you watch one, it stops. That is not shyness. It is method. In this place things are built when not quite observed, and they have adapted by making observation itself a kind of weather they wait out. You blink, or you turn to follow a drip, and one hammered leg has come down, rivet by rivet, with a sound too small to locate. If they stop in the middle of a step — as they do, often, for hours, for days — they are not resting. The pause is the habitat. A stopped dragger dries unevenly, warms with the slab beneath it, and in time it is indistinguishable from floor. When that section is lifted and revised, something pale and unformed comes up with the mortar and re-forms, elsewhere, around a brick.

The legless ones are older, or worn. Their shells are smoothed by dragging. They move even slower, and the water they secrete is warmer than the puddles around them, alkaline, tasting of metal. They do not leave tracks. They leave a damp course that fills slowly behind them.

There are also inhabitants that exist only on a step. Each time the stair in the middle of nowhere grows — twenty steps to air, the top step always damp — a film appears on that new top surface. It lives in the dampness, feeds on the binder while it is still soft, and dies, or descends, when the next step appears above it and it becomes merely the second-from-top. Its whole life is the interval between growths. To see it you would have to look away for a generation. Similarly, the extra two steps in a corridor that was twelve on the way out and is fourteen on the return, wet and smelling of fresh clay, are not empty. Something lives in the measurement drift, in the part of the room that has more interior than its exterior could allow. It lives in the inserted length. It has no fixed address because the address has been revised.

At the largest scale, the inhabitants are the weeping blocks: misfired bricks the size of houses, standing like boulders but rectangular, still sharp-edged because nothing has worn them, weeping warm water down their faces in dark vertical streaks. They breathe very slowly. A sigh, a slump halfway through, a settling by degrees, is a respiration. Fungi live on their sheltered sides, salt gathers where their heat has dried the streaks, draggers gather at their feet as if at a wall that might one day be finished. They may be the final form of a dragger that stopped and swelled and never finished, or they may simply be what this place makes when it tries to make a boulder with hands. Either way, they are animals in the sense that they take up space, give off heat, and refuse to hold their shape. A platform that was a platform becomes a shallow trough full of warm water, and the water becomes a platform again with the trough's outline visible like a memory. That cycle is also a life cycle here.

That leaves the builders, if there ever were any in number. You hear irregular hammering very far away, a heartbeat of work that does not keep time, and then nothing for a while. No builder answers it. Perhaps the builders are gone, and what remains is their successor ecology: things that live by eating unfinishedness, by dragging it a little closer, by turning it under the floor until it is warm, by letting it fall back to dust. Perhaps the builders are still here, but not as separate people. A dragger with its brick *is* building. A veil eating glue *is* unbuilding. The under-floor pale things turning water in soft mortar *are* the kiln firing. Creation and destruction are not opposites here, and neither are living and making. They are phases of the same damp labour.

And there are inhabitants you will only meet earlier or later. A puddle reflects a tower intact and white while the tower twenty paces away is half-built and open to the sky. The reflection is not wrong. It is a resident of the puddle, from yesterday or from a decision not yet taken, and it lives there with the steam. A doorway opens on a courtyard that had a fountain basin yesterday and has only a damp patch today. Something lived in that basin, in the implied fountain, and it has not quite stopped living in the patch. These are the briefest and the longest-lived of all: they inhabit the world's habit of forgetting, and of remembering in the wrong order.

So who lives there is not a list. It is sparse, as the built things are sparse, islands with long pauses between sentences. It lives in wet mortar and under slabs, on the inside face of walls, on a single damp step, in a groove of warm water a legless shell has just left, in rust rings and salt clusters and spore weather. It is blind, or it has no face at all. It moves when unobserved, stops mid-step, grows by a step while you are elsewhere, slumps halfway and sighs. Nothing is natural, nothing is finished, nothing stays — and the inhabitants are exactly that: not a population occupying the place, but the place's attempts to occupy itself, crude and huge and damp, already beginning to forget themselves by the time they are done, and beginning again somewhere else under the vapour, in the heat rising from below.
