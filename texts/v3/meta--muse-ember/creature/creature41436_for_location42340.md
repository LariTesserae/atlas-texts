By number, Selan is crowded past counting. By eye, it looks empty.

The first inhabitants are the ones you will never see directly. They live in the ceramic weave of every tower, in the black glass seams between the ground-plates, in the fine dust that never settles and takes sound apart. They are too small to be insects, too purposeful to be mere chemistry — you become aware of them only when they are working. Scratch a solid wall and a faint silver film spreads over the scratch and closes it while you watch, without heat, without sound. That is not healing. It is editing. Matter is rewritten in place by presences moving at a scale below sight, along the lines that look like woven light.

They are the reason nothing here erodes. They are the reason the stone stays razor-sharp, the fractures stay fresh, the soil stays black and unfinished. A person, a plant, a beetle, might die in Selan, but the Repair does not allow death to accumulate. It collects what is spent, rewrites it, precipitates it again as a tower fringe or a leaf or a pocket of vapour. If you stand still long enough you feel them less than hear them: a low hum in your teeth, the dry tick of the ground settling a few millimetres, the soft clicking at night like fingernails. That clicking is partly them, partly the plants they help to grow.

Every other inhabitant lives, in one way or another, inside that cycle of condensation.

The plants are the visible majority. Vines the size of hands climb the bases of the towers, roots disappearing into seams in the ceramic, leaves sweating visibly in the morning and sending that sweat *upward* into capillary veins in the walls. Trees grow out of plazas, out of roofs, out of the sides of walls, their canopies not branching so much as unfolding — a lattice of filaments and leaf-plates. Look away and look back and they are wider. At night they grow a hand's breadth. In the dry hours they pull themselves back into tighter spirals, conserving, flexing, as if storing the dawn inside themselves.

They are lungs. At night every vertical surface sweats and the plants drink at a rate that seems impossible under a sun that never gets brighter than late evening elsewhere. At midday, when the beads shrink, they open wider and give the water back as vapour. The whole cluster smells then of warm wet stone and crushed leaf and something sweet, like a greenhouse left shut.

Their flowers never fully close. They keep a low, lamp-like glow because here there is no proper night for them to close against. Pollinators might come at any hour, and so they stay open at every hour.

The only pollinator that matters lives entirely inside that decision.

It is a small, iridescent beetle. One kind only — where there is no cluster there is not even that. It visits the lamp-flowers, slipping into the glow that never goes out, drinking leaf-sweat, carrying something from flower to flower across a tower roof or a plaza tree that is far heavier than it looks in the air. Within a cluster it is constant, unhurried, iridescent against green. Between clusters it does not go. An hour of bare, sharp plates is as closed to it as a sea. Its world is the sudden greenness, the breathing walls, the towers exhaling around it. The flowers stay open for it. It, in turn, keeps them from ever needing to close.

The animals proper are rare enough that to see one is an event, and they seem to know it.

At a pocket — one of those perfect ovals set into the stone, no larger than a room, unnaturally still, reflecting the grey sky so exactly it looks like a hole cut out of the world — you may find a drinker at dawn.

It is tall, pale, almost translucent, with a neck too long for its body and legs that make no sound on the plates. It approaches in the damp before the mist-buildings thin, lowers that long neck not quite to the water but to the vapour just above it, and drinks the mineral-cold, faintly green air of the pocket. It does not ripple the surface. Birds rarely cross the pockets for the same reason; to disturb the reflection would be to disturb something more solid than the water.

It will lift its head and look at you with an eye like a polished bead. The eye reflects the mist-towers more clearly than you can see them with your own. It does not startle. It steps backward — always backward, as if turning would be an unnecessary expenditure of a life built for reaching — into the green of the cluster, and is gone. Not fleeing. Dissolving into the lattice. Its translucence lets the filaments of the trees show through it. When the air dries it can thin with the mist-buildings. When condensation returns it can condense again at the same pocket. Whether it sleeps in the cool hollows below ground, or in the towers' dissolving fringes, is something the basin does not give up. What is certain is its solitude. One drinker to a pocket, in effect, each keeping its small, still mirror.

Above them, once a day, crosses the only traveller.

Birds, if they are birds, are slow motes high up, wings beating with a deliberateness that makes them look like they are thinking about it. They cross the whole basin in a straight, unhurried line from cluster to cluster, never descending to perch on a tower that might feather and dissolve under them, never dropping to drink. The plain below takes a person hours to walk; they take their time crossing its length, pausing mid-beat, hanging in the thick, dusty air that dims the sun and muffles sound.

They are the only inhabitants who know there are eleven, twelve, more islands of city out there. The beetles do not know. The drinkers do not know. Even the plants, breathing as one lung per cluster, do not know. The birds carry whatever is carried between clusters — a filament, a spore, a trace of the Repair's instructions — and they carry it at their own, contemplative pace.

The human presence is rarer than that.

In a day you may see one gardener, far off, tending a vine wall. He — or she, the slowness makes the distinction hard to hold at distance — moves with the slowness of everything else. Nothing rushes in Selan and nothing rests, and the gardener does not build. There is no need to build; the buildings precipitate themselves out of the air above the ground, solid at the core, feathering at the edges into particles that catch light and let it go. The gardener prunes. He guides roots into the seams where the Repair wants them. He listens for the ticking of dawn drops from a roof, a small separate weather, and for the hum that says a mist-building is changing phase. He wears a weave like the towers, warm to the touch, and his own skin beads in the morning like a tower's. He sleeps, if he sleeps, leaning against that warmth, letting the night condensation lie on him.

He is one per cluster, in effect. Not a population. A function. He will tend a wall for a morning and when the mist thickens, which it does for long stretches, he is no longer there — not because he left, but because he phased with the mist-building he was leaning against. His solid hours and his mist hours are the same life.

And that is, perhaps, the true description of the mist-city's inhabitants, whether they are gardeners or drinkers or something that belongs only to damp air.

When the air is damp you can see faint structures standing between the solid ones, and sometimes alone on the plain: arches, spires, folded slabs, the same geometry as the towers, but passable in a way towers are not. Lean against one while it is wet and it holds you. Scratch it and the Repair closes it. As the afternoon dries, it thins, loses definition, becomes only a suggestion of cool air, and is gone, leaving no shadow. In the morning it solidifies from the fog inward, as if the city inhales.

Something lives in that inhalation. In the pockets, the reflection sometimes shows the mist-buildings standing solid while in the air they are still only fog. At dusk, in amber-grey light, the whole basin can seem to have a second horizon underneath the first, made of buildings that will only exist tomorrow. The inhabitants of that tomorrow are already condensing, edge first. A week later the mist-buildings appear in a slightly different place. The city is walking in its sleep, and its walkers shift it by degrees — unseen, unheard except for the faint rearrangement of pressure in your teeth.

Even the towers are inhabitants in this sense. They do not sit on the ground so much as precipitate above it. They re-weave their fringes in the dry hours. They exhale mist in the late afternoon, not from pools but from their own skins and from the plants, a slow exhalation that falls not as drops but as a fine sighing condensation on your shoulders and on upturned leaves. They repair themselves. They extend themselves three storeys down into hollow chambers below ground that have no doors and no weathering — chambers no one enters by a door, because inhabitants there, root-minds or Repair-weavers or drinkers at rest on cold plates, arrive by condensing in place and leave by evaporating out of it. The black glass seams between the plates, the young stone still lifting, the tick you hear far off like a knuckle — that is below-city life, turning the basin over in its sleep and being instantly rewritten young again.

So their lives are not separate from the place's life. A plant drinks the night condensation the tower sweated. The tower takes the plant's upward sweat into its ceramic veins. The beetle lives in the flower's perpetual glow. The drinker drinks the pocket that mirrors the towers that the Repair keeps new. The bird crosses above them all, thinking about each wingbeat. The gardener phases in and out of the mist the plants exhale. The mist-being stands solid in a reflection before it stands solid in air.

Nothing rushes. Nothing rests. Sound is taken apart by the dust. The light pales, thickens, pales again, never setting properly. And in that perpetual, warm, dim evening, the inhabitants go on condensing themselves into being, one breath at a time — growing, spiralling, visiting, drinking, crossing, tending, rewriting — and then, when the air dries, giving themselves back to be made again at dawn.
