It is not at first apparent that anything lives here, because the first life is not shaped like a life.

It spreads as a feeling underfoot. On the wettest black stones, where water beads even though there is no rain, there is a film — grey, translucent, slick to a fingertip — that is not moss and not fungus and not quite a plant. It grows on inorganic wetness the way damp grows on a wall, taking minerals from stone and warmth from below and a faint acrid salt from the seep, and making of them something edible. Your boots leave a dark print in it and the print fills again in seconds, thicker where something has recently passed. Everything else in Istrenkor is eating that film, or eating something that ate it, and the film is the only ground cover there is. No green ever crosses it. It thickens in wakes and thins where the rock is too new, and the inhabitants read it the way you would read a track, because it remembers who has been there and what they were hungry for.

The grazers are the first you must learn to step around.

They come in tides. Low, slate-grey, amphibian-smooth, the length of a forearm and shaped like a toad that has been pulled long, they move shoulder to shoulder across a plaza in a body so continuous it takes an hour to pass and does not seem to have an end. Their eyes are gone — smoothed over, wasted, mist would have blinded them anyway — and they navigate by the heat coming up through the basalt into their bellies, by vibration through their skin, by the electrical shiver the city makes when a wall decides to firm. On their necks are feathery gills, pale and damp, that filter the condensing mist as they breathe. Droplets bead and run off them constantly. They are always wet. They leave a faint mineral sheen where they shuffle, and behind them the grey film grows thicker, fertilized by their passing.

They eat that film, and the mist, and the dead of their own kind. Nothing is wasted in a tide. When one dies in the middle of the flow — and they do, quietly, in the press of bodies — the others do not stop. They nudge it, they eat it where it lies, they take its minerals into themselves and carry them a few meters further before excreting them back into the film. That is their whole economy. A young one is thumb-long and darker, born in the warm basins where pools are deepening, and it knows at once how to shuffle, how to lift its gilled neck to drink the air. An old one is paler, its skin almost translucent at the edges, beaded with its own slow light. For every one of them glows, a little. Not bright. Blue-white, dim, surfacing in the beading on their backs when they are feeding, steadying when they are resting. When a tide crosses a street the street lights up in a moving pattern, thousands of small constellations crawling in the same direction, like stars deciding to walk.

You become part of their route only if you wait. If you try to cut through, the tide parts around your legs with a slow, deliberate patience, re-forms on the other side, and forgets you. Their paths are not roads. They are chemical memory worn into the film over decades, and a tide will follow a path it has followed for a hundred years because the film tells it the way. They do not hurry because food is everywhere underfoot and so is everything that eats them, and there is nowhere to hurry to.

Above them, so dense at times the air itself seems to move, are the drifters.

Large midges in a cold climate, but their wings beat slow enough that you can watch each stroke, and their bodies are translucent and faintly lit from within. They hang in thermal columns over the heat vents where the city exhales, by the thousands, rising and settling and rising again on air that is warmer than the mist around it. They are blind too. They navigate by warmth and by the blue-white excess light they themselves make, which shimmers up the whole column when they rise together, as if the vent were burning with cold fire. You breathe them in by accident. They settle on your face in a fine, cold snow and take off again, leaving your eyelashes damp. They eat the grazers' cast sheen and the film dust that rises on warm air, and the grazers' dead, when they fall, feed them in return on the ground.

Their young are not in the air. Down in the black pools and slow channels, something paler and longer swims just under the surface, too long for a grazer, ribbon-smooth, stirring the black water so that the lights below seem to move when nothing above them does. It has no eyes either, and its mouth is a soft frill that filters minerals and microbial felt and the drift of dead drifters. It lives for a long time just under the reflection, growing slow in the warm, salted water that will not freeze, and one day it climbs — slowly, blindly — up a wet tower side, where the clingers have worn a path, and unfolds.

The clingers work the walls.

Hairy, dark, many-legged, they move upward in long processions a meter wide and a hundred meters long, single file, unhurried. Their hair is not for warmth. Each hair traps a droplet of condensing water, and they filter it, harvesting microbes from the matte ceramic of the streets and the precipitated lattice above, eating what the city exudes as it breathes. They prefer the vertical because water runs down and they run up into it, meeting it coming. Where they have passed, the ceramic is cleaner, darker, and the tower's membrane does not clog. They chitter as they climb — a close, dry sound, thousands of small throats working at once, rising at times to a deafening chorus that you feel as pressure behind your ears, and then falling away to the bells of water dripping inside hollow stone. Their own light is the faintest of all, a fringe of blue-white along their hair, so a procession at night looks like a slow river of sparks going up a wall that should not be climbable.

They are not the only ones in the walls. In the joints of the towers, where amber warmth leaks out, there are colonies of something smaller still — blind, soft, squid-like custodians that live inside phase-change membranes, cleaning them, eating the mineral crusts that would seal a vent shut, laying their eggs in the warm seam. The amber is their glow, concentrated. They never leave the joint. The city would not breathe properly without them, and they, in turn, are made in part of the city's offcuts; their bodies incorporate ceramic dust and precipitated lattice fiber the way the grazers incorporate mineral film. In the deep lattice under the city, where heat engines turn over in aeons and the drone comes up through your teeth, there are larger, slower shapes that fit the tunnels like plugs, feeling the uplift flex stone by stone, letting water through their bodies. You never see them whole. You hear them as a change in the hum.

And in the cuttings and vaulted tunnels lit from underneath, there are hangers — pale, sponge-soft animals fixed to ceilings, mouths open to the drip, swaying a fraction with each vibration of the plateau. They do not move for years at a time except to swallow. The lights below them are not lamps. They are reflections, and they themselves, and the swimmers below rising up almost to touch them.

It is a closed loop with no outside. The film feeds the grazers; the grazers feed the drifters and the clingers; the dead fall into the water; the water carries their minerals to the growing lattice, where they are built again into tower and bridge; the tower and bridge exude heat and moisture, growing more film. The inhabitants live in the city's pores and clean its membranes and carry its spores in their gut. The city, for its part, was precipitated to work with them, or has learned over slow centuries to be indistinguishable from them — a wall that was soft yesterday is firm today because a colony has eaten the excess, a doorway warms before you reach it because a procession of clingers has cleared its seam.

Nothing here lives alone, and nothing lives quickly. A pool fills by a centimeter a year, and a swimmer will grow in it for a decade before it climbs. A ridge rises while you stand on it, and a tower seals a wound in its own skin over the course of a winter you will never see end. The animals do not hurry because their food is everywhere and their predators are everywhere and the place itself is still being born underneath them.

By the second day the strangeness is ordinary, which is the most unsettling part. The buildings breathe condensation and do not get wet. The black pools have lights in them that are not reflections. The streets re-pave themselves under grey backs. The rock is still being born and the city on top of it is still being born and the multitudes that cover both are living their entire overwhelming lives in a present tense so long it looks like stillness — blind, shining, wet to the bone, eating stone-wetness and each other's leavings, moving in tides and columns and processions, never finished, never dry, never the only thing of its kind, and never, it seems, intending to hurry for you.
