Nothing lives here in a crowd.

The first life you can be certain of is not an animal, and it does not stand anywhere. It lives in a drip-line.

Down the inside faces of the rock, water has worn a groove no wider than a finger, curved and darkened by centuries of use. In that groove is a film: rust-orange where the light reaches it, green-black where it does not, slick as oil, smelling faintly of iron and wet earth. It is algae, or something with algae’s habits, and it lives only where water is obliged to travel in a line. On the broad warm stone the film has no purchase; in the line, minerals are delivered, spores are carried down, and every new drip renews the road. Its world is not an area but a route. Where the groove ends at a lip and lets its water fall, the film thins, perhaps becomes the faint skin on a pool below, perhaps dies. No one can say where one film ends and another begins, and it has probably been travelling down its particular groove longer than any animal in the hollow has been alive.

Next, in scale, come the discs.

On the sun-warmed stone — for the sun does find this place, white and ordinary through the mist — lichen has made perfect circles. A disc may be as wide as your palm. One disc is one life, complete in itself, needing no soil, no root, no pool. It sits on bare rock and takes what the condensed air and the thin mineral wash give it. The circle is so exact that it seems drawn by the regularity of the water: drip, pause, drip, the same small accident repeated until the edge becomes a law. Its growth is so slow that the word “growth” is almost a courtesy. The centre is the oldest part; the edge is still arriving. A disc does not spread into a carpet. It remains a disc, alone on its warm stone, with bare rock and perhaps another disc ten paces away.

Where grit gathers, and still more where wood has fallen, moss takes over by accretion. It pads the stone in hollows, it cushions the thin black soil, it heaps an inch thick over a fallen trunk until the trunk is only a rippling moss-ridge running across the hollow. The soil beneath is black, spongy, saturated a few centimetres down, springy underfoot because it is not soil alone: it is a shallow mat of moss stems, root tips, pale fungal threads, and held water, a hidden reservoir that sighs upward. The tree that fell there before your grandparents’ time is still present, though not as wood. It is present as a line in the moss, a raised route through the hollow along which water now creeps more slowly and in which a beetle may spend its life. Death here does not remove a plant from the place. It demotes it.

The plants that rise above the moss do so one at a time. There is no forest, no stand, no grove. There is a tree-fern, a broadleaf clump, a vine on a leaning slab, a rosette in a shallow cup, and a few small trees whose crowns lean inward over the empty stone toward the centre of the hollow. Ten or twenty paces of warm rock and moss separate them. Each is a settlement of one.

The tree-fern’s trunk is furred and ringed, standing in damp grit where a groove ends and gives up its water. It unfurls one frond at a time, each frond as long as an arm, and the frond now coming is still a tight shepherd’s crook that will not open for another slow season. Around it stand the dead stems of old fronds, dry and upright, combed by the wind into notes. If it lives for centuries — and there is every reason to think it may — its life is measured in fronds, not in years. It leans with the rest, its crown tipped toward the middle of the hollow where the mist thins first.

The broadleaf clump grows in a shallow warm hollow. Each leaf is a hand across, thick, and in the right light you can see through the veins where the water is moving inside, a slow upward circulation against the stem. The leaves drip in private rhythms, each to its own patch of stone below. The small trees beyond it are sparser still, their canopies open and inward-leaning, as if they had decided long ago that the centre of the hollow was the direction from which anything important would come. The vine has climbed no tree at all; it has taken a leaning slab of rock and pinned itself there with aerial roots like guy-ropes, the roots creeping along the drip-lines, following the film’s roads. The rosette, low to the ground, holds in its centre a perfect still pool that may have been refilling, in the same place, for years.

These plants cannot hurry, and they do not need to. A fern makes spores for the mist; a seed washed from a leaning crown travels only as far as creeping water will carry it, to a basin or a patch of soil a few paces away. Most fail. The next plant of a kind, when one appears, is far off and alone. The hollow is lush because every plant is well watered. It is empty because no plant has yet had time to become a crowd.

The animals, when they come, are bound to even smaller worlds.

In one of the warm stone basins — no bigger than a bowl, unconnected to any other pool, its footprint already full of sky — there is a pale amphibian. It has no eyes. It does not so much swim as reposition itself, perhaps once an hour, shifting a body’s length toward the warmer side of the bowl or away from the drip that is slowly filling it. Much of its time it spends wedged in the dark wet seam beneath the basin’s lip, where the stone stays saturated and its skin, which is its lung and its mouth for water, never dries. It is pale because there is little light where it lives, and translucent enough that the shadow of its own organs shows through. It feeds, if the word is not too energetic, by pressing slowly into the surface film of the pool and taking in what the water has gathered: algae, fallen spores, the mineral dust of the stone. One bowl is its horizon. It may have been born there. It may die there. When the pool sinks away, as all these pools do, it can retreat into the spongy soil under the rock and wait in the damp until a drip rises again. How it makes another of itself, in a place where pools do not flow into one another, is one of the hollow’s withheld facts.

The beetle is the next animal you might actually see, and seeing it is mostly a matter of watching it decide nothing. It is shiny black, on a moss hummock, its shell polished like wet stone. It moves with great deliberation: a step, a long pause while a drip lands somewhere on the rock, another step. To cross its own hummock may take it a season; to cross the moss-ridge of the fallen trunk may take it years. It grazes, slowly, at the boundary between moss and lichen or on the spore-caps the moss lifts in the wet air. It does not chase. Nothing here is in a hurry to eat it, and its food grows where it stands all year round. The hummock is its territory and probably its whole biography: eggs laid in the black soil beneath the moss, a replacement adult in time, the same small green world renewed without ever being crowded.

Then there are the inhabitants known only in passing, and they are the most unsettling, because they are fresher than everything else.

A trail is compressed in the damp soil, days old and still holding its shape because the soil never freezes and never quite dries. Beside a basin is a footprint you cannot match to anything you have seen — broad, unpadded, turned a little inward. On the underside of a fern frond, something like a hair is caught, long and coarse, snagged as some animal brushed past. These signs do not belong to the amphibian, who never leaves its bowl, or to the beetle, who crosses its hummock in a lifetime. They belong to a life whose home is elsewhere: in the warmer cloud below the rim, perhaps, or in the high wet air that passes through. It puts a foot inside the hollow, or brushes a frond with its flank, and is gone before the bursting film of its own trace has settled. You will leave knowing it only by these small disturbances, which are nonetheless the newest things in a place where everything else is ancient.

And once, very rarely, there is a voice. It begins beyond the rim, in the air or the cloud below, and comes into the hollow and is enormously amplified by the concave rock, as if the caller were directly overhead. Then it stops, and does not resume for a very long time. The trees lean toward the centre; the pools hold the sky; the hollow itself is shaped like an ear. The call was probably not meant for the residents — it may belong to a creature that lives in the warmer layer under the rim and calls upward, or to one crossing the sky above — but for its brief duration the whole hollow attends to it. When it ceases, the place answers only in its own way: a drip finds a hollow stone and booms, a frond shivers, and the silence gathers again. The booms are not animal sounds. The hollow stones are not occupied. They are empty chambers that water strikes like a drum.

That is the population, if the word can be used for something so spaced out in space and time: a film running down a groove, a lichen disc on warm stone, a moss forest over a dead tree, a fern unfurling one frond a season, a leaf with water moving visibly in its veins, roots pinned to a slab, a rosette holding its pool, an eyeless amphibian in a bowl, a beetle on a hummock, a hair on a frond, a call from beyond the rim. Each occupies a different extent — a line, a disc, a patch, a plant, a pool, a hummock, the whole hollow, the air above it — and each persists by the same means: by being wet, by being slow, by being alone, by never needing to build or to hunt or to hurry. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is in the middle of being made. The lives have decided what they are, and the hollow has decided what it is, and they are maintaining the agreement drip by drip.

Stay long enough and you may see one repositioning. You may see one step of the beetle. You may hear one boom roll across the rim and watch a frond shiver in its wake. More likely you will see none of it. The film will go on travelling down its groove, the disc will go on widening by an amount too small to measure, the amphibian will shift once more toward the warmth, the moss-ridge will go on digesting its tree. Your own footprints beside a basin will soften, fill, and sink back into the sponge of the ground. The residents will not notice. They were here before you arrived, leaning inward, listening to the water strike the stone.
