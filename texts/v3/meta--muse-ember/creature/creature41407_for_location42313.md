Everything that lives here was, at first, difficult to see as living at all.

The plain makes a sound before it makes a shape. At dawn, in the cold pressure-drop, the moss contracts and the whole plateau sighs — a crackle like ice breaking under a great weight, felt in the teeth and in the soles of the feet. That is one inhabitant, or one kind of inhabitant, and it is not a single creature. It is the skin.

The anom moss is not ground cover. It is ground. Blue-grey, dusty-green, matte except where its silica scales catch the green-grey light, it covers boulder and tower and ruin with the same felt. Where it is thin you can see the old stone through it. Where it is thick it is inches deep and springy, and if you kneel you can feel it pulse — slowly, without malice, on a rhythm much longer than a day. It opens millions of microscopic mouths at dusk to take in gas, and at dawn it un-roots minerals from the rock beneath it grain by grain and turns stone into itself. It drinks no water. It pumps a clear silica fluid instead, which hardens at the surface. Its waste is glass. Its body is erosion.

Because of it, nothing sharp lasts. A boulder is a worn tooth. A dead tower from the first builders has been softened to a shoulder. Sharpness is a young thing here, and young things are not allowed to stay sharp.

The Stanions stand up out of that skin a kilometer apart, a hundred paces tall, hollow, thick as a house, banded with rings you could lie inside. You think at first they are trees, and then you think they are buildings, and then you realize those are not quite the right categories. Their trunks are petrified in places and living in others. Their crowns are vast translucent fans that open and close with the light, spilling a green wash outward at midday and shutting, one by one at night, with a sound like a book being shut very slowly.

They are awake in the way the moss is awake, and they are not alone in their own bodies. A Stannion is a city, a weather system, a memory, and a species all at once. In the heart of each one, in a chamber of dark metal and fired clay now infiltrated by mycelium, there is an entangled node. It does not transmit a message. It *is* the message, in another place at the same time. A Stannion in one ward will shed its fans an hour before the wind arrives in another, not because it was warned, but because the shedding and the knowing are the same event in two locations. The people who live in its root-fields do not find this remarkable. They check the node the way you would check a pulse.

Between the Stanions, in the kilometer-wide gaps, the other inhabitants move — or rather, the other *forms* of the same inhabitant move. Leaf-flocks tack over the plain at dawn, broad jointed kites of living leaf-tissue, screaming with metallic chatter, hunting spore. A flock is not a bird. It is a thicket that decided, for a season, to fly. When it tires it lands, roots, and becomes a reed-bed of hollow stalks again, playing the wind as organ pipes when the spore-snow falls.

Root-eels surf blind in the aerated soil under the moss, making a low drumming you feel through your boots. Browsing pods — six sprung legs, bellows-lungs — crop the moss in slow silent herds. Mimics follow them, trunk-shaped, standing so still for so long that moss grows over their feet, then folding and snapping with a patience that can take a year to complete a predation. None of these are separate from the plants. If you watch long enough you see a hunter is a Stannion polyp that began, inch by decade, to walk toward its prey. A herd is a root-field that learned to graze its own edge. The ecology does not move quickly because it is moving on plant time. Reproduction is a sporefall. Death is hardening.

If you weigh a hectare, ninety-nine parts in a hundred are plant. The rest is noise. The rest is the people in the wards, and even they are noise only if you measure by weight.

Every few kilometers, where a Stannion is especially large or two root-fields meet, a ward has grown. You do not find it by looking for walls. You find it by the change in the hum. The rootways run just under the moss surface in a lazy regional loop, warm living tubes just wide enough for two people, and transit pods — soft pneumatically driven sacs — sigh through them. The wards are urban in scale: markets, workshops, archives, dwellings. But they are not built upon the plain. They are cut from living domes and coaxed over years into shape. Walls of woven fiber fused with ceramic ribs. Roofs of resin bubbles that thicken and thin with the light. Doors that are valves of petal tissue, opening by pressure and closing by patience.

The dwellers there are human-sized because the rootways are human-sized, but they do not move like visitors. They move like gardeners inside a very large, very slow body. Their bones are denser in the towers, where gravity is fractionally stronger — fractionally, but enough to keep a hundred-meter hollow trunk from swaying in the high wind and enough that a child born in a Stannion learns to lean into steps. Near the rootways time runs fractionally slower, so a wall grown a century ago does not age, and neither, quite, do the hands that grew it. They are weathered, not wrinkled. Their edges, like everything else here, have been smoothed by exposure.

They do not drink water. There is none to drink. They sip the clear silica fluid the moss pumps, thinned and warmed in resin condensers under the domes, bitter with cold iron and ozone. They eat what the moss exudes at dawn when it contracts — mineral sugars, mineral memory, stored light translated into mass. They sleep in the domes where the roofs have thickened for night, listening to the amber pulse coming up through root-fibers. That pulse is their lamp-light. The sun, filtered through dust and the Stanion fans, is never bright enough to be trusted after dusk, so the ward carries its own light underground, up from the roots, in a faint, steady glow that makes the moss bed at night look veined, as if the whole plateau were the inside of a leaf.

The caretakers are the ones you notice last, because they are trying not to stand out from the substrate. They tend the ward the way a gardener tunes a trellis, except what they tune are constants. A caretaker will put a hand on the Stannion heart-node and listen, and gravity in that tower will strengthen by a fraction for a season, or time near the rootway will slow by a breath, or a petal roof will be permitted to hang open to the sky without tearing. The machines that help them are not quite machines. They are woven fiber and fired clay and dark metal, held together by entangled mycelium that remembers the shape it is supposed to be. They do not move fast. They grow repairs. They grow arms. They grow opinions, in the slow sense that a root grows toward a crack.

In the markets they trade in things that do not rot: resin glass, mineral memory casings, spore drifts collected after the fall, fractions of tuned gravity stored in ceramic ribs. In the archives they do not read books so much as consult Stanions, which remember in the way a place remembers — by having been there, hardened, and remaining. Because nothing here rots, the dead do not leave. What dies hardens into fabric, into soil, into architecture. Your ancestors are literally in the wall you lean against. A builder who cut a dome three hundred years ago is still, in mineral form, holding up the roof of the workshop where her granddaughter now coaxes a new valve shut.

That is why the cities do not crowd and do not soften. The builders are old. The wards are middle-aged in a way that makes them look permanent. The plateau is older than both and shows no intention of changing. And the people who live across it understand, without needing to say it, that they are not residents of a location. They are a function of it.

They breathe when the moss breathes at dawn. They shed when the flock sheds. They hunt slowly, they migrate inch by decade, they spore in the dry fall when the pressure drops too fast and the air fills with ticking seeds piling in drifts against the Stanions. They share a memory instantly across a kilometer of empty moss because they have learned to live inside the route that does not pass through air.

Stand in one of the empty gaps, halfway between two wards, where the moss is thin and the rounded plateau rolls away into haze, and you will see them at last — not as a crowd, not as a forest, not as a desert made animate, but as one cold, high, waterless plain that is entirely plant, entirely old, entirely awake, and that has grown its inhabitants out of itself the same way it grows everything else: grain by grain, without ever softening its edges.
