Most of what lives here you would never notice, because it is not, in any familiar sense, an animal. It is a settling.

The mud that looked empty is full to packing with bacteria and archaea, cell pressed against cell in the top few centimeters, pillar after pillar of them, ten million to a gram. They are the plain. They take the snow when it lands — the fecal pellets, the empty houses, the skin fragments, the bodies too small to see — and they unmake it. They breathe what is left of it, fix what little nitrogen dissolves in the cold, reduce sulfate in the airless millimeters below, and in doing so they make it possible for anything larger to exist at all. They do not move far. They divide when they can, which in this cold and hunger may be once a day, or once a month, and they live for as long as a cell lives, which may be longer than you would think. Colorless, eyeless, without a mouth you could recognize, they are nevertheless busy in the only way the place allows: a slow chemistry in the dark, unchanged for millions of years.

And inside that chemistry, riding the currents of it, are the grains that learned other tricks. Foraminifera — single cells, but cells the size of your thumbnail, or in the case of the xenophyophores, the size of your hand, a soft body threading through a house it has built from its own mud pellets and the spicules of dead sponges. *Syringammina* and its kin sit in the silt, fragile as wet paper, extruding protoplasm to catch what drifts past, to hold the mud together, to make a small architecture that persists long after the animal inside it has died. Around them, almost invisible in the same centimeters, nematodes whip and pause and whip again, copepods graze on bacteria with raking mouthparts, kinorhynchs step in their slow armored way, polychaete worms lift their gills into the current or retreat into tubes glued from grains. This is the meiofauna, life at the scale of a pinhead, and it is more numerous than everything else put together. Millions to a square meter, still sparse by surface standards, still moving through a world that is essentially a wet stone.

Only after you have learned to see that scale does the larger begin to assemble itself, and it assembles itself very slowly, and very far apart. A worm is not a community here. It is a single presence, hundreds of meters from the next creature you would call an animal. Because there is so little to eat, and what there is arrives as dust, no one can afford to crowd.

On the nodules the situation changes, just slightly, and that slightness matters. A nodule is the only rock there is. To a sponge, it is a continent. White and glassy sponges rise from them, millimeters a year, filtering the snow as it falls and turning their bodies into a sieve. Anemones settle on the black stone and open pale crowns, waiting. Hydroids sprout, stalked crinoids lift their feathery arms on a stem that may live for decades, brittle stars curl around the lumpy edge and raise their arms into the current, not to swim but to comb particles from the water and fold them to the mouth. They are not reef-builders. They do not make a structure that shelters others in the way a tropical coral does. They are tenants, each one using a single nodule as its whole world, and when the nodule, growing a millimeter in a million years, finally is buried, they will be buried with it.

On the open mud, the great plowers come. Holothurians — sea cucumbers, but wrong in every surface expectation of them. Some are translucent pink and almost gelatinous, with wing-like flaps along their backs and a row of stiff legs underneath, walking on tube feet across the sediment at a pace you could miss if you blinked. *Psychropotes*, *Benthodytes*, *Paelopatides* — they do not hunt. They bulldoze. They turn their mouths into the mud and swallow it whole, extracting the bacteria and the organic film and passing the rest out behind them in a neat, coiled cast. The track they leave — a trough with the mark of their legs, looping back on itself in a pattern that looks deliberate — will stay there for days, until the current smooths it. If you watched long enough you would see a whole plain in miniature, in a single animal's path: feeding, excreting, recycling, moving on without hurry because there is nowhere more promising to go.

Between them sit the animals that have decided not to move at all. A tripod fish props itself on three elongated fin rays, its body lifted a hand's breadth off the mud, its head turned into the flow. It does not chase. It waits for snow to fall past its mouth, and when it does, it eats. Its eyes are small or absent, its flesh is soft and low in calcium because pressure and hunger both punish solidity, its skin is unpigmented or brown-black to match the mud. Nearby a grenadier — a rattail — glides low, its long tapering tail undulating behind it, its chin barbel brushing the sediment for amphipods and worms. It can live thirty, sixty years, growing a few millimeters a year, its metabolism turned down to a level where a meal every few weeks is enough. A cutthroat eel, *Synaphobranchus*, will lie half-buried, jaws able to distend around something much larger than itself, because in a place where food is unpredictable, the ability to take what comes, when it comes, is the whole strategy.

These are the vertebrates, and they are rare enough that a lifetime of watching might still count them on fingers. A snubnosed eel. A cusk eel. A deep angler with a lure that glows faintly blue, not to attract prey so much as to make its own presence known in a world where being seen at all is an event. A sleeper shark, perhaps, once in a year, crossing the plain at a distance so great it is only a shadow in the current. They have no swim bladders full of gas — gas would kill them at this pressure — or they replace it with fat. Their muscles are full of trimethylamine N-oxide, a molecule that keeps their proteins from crushing under five hundred atmospheres. Their flesh is watery, their bones are light, because there is no need to hold yourself up and no food to build strength with.

Walking in this economy are the animals that make the fish possible: amphipods with scythe-like legs, isopods that curl and uncurl, benthic shrimp that stalk on long antennae, tasting the mud for what the holothurians left. They are scavengers first. Most of the time they are scattered, eating snow and detritus and each other by accident. But drop something large — a whale that died a thousand meters up and sank, a shark, a fish in numbers — and the plain reveals what it has been hiding. Within hours amphipods arrive in their hundreds from hundreds of meters away, shrimp gather, fish follow, and for a week or a month there is a crowd, a brief, frantic abundance, flesh stripped to bone, bacteria bloom white and thick over the remains. Then it is gone, and the plain returns to its rule. The crowd was never really there. It was waiting, spread thin, for an exception.

That waiting is the shape of every life here. The bacteria wait in the mud. The sponges wait on the nodules. The anemones wait with mouths open like small hands. The tripod fish waits on its stilts. The grenadier waits in its slow patrol. Even the light, when it appears, is waiting made visible: a copepod disturbed flashes blue and brief, a shrimp carries photophores along its belly, a brittle star makes a pinprick of light along its arm, a fish countershades itself with its own glow so that from below it disappears. No one glows for beauty. They glow because in a black world a small chemistry is the only spear, the only shield, the only announcement that a body is here and not edible, or edible and already gone.

At a few places the plain is not quite plain. Where old sediment seeps methane upward, pale mats of filamentous bacteria spread like felt over the mud, grey-white and sparse, chemosynthetic — living not on sunlight but on the chemistry of the earth itself. No animal built them. They built themselves, thread by thread, and other animals come to graze their edges or to shelter in them. They are the only patch of color that is not black, brown, or the pink-transparent of a sea pig's skin.

It is not a forest and it is not a desert. It is a thin skin of life stretched over an ancient floor, packed at the bottom with microbes, packed in the water with falling snow, and in between, almost nothing you can point to for more than a minute at a time. A brittle star waves. A sea pig plows a loop that will vanish. A fish stands, facing the current, for an hour, a day, until the current brings it something. The snow keeps falling through the black, past the grenadier's eye, past the sponge's mouth, down to the bacteria that will finish what the others began. Nothing hurries except the water that carries it, and everything that lives here has learned, over timescales that dwarf its own body, how to wait in that water without being disturbed.
