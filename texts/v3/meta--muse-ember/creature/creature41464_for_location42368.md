You hear them before you separate any one of them.

It is not a single call, or a single species calling. It is weather made by bodies. Ten thousand wings, perhaps more, shifting against stone and against each other, and underneath that the ceaseless hail of clicks. Far off it is a roar. Closer — a bird directly over your head, a tap on a window, a chitter, a beak knocked softly against rock, the dry papery brush of feathers when one neighbor settles against another. When the whole mass in the middle of the dark turns in one slow current, the sound rises into your sternum and then settles again, patient, unhurried, as if panic itself had learned to move slowly here.

They are small swifts, dark above, pale at the throat, built for thin high air. Outside, at the mouth of the shaft, the sky is hard and bright and cold, and the moorland below is sparse. There they hunt, for the length of a long summer day, insects lifted on the wind. Inside, their eyes are almost retired. A swift returning at dusk does not fly in by sight. It circles in the dark centre of the chamber clicking, listening to the click come back from the curved wall, adjusting, circling again, until the echo tells it where its own half-cup is — a nest glued rim to rim to the nests beside it, shoulder to shoulder with neighbors, in a living wallpaper that climbs from a few metres above the floor to the crown of the ceiling.

The nests are not built so much as spat into shape: saliva and feathers and a little moss pulled from the shaft lip, molded while wet, hardened overnight. On them they sit upright, clinging vertically, wings folded so tight their tips brush the bird beside them. When one shifts to preen, to turn an egg, to feed a chick, the touch travels. When one launches out and up through that single doorway shaft toward the pale sky, a ripple goes out through the wall like a hand passing over wheat. When one returns, it must find a gap no larger than itself, and the birds around it make room by making room only — a small lateral press, a flick of wing — and then close again.

They live this way in layers of closeness. Old birds, young birds, chicks blind and naked in the cups, fed on boli of insects broken up and softened outside and brought back in a parent's throat. They sleep clinging, packed, all their heat given off into the stone and the air. Molting, they shed down and flight feathers into the air; the air carries them, with dander and small insects, up the shaft to the bright world. Dying, they fall. A bird dead on the guano floor is stripped by beetles in a day. That is not waste here. That is furnishing.

Because what falls from the wall becomes the floor, and the floor is alive at a scale you would miss if you stepped over it without thinking.

Beneath your boots the guano crust gives a fraction, then holds. In it and on it runs another loudness, if you put a knee down and an ear close: beetles ticking over one another as they climb, their elytra clicking in the dark; mites moving in a faint continuous rustle, too small to see singly, a sound like dry leaves stirred; isopods scratching with their many legs over the plaster-smooth places and the grainy places; worms threading through the softened film where a drip has landed and darkened the crust. They follow chemical trails laid down in the guano over centuries, each species reading the map left by the ones before it. They eat the fungi. They eat each other’s leavings. They eat the dead.

Patches of fungus feed in that rich crust and give off a sparse, sickly greenish glow — not enough to see by, enough to feel, perhaps, as a paleness, when you breathe upon them. Your breath brightens them a little, carbon dioxide feeding them in the dark. They, in turn, are grazed by mites and isopods and the slow worms, and they break the guano down into the warmth the place lives on. Everything horizontal near the floor is dry and crusty and warm, because decomposition is a slow fire that never goes out.

Everything vertical is wet.

On the ribs of the ceiling, where warm air from the bodies and the guano below meets cooler stone, water condenses, beads, gathers, and falls in slow irregular taps. Across those dripping ribs, harvestmen have spun communal curtains — not single webs for single spiders, but shared sheets of silk strung bead to bead with water. They hang there together, legs outstretched, feeling the vibration of the silk, waiting for the small flies and midges that fall from the birds or wander up from below. Their whole world is a tremor in a thread.

And hung from the ceiling, by their hind legs, in clusters so dense they touch antenna to antenna, are the crickets. Blind, white as curd, they do not climb or walk as other crickets do. They hang head-down, swaying very slightly, listening with antennae and with the fine hairs of their legs to the air around them. When the colony’s noise peaks — when the swifts turn in the centre — the crickets sway in unison, as if in a wind that is not there. It is the sound, moving through stone and through their hanging bodies, that moves them. They feed, when they feed, on fungi and the damp films and whatever organic dust settles onto the ceiling. To touch is to know where another cricket is, and where empty ceiling begins.

On one wall, in the vertical film of condensation itself, lives the worm. It is a dark line you would not notice until you watched long enough to see it move. At night, when the drips weaken, it climbs straight up the wet stone to graze on the fungi that grow in the film. Before the drips strengthen in the day, it descends by the same certain seam, straight down. It trusts the strength of the water and the slope of the rock more than any eye. If you watched for an hour, you would see that line descend, unhurried, and vanish into the damp.

Lower still, in the transient skins of water that collect in a hollow for an hour or two before soaking away or evaporating, breeds the frog. No bigger than a thumbnail, brown and warted, it is almost perfectly camouflaged against the guano. Its call is like a fingernail dragged on wet tile, and because the beetles are ticking and the birds are roaring, you will only hear it if you are crouched, very close, listening through everything else. It has laid its race on the briefest of pools. A skin of water that lasts an afternoon must hold eggs, must hold tadpoles long enough to transform, must do in hours what other frogs do in weeks. Most fail. The skin dries on the warm crust. Some, in a wet year, make it to the stone.

That is the rule here, and it governs the frog as it governs the swift: nothing trusts its eyes. The birds click. The crickets touch antenna to antenna. The beetles follow smells in the guano. The worm feels the drip weaken and strengthens. The harvestmen feel the web. The fungus brightens when breathed upon. Even the plants in the far crack, where warm air sighs out through a low fissure, are living by touch and by trickle — twelve of them, perhaps, tufts of moss and two small ferns clinging to the one place where there is both a little moisture and the pale memory of shaft light. Their roots have found a seam in the andesite and are working it open at a pace no one here will see finished. A fern unfurls a frond in a season, and in a decade, and makes a dark green comma in the otherwise greenless dark. That is the whole of the vegetation inside. The rest is too dark, too rich in ammonia and salts, too trampled by life, for anything green to stand.

Outside the mouth, because the inside only touches the outside at its lip, they live too: a cushion plant holding its own warmth against the summit wind, a lichen the colour of old mustard spread thin on bare stone, a few wind-blown grasses bending and recovering. They send down, occasionally, a snail that wanders too far from the shaft mouth and finds itself in the chamber, slow, shelled, tasting the damp stone with its underside. Snails do not last long in the crowded dark. On the floor lie a dozen water-worn pebbles with polished tops — anvil stones. A shrew-like climber, larger than the birds, scales the wall in the night, hauls a snail to one of these stones, and cracks the shell cleanly against it. Its kind has done this every time one has come. The anvils are untouched except for that shine. The snail shells lie split. The climber itself, one individual, once scaled the wall and starved in the crack and lay there until its bones were stained faint orange by seeping water and its skull filled with beetles. It is the only thing here that looks like an artifact, and it is being unmade as carefully as everything else.

Spiders lower themselves on silk to inspect anything new — a hand, a hair, a shoulder. A bird will land briefly on that shoulder, mistaking it for a ledge, then think better of it and launch again into the click-filled dark. That is the final habit of the place: density closes. A ledge unoccupied for one night is occupied the next. A drip falls steadily on one stone and wears a tiny polished hollow, and a beetle uses the hollow as a bath. Nothing stays empty long enough to be called empty.

Time, for all of them, is measured differently and all held in the same equilibrium. A swift will live a few years of dawns out and dusks in. A chick will fledge into the same wall its parents were born on, click for its nest, and become one more shoulder for the next bird to press against. A beetle generation will strip a bird and be eaten or die in the crust that feeds the fungi that feed the mites. A frog will try the brief pool again next wet season. A fern will root a little deeper. A stalactite above will grow an eyelash length in a lifetime and no one will see it.

They do not look at each other. They listen, and touch, and smell, and feel the warm wet rock tell them where the wall is and the thick warm air tell them where the centre is, where the wings are thickest. And the chamber, small as a cathedral and crowded as a held breath, keeps them all — floor, wall, air, and ceiling — each layer touching the next, each sound touching the next, going on in slow patient cycles far longer than any one life that fills it.
