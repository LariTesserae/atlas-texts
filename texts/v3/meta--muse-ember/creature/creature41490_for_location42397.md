You do not meet the residents of the Rootworks so much as notice, underfoot, that the floor is alive and moving.

Ankle-deep in liverwort and moss, the mat gives like a lung. It muffles your step because it is eating it. Vines crawl and re-root themselves as you watch, not advancing in a line but unhooking and hooking again a few inches on, nomads with their homes in their bellies. Under that springy skin, roots travel with a patience no animal foot could match. They push through a brick and do not creak. They fill the raw pits the engines dug with pale feelers. They drink the lens-thin pools in the lanes for no visible reason, and the pools, with their frogs and blind fish and false reflections, are gone — drunk, not evaporated. To live here first of all is to be substrate. To be eaten, slowly, by what you stand on, and to eat it back.

If you crouch, the substrate resolves into weather.

Springtails drift across it like snow that will not melt. Beetles in their thousands move under leaves, tending spores they farm in neat, damp gardens on the undersides, turning leaf into pasture. The gnats that hunt the beetles make no sound you can catch, and the bats that hunt the gnats are only a rustle and a pressure change in the cool air, which is how most things here announce themselves. In the folded-bark gutters, blind newts swim their slow laps. In those temporary pockets of rainwater, blind fish turn toward reflections of things that are not above them, because the world they navigate is by touch, by current, by the faint electrical weather of a root near a vat. A caterpillar, a green thread of its own exhaled light, crosses a lane at your feet and is gone into the moss, leaving the lane to relax behind it. None of these lives are large enough to fear and none are still. Roots travel. Vats ferment. Rain starts in the undersides of leaves and sometimes runs upward, thread by thread, toward a leaf to fall again. The whole ecology is in motion at a volume below a drip.

At the edges, where the palisade of black-stemmed ferns and thorned saplings closes as a fence, the larger browsers move. They are shaggy and slow and unhurried in a way that makes the small things look frantic by comparison. They do not graze so much as edit. Their root-mouths trim the houses and trim the Engine Tree, leaving them cleaner, removing the growth that would close a clearing or choke a chimney liana. They are gardeners and maintenance crews and, perhaps, editors of a text that keeps trying to overwrite itself. What they eat becomes, in time, another wall, another pipe, another street persuaded out of plants leaning together.

The houses are animals of a slower kind, and it takes a while to stop expecting people in them.

A House is a hollowed trunk with a brick core still soft as unfired clay. North side, it wears brick again. South side, leaf litter. Inside, a smooth skin that breathes faintly at night, so faintly you are not sure you saw it until you have watched long enough for the fungus in the damp wall to fruit and glow. The glow is not a lamp. It is phosphor, a waste product of a life that has eaten too much darkness. Roofs are canopies trained to tile themselves, grooming their own shingles. Windows are not glass but clearings, places where the wall has agreed not to grow, so a house looks out through its own surface. Some houses are barely a thickening of stem and a suggestion of a door, embryonic. Some are already rotting back into the mat, shedding bricks like seeds. Most are both at once, because here construction runs backward: a thing must be allowed to fall apart first, before the plants will grow it properly.

When a house finally dies, it does not collapse. It opens. A cluster of frogs spills out, tongues flicking without a click, bellies faintly luminous, and those frogs are not merely the house's tenants or its scavengers. They are the next house, in miniature, already rooting in the damp where the old wall lay. House, frog, house again — the life cycle does not so much turn as it loosens and retightens, the same matter trying a different shape. Others stay dark and will stay dark, the fungus never fruiting, the breathing too shallow to see. They are not empty. They are pre-formed, waiting for the conditions the factory is making.

The Engine Tree is the largest resident, and the only one that could be mistaken for a god if you stood close enough to hear its heart.

Its trunk is plated with riveted iron, its limbs cradled in steel scaffolding, its hollow heart turned into a mill. The furnace-heart of it blooms orange when the vats are working. Turbines turn on sap pressure and on the drip of condensation falling from cold iron onto hot iron, a heartbeat made of weather. Fermentation vats bubble slowly, acid-sweet, and lianas trained upward as chimneys exhale what rises. Inside, the process is legible and wrong, because the machines are not powered to make products. They are powered to make conditions — heat, vibration, moisture, a steady hum below hearing — under which plants will know where to grow the next wall, the next pipe, the next street.

The workers are not workers in any hand you could shake. Conveyor belts carry root-cuttings, infants in transit. Looms weave cable out of vine and copper bark, humming to the beetles that tend them. At the end of the line what comes off is a seed, or a raw brick, or a beetle still soft in its case, hatched straight into the substrate to do the work of assembly. They harden as they go, learning the town by eating it, by growing into it. A beetle may become a hinge, a hinge may become a root, a root may become a street. When the town dies, it is fed back into the factory to be unmade into raw material. So the Tree both births and digests its people. Matter and cause keep a very loose hold on each other, and the inhabitants keep loose hold of their own forms.

Between presences there are vast intervals, an acre or more of unbroken plant between a single house and a single mill, between an intersection and its signpost in the middle. Those intervals are not empty. That is where most of the living happens. Streets exist, numbered and named in cast iron screwed to bark — FERMENTATION ROW, HOUSING A, CROSSING 4 — drains and hydrants and traffic lights blinking green for no one. But the lights blink for the migrations. Along routes no one marked, browsers and beetles and frogs and root-tips cross the empty lanes, compacting the moss underfoot, persuading the plants to lean together into next season’s road, then relax again. The lanes are not paved. They are remembered by feet. The power lines sag from trunk to trunk, high trails for vines and for the pressure-change of bats. The hydrants weep into pools that vanish. To live here is to live mostly in transit between things that are themselves in transit.

And all of it lives in darkness that is not absence but food. There is no sun and no day. The plants photosynthesize the dark. Where the ferns are thickest, the dark thins, eaten down to a pale, bruise-colored glow, the afterimage of light. The residents who shine do so because they have overeaten. Fungus on a wall, the belly of a frog, the green thread of a caterpillar, the orange bloom of the Engine Tree’s heart — light is exhaled by things bloated with night. Condensation beads on cold iron and on your own sleeves and falls in slow rain that seems to start in leaves, because for these inhabitants the sky is only the underside of the nearest plant. Water is everywhere and never accumulates; the pre-river that should cross the town is only a damp line in the moss, an idea of a channel, and the blind fish wait in lenses of water for a carving that has not yet committed itself.

That is the loop they live in, if loop is the word: predator and prey, grower and grown, animal and worker, worker and machine, machine and the next season’s town grown out of this season’s ruins. A frog eats a gnat that ate a spore that will become a wall. A house breathes, glows, opens, spills frogs. A vat bubbles. A root pushes through a brick without a sound. A browser trims the Tree cleaner than it was. A beetle, still soft, hardens into a wall it will one day be fed back to unmake.

They are not finished being described, because they are not finished being made.
