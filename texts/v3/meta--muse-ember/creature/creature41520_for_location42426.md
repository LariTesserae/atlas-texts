If you stay long enough to stop looking at the terraces and start looking at what moves between them, the place resolves, slowly, into living things — not dramatically, not all at once. First it is people, because people are the largest animal here and the only one present in numbers that matter. Then, underneath and alongside them, the smaller, quieter tenants who have learned to persist in the damp and the dim. Then the green things, which are everywhere and never quite wild. They are not separate populations so much as interwoven habits, each taking up the space the others leave.

The human inhabitants are not a crowd. A crowd implies a street, a moment, a gathering. Here there is no street wide enough for that, and no moment that is not already a shift change or a delivery or a repair. What you see instead is a continuous, unhurried flow, as if the whole plateau were breathing in and out through its walkways. Men and women in the same practical, weatherproof clothing — jackets that never quite dry at the shoulders, boots polished by wet decking — moving carts, carrying bins, opening shutters, tapping tanks. They do not hurry, not because they are told not to, but because hurry would be wasted on the terraces. Everything is close. Nothing is far. A job three levels down is still only minutes away by ramp and stair, and the engines that move heavy things are slow by design.

They live where they work, or just above or below where they work. A dwelling is rarely just a dwelling. One terrace level might be workshops in the morning and drying rooms in the afternoon, with a family sleeping behind a thin wall that hums with transformers. The next level up, the underside of the deck above is a ceiling lined with ducts, and a kitchen kettle can be heard two decks over through packed walls. Units are small, saturated, defined less by doors than by curtains, by changes in light, by the smell of food or plant fiber or warm metal. There are no empty rooms to wander into. If a space stood vacant for more than a few days, someone would have filled it — a storage rack, a trough of seedlings, a cot, a nest-box, a tank. The inhabitants know this rule the way they know drainage: not as morality, but as physics.

Their bodies have the patience of the place. Skin stays a little too damp. Hair never quite dries in winter. Eyes are used to grey-white light and low amber, so a bright lamp startles more than it helps. Voices have settled to the working murmur the terraces require; shouting would simply be absorbed and lost, and would disturb everyone whose life is pressed close on the other side of a wall. Children learn early to move sideways in narrow walkways, to read the color of a terrace — brown means a drain is blocked, too thick a green means water is pooling where it should not — and to keep their hands out of the channels under the grating where water runs fast and cold. Elders sit, often, near windows or greenhouse glass, where the condensation runs down and they can watch the flow of carts and people without needing to join it. Generations have been born here since the plateau was full. Most do not find it strange. The ridge road and the valley below are where elsewhere is.

Work divides them more than age or family. There are tenders who live by the food terraces, hands green-stained, coaxing beans up cable and training stunted trees in concrete tubs whose roots follow the seams of the deck. There are repair crews who take a forty-year wall down block by block in a morning because a culvert has shifted, and re-lay it by evening, planting ferns into the fresh mortar before the concrete has fully set. There are filter and algae-bed workers in the lower levels, where the air is warmer with waste heat and the fish tanks sit in basements no animal larger than a forearm ever sees. There are runners and carters, crane operators slinging beams from roof to roof, sorters at the belt conveyors where scrap dribblingly becomes something else. All of it is mundane, scaled to the terraces, making things for the place itself and for the valley below: components, bound fiber, processed food, glass, tools, the endless supply of replacement parts that keep the churn from becoming collapse. No one is a hero of industry here. They are maintainers of a settled thing.

The nonhuman animals are rare to the point of being notable when you do encounter them, and discreet to the point of being mostly heard or inferred. This is not a place that supports largeness. It is too cool, too wet, too made, too constantly disturbed, and people occupy every niche a larger creature might try to claim.

In summer, moths gather at the lamps — silver, battered by damp, fluttering in the low amber glow that never quite turns off. They are incidental, a by-product of light and warmth meeting cool air. Drain eels move in the culverts, slow punctuation in the flow under the gratings, visible only if you crouch and look where the water runs shallow and fast over concrete. They are not kept. They persist. People know where they tend to be because the water is cleanest there, or because the eels have learned the rhythm of the recirculation.

Pigeons hold the highest terraces in stubborn pairs. A few, no more. They nest in eaves and in the empty nest-boxes bolted to walls, cooing at a volume that respects the quiet, pecking at spilled grain near the food terraces and then retreating. Rats live deeper, in the shared service spines between buildings where it is damp and green at the bottom and the ferns grow thick in the drip. You hear them more than you see them — a scurry, a soft thump — and the inhabitants tolerate them with the same tolerance they show for moss on iron: not affection, but recognition that they are part of the system, eating what would otherwise rot and clog. A cat or two per district, kept mostly indoors, formal owners of a particular windowsill, sitting in the grey light with an air of absolute tenure while the terrace goes on moving around them. That is nearly the whole list. No dogs. No livestock in the open. No flocks. Anything larger would need space to be large, and there is none that is not already used.

The green life, by contrast, is everywhere, and it is not decoration. Ferns push from expansion joints. Vines trained over conveyor housings escape their training. Watercress and algae fatten in the gutters where runoff gathers. Moss thickens on damp iron until the iron looks soft. On the middle terraces, the food terraces are long and low and perpetually raining inside their glass. The plants cool the walls, slow the water, hold the dust. They are indicators and workers. Cut back when they block a drain or a walkway, they are composted into bins and fed to the beds again in a loop as tight as any pipe. A terrace that goes brown is failing. A terrace that goes too green is also failing, but slower — the inhabitants read them both the way a doctor reads a pulse.

Under all of that, the smallest inhabitants do the foundational work: algae in the filter beds, bacteria in the warm outflow, the slick biofilms in the channels. They are fed by the greenhouses, they feed the fish, they keep the recirculating skin of water just below the decks from turning stale. No one sees most of them, but everyone depends on their steady, invisible turbulence.

What binds them together is not love of the place, and not mystery. It is the ordinary rules, repeated until they become instinct: everything must drain, everything must be reused, nothing natural may be exposed for long because the damp will take it back. Plants may grow where they like so long as they do not block anything. Noise stays low because walls are thin and air carries. Structures are inspected after rain and heavy condensation not because collapse is dramatic here — it is not — but because a missed drip becomes a softened joint becomes a softened foundation over years.

By night, when the dimming is slight rather than dark, the lamps come up everywhere and the inhabitants — human, animal, plant — settle into their layers again, reflected in wet decks and running channels and clouded glass. The carts slow. The presses thud three levels down. Water keeps dripping in rhythms that vary by level. And the whole crowded, quiet, constantly remade habitat continues as it has for a long time: completely made, completely lived in, never quite finished, and never, for that reason, quite falling apart.
