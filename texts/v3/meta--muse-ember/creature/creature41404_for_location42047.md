At first it is hard to say what “lives” means here, because the place itself has not decided where rock ends and body begins.

The stone weeps. The vents sigh open. A crack gets a white rim overnight while you sleep. A footprint in wet sinter fills with water before your foot lifts. All of that could be geology. It could also be the first, very slow movements of something that is still being born. In a bowl this young, hot, high and wet, life does not arrive as animals marching in. It accumulates. It starts as color.

The first inhabitants are not seen so much as stained. On the black glass, in knife-edged hollows that never dry, rust-red films spread and retreat with the day — iron-oxidizers, algal mats, bacteria that eat fresh basalt and sulfur and exhale a faint rotten-egg sweet smell. They are the reason some shards look bruised, others pale. They live in the white sinter plates too, contributing to them, holding the chalky crust together with a living mortar. Where the ground is hot enough to blister a boot, they do not live. Where a fracture line beads and runs and beads again, they do, in a thin, slick skin no thicker than paint. They are everywhere and almost nowhere in bulk — a population measured not in individuals but in coverage. They make the first soil inside cracks, very slowly, by dissolving stone and dying in place. Everything greener that comes later comes after them.

The biggest single living thing here is under the marsh.

If you probe the bright mineral crust in the lowest dent, your stick does not meet stone. It meets something taut and flexible, just below the surface, that gives and then pushes back and hums — more in your teeth and jaw than in your ears. That is a lattice, a skin, a colony grown at a scale we do not have a good word for: part microbial mat, part polymer, part mineral scaffold, fused with plant root tips that have found their way down through the crust. It holds water up the way a drumhead holds tension. It is why puddles perch on a thirty-degree slope. It is why the marsh can spread, warm and shallow, over stone that should have drained in an hour. It is not a dam. It does not stop water so much as persuade it, minute by minute, to linger.

You can hear it change. At dawn, heavy with condensation, the hum is low. By noon, as the marsh visibly loses a finger’s width to evaporation, the pitch tightens. When the afternoon cloud builds — only over the bowl, dark-bottomed for twenty minutes — and rains straight down, the hum drops again as the skin drinks and swells. At dusk it settles to a steady, low note under the steam, as if exhaling the day’s heat. It has no mouth, no eyes, but it anticipates pressure. That is why your footprints fill before you lift your feet: you are pressing on its extended nervous system, and somewhere in a capillary of sinter it has already pushed water up to meet you.

The green islands are the second great presence, and they too are more animal-like than they first seem.

A single plant can be the breadth of a table, its leaves arching over black pools. A fern can unfurl in an audible burst at noon, fronds cracking open with a soft pop you can hear in the thin air. A vine can heave, visibly, during the twenty-minute rain, pushing itself across a ledge in increments you catch if you stare. They do not grow in soil. They grow in the wet skin of the rock, roots threading fresh cracks, feeding on steam and dissolved mineral rather than on humus. They photosynthesize, if that is still the word, in steam and half-dark under their own leaves, and they unfurl toward heat rather than light — because here heat is the reliable currency and light is ordinary, trapped and glared back by white crust.

They are lush precisely because they are isolated. Between islands is bare, blistering stone, white salt, ash-pale ground. No root crosses that emptiness; it is too hot, too new, too uncarved. Each island is a world unto itself, a sudden cool green room smelling of crushed sap, dripping until mid-morning and then steaming, then suddenly rain-wet again, then dark. The moss that looks dyed vivid green lives only where water is held longest, on the shaded side of a black shard or at the base of the dome. The orchid-like flowers open only for the hour of the storm — not for pollinators you would recognize, but for the one daily moment when the air inside the rim fills, closes, and condenses all at once. They are timed to the bowl’s breath.

Animals, by contrast, are provisional.

There is no birdsong, no frog chorus, though the marsh ought to be full of frogs. The frogs are silent because, perhaps, there are none left to croak — or because sound itself is muffled by altitude and heat and the pressure of the quiet. What lives here as animal life is rare enough that a day can pass with no sighting at all, only traces.

In the warmest pool, over white mineral, lives a pale newt, nearly translucent. You do not so much see it as lose it: when you look directly at it, it vanishes, its skin going glass-clear against the bright bottom. Side-lit, you can make out the faint green of algae living inside its gut, photosynthesizing through its body while it sits in near-boiling water. It feeds on the microbial film at the pool’s edge and on whatever falls in as vapor. It is bound to that one pool. It cannot cross the hot stone to another, and it does not need to. Its world is the size of a grove, and it is whole.

On a black shard in the shade of a green island lives the thermal lizard, if you are lucky and still enough to wait an hour. Its skin is the exact grey of cooling stone, indistinguishable from the shard it suns on. It does not bask the way a desert lizard does, for coolness. It sits on the black glass to take in heat, its throat pulsing slowly, visibly, once, then again — a living thermometer storing the day. At dusk it returns to its warm shard and goes still in the fog. It is, as far as anyone can tell, only itself. One species, perhaps one individual, a decision the place made recently and has not yet revised.

At dusk, in the thickening steam, come the vapor-moths. They are slow and blind, blundering into steam at the rim until they die. Their wings are not for seeing the world but for condensing it; they navigate by heat and humidity on their antennae, because eyes, here, would boil. They pupate all day in hot sinter and in the cold beads of water that form only on the stelae, and emerge when the fog comes in to live for a few hours. Their tiny corpses are food for the marsh. They are the only animal that regularly leaves the bowl — steam at the rim sometimes falls back down as warm rain on the outside, and moths go with it, briefly, to die in air that is not theirs.

And then there are the ones that were here before the green.

The built things — the phrase is inadequate — are sessile, ancient by local standards, and by far the most at home. The low half-dome on the western terrace is matte grey-white, seamless, hot to the touch outside and a steady 21 degrees inside, with no cable, no vent, no door until the section of wall simply becomes less present as you approach. Moss has colonized its base. A vine grew across a hairline crack; the crack healed overnight around the vine, without harming it. That is not construction. That is a living material remembering its shape, fusing with root at a nanoscale level, self-cleaning, self-repairing, maintaining a microclimate the way a plant maintains shade.

The line of ankle-high stelae across the dry ridge are cool at noon when everything else burns, bone-cold in sun, beading with water from air that beads on nothing else. At dusk they tick once, softly, like cooling teeth, and are quiet. They are not markers, though they mark a path. They are spines, or lungs — cold sinks where the bowl’s daily breath condenses and where tiny life can drink. The flat, mirrored plate half-buried near the marsh, tilted slightly to the sky, does not attract water by magic. It is a cold leaf. Water gathers toward it in a trembling sheet because it condenses first, and the lattice under the marsh, sensing a cold gradient, swells toward it. Pools near it reflect a slightly deeper blue than the sky overhead, as if reflecting a moment an hour earlier — because that is when their skin was coolest, and most itself.

These grown artifacts do not age as stone ages. A piece broken off by a falling shard smooths itself back into the whole over days. They do not rust, do not rot, do not need power. They were, perhaps, planted here long before any fern unfurled — and now new life is colonizing them, moss at their feet, vines across their healed skin. They are the elders, and the green islands and the marsh skin are the newcomers learning to live around them.

All of them together — microbial paint, humming lattice, heat-seeking leaves, translucent newt, grey lizard, blind moths, cold stelae, healing dome — are synchronized to a single cycle, because they have to be. Condense at dawn, when water drips from leaf edges and your own hair. Burn off by ten. Storm inside the rim in the afternoon, rain that hisses on stone and does not run off and is drawn back up as steam. Fog at dusk, honeyed light turning grey, dome glowing faintly from within though no one is inside and there is no light to glow with, marsh steaming harder.

That is when you see how they fit. The plants heave during the rain. The lattice hum deepens. The lizard’s throat slows on its warm shard. The moths rise and die. The stelae tick. The newt, invisible in its pool, lets the day’s heat soak out of its translucent body into the water, which the lattice will hold until morning. Nothing is loud. Everything is moving in a held breath.

You can walk the whole bowl before dark and see from anywhere all the way to the far rim and beyond it to emptiness. The inhabitants cannot. Each lives in a pocket no larger than a room, a pool, a shard, a ridge line, and must survive the crossing between pockets by never crossing it. That is why there are so few of them, and why each one feels disproportionately large, disproportionately patient, disproportionately out of place — a single lizard on black glass, a single newt in white water, a single skin humming under a grove-sized marsh, a cloud that forms for no reason outside the rim and rains only inside.

They are not a finished community. They are a place in the act of inventing one, process upon process at once: vents ticking open, water climbing, ferns bursting, moths dying, stone healing around a vine. Whatever lives here, lives lightly, and mostly out of sight, and on terms the bowl sets daily: linger, endure heat, drink steam, wait for the rain that comes only here, and hold.
