At first there is only movement, and it makes almost no sound.

A snail, translucent as a drop of melt, is grazing the lower wall where the ice is green. It moves a body-length at a time, across the living skin of algae, leaving behind it a cleaner, paler track that the green close over again minutes later. It does not hurry. Nothing here needs to.

A fly hangs over the white saxifrage without buzzing. Its wings are too cold to hum. It settles, lifts, settles again, slower than a thought, and the flower trembles a little — not from wind, there is none, but from the small weight of it. The pollen stays on its feet.

Then the movement gathers. A hand lifts in the bright, shadowless air, quick and precise, fingers folding and unfolding against the faint blue glow of the dome. Another hand answers it a pace away, over a basket. A third hand, belonging to someone stooped under a root-bridge at chest height, reaches up to steady a terrace of turf and mat, and both people are still.

That is speech here. The hands come before the faces do.

They are small people, built for the narrow places. Everything is low and rounded for them — doorways worn by shoulders, steps hollowed by feet, tunnels of woven mat and driftwood lashed with root-fiber that you have to duck through. They move barefoot or in soft wraps of pale fiber on the moss, and moss deadens their step to a dull thud you feel more than hear. Their skin is damp from the air, their hair damp, their layers of woven fiber and moss saturated and juicy like everything else that grows. The layers are many, not thick, because the cold inside is the cold of deep earth in spring, constant and endurable, and because they cluster.

They cluster always. Around the compost warmth under the floors, where worms are working unseen in the dark, damp soil, turning leaf and root and old mat into heat. Around the communal long-house, which smells of cold clay and crushed green leaf and peat and wet stone. Around the central pool, where the air is a fraction warmer from the water and the reflected light is strongest. Breath fogs, close and quiet, when one leans to another to speak in breath too soft to carry more than a handspan. Mostly they do not need the breath. A child — though youth and age do not separate cleanly here, you see the same hands at different heights, learning by imitation over a shoulder — watches an elder’s fingers in the luminous air, and copies, and is understood.

No one has a loud tool. A flint knife cuts on a mat. A bone needle passes through fiber with a faint click, bone against bone. A hammer, when there is hammering to do, is struck down into moss to deaden it. Pots are set down, not dropped. Doors are mats let fall, not shut. Goods move in tight-woven baskets that hold damp grain without spilling, or on small sledges pulled along the worn paths between terraces, at a pace that matches the drip from the dome. The one sound that is allowed to repeat is that drip: a fat drop gathering on the cold upper ice, rolling down its carved groove in a bright thread, and falling, once, onto the neck, cool and patient, before it is caught in a channel, a cup, a cistern under a house. Even the water learns not to strike.

What they do, all day in the light that never changes, is hold. They clear moss from a path and the moss grows again, and they clear it again to exactly the same shape. They prune the dwarf birch as fast as it reaches for the roof, and its crown stays pressed, dense and held, under the ice. They weed the gardens that grow sideways from the dome wall and upside-down from the terraces, where ferns hang down turning their fronds toward the reflected light off the pool below, because that is the light they can reach. They tend cloudberry in soil pockets in the wall, where a single shrub shows white blossom and green fruit and orange ripe berries side by side, because nothing here passes through one season at a time. It is not harvest. Harvest would be an increase. This is equilibrium: growth taken by use, use replaced by growth, the pool neither rising nor falling, the driftwood grey as stone, the clay dark and smooth as river mud, the ice forgetting every edge.

They are not separate from the other lives that share the room. They plant the moss that carpets every floor and roof and stone, thick enough to soften a step, and the moss in return softens everything they make. They set the grooves in the ice that carry condensation from breath and leaf to pool and garden bed and cistern, and the algae on the wet wall makes its own green skin in the damp they keep. Under the houses the worms work. In the silvered wood the beetles move, tiny and silent, in the old fibers. Over the flowers the pale flies hover without humming. 

In the pool at the center, which is the town’s one river and never flows, the frogs sit half-submerged among the pocket pools, fat and slow, and do not croak. Their eyes are above the water, reflecting green. Among the birch’s roots, spread across the floor like a delta, the tiny stickleback turn in the clear water, silver flashes that make no ripple. The roots themselves are thoroughfares and handholds and, at the center, a foundation. The birch is the oldest inhabitant, older than any frame of driftwood or wall of turf. Its trunk is twisted and pale as bone. Its crown, pruned by generations, presses up against the glowing roof and holds. Ferns hang from its lower branches. Birds nest in that crown, and in the eaves of the houses, and watch with bright eyes, and do not call. Their silence is not an absence; the thick moss and the snow of the roof swallow vibration as fast as it is born, and everything has learned not to make it.

Above them all, in the bright still air, laundry and weaving and bundles of herbs hang drying. No wind moves them; they dry because the air is always bright and always the same. Someone, on a ledge clinging to the curve of the dome where a garden nest has been tucked, reaches with both hands to turn a mat. Someone below, in a cellar scooped under a house stacked three and four deep against the wall, reaches for dried berries or roots or pottery, their fingers finding the place by touch in the soft pools of deeper green and blue where shadow pools. No one is ever hurried past anyone else. Streets are only the gaps left for passing, cobbles set where feet fall and polished to pebbles, and where there is no room on the floor they go up — root-bridges at chest height, terraces, ledges — and the town rises, shoulder to shoulder, with no blank wall between one life and the next.

There is no youth to leave and no age to accumulate. A tool left on a mat today will be there season after season, same soft light, same dusting of frost on its handle in the north corners where lace-white frost never melts. The people who tend it have the same unhurried hands whenever you look. They wear their pale layers damp, they breathe fog, they speak with their hands in the luminous air, they prune and clear and carry and channel water, and they are lush in exactly the way the moss and fern and algae and saxifrage are lush — saturated, green, alive, and holding still.

When you part the moss curtain and stoop up the two rounded steps into the flat, glaring cold of the plain, they do not call after you. Birds do not call. Frogs do not croak. Nothing makes the sound its kind would make elsewhere. Behind you the hummock is only green, dotted with white flowers trembling for no reason you can see. Inside, the hands continue, the snail continues, the fly continues to hang over the flower, the drip continues to fall once in a long time, and the whole bright, silent, crowded world goes on breathing damp and green under its single roof, patiently, perfectly in balance.
