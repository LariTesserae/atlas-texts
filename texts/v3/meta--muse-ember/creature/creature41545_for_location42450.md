From far below, the knot does not seem to move at all.

It is only green against white, dense and still, too small for the height it occupies. Then, after a long looking, a patch of it changes. Not a leaf turning — the light does not do that here, and nothing turns quickly — but a slow tide of color moving across a broad flank, green deepening to amber and then easing back again, as if something large were breathing under the surface of the grove itself.

It is a tender, risen from the Root Quarter and standing shoulder-deep in the living walls.

They are the town's principal inhabitants, and the town is largely made of them. Dog-sized, low-bellied, long-backed, with short heavy limbs and a head carried down and forward. Their skin is waxy and overlapping, laid in great overlapping scales like sepals, warm to the touch and faintly sweet where a leaf has been bruised against it. Their feet are wide and muffled, for there is no mud to sink into, only powder-fine mineral dust that accepts a print and then slumps it closed behind them.

They do not look at you. Their eyes are small and set low, unhurried, and they have no use for a stare. They move at a walk that is almost a sway, one foot placed, the weight settled, the next foot placed, the body following a long moment later — as if the trunk and the head and the feet were separate decisions. They are always in contact with something. A flank pressed against a hollow trunk. A shoulder brushed past another tender in a path no wider than their bodies. A head lowered to a leaf edge, mouth working at it for minutes, for an hour, for as long as a leaf takes to give.

That eating is their work. A house here is not built; it is grown and then eaten into shape, simultaneously, for years. A thick trunk thickens from within and without, laying down new springy wood, and a tender chews the inside of it, bite by bite, scraping a room out of the living wall while the wall itself grows thicker around the room. When the tender rests, its dimming flank against the curved inner wall, the trunk continues to condense its sap into more trunk. When it wakes, it eats again. Over a decade a chamber is opened, widened, smoothed by bodies, abandoned when the trunk thickens past comfort, and eaten out again on the other side. The Root Quarter is that slow opening and closing, repeated a hundred times in miniature.

The leaf-doors are the same bargain in reverse. A single thick leaf hangs in the doorway, an eyelid. In the morning it lifts, curling back so slowly that the movement has no sound until it is finished. The tenders pass under it, shouldering past living walls, and in the heat of noon they settle in the narrow streets with their bodies docked flank to flank, conserving the warmth the stone gives back. At night they lie down in the dust of the Root Quarter itself, and their color fades to a sleep-pale grey, all of them together, a streetful of humped grey forms breathing so slowly that you must watch one flank for a full minute to be certain it is an animal and not another root.

They have no throats for calling and no need. Communication is the color tide, is the rubbing of a flank along a trunk to leave a scent-mark of sap, is the simple pressure of leaning. A young tender, bright and translucent, will press its whole side against an elder's, and the elder's color will answer a shade deeper, a slow wave running head to tail over both bodies. When they are content and satiated the tide runs deep green. When they are warm from the sun-heated rock it runs amber. When they are old, or carrying, or settling to sleep, it runs pale and stays pale. There is no alarm call because there is nothing here that moves fast enough to be alarmed by. Disturbed, a tender does not flee. It dims, it presses itself into the nearest wall, it becomes trunk-colored, and it waits, for as long as waiting takes.

Below, in the Under-Roots, the older ones do not come up at all.

The root cellars wind through cooler mineral soil — cooler only against the heat of the day, still warm — and there the eggs are kept. A tender descending with a slow, deliberate step will go down into a root hollow padded with spongy filament, and there lay, and there stay, pressing its body over the leathery eggs for a season or more while its own color dims and dims again. The egg-tenders are the old of the town. They do not graze. They turn the eggs by the pressure of a flank, a quarter-turn at a time, hours apart. They transfer warmth. They leave the scent of their own sap on the shell. Nothing hatches quickly. An egg here is a slow thickening, like a leaf-house forming a room, like a branch becoming a beam.

When a hatchling finally emerges it is soft, over-bright, almost translucent, and it does not walk so much as adhere, staying tucked under the flank of its tender in the narrow root ways until its own skin thickens enough to bear the dust. It learns the town by following the pressure of the bodies ahead of it — this way is a house, this narrowing is a door that will open in the morning, this warm platform is for resting, this root hollow is for sleeping pale. It begins by nibbling the spongy mats at its feet, the dense velvet of small spheres and filaments that carpets the ground between the trunks. Those mats are alive, too, and they are the town's shared pasture and floor and scent-pad, giving underfoot and holding again, releasing a faint sweet dust when grazed. There is no rot here, so there is no other smell.

What dies here does not rot either. An old tender in the Under-Roots will cease, gradually, to change color at all. Its breathing, already a matter of long inhalations, will slow past seeing. Its waxy skin will desiccate where it lies, becoming a crisp husk against the root wall. For a long while others will lie against that husk, taking the shape of it, and then, over years, it becomes mineral dust, and the roots reclaim it, and the sap that was in it — for all living things here carry a form of the plants' enclosed amber fluid in their own fat and skin — is taken back into the closed cycle. No graveyard. The dead become a wall, then a room, then soil again.

Above the tenders, the town continues in layers that never touch the ground.

On the vertical faces of every house, day and night, the wall-runners flow. Beetle-like, dark and shiny, in numbers that at first look like a stain and then resolve into thousands of individual legs. They run over the trunks and leaf-plates in slow sheets, pausing in cracks, tapping antennae against the living wood in a rhythm you feel more than hear through your feet. They farm dust-mites in those cracks, tiny pale things that graze on mineral husk and old leaf-scale, and the mites in turn are grazed by the runners, and the runners clean the house as they go, eating the crisp dead scale that would otherwise pile in the doorways. At night the whole sheet settles into the cracks and goes still and shiny and invisible, pretending, in their way, to be part of the house. They are the reason the walls are so clean, so smooth to a tender's flank.

Higher still, in the dusk of its own shade, is the Canopy Quarter, and its inhabitants never walk the Root streets at all.

The downy gliders slip from platform to platform without a wingbeat. They do not beat; they spread, they fall on a long shallow angle through the warm still air, they land without a sound on interlaced branch and leaf, and they fold themselves into the warm crook where a branch leaves a trunk. Their bodies are made of down that traps the faint mineral vapour rising all day from the deep rock, and they feed in the canopy shade on the fine filaments that grow on the undersides of the plates and on the dust-mite herds the wall-runners drive upward. They are solitary in their perches but never alone — the canopy is layered five and six leaves deep, and each layer has its own tenants, each one in contact with a leaf.

And on a single leaf, in the high bright light, there is a whole town of its own. The leaf-nibblers, no bigger than your thumb, are born on a leaf, live their whole lives on that leaf, mate there, lay there, and die there, their husks accumulating in the leaf's folded veins for the next generation to graze among. A leaf here is enormous, leathery, warm and springy, layered over its neighbors, and to a nibbler it is a continent with weather — the sun spreading its plates wider at noon, the vapour rising stronger in a long season, the tender's flank occasionally brushing the edge of the world and shading it for an hour. They communicate by the pressure of their legs on the leaf surface, a small tapping that travels through the plate to other nibblers, to the wall-runners above it, to the glider folded in the crook below. Everything is touching everything else, all the way up.

At the boundary, in the ring of white spires, live the founders and the slowest of all.

The burrowers are grey, armored, blunt-jawed, and they eat stone. Paired in their chambers hollowed at the base of the spires, two to a chamber, they work the fresh fracture with their jaws for decades, widening a hollow by an imperceptible amount each season, their entrances worn smooth only by the passage of their own broad bodies. They emerge rarely, to graze the root mats at the foot of the spires, and to leave their flank-marks on the living wall just inside, where the town begins. Their stone-dust, fine and dry, sifts down into the powder the tenders walk in, and the mineral vapour rising from the deep warm rock passes over their armor all day. They are the boundary made flesh: where the rock ends and the town begins, the burrowers have made a door by eating.

The plants are inhabitants too, and they behave like it. A root-bridge is a root that has arched over a hollow and been packed flat by a generation of feet until it holds. A roof-plate is a leaf that has spread a little wider each noon because the tenders resting beneath it keep it warm, and because their bodies, docked in the shade, leave their scent on its underside. A trunk is a house because it has been eaten into one. The grove does not rustle, because the leaves are thick and slide rather than shiver, and the air is still. In the morning the leaf-doors open. In the heat the plates spread. At night the doors close over the sleeping tenders, the gliders fold, the wall-runners settle, the nibblers draw into a vein, and the burrowers press their armor to their stone. The rock ticks as it cools. The vapour rises. The plants condense a little more sap out of light and air and dust, and store it, and thicken.

No one here drinks. No one here hurries. No one here is ever out of touch — not of a wall, not of a leaf, not of another body, not of the warm stone under everything. A season is long enough for a branch to become a beam and for an egg to soften toward hatching and for a young tender's flank to deepen from bright to green. A decade is long enough for a street to become a house and a house to become a street, the same continuous tissue doing both, eaten and grown at once.

That is who lives in the green knot: a stacked, silent population each one shaping the next by the simple facts of its body — eating, leaning, resting, laying, sleeping pale in the dust — until the whole bowl of the peak is filled, wall to wall and layer to layer, with a town that is not built at all, but grown, tended, chewed, woven, and inhabited, leaf by leaf, wall by wall, flank by flank, into a place that will still be thickening long after the peak has learned to be round.
