The first things that live here live in the wet.

Moss over every stone, and underfoot on the causeway slabs where it makes the stone give a little. It drinks the mist before it can drip, holds the sheen the cliff makes on the rock face. It is the softest thing in a place of sharp rock. In the hollows it thickens to a mat, in the channels it lines the stone, in the pools it crowds the edge with the water plants, leaf upon leaf floating and rooted, no fish to browse them, no frog to sit among them, so they mat and re-mat year on year and are thinned only by hand.

Around the pools and between them the meadow lives in patches because the ground is patchy under the water that is always near the surface. A dry hummock — dry only by comparison — carries knee-high grass, each blade heavy and shining. A shallow hollow carries ferns, unfurling slowly, never opened by anything but their own weight of water. Along the straight channels the broad-leaved shrubs stand, leaves too wet to rustle in the still air, flowers mostly pale and unscented. The grains in the furrowed fields are of the same kind: wind-spread, water-spread, spore-bearing in their way. They do not need a scent, or a bee, or a bird to carry what they make. There is no bee. There is no bird over the meadow, no fly to your face, no beetle under a stone. The green is complete and the animal life under it is not there.

That is the ecology that the people live inside, and by now they are part of it rather than above it.

They are the only animals on the shelf.

You see them at walking pace on the basalt causeways, wide enough for two, always damp, their bare feet leaving prints that fill at once with level water. Their skin stays damp without sweating, condensation beading along the walls of their houses in the morning and along their arms when they stand in the mist. The air is thin and warm and close, and they breathe slow, a shallow rise of the chest, accustomed to the height in a way your chest is not on the climb. They do not run on wet stone. They speak low, in the unhurried register of a place where a sound carries over the water and there is nothing to shout against. Their dress is woven fiber, loose tunics and skirts and shawls, hung inside the stone-and-timber houses to stay cool and never quite dry. Wood that never dries takes on their houses the same dark polish it takes on their tool handles. Thatch is meadow grass, repaired patch by patch before it can fail, because nothing here is allowed to fail.

They live far apart by any town standard — fifty meters of wet green to the next household, a hundred to the next — and that distance is itself a habit. A visit is a deliberate walk: across a causeway, over a small arched bridge set stone by stone, past an empty furrowed field or a pool that shines between the grass. The plaza is the only place they gather as a whole, and even there they do not crowd. The central basin is always full to the same mark, water plashing softly from its pipe, and the day's work often begins with someone standing there a moment to read the mark, the way you would read a familiar face. It has not moved in living memory. Neither have they, in the larger sense: no new quarter is built, no field is cleared beyond its terrace, no house is raised higher. A house is kept. When a stone is lost to the drip it is replaced with stone cut fresh from the weeping face itself, carried up the hand-cut steps, so the walls look perpetually new and perpetually the same.

Their lives are organized around the things that have to be done by hand because nothing else will do them.

In the morning, while the fruit trees in the gardens are still beaded with condensation, the pollinators go out. They are young people mostly, and some elders with the patience for it, carrying small brushes of soft fiber. Tree to tree, flower to flower, a touch and a pause. The trees are tended clones, their trunks polished dark, their branches low and gnarled from producing only what a brush can set. Without the brush there would be no fruit, and the town has learned to build its calendar not by seasons — there is hardly a season, only the same warm, even light — but by the rounds: pollination, then channels, then thatch, then weaving. A child learns the brush before he learns much else, learns to tell a flower that has been done from one that has not, learns to move slowly enough not to bruise a petal that is already saturated.

Other rounds keep the water at its level. Channel-tenders walk the straight lines cut across the curve of the land with stone spades, redigging where the moss has thickened, clearing the clay pipes that run under the causeways, checking the low retaining walls that terrace the meadows into levels that hold water constant. They carry away the water plants from the pool edges in woven baskets, the shoots for the pot and the rest for the root pits, because no animal will prune the pools and the pools, left alone, would close over. Stone-cutters go down — hand over hand down the slick chipped steps — and cut more of the cliff that is already giving its stone away, and bring it back up for a wall or a bridge or a basin edge. Weavers sit in the open workshops or under the eaves, mending cloth that no insect will eat and no moth will find, the cloth staying cool against the skin all day. Potters dig clay from the margins of the pools and fire it in the small sheltered kilns, where damp wood makes a low, steaming heat and the food is steamed more often than roasted.

They eat what the terraces give them without any animal but themselves in the chain. Root crops from the saturated levels, boiled to softness in clay pots. Greens cut from the hollows and the pool edges. Grains from the dry hummocks, ground on stone and made into porridge and cakes. Fruit, hand-set, eaten fresh and stored in the communal storehouse in cool damp urns. No milk, no eggs, no meat, no honey. No dog in the yard to beg, no chicken scratching, no livestock that would need dry bedding it could never have and would trample the levels that hold the water. Their bodies are made from starch and green and fruit, lean and slow, fed also by the ferment that works in the buried pits and in their own gut, a sour, plant-born chemistry that makes the roots give up more than they appear to hold. With no biting flies, no mosquitoes over the pools, no predators in the sharp gullies, sickness comes rarely and in the same mild forms, and a person can live a long time at that walking pace. Children are born into the damp houses, learn the wet stone underfoot before they learn any trade, and grow up without ever seeing the basin rise or fall, the cliff weep in a new place, the meadow turn brown. The mark is the same. The work is the same. The mist returns to the same hollows in the evening.

What lives here, then, is not a balance of many kinds. It is a balance of few kinds, held steady. Moss and fern and grass and shrub and water plant, each occupying its patch of wet ground and never eaten down. Cultivated root and grain and fruit, each in its level of water, each dependent on a human hand for its continuation. And the people themselves, spread far across the green table in their stone-based, timber-crowned houses, moving on their causeways from pool to field to plaza, speaking low, stepping carefully, keeping the channels level and the bridges true and the thatch whole.

From the plaza looking west you can see them all at once in pattern: lines and pools and roofs scattered far apart, a figure with a basket on a bridge, a figure with a brush under a tree, the ridges sharp and young behind it all, the high plain unbroken to the other side. Nothing rushes. The water keeps finding its way out of the rock, the plants keep taking it in, the people keep tending the space between the two, and the shelf stays exactly where it has made itself — young in its stone, old and unchanging in its habits, quiet enough that you can hear your own footstep on wet slab and a drop falling into a basin, and then the faint thread of water falling far away off the edge, day after day, the same.
