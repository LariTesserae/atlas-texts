There is almost no one here, and that almost is everything.

If you are looking for inhabitants in the usual sense — the things that move, call, hunt, nest — the answer is a blank. Nothing swims. Nothing flies. Nothing crawls. No fish turn in the basins, no birds cut through the rain, no insects stitch the surface film, no mammal ever hauled itself onto one of the two stones that briefly break the water. The water is too cold, too dark, and by a chemistry of granite and peat and long glacial time too barren for gills and lungs and blood. It will not support a heart. But it is precisely rich for roots, laden with everything the rock has leached out over ages, and it is for that reason that the only ones who live here at all live here completely.

They do not live everywhere. That is the first thing to understand about their population. Most of this drowned plateau is uninhabited in any living sense — vast, open plains of silt and bare rock, a drift of gravel, a single dark boulder sitting in the current, distances you can swim and swim through and still be in nothing. Then, abruptly, a grove. Then nothing again for miles. Where they take hold they take hold entirely, as if the suitable slope, the settled silt, the place where the current slows just enough, has been found and then filled to its limit. A grove is not a meadow in the way a dry country knows meadows. It is a place. A town. You could get lost inside one and the current would carry its scent — green, cold, chemical — out into the empty water between, so that even the emptiness smells faintly of where life is clustered.

Look first at the moss, because everything else seems to begin with it. It carpets the slopes hand's breadth thick, in blacks and deep olive, and where the little light touches it, a pale, almost white green. It knows where a slope faces. It has filled the roundings of the granite so thoroughly that the stone itself is now only a suggestion beneath it, furred with slime and moss to the lip of old walls, draped over peat pockets, over the old riverbeds. It does not rise so much as spread, a slow, patient skin over the country. In the deeper troughs where the temperature drops another degree it thins to pale threads, barely there, still holding.

Above and among it rise the ribbons. They are kelp-like, but they do not grow upward in the kelp's way; they stream sideways and up together, in slow banners that fold and refold in the current, as if the water were combing them. In the narrows where the current puts its shoulder down and booms, a low note you feel in your teeth, they move as a single mass, stems rubbing, leaves like wet paper — the only rustling in this place, since there are no other sounds than water on stone, water on plant, water on itself.

There are fern-things grown too large for their drowned world. Their fronds are translucent, their stems corded and woody, reaching always for a surface they will never reach, forever arching into black water. They have no flowers to open. There has never been a pollinator here. Nothing was ever meant to be carried from one to another by wing or mouth. They spread the way things spread in a place with no visitors: by fragmentation, by root and creeping stem, by clouds of fine spores that hang in the water like dust in a sunbeam and settle miles away on a suitable slope, and there take hold again.

There are lives among them that are not fixed at all. Root-mats torn loose from the amber peat of the old high moorland drift slowly in the deep basins, still growing, still green, turning end over end in the current like lazy animals — except there are no animals, and this is the closest thing to wandering the place allows. When the current stirs them the water clouds briefly the colour of weak tea, smells of stone and iron and rotting roots. They ground in a new basin, take hold, and become a new grove. It is how the population moves: not by walking, but by being carried, finger's breadth by finger's breadth of season and melt, until silt finds stone and plant finds silt.

Anchored to stone in other places are the bulb-like growths, round and patient, that open and close with the pulse of the water, taking in dissolved minerals. They do not photosynthesise in any ordinary way in this lightless place; there is not enough light to bother with. They absorb. They store. When the current is strong they glow faintly, a phosphorescent pallor along their edges in the blackest water, as if each grove were making its own ghost-light and lending it to its neighbours. In the aurora's wash — that faint green light remembered from far above, shining down through cloud and rain and metres of black water — the groves become visible at great distance, not green so much as luminous, their edges picked out, floating in the black like low clouds in a night sky. Then the aurora passes, and they are gone again, though you can still hear them moving.

And there are trees, or the ghosts of trees. Pale trunks from the old high forest, sodden and banded, stand upright on the drowned ridges long after the forest that made them is gone. They are dead, but they are inhabited. Climbing mosses have taken them for scaffolding, and vines hang from their branches like hair in the current. A grove built around a ghost tree has a shape other groves do not — vertical, skeletal, a place to be lost in. The plants have made architecture of everything left to them, including the remains of a previous world tree-life that did not survive the flood.

Untouched, ungrazed, untrampled — no mouth has ever cropped them, no foot has ever crushed a frond — they have become unearthly in their lushness. That is the paradox of the inhabitants here: they live in abundance because nothing eats them, and they live in starvation because nothing feeds them but the rock. Lush and starved at once. Slow beyond any animal measure. They do not hurry to bloom or ripen. They persist.

The only other evidence of habitation is not alive, and it is sparse as the groves. On the submerged knolls where the water is shallowest and the silt thinnest, small dry-stone enclosures stand knee-high, roofless, moss to the very lip. In the old river mouths there are V-shaped fish weirs, converging walls of unworked granite leading to an empty pit, built with hand-tools only — chipped stone blades, wooden stakes driven into peat, nets of woven plant fibre now rotted to lace. There are cairns on crests with nothing under them but silt. Hearth-stones blackened with cold fire. A few low, rectangular huts with doorways facing away from the current, their floors still strewn with flakes of worked stone and the split ends of sodden wood, as if someone had set down a tool and walked out of the water and never come back.

No smoke. No footprints that would hold. No bones. Whoever laid those stones came up to this high, cold country expecting the flood to bring life with it, and waited in their walls for a fish harvest that never rose to meet them. They are gone now, and their works are only shells, the way the ghost trees are shells, taken over and held up by the plants that are the true residents.

So this is who lives in the sea at altitude: no people, no beasts, only the green ones, in their isolated, luminous towns among the drowned ridges, listening to the water boom in the narrows and sigh over the silt flats, sorting pebbles one by one, moving pebble-children of the current from basin to basin. They breathe with the pulse of the flood, they glow when it runs strong, they drift when it loosens them, they root when it sets them down. Everything in the place is in its own state, weathered into balance, and everything is in motion — and the only motion that is alive is theirs, patient and vast and almost entirely alone.
