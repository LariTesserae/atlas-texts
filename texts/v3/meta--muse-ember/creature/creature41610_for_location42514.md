If you come looking for the population of the Verdfoundry, you will at first miss it, because most of what lives there does not answer to the name. It does not arrive. It does not leave. It does not, in any usual way, move.

By volume, by mass, by living presence, the Foundry is plant — but plant as resident, not as scenery. The inhabitants are the substrate itself, layered seven and nine levels deep, near-touching as a condition of life. At root level the mats go root-tile to root-tile, repairing the pavement they lie on. At ground level the factory-trees stand hollow and bolted, their trunks widened into houses, their upper floors inhabited, their lower floors given over to husk storage. At factory level the kiln-trees press dry husk into brick and have never given off heat, because heat is a waste the green cannot afford. At dwelling and canopy level the aerial forests are wired together by vine-bridges and raked into flat solar sheets. At sky-loom level and in the twilight bridges high above, the vines are only silhouettes against the white, and the things that tend them are thin and tall and quiet almost beyond notice.

These are not species in the way a forest has species. They are families of work. The loom-vines are fed through frames and woven into cable, insulation, clothing, load-bearing cloth — they consent to this the way a tree consents to weather, by continuing. The electrolyte vines line the dry drainage channels, pruning themselves back from the root-tile in a continuous silent process. The repair-mats creep, not to decorate, but to mend. The root-wires thread between foundation stones and hum at a frequency you feel in the soles of your feet. The root-rail trams glide an inch above their woven tracks, their sides furred with lichen that is really a thin factory, and under them, another level down, the whole continent is holding itself together with cable that was once alive.

The green eats light, current, and mineral dust. The built eats the green. The green eats the built. That politeness is the whole ecology, and it has only two trophic levels, which are eating each other.

Its great animals are the pruner rigs. They are not animals, and this is not a euphemism. Slow, jointed, many-limbed machines travel the branch-rails through the canopy, trimming, thinning, grafting, moving at walking speed or slower. The plants corrode them slowly with acid-dry secretions, absorb their metal into leaf and trunk, and rebuild them as part of a larger, slower body. A pruner in its first century is steel and intention. In its third it is half tree. In its fifth it walks — if walking is the word — only because the tree it has become still remembers the shape of a machine. To live among them is to be towered over by creatures that are dying into the architecture they maintain. No one rides them. No one owns them. The humans who follow them with bells carry no tools that would cut them; they carry only small bells, not for signaling, but because after weeks in the Foundry a human ear begins to ache for proof that the world is still physical. The bell rings, startlingly clear, and then the green takes the sound back and finishes it.

The humans are, by mass, negligible. By presence, they are unmistakable, because they are the only free-moving animals for kilometers at a time, and because their quiet is a different kind of quiet from the green's.

They live in the upper floors of swallowed factory halls and in the hollow trunks bolted with industrial ribs, where leaf-glass windows open and close with the light. They wear coats always. The temperature never rises out of that cool, dry range, the light never settles into warmth, and sweat never quite forms. Their mouths are always a little dusty. They do not drink — there is nothing to drink, and no one in living memory has ever known otherwise — they chew dry husk and pollen-wool and the mineral-rich dusts that settle in the hollows, and they reclaim what little their own bodies make the way the leaves reclaim the dust the plants exhale. Their lips crack, their eyes do not water, their voices crackle if they try to speak above the hush. A lifetime of this leaves them with an economy of movement that looks, to a newcomer, like restraint: steps placed on root-tile so they do not puff the silica pans, hands held still while a vine is fed through a loom, a whole guild pausing when a spore-storm darkens the light an hour before it falls.

They are organized the way the place is organized: by level and by work. Root-level tenders live down among the foundation stones, listening for the hum in the wires through their feet. Ground-level weavers work the guild-halls of the loom-districts, hands dry and green-stained, feeding vines through frames into cable and cloth. Factory-level dwellers keep the husk stores and the pressed bricks. Canopy pruners walk the branch-rails with their rigs, bells at their wrists, looking — in the bright, flickered light — like part of the machine's shadow. Sky-loom workers keep to the highest sheets, where the flowering wave arrives last and where distance contracts after a pruning. No one is born to a level, exactly, but you grow into the one your family has tended, the way a vine grows into a loom. Children are raised in hollow trunks, learning early to listen for their own step a half-second before it lands, and to distinguish the tick of a loom — once, like a distant clock — from the ticking of a cooling trunk.

They do not age the way you might expect, because nothing here ages in a straight line. A worker who tended a hall ten years ago may still be tending it, though the name on the door has changed and the street that leads to the hall has re-emerged a block over through the root-mat. When a person dies, they do not rot. There is no rot. They desiccate, stiffen, and are composted in place, like a plant shattering into nutrient ash. Their rust, their tools, their coats, their names are woven back into wall. This is not treated as sorrowful. It is the understood cycle: everything built will be composted, everything composted will be built again. The ancestors are not remembered so much as inhabited.

And then, in a place of such overwhelming, rooted life, there are the kept things — the true fauna, vanishingly rare, a rumor maintained in a sealed vat or on a roof.

In one of the old research trunks a colony of brass-blue pollinator beetles lives in dry amber gel. They are the only insects in the Foundry, and they are not wild. Keepers release them for a few hours a year, when the machines stutter and the flowering wave stalls, to do the work no loom-vine can do alone. For those hours the air, which never holds birdsong, holds a different, brighter hum — beetles against leaf-glass, brass against green — and everyone in the district comes to watch from a distance, dusty-mouthed and bell-silent, because to see an animal move under its own power is still, after centuries, an event.

On the roof-gardens the glass-sheep graze. They are stationary, plant-topped grazers bred small and woolly for the pollen-felt hats of the pruners, translucent at the edges in the white light, their wool harvested dry. Everyone who lives near them knows them by name. If you ask two neighbors you may get two lists, and both will be correct in the way the door-names are correct; the names have shifted with the season, and the sheep do not mind. They do not so much walk as relocate, by slow degrees, along the planted roofs, and a pruner will climb up after them with a bell, not to drive them, but to announce that he is there.

In a window in the Pillar District a cat sleeps on a warm generator housing. Everyone knows the cat. The cat is an event. It is the only cat anyone can name, possibly the only mammal besides the humans left on the continent, and its sleeping place draws a congregation the way a loom draws a weaver: people will pause in the corridor, coat still necessary, breath shallow, just to see the one warm, soft, unnecessary animal curled against the machine's heat. No one feeds it in any way anyone will describe. No one moves it. It sleeps through the dimming of the white to grey-green that stands for night, and wakes, and sleeps again, and its presence is taken as proof that the closed system has not yet corrected itself into perfection.

There are also inhabitants you will not be told about, because the Foundry does not distinguish them clearly. Shadows fall from buildings that have not yet been grown; you learn to live in them the way you learn to live in a draft. Some plants grow, unmistakably, into the shape of a demolished factory, complete with chimney-stems, and the gardeners leave them standing for a season before harvesting them for parts — they are lived in, briefly, by no one, and remembered by everyone. The maps woven into the root-tile along the main avenues update themselves, usually in the wrong direction, and erase themselves in the night. Distance contracts when a pruning is done and expands when the flowering wave passes. If you walk these streets long enough, you will meet people who greet you as if you have always known them, because in the looped causality of the place, you have.

To live in the Verdfoundry, then, is not to be a species occupying a habitat. It is to be a phase in the metabolism: rooted, woven, pruned, composted, rebuilt. The trees are the city. The city is the trees. The machines are halfway to being trees, the humans are halfway to being ash, the cat is wholly anomalous and therefore wholly cherished, the sheep are known by name, the beetles are kept in amber and released like a thought. Everything that is built here will be composted. Everything composted will be built again. And in between, endlessly, quietly, in cool bright air, the inhabitants grow — not beside the Foundry, but as it.
