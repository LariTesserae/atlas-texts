What lives here does not at first appear to live at all.

If you stand still long enough to let the darkness settle around you, you will think you are standing on a floor and under a ceiling and between walls, and that none of them are doing anything. The drip you hear will seem to come from nowhere and fall on nothing. The glows will seem like lights left on. And then your feet will feel it first — a slow, heavy thump travelling up through the soles, through the bones, a second before anything reaches your ears. The floor has moved. It has squeezed.

That is the first of them, and the largest by reach, if not by height. The ground is not ground. It is a weave of them, root upon root, stem flattened upon stem, pulp pressed into bark, all of it living and all of it near-touching. They are not rooted in soil the way a tree in a plain is rooted. Soil is only a thin skin that sometimes collects between them and is taken up again before it can be seen. They are rooted in each other.

They live by taking stone apart. Along the weave, in pale green points of light in the roots underfoot, are the miners. Their root-tips are blunt and acid-wet and they grow directly into the young basalt where the earth has broken through in sharp, glassy plates. They do not weather the rock by water or by cold. They drink it. They secrete, they hold the stone against themselves, they dissolve it locally and draw the minerals up in solution. Above them the weave works as a pump. You can feel it if you press your weight down and stay: a wave of contraction passes laterally, not fast, not rhythmical like a heartbeat, but immense and peristaltic, the whole mat tightening and releasing over minutes. In that wave, fluid moves. The root-plant is not one plant. It is a pumping tissue made of thousands of grafted individuals, each one only a segment of root and a short length of stem braided into a cable, each one spliced seamlessly to the next where they have grown together. There is no seam to find because the splice has been melted smooth by living growth. To pull one out would be to tear the floor.

Where the weave thickens, it lifts. The platforms and arches are the second kind of life, though they shade into the first without any border you could point to. These are trunk-plants, jointed, collared, fused into vaults and leaning towers that bear weight the way a many-legged animal would bear weight, except they never step. Their work is structural. They hold the hanging storey off the floor and hold the canopy up over the hanging storey, creating the low, closed rooms that make the place feel like an interior. They breathe very little. Their leaves are few and small compared to their mass. Most of their surface is bark made thick with insulation, wrapping cold pipes that run inside them.

For the pipes are part of the life too. Look along a trunk and you will see them: tubes running in straight lines, too regular to be vines, crossing over and under one another with a plumbing logic. Inside, sap moves under pressure, up and then down, in a circulation that has lost its beginning. These tube-plants have given up leaves almost entirely. They are mostly conduit. Their walls thicken and thin in sequence, driving the fluid forward. At their nodes they swell. And then they are no longer only tubes.

The swollen nodes are the most animal-like thing here, and the least animal. Round, smooth bulbs lashed to the main stems, faintly glowing amber at waist height, they turn fluid slowly inside themselves, translucent organs that you can see working even in the dark. Inside them, minerals brought up from the rock by the root-miners are separated, refined, stored as pure things — glass-clear resin, metal-like crystals, concentrated salts — in hollow chambers. A bulb-plant lives its whole life as a vessel. It does not grow taller. It fills and empties in the same motion, re-dissolving what it has just made solid, re-separating what it has just dissolved. Its pulse is the pulse you feel in your palm if you touch it. It will pulse forever without finishing.

Between the arches, in every crotch and angle where two stems meet, are the fillers. Small shrubs, moss like felt on every surface, leaf-mats floating on every film of standing water. They are not decoration. They are insulation and sealing. The moss-plants coat the trunks and keep heat in and cold pipes from sweating. The leaf-mats take up the water that beads and hangs and drops a few centimetres from the weeping stones onto a leaf below, which takes it in and does not pass it on. There is no stream because these plants have made a stream unnecessary. Water rises through the weave, condenses in the still air, collects on the radiator-leaves above, and is drawn back down through the hanging curtains.

The hanging storey is almost entirely still. The curtains and lianas do not sway because there is no wind to sway them and because swaying would be wasteful. They hang in tubes and sheets, translucent in places, filled with clear fluid that slowly turns. Their petioles, where they join the canopy, flex as pistons — slow, immense pistons — driving fluid from chamber to chamber. If you watched one for an hour you would see no net movement, only a working. A section fills, a section empties. A fruit forms inside a sealed chamber, ferments, smells sweet and hot-iron in the dark, and never falls. No bird takes it, no animal gut carries it, no rot breaks it down from outside. The chamber that made it reabsorbs it through the same stem it came from, and makes it again.

Above all of this, sealing it, is the canopy. It is only a few metres above your head and it is also endless. These are the radiator-plants. Their leaves are broad and thin and they do not catch light — there is almost no light to catch. They pass the earth's warmth out into the dark air and keep it there. That is their entire life: to be a heat-exchange surface held at equilibrium. The bluish pinpricks in the knit-shut canopy are their indicator glows, holding steady. The heat comes up from below, from the young earth still giving up the heat of its making, and these leaves give it away to the fog they themselves have made. The fog does not drift because they do not let it. It condenses where it is and evaporates where it is. It is not weather. It is the breath of the canopy, held in place.

No one of these lives alone, and no one of them could. The root-miner without the pump would poison itself with its own acid. The pump without the tube would have nowhere to send fluid. The tube without the chamber would carry nothing to refine. The chamber without the radiator-leaf would overheat. The radiator-leaf without the moss-insulation would lose the heat it was made to keep. They have grafted, spliced, braided, and plumbed themselves into a single continuous tissue, but they have not become one organism. They remain many, differentiated by form and by work, each stopped at exactly the point where one more cell would have to be taken from a neighbour who needs it. Growth here is not absent. It is matched. A leaf secretes wax at the same rate its twin on the other side of the world dissolves it. A bulb fills at the same rate another empties. The water that beads on basalt is the water that was breathed out yesterday at the same spot.

And so none of them age in the way things age where there are animals to eat the dead and winds to scatter seed. Nothing falls and rots in the animal way. What is shed is taken back up through the same root it came from. A footprint in the root-mat does not stay because the weave closes over it, absorbed, not filled in. A droplet on a leaf swells and holds and swells and holds, taken in at last not by falling but by the leaf itself drinking it through its surface. They do not reproduce by travelling. They reproduce by replacement, in place, grafting a young segment onto an old one and reabsorbing an old segment elsewhere, so that the total mass never increases and never decreases.

They are, in every sense, an industry without a factory and without a maker. They mine the rock, pump the fluid, refine the minerals, store the products as bright, useless crystals, radiate the heat, hold the fog, turn the fluid in translucent chambers, and then undo all of it and begin again in the same order, in the same places, at the same slow pace. Their glows — pale green in the roots, amber in the stems, blue in the canopy — are not signals to anyone. They are the by-products of the slow electrical potentials that move through their tissues as they work, indicating, like lamps seen through wet glass, that a process is occurring and has occurred and will occur again.

They make nothing that leaves. Nothing is eaten, nothing is burned, nothing is shipped out beyond the edge of the plant-world, because there is no edge where plant stops and something else begins, only more plant going on past walking distance into the same warm darkness. The crystals stay in the bulbs. The resin stays in the tubes. The fermenting fruit stays in its sealed chamber. The heat stays in the fog. The water stays in the endless bead-and-drink cycle. It is a perfect closed loop, and therefore a perfect uselessness, and therefore a perfect completeness.

That is how they live: not fast, not young, not old, not single, not many in any countable way. A continent of plants that have divided all the work of the world among themselves — eating stone, building structure, carrying fluid, separating minerals, insulating pipes, radiating heat, holding water — and divided it so exactly that nothing progresses. Everything is active. Nothing changes. The canopy is sealed, the weave is saturated, the pumps are pulsing, the chambers are turning, the stones are weeping onto leaves that drink, and the whole vast low world breathes in and out in the same place, forever, industrious and silent and complete.
