If you stand long enough in one place — and here, standing is the natural tempo — you stop looking for inhabitants in the way you would look in an ordinary town. Nothing darts from cover. Nothing calls out from a tree. The first thing you understand is that whatever lives here has agreed, like the machines, not to hurry.

It begins as a skin.

The town itself is filmed over. In the canals the water greens most thickly in a halo around the lamps, where the algae have found both light and the slow fall of moths. Inside, on the paper left in drawers in the flooded office, a soft grey mould has made its slow continent. On the north faces of walls moss climbs in a tide-mark, imperceptible day to day, always a little higher in memory than it was. Lichen has found the rust on the iron cladding and settled there, pale and patient, turning a corrosive bloom into a protective crust. This is the first population: photosynthetic, fungal, microbial life that does not need to move at all. It feeds on damp and dimness and the faint warmth of engines, and it turns everything — brick, timber, steel, a coat hem by the door — into a usable surface. The town is not rotting here. It is being kept, and the keeping is alive.

On that skin, other things have come to stick.

Snails go about their silver trails across wet concrete even where the floor is flooded, grazing the algae off the legs of presses, off the submerged rails, up the lower walls of the sheds. Mussels cluster where the canals meet the factory floors, shells half-buried in silt, mouths open to the brown current, filtering it slowly clean. Tiny barnacles — a harbour animal, out of all sense in this inland freshwater — crust the ironwork of the machines themselves, the conveyor housings, the pump pipes, each one a small volcano fixed in place, opening and closing in water that never really moves. They have made a coast where there is none. Between the sweating pipes at head height, spiders have strung their webs, each thread beaded with condensation, each web laddered between joints and patched brackets. The spiders sit at the hub for hours at a time, round-bellied and still, descending only when a moth from the lamp blunders in and is taken, wet and quiet, into the architecture.

The water, when it is not film, is full of fish.

In the clear seepage where the quarry walls weep black-green, small bright perch hold station in the threads coming down the rock. They are the only true flashes of colour down here, a flick of gold in the coldest, cleanest water, turning against a current you can barely see. In the canals between the warehouses, carp turn slowly in the brown, full-to-the-brim water, huge and unhurried, mouths breaking the surface now and then to take air, their eyes reflecting the doubled lamps. In the flooded sheds and across the flooded office floors, eels make their processions. They do not so much swim as pour themselves forward, sliding between the legs of presses, over submerged desks and filing cabinets, across two centimetres of water over concrete, in a slick, continuous line. At your feet, walking through the main shed, you step around a shoal of fish crossing the floor, and they do not scatter.

Life follows the water in, and then it stays in the margins the water has made.

Frogs and toads are everywhere in the marshy lots and ditches where a building stood and the ground remembered water. The toads sit for days at the edge of a ponded yard, stone-coloured, barely distinguishable from the silt, blinking once an hour. The frogs are louder and more restless, inflated throats working at dusk until their chorus rises — the loudest conventional sound the town makes, and even that arrives muffled, as if the fog and the water have taken half of it away. They hunt the moths and the midges around the lamps, and the slow flies over the marsh, and they are hunted a little by the eels, who will rise for one in the shallows. The reedy tufts and the pale, stunted willows lean over these same margins, leaves always wet and heavy, never quite unfurling, their roots in water that never drops. Nettles grow up through the cracks of the square and sting the ankles of anything that passes too close, plant and frog and human alike.

Above, in the narrow strip of open air between the wet roofs and the bright wedge of fog at the rim, the air is crowded.

Moths come first, drawn to the only warm brightness there is. They crowd the tired amber lamps, wings browned with damp, falling dead into the puddles to feed the algae and the snails. Spiders take the living ones. Swallows and pigeons have taken the chimneys and the undersides of the gantry roofs by the hundred — mud nests softened and re-softened on the chimney pots, droppings white-washing the rust. They wheel and wheel in that low ceiling of air, never rising to the rim, never needing to, because there is food enough in the stillness: midges over the square, flies over the flooded lots. The pigeons prefer the pipes and ledges, cooing into a space that returns their voices softened, as if through cloth. In the dark mouths of the kilns, bats hang upside down in clusters, ears and folded wings, breath visible in the cold. At dusk they drop into the dim, navigating by the dripping from beams and the faint clicks of their own making, hunting what the lamps have missed.

On the ground, warm blood has found the few dry perches and learned to share them.

Ducks land on the shallow sheet of water standing in the square so gently they leave no ripple for long seconds, then waddle onto the wet stone of the steps, past the hedge and the gate, as if they were coming home. Rats run the drains and swim the canals with their noses high, whiskers beaded. Feral cats have claimed the driest inches of town — the top step above the waterline in the stone cottages, a windowsill now level with the street, the warm housing of a motor in the pump house. They sit for hours, paws tucked, breath pluming, watching eels pass through an office a foot from their noses. They do not strike often. There is always another frog, another fish in the cooling pool, another rat in the drain. In a place where everything is preserved by cold damp, a cat is a small, deliberate furnace, and it knows exactly where the warmth collects.

And people — slowest of all, rarest, most camouflaged.

There are not many. The rows were built for three times the number who live here now, and the empty lots between the factories are left to marsh because there is no one who needs them. Those who remain live upstairs. The ground floors of the oldest cottages at the low end have been given up to the water; the fish move through where a parlour was. The brick terraces above them are dry enough at the top, never dry at the hem. Coats hung by the door never quite dry. Boots by the door fill with cold seepage overnight and are put on wet in the morning. People move through the town at the speed of the conveyor — one bucket of aggregate an hour toward a silo that may already be full — because that is the speed at which anything can be done here without slipping on algae or tearing a wet sleeve. Voices are low, not from secrecy but because a shout off the quarry wall comes back dampened and strangers would hear it too clearly in the fog.

They keep the pump house turning, that flywheel making its minute-long circuit. They dig out a doorway that has filled with sediment, slowly, like a mouth, and know it will fill again by next year. They leave a chipped bowl of water and scraps on a dry step for the cats, and the cats accept it without gratitude. Children, if there are children to be seen, learn early not to run on the cobbles where the tram rails lie like canal edging, and to look down — for the shoal crossing the shed floor, for the toad that is not a stone, for the eel's procession across the office. Their skin is pale from the colourless light that only touches the chimney tops. Their breath shows all year.

No one species is dominant. The algae feed the snails, the snails feed the frogs, the moths feed the spiders and the swallows, the carp feed on what the town sheds, the eels feed on almost everything and are fed on by nothing much, the cats watch them all, and the people watch the water level in the square rise by a fingernail a week and do not disturb the ducks landing on it. The bats hang in the kilns above the banked furnace that breathes warm vapour and rains it back onto its own roof. The pigeons nest in the chimneys that were built to make smoke that has nowhere to go.

Stand in the square at evening, when the lamps are doubled in the standing water and the rim has disappeared entirely into mist, and the inhabitants declare themselves without moving much at all: a carp turning just under the reflection, a frog inflating in the ditch, a swallow settling on a pipe, a cat blinking from a dry step, an eel crossing the flooded floor behind you, a human in a coat that will never dry pulling it closer, breath showing, listening to the dripping from the beams — each one in balance, each one patient, each one having become, very slowly, exactly what this cool, dim, sunken bowl requires.
