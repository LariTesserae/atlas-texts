The first lives you are aware of are not the ones you can see.

They are under your feet. The root-decks that make the paths spring faintly, and something answers the spring with a soft pop, then another, below the woven floor. Schools of fish keep to the channels under the decks, in the dark flooded work where the stone continues down out of sight. They do not come up into the open. The water above them is too still, too exposed. They travel in the narrow, made waterways — under causeways, along the feet of retaining walls, through tunnels in the root-work spliced into the terraces — and they pop against the underside of the decking as they turn, as if the whole flooded architecture were hollow and breathing through them.

They are cold, pale, and made for the gloom. In water that numbs a human foot in minutes and never warms, they move slowly and without effort, and they do not hurry even when startled. A school will scatter with a soft explosion of bubbles and bodies brushing your legs, and reform a few paces on, in the same channel, going the same way. Some have lived there a long time. Nothing in the water changes enough to make them move on. The level is the same today as in a year. The current does not exist to carry them elsewhere.

Beneath the causeways, in the root-work itself, are the eels. You will almost never see one whole. A sliding length of grey will pass through the weave under a pillar, or ripple the black water at the edge of a basin and be gone, leaving the surface to close again at once. They live in the decking the way the decking lives on the stone — threaded through it, holding it, fed by what sinks into it. They eat what falls and what dies and what is left in the water by the things above, and because of them nothing down there rots into smell.

In the wide basins, where the water is waist-deep and still and the walls go down into darkness, the turtles surface. It is an event, because so little surface breaks. A dome rises, impossibly slowly, far off across open water, breaks the film, sits with eyes just above the line that never moves, and sinks again without haste. They are not many, and they are not young by any measure you would recognize. There is no season for them to hatch or hide from. The moss-line on the stone is the same above them every time they come up, in the same basin, after an interval that feels less like time than like a tide that has forgotten to be one.

On the shallows, the frogs are the fixed points of the sound. They keep to the same lily patches, to the same broad leaves, night after night, year after year. Each patch has its own pitch, tuned by the depth of the water and the size of the mat, and the frogs on it blink at the gloom with eyes adapted to almost nothing. They do not wander between patches. A frog that leaves its leaf would be lost in a place that offers no landmarks but repetition, and so it does not leave. It sits, it calls, it takes in the midges that rise from the undersides of the leaves, it lets its waste sink. When a leaf falls, the mat is the same mat tomorrow. The frog on it is the same frog, older, its throat working in the same place in the chorus that never pauses for breath.

Above the pools, where the paler leaves spread and interlock into a low vault, the density thickens again, but invisibly. The birds live in that vault and in the dark above it, passing overhead in flocks you hear more than see. Their wings are thick in the wet air and their calls cross and recross in lines that never vary. They follow the same invisible routes between the same vaults, over the same causeways, from one dark summit of leaves to another, at the limit of sight. From the ground they are motion and crying, a presence that explains why the air seems full even where nothing moves. They do not descend to the water. They do not nest where you can find them. They prune the canopy by their passage and they carry the dust and pollen of one vault to the next, and then they return, because there is nowhere else to return from that would still be this place.

Closer, under the leaves themselves, are the insects, and they are the most immediate proof that abundance here is not spread thin. Beetles and midges work the undersides in clouds so dense they darken the gloom locally, humming in layers that never thin out, and then end just as abruptly at the edge of a leaf, as if the leaf were a country they are not allowed to leave. Each species keeps to its plant, to its side of the leaf, to its height above the water. They are food for the frogs and the birds and for each other, and the work of eating is the work of keeping the leaves clean. Nothing grows within sight because everything that would grow is eaten, in place, before it can.

And the plants — the plants are not the setting for these lives. They are the bodies the other lives live in.

The root weave is a living wall and floor, holding stone to stone so the whole mountain cannot wash away, drinking from the flooded steps it helps to hold the water for. Moss and fern armour the stone against the water that has already finished it, so the water cannot take it any further. The broad water-leaves mat the surface in scattered patches and leave long stretches of open black water between them, and the patches do not spread. A plant grows exactly to the edge of the stone and no further, as if it remembers the plan the builders left. The tree-like growths where pillars once stood have wrapped their trunks around the stone and spliced their roots neatly into the terraces, and they stand far apart in the water, vaulting overhead where they can, keeping what little light there is from ever reaching the pools directly. They drink. They drip in the same pattern on the same leaves. They feed the water with fallen leaves that become mat in a night, and the mat is the same mat as yesterday.

In the root-decks, invisible, is the rest of the population that makes the loop close: the larvae and small burrowers in the weave, the algae shining in films down the walls, the microscopic life in the tea-colored channels that turns waste sinking from above into food for the roots that made the decks. It is a population without a face, and without pause. The chorus of frogs, the popping of fish, the hum of insects, the drip and the lap against the causeway legs — all of it is the sound of that loop working. Stone held by roots. Roots fed by water. Leaves feeding the water. Animals stirring and eating the dead before they can decay.

None of them wander. None of them are new. None of them, so far as the place allows, are ever entirely gone. A fish kept to its channel may be the grandchild of one that popped under the same deck a century ago, and there is no way to tell the difference, and no need. A bird on its line, a frog on its leaf, a turtle surfacing at the same far basin — they have arrived at the last form their lives will take, and they are staying there, submerged, planted, built, and humming in the dark at the top of the world, while the cloud-sea moves thousands of metres below and nothing falls.
