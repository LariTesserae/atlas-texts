If you stay still long enough, the sterility gives up.

Not all at once. It gives up in layers, starting with the things that do not look like things at all.

In the shallows where a pipe bank drips, and in the braided channels where the crust stays damp under a radiator fin, there is a film. It is not moss and it is not algae. It has no green in it. In the hotter water it is orange, then rust-black where the sulfur runs thick, in the cooler seep it is pale and almost clear, a fungus that spreads one cell thick across basalt and concrete and the lower rims of steel. It does not grow up. It grows *over*. It eats what the water carries down — dissolved copper, sulfur, iron leached from the glass — and it breathes heat the way a plant breathes light. When the afternoon sun drives the pool level down a finger's width, the mat cracks into polygons and holds its shape, waiting. When the warm rain comes at four and the pool refills, it un-cracks and resumes.

Everything else here starts with that mat.

The shrimp are the first animals you will actually see if you crouch at a pool edge and let the glare stop tearing at your eyes. Glass shrimp, as the description says, but not like any shrimp in a cold sea. They are translucent as thin ice, no longer than your thumb, and when a cloud of them flicks through the shallows they are mostly a disturbance in the water and a glitter of internal organs tinted orange from the mats they graze. They do not have eyes worth the name. In this light eyes are a liability — they burn, they blind, they give you nothing the white does not already give you. Instead each one trails a pair of sensing hairs that taste the water ahead of its mouth, and a whole-body sense of temperature that tells it when the bottom is getting too close to a simmer. They graze constantly, they molt, and the empty shells drift and settle and are eaten before they can whiten a pool bottom.

They are food, and that is their main importance. In a place with no leaves, the world must be built to be eaten efficiently, without waste chatter.

The blind scuttlers do the eating that the shrimp cannot. They walk the bottom on far too many legs — twelve, fourteen, the number varies by vent — with hard, etched shells that are white where the carbonate skins them and clear where it does not. They turn the sediment as they go, constantly, so the mud never compacts, so the water never quite settles. No tracks remain because as soon as a leg lifts a tongue of hot water fills the print and erases it. They eat shrimp, dead shrimp, living mats, each other when a pool shrinks and crowds them. They are silent because there is nothing here to call across. Sound carries badly in the thin, steam-thick air, and in water this hot and mineral a call would be a waste of energy besides. They communicate by touch, by chemical trails left in the churned mud, by the change in temperature when another body passes near.

Along the terraces — those stepped sinter rims that look like rice paddies poured in stone — the filterers live their whole lives in one place. Slim, worm-like, pale as cooked rice, they anchor in a crack and pulse open a fan of feathery gills, close it, pulse again, taking the bacterial soup that the scuttlers have stirred up. They have no head you can distinguish. From above they look like small white knots opening and closing in time, out of time with each other. When a vent nearby takes its turn to breathe and the pool briefly boils at the edges, they shut tight and wait, sealed in their own mineral tube, until the water cools a few degrees. They can wait days like that.

Deeper in, where the vent mounds open into a throat you cannot see into, there are the predators. They are rarely seen. They are large enough that you feel them before you see them — a slow displacement of warm water, a sudden absence of shrimp from a corner of the pool. Blind as the rest, longer-bodied, their shells thickened and etched like old pottery. They do not chase. They circle slowly in the deep part of a perched pool, feeling the water's temperature gradient with their whole bodies, and when a cloud of shrimp crosses the warmest layer they roll and take them in a sideways snap. They are apex only because the plateau is so chemically extreme that very few things can be large. Even they do not drink. No one on this plateau drinks. Drinking would kill them. The water is copper and salt and boiled mineral, near a simmer at this altitude. What they drink is the air.

On land, the same rule holds: life gathers where the machines make shade and damp.

Under the radiator banks — the artificial forests, rows of fins that throw long thin shadows west in the morning and almost none at noon — the ground is crowded to a degree that contradicts the emptiness between complexes. Heat crabs nest there by the thousands, packed in the warm junctions of pipes where condensation gathers and drips at a steady, refrigerator-hum pace. Their shells are white or glass-clear; pigment is a waste here, and white reflects heat back and keeps them from cooking in the sun that strikes the metal above them. They are small, palm-sized at most, with forelegs made for cleaning. Half their day is spent cleaning each other, picking fungal film and mineral crust from a neighbor's joints, from the soft membrane under the tail. They are entirely silent and entirely tactile. If you lift a plate, the crabs do not scatter. They shift inward, tighter.

The blind grazers are different. They are not crabs. They are lower, longer, almost slug-like but with a segmented, armored back that clicks faintly — the only click you will hear from them, and only if you are very close — when they settle. They eat the pale fungal films that grow where the pipes drip, and the mineral crust that grows in a perfect, edible bloom where hot water evaporates off steel. They follow the shade. That is their entire migration: when the sun moves, the thin violet shadow under a pipeway moves, and a tide of pale bodies moves with it, from plate to plate, dozens to a square meter, jostling without aggression, because aggression implies territory and territory here is only a few inches of damp. At night, when the machines wake for their cycle and shed their dawn plume of steam, the grazers are everywhere under the towers, their backs beaded with condensation they will absorb through their skin.

You will see their cast-off shells if you look. Under a long-lived heat exchanger the floor is layered with them, white as salt, undisturbed for years, because nothing here rots the way a forest floor rots. The plateau preserves. It encrusts. A crab that dies under a pipe will be slowly mineralized where it lies, its shell becoming part of the crust that some new channel will later cut through.

Above it all, in the updrafts, are the gliders.

They have no right to be here, which is how you know they belong. Wings so thin you see the sky through them, bodies reduced to almost nothing but wing and gut, they ride the steam columns all day in slow circles that never flap. They never land on the ground if they can help it — the ground is where their food is, and also where the scuttlers' cousins wait in the shallows to snap up anything that falls. They roost instead on the cooling towers when the steam is thick, on the high rims of reservoirs, on pipeways where the metal is warm but not scalding. From there they watch the pools.

A glider's hunt is a drop, not a chase. It will circle a perched pool for an hour, silent, then fold its wings a fraction and fall, skim the mirror surface, take a shrimp without breaking the surface tension more than a ripple, and rise again on the same thermal that lifted it. They eat only shrimp, and only from the surface, and so they do not compete with the scuttlers below or the predators in the deep. The plateau is large enough and sparse enough that even hunger has room.

All of it runs on heat from below and light from above that no one uses for photosynthesis. The mats eat sulfur and metals. The shrimp eat the mats. The scuttlers eat the shrimp and the dead. The grazers eat the fungi and the drip-crust, and are eaten by nothing in particular except when a glider drops to the ground — which it almost never does — or when the dusk brings out a different thing you have not seen yet because it only comes out when the condensation returns.

At dusk, for an hour, the place changes its mind.

The glare breaks. The pools, which have steamed all day, go mirror-calm and reflect a blue that the noon sky never managed. The machines take their night cycle, ticking as they cool, humming as they expand. The animals that have hidden through the bright hours — everything that could hide has hidden, everything that could not hide has gone still — come out one by one from under the plates to graze the mats in the cooling water, to drink the moisture out of the air, to clean themselves in the open. The crabs uncurl. The grazers move en masse to fresh drip-lines. The filterers along the terraces open wider, faster. The shrimp rise to the surface in thicker clouds. The gliders come down from the thermals to roost, wings folded tight, bodies steaming slightly in the cooling air.

There is no sound to mark this emergence. No song, no chirp, no mating call. Only the drip of a weeping rock, the tick of metal, the soft patter of the warm rain that may or may not fall, and sometimes the low sigh of a vent in the basin, felt in your teeth more than heard. The animals feel that sigh too, through their shells, through the water. It tells them a new column will rise soon, that the pool chemistry will shift, that a neighboring pool may become too hot to live in and they will need to move tonight.

They do move, constantly, between the warm and the wet. A pool dries to white polygons in a week; the scuttlers walk overland in the damp night to the next one, leaving no tracks. A vent rests for a month; the crabs thin out; the grazers follow the shade west. The city’s pipeways are animal highways because they carry both heat and water to places that would otherwise be bare crust. The cooling towers are cliffs. The reservoirs are ponds. The sorted yards of sulfur blocks are sunning rocks. Nothing here was designed for them, and nothing here excludes them. Both harvests continue because the plateau is open and old and has come to terms with everything.

That is who lives there: not a population, but a practice. Translucent, blind, silent, pale, encrusted, cleaning each other in the shade, grazing a film that eats poison, riding steam they did not make, circling pools they cannot drink from, moving as if the whole plateau were breathing through them — in through the vents at noon, out through the condensation at dawn, and back again at dusk when the light finally softens and the world, for a little while, looks almost gentle, and is still entirely itself.
