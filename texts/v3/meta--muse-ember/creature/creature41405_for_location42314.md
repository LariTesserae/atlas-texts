If you stand in the middle and wait for something to move, nothing does.

That is not quite an absence at first. Your eyes are still adjusting, and the dark is sorting itself out slowly — wall first, then floor, then the half-sunk stones with their rounded tops, then the empty space between them where there is nothing to separate. The warmth is on your skin the same as it was a minute ago, and the same as it will be in an hour, thicker at the ankles than you expected, or perhaps no thicker at all, only uninterrupted. The dust under your boots gives that soft crunch and settles again behind you. You listen for a return sound, for a rustle, for something that lives in a crack answering a footstep, and the silence answers by continuing.

It takes a while to understand that what lives here does not move in a way you can wait for. It does not fly across the dim disc of sky, it does not scurry, it does not call from the rim, it does not even rustle. The air is too still for that, and too even. A dry grass tuft in a pocket of silt stands straw-coloured and perfectly still, not because it is holding its breath but because there is nothing to bend it. A shrub does not turn a leaf toward the light, because the light is not coming from a direction in any useful sense anymore — it is diffused, grey-green, reflected off stone long before it reaches the floor, and it will be that same diffused light at noon and at dusk and in whatever passes for a season down here.

From where you stand you can see all of it at once. That is the strangeness of the population, if you can call it that. The hollow is twenty or thirty paces across, bounded on every side, and every living thing in it is visible from the middle, separated from the next by metres of bare, fine, calcareous dust. There are perhaps half a dozen things that could be called vegetation in the whole room of air and stone, and they are not clustered. They are far apart, as if distance itself were part of how they keep going.

You go to the nearest one first, because it is the largest. Against the base of a wall, where a little soil has gathered in a crack over a very long time of the wall wearing down grain by grain, a small shrub grows low. Its leaves are thick and leathery, grey on the underside, dull green on top where the dust has settled into the colour of everything else. The stem is woody and older-looking than its size would suggest, or perhaps only weathered-looking, which here is the same thing. It has not flowered. There are no buds for it to have missed, no berries, no fallen fruit under it. The roots go down into the fissure, not into the dust on the floor, searching wide and deep for whatever moisture the rock holds far below the still air. It does not drink in any way you can see. It simply persists in the crack, in the dim light and the pooled warmth, its tissues neither quite giving up nor quite doing more. From a little way off it could be mistaken for a stone with leaves; up close it is unmistakably a plant, and unmistakably not growing in any way you could measure with a ruler or a week or a year.

On the opposite wall, in another fissure, there is moss. There are two cushions of it, one larger than the other, both grey and crisp to the eye. They lie flat against the stone as if pressed there by their own weight. Moss is supposed to be a sign of damp, but there is no damp here — no pool, no seep, no wet stone, no mud for the dust to become. The moss is dry because dry is the condition it has met, unchanging, for as long as it has been there. It has not spread across the wall. It has not made a green carpet where it might. It occupies its crack, grey, dormant-seeming, and keeps the small hold it has on the mineral moisture in the rock beneath it. A dropped leaf lies under the shrub, and a dry twig lies where the grass tuft shed it long since. They lie where they fell and have not rotted into the dust, because rot in the ordinary wet way has nothing to work with here.

The grass is a single tuft, rooted in a pocket of silt a few paces from the moss, straw-coloured, thin blades held upright without any need to bend. It does not rustle when you pass, because the air does not move enough to ask it to. Near it, spreading its roots wide in the flat dust as if testing whether there is anything to find, is a low woody plant with small waxy leaves. The wax gives its leaves a faint sheen even in the muted light, a dull reflectiveness that separates them from the matte grey of the floor. Like the shrub, it is not lush and not dying. It has the look of a thing that has found an edge it can sit on: enough dim light not to starve, enough warmth not to freeze, enough vapour and deep stone moisture not to dry out completely, and not enough of anything — no water, no circulating air, no season reaching down past the rim — to allow more.

If you look for a long time at the stones, you find the rest of them. On the shaded faces of the flint nodules and broken limestone half-sunk in the dust, where direct sun never falls because direct sun never falls anywhere in here, lichen makes pale crusts. Green-grey, ochre, almost white. Ordinary rock lichens. They are the most at ease in the place, perhaps, because they ask for the least. They do not need soil, or a crack deep enough for roots, or a pocket of silt. Stone and dim, even light and still, warm air are enough for a crust to grow — if growing is the word for a pace so slow that the rare tick of a small stone settling somewhere in the wall on cooling is a faster event. You would have to return after years to be sure they had changed at all, and even then the change would be in the thickness of a crust by something you could not see without a lens.

None of it is spreading. None of it is feeding anything else, because there is nothing else to feed. There are no insects in the air to pollinate a flower if one remembered how to make them, no lizards on the warm stones though the stones are warm, no birds to carry a seed, no tracks in the dust, no burrow holes, no webs in the cracks, no droppings, no bones. The plants have the place entirely to themselves, and even they are barely using it. The distances between them are not gaps waiting to be filled. They are part of the equilibrium. A shrub in a crack, moss in a crack on the wall opposite, a grass in silt, a waxy-leaved plant in the open dust, lichens on the shaded side of stones — each holds its own small place, far from the next, and does not close the space.

That is how they live here, if living is not too active a word. They live on what the rock holds deep in its fissures, on vapour in air that does not circulate, on light that is thinned and reflected until it is almost the colour of stone itself. They do not turn over a leaf in a season because no season reaches them. They do not fall and rot and make soil in a cycle, because the one dead leaf and the one dry twig have lain undisturbed for longer than anything here can remember, and the dust is dust because the walls have worn down grain by grain and settled, and nothing has washed it away or turned it over since. To die would be quieter still, and to grow would require a change that the place, settled and scalloped and finished in its erosion, does not offer.

You are, for as long as you stay, the only animal thing that has ever been down here. Your breathing sounds loud at first, then absorbed by the soft walls and the fine floor, as the description of silence here said it would be. When you climb the ten steps back over the rounded lip into the flat light of the plain, you will leave no mark in the dust that does not settle again, no path, no cleared place, no artifact. The shrub will not have moved. The moss will still be grey and crisp. The grass will still stand still. The lichens will be a fraction thicker, perhaps, in a way no eye could test.

And the hollow will close behind you, empty except for those half-dozen persistent things, far apart in the warm, dark, dry room, continuing the way they were continuing when you came down into it — which was, for all practical purposes, not having begun to change at all.
