No one lives there.

That is not a figure of speech. You go looking for the inhabitants because the place was made for them, and the looking itself becomes the shape of the answer. Room after room, pressed cell against cell until there is no courtyard left and no street and no empty patch that is not already somebody's wall, the work is still waiting for hands. In the niches the tools are still where a hand last reached for them — hammerstones pitted, copper chisels thin as if an amateur had tried them once and set them down. Dust is in the cisterns. The jars set into the walls are empty. The hearths are cold, the kilns bricked up from the inside, their mouths sealed by sleeping chambers built over them in a later generation, and those chambers sealed in their turn.

No one has come back to finish the gesture.

It would be easier if something else had moved in. Nothing has. No birds circle the summit against that thin, high air. No insects rise from the warm pockets where the brick mass gives back the sun all night. No rats in the stores that are no longer stores, no lizards on the sunlit bricks, no root finding a crack to split, no green thing anywhere on the plain or on the Stack itself to mark a season. The air does not carry smell except for what already belongs to the building: fired earth, copper, old ash, dryness so complete your mouth feels lined with grit. No dew forms to soften it. No damp comes up from below to make the work rot or freeze or settle. The place is post-life in that literal, unadorned way — not haunted, not abandoned in the romantic sense of a door still swinging. It is finished.

The only thing that resembles growth is mineral, and it does not hurry to be mistaken for life.

In a few shaded cracks, where the light comes in as a sliver and does not move across the floor, there are pale whiskers and beaded films. Silica and salts exhaling out of the brick itself over immense time. They are dry to the touch, white-grey, colorless in the dimness, and they creep at the rate of stone. They do not unfurl. They do not fruit. They do not need water, and none is offered. Given a century they might join one film to another across a mortarless joint, sealing it again with a second firing that never happened. That is what the Stack does instead of living: it sweats mineral, very slowly, in the places the sun does not reach, and otherwise holds still.

Everything else has reached the same state. You learn it by returning, or by staying an hour and finding that staying changes nothing. The corners have been rounded by dry grit, not by crumbling. The stair treads are dished into shallow bowls by feet that are no longer there. Thresholds are concave. The faces of the bricks have been polished until the firing marks show like grain in wood. Nothing is about to fall. Nothing is actively drying out or swelling or freezing. The warm air in the enclosed rooms is the same warm air, the perpetual late-afternoon in the slivers of light is the same light. It does not get later here in any way a body can feel.

And yet, in that stillness, something is unmistakably present, and it is the closest thing to a resident the Stack will admit to.

You hear it before you see it, but hearing does not make it ordinary, and standing inside it does not make it a background. It is a held note, low and architectural, a roar up through the soles of your feet and a knife-thin whistle just behind your teeth. It does not rise and fall with weather. The current over the summit is hard and unvarying, from the same direction day after day, stripped of weather, and it is forced in through hundreds of kiln mouths and flues on the windward course — some no wider than a thumb, some large enough to crawl through — and out elsewhere, through the packed, hollow mass below. The mass answers as a single resonator. The large chambers boom. The narrow flues scream. Whole walls hum under your palm.

Move one step and the chord changes pitch. Wait, and it does not. There is no attack and no release. It is pressure, held. Speech comes back flattened and wet, as if swallowed by brick, so visitors learn to touch shoulders and read hands. And that, finally, is what lives there, if living is the word: not a creature, not a people, not a plant, but the note itself, taking up all the room that people left when they packed the Stack cell upon cell until it was saturated. It does not feed. It does not sleep. It does not grow, except as the mineral films grow, by accretion and by time. It is the sound a finished thing makes when there is nothing left in it to change.

From the plain below, which is featureless and still and offers nothing to hear, you can hear it first.
