Nothing on the Thren Table lives alone, and nothing lives quickly. That is the first thing you learn about its inhabitants — not their shapes, which take longer to resolve in 0.2 light, but their tempo. Everything here is paced to dampness, to warmth rising from below, to a migration that never has a season because there is no sun to mark one.

The base of it all is not an animal. It is stonebloom.

It does not grow so much as it spreads, in patient, flesh-colored mats over basalt, painted on in the exact ochres and blacks of the rock itself. Up close it is soft and slightly greasy, a fungal-lichen skin that thickens in damp hollows into a luminous carpet, thinning to almost nothing on the iron pavements between them. It eats nothing green. It is chemosynthetic, taking its energy from the chemical exhalations of the Table itself — sulfur, iron, the mineral freight carried inland in fog from the lowlands — and from the condensation that beads on stone and on its own felted surface. It is slow, and it is everywhere a square meter can be covered, and where it is, other things crowd in after it.

 threaded through it, in cracks and in the soft silt of basin floors, are the thread-grasses and thorn-scrub. The grasses have no blades to speak of, only a thousand pale hairs that comb fog out of the air and funnel it to a root that lives all year in wet stone. The scrub makes swollen, thorned stems, water reservoirs that can keep a hollow green-black through a long dry spell when the transient pools have sunk away. They do not feed kettles. They wait for the swarms.

Beneath and between both, running continent-wide in the drier interfluves, are the electrogenic mats. They are not one organism so much as a weather system of bacteria and fungi holding a charge. You do not see them. You feel them first — a prickling along the back of your neck on a still night, a faint crackle when your palm touches wet rock, the sudden pop of a small discharge in fog that lights a trackway for half a second. Stonebloom uses that field to signal stress and growth across distances no scent could reach. Smaller animals use it to navigate. The Table hums underneath everything, below hearing, and most of what lives here is tuned to listen to it.

The foundry-motes live in that hum.

They are beetles, blind, winged, and entirely nomadic. A swarm is a cloud with a center of gravity, turning slowly in a fog pocket, clicking softly as it turns, and when it settles it settles hard. In a single hollow a swarm can black out what little light there is for an hour, millions of bodies feeding on stonebloom and on the edges of the electrogenic mats. What they are doing is industrial work at insect scale. In each gut is a strain that can take basalt apart and rebuild it — as a tough amber polymer, as ferrite, as fine coppery wire. They do not nest. They leave. Their molts are left in thousands where they fed, and after a season of damp and stone-grit they weather into objects that look, to any eye expecting a maker, uncannily manufactured: riveted plates, articulated joints, hollow tubes. They are only exoskeletons, but the Table has spent so long making metal that even its shed skins expect to be mistaken for parts.

Where the motes have been, the stonebloom is groomed, laced with microscopic residue, and richer for it. Where the motes go next is decided by heat and smell — a warm pool lifting, a predator stirring, a fog front arriving carrying new minerals. They follow the kettles, in a way, though they never stay with them.

The kettles are the Table's great slow furnaces, and the most obvious presence when you finally register one against fog. Six-legged, barrel-bodied, blind, with paired heat-sensing pits along the flank that flare dull orange when a warm body is near. The mouth is a beak, down-turned, made to scrape mat from rock. They are not silent when they move. Feet the size of dinner plates press into silt and the ground trembles faintly through your soles, and the chambered stomach in the abdomen — bacteria and fungi working stonebloom and stone itself — makes a low rumble you feel in your teeth before you hear it. They exhale sulfurous steam. Touch one along the side, if you are close enough to risk it, and it is warm, persistently, unmistakably warm, like a boiler that has been running for days.

A kettle does not eat to live so much as it eats to process. Heat is the byproduct of its digestion, and metal is the other. What comes out the far end is not dung. It is slag: glassy black-red pellets, dense with iron oxides, laced with ammonia and nitrates distilled from fog and rock. Left alone, a pellet is just a weight on the stone. Left for a thousand years in a line, along a herd's preferred track between condensation basins, it becomes a band of dark, fertile soil, a faint linear terrace that will outlast the animals that made it and eventually be smoothed by erosion back into the Table. Where a herd has camped — hundreds of animals in a wide basin, furnaces lit after feeding — the ground stays noticeably warmer for days afterward. You feel it through your boots after they are gone. The territory of a herd is larger than a small country, and its movement is seasonless, a slow attrition from one damp basin to the next, always following stonebloom thickening after fog.

They are not social in any intimate sense. They move as one because the ground is warmest where the herd has been, and because alone a kettle is slow and blind and its heat advertises it for kilometers. Together the heat is a column. Together the rumble is a ground-current the pack-hunters can track through stone.

The pack-hunters are the other end of that adaptation. Blind, low-slung, built for stillness more than speed, they carry heat pits larger than a kettle's, and an even finer sense of that stomach-rumble, that furnace ignition that travels through silt and basalt. They do not chase. They attrit. They follow a herd at a distance that puts them at the edge of its weather, waiting for the animal that lags, that feeds poorly, that runs hotter because its gut is souring. Then they cut it out, quietly, in the dark between fog banks. What they leave is the work of the motes. Bones are polished black in a season, scattered in a wide, neat arc, and then they are soil too.

Between these large economies there is a whole smaller, brighter traffic, mostly in the air of fog-gardens and pool margins. The small flying things with ember-lit bellies — not motes, though they travel in the same pockets — flash and dim to find mates in a darkness that is never entirely black. Their light is communication and also, in the gloom, a kind of pollination: they brush thread-grass, carry spores and bacterial strains from one luminous lichen mat to another, and leave the thread of green-black that draws the kettles. The bioluminescent lichen themselves, ember-tips on stone, pulse when the electrogenic field surges, a faint signalling across a hollow that the insects read as a map.

And in the pools — those shallow, ankle-deep pans in dry basins where rain has lingered and groundwater warms from below — lives the opaque life that makes the Table possible. There are no fish, no clear water things. The pools are warm from below and evaporating from above, mineral-rich and cloudy with microbes that thrive on kettle slag and mote residue, that fix what the fog brings and exhale it back as heat. When a fog front advances, a pool can wake all at once, scent rising, vibration chuckling through wet stone, motes erupting from its surface by the million, drawn by the sudden warmth of a predator or a kettle drinking at its edge. Then the pool sinks, as all pools do, into porous rock within days, leaving its crust in the basin floor for the next rain to dissolve.

No one lives here the way a person lives in a house. No one builds, no one tends, no one stays in a place long enough to name it. The inhabitants are all migratory, all low-density, all spread thin across a plateau that makes its own weather. A square meter can be alive with stonebloom and insects; the next hundred can be bare polished rock, a single dry dung ball, a trackway in silt, a shed mote-molt gleaming like a rivet. Emptiness is not absence here. It is the distance between processes.

That is why, walking it, you keep feeling presences that are not quite there — the warmth left in a depression after a herd has moved on, the prickle of a mat under your hair, the click of a swarm turning somewhere in fog you cannot see into, a faint green wash high up when the aurora finds the pole and the whole Table briefly shows its silhouettes: grazers moving like slow weather, hunters lying flat to feel them, everything else small, bright-bellied, and moving between them. They have been doing this long enough that geology and life have worn each other smooth. The slag becomes soil. The soil becomes stonebloom. The stonebloom becomes heat, and metal, and fog, and again stonebloom.
