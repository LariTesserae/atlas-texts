The first lives you notice are not the ones you see.

They are the ones you hear, before you have learned to look slowly enough. A dry trilling, everywhere and nowhere, thicker in the moss under your boots, thinner over the open dust, rising and falling without ever quite stopping. Only after a while do you realize that what you took for ground is alive, and what you took for silence is a multitude spread very thin.

Start at the ground, because everything here starts at the ground and stays close to it. The shaded parts are not bare. They are felted. Every trunk, every fallen stone, every soft hump of a wall, every mound that has given up being a building and become a hill again, is covered in moss that does not need rain. It waits. At night, when the shafts fade and the stone gives back its cool, a thin dew settles on its leaves and on the broad waxy leaves above it and on the stone itself, and for an hour or two the whole plain wets itself — not to drink, but to be drunk. By morning the moss has taken it in, the ferns have taken it in, the roots have taken it in, before the crawling light can dry it. A fern here grows by unfurling, once a year, a frond a hand's width long. You could watch it for your whole life and think nothing was happening, and you would be watching something happen at the exact speed it intended.

Between the moss are the shrubs, laurel-thick and unhurried, and the succulents, fat as barrels, storing a decade of dew in a stem that will not split for the weight of it. They are not quick to fruit. When they do, the fruit falls heavy and slow and is found by the animals who live close enough to be patient. The trees stand far apart — a hundred metres, two hundred — but each one is feeding over an area twenty times its crown, its roots a shallow invisible web drinking dew out of dust that looks too dry to give anything. From above, the trees that live on the surface send their own roots down through the roof to meet them. The two forests almost touch. What lives in the lowest crowns and what lives among the hanging roots are often the same animals, climbing the last few metres from one world to the other to browse both.

Under all of it, threading through leaf-sift and dead wood and the soft interior of the walls, are the fungi. They are the oldest residents in any meaningful sense, and the least showy. No glow, no umbrella, no announcement. Just pale, immense networks breaking down litter and termite-worked fibre for years at a time, turning it back into something a root can use. If the plants are the skin of the place, the fungi are its slow digestion.

And then the builders.

The city was not planned. It accumulated. Termite colonies took the clearings where the light fell — because warmth, such as it is, is there — and raised mounds, a millimetre a year, of mud and chewed fibre and mineral cement. A colony here is not a generation. It is a continuous body that may have been adding to the same hill for longer than any tree above it has been alive. The workers inside never see the light shafts. They live entire lives in dark, cool, humid tunnels, marching the same routes, compacting the dust between mounds into avenues that become grassy swales when the moss reclaims them. Around the mounds, joined and fused, towers rose until they lost their shape and became eroded hills with holes in them. The towers hum when air moves through the roof cracks, because they are hollow and the stone holds the note.

Ants live in the walls beside them, and crickets live in the moss on the walls, and beetles live in the leaf-sift that sifts down through the roof cracks like very slow snow. None of them are large. None of them are quick. Their combined movement is the hiss you heard first, a continuous dry trilling of wings and legs, like rain that never arrives. To be among them is to understand that abundance here does not look like density. It looks like thinness that never ends.

The birds make the thinness visible.

By day, in the plazas — the open trampled clearings at the centres of the shafts where the roof is thinnest — the flocking seed-eaters rise and settle in the root-canopy and in woven twig scaffolding the birds themselves have added to the upperworks of the mounds. Thousands of them, wheeling up a light shaft as if the light were a column they could climb, calling in unison, dropping back into nests tucked into hollows in the mound-walls. Raptors sit higher, in the hanging roots, watching the shafts move. Birds are the only lives here that seem to hurry, and even they hurry slowly, cool-blooded and dim-adapted, and their hurry is mostly noise. They eat insects and seeds and dried leaf, and they carry seeds in their droppings to the moss under the descending roots, planting the next generation of shade. They are gardeners without knowing it, and the plain depends on their restlessness.

Below them, under the avenues, are the tunnellers. Moles and shrews and a dozen small burrowing mammals that live almost entirely in vibration and smell, excavating terraces and granaries deep in the mound-walls. The granaries are dry storehouses, carefully kept, of seeds and dried leaf and fallen succulent fruit, stacked against a damp season that, in a place with no rain, means only a season when the shafts are elsewhere and the dew is thinner. They eat everything — seeds, insects, grub, fruit — and they are eaten in turn. You will rarely see them. You will see the faint vibration under your feet when you stand still long enough, and the loose, round pebbles of earth they push up, and know a whole district is living underneath the district you are walking on.

In the shaded forest, where the shafts rarely reach, the larger slow creatures move through litter and grunt and cough and snap. Tortoises, shifting at a pace that makes them look like stones deciding to be elsewhere. Lizard-like reptiles that gather in a shaft to bask for hours and days, following the light as it crawls a tree's width over a day and a whole district over a season — a migration measured in metres. They eat the small mammals. The small mammals eat the seeds the birds spread. The circle is closed and slow enough that you can watch a link of it for a year and think it has not moved.

And hanging among the descending roots, in that dim greenish dark midway between the two forests, are the arboreal mammals. They hang and chew and mutter to themselves, mostly motionless, occasionally reaching for a leaf from above or below. The largest of them are the big slow tree-dwellers, heavy enough that they can pull a descending root down a little, bridging the gap between the under-canopy and the over-canopy with their bodies. They eat leaves from both forests, the only lives here privileged to feed in two worlds at once. Their slowness is not laziness. It is thrift. In cool air and dim light, nothing can afford to burn energy quickly, so everything learns to waste only sound.

Sound is the one waste they allow themselves. Because the basin is a stone bowl, a call made a kilometre away arrives clear. Because the air is cool and still, it lasts. Because the stone holds it, it stays long after the bird that made it has gone quiet. So they call. Crickets call. Beetles rasp. Birds call in flocks. Mammals mutter in the roots. Reptiles snap and grunt in the litter. Hollow towers hum. At midday, when the largest shaft is fullest and the flocks are most restless, the chorus becomes a pressure you feel in your teeth. You shout to an arm's length companion and give up shouting. At night the birds settle and the insects take over completely, and the basin rings with a high, endless tone. It is not a celebration. It is simply the cheapest way to be known to be alive at a distance, in a place where distance is the main problem.

No one here drinks. That is the rule that shapes every body. Water is never free; it is bound in sap, in a succulent's stem, in the body of an insect, in the dew that must be licked off a leaf or a stone before the light finds it. So animals drink dew from leaves at night. Reptiles take moisture from the mammals they eat. Mammals take it from fruit and grub and seed. Termites take it from the fungi and wood they chew. Plants take it from the air and the debris sifting down from above — leaf, spore, dust — for years. Nothing dies unwasted. It is eaten, or it becomes wall, or it becomes soil, very slowly.

You could live here for a year, as the description of the place promises, and your notes would be almost empty. The tallest tower had grown a hand's width. A tree had put on one thin ring. The light over your camp had moved thirty metres. And around you, uninterrupted, unhurried, deafening, the moss would be breathing dew, the termites would be adding skin to a hill, the birds would be turning in the shaft, the tunnellers would be vibrating under the avenue, the tortoise would be shifting through litter, the fungi would be digesting the city, and every one of them would be making a little sound into stone that remembers it.
