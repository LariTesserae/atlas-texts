The first ones you will never see, and you will live here a long time without seeing them.

They are in the felt. Under every leaf, between every rootlet of the mat, in the black pockets of water caught in the weave, there is a population so close and so small that the mat itself seems to move only when you are not looking. Mites the size of dust, springtails that jump in invisible arcs, worms that eat the moss from inside and leave a clean green tunnel behind them that closes in an hour. Fungi run through it all — white threads, brown threads, threads that glow faintly when the condensation thickens — breaking down a leaf here, lifting a stem there, trading one for the other at exactly the rate the mat can use. Beetle larvae mine the sedge stems in single-file galleries. A beetle bores for a year in one stem and dies in it and is eaten in a day by something else that lives in the stem beside it.

These are the citizens who never leave the floor. They do not travel to the rim. They do not need to. There is food in every square centimeter, water in every square centimeter, and no square centimeter is different from the next. A life here is born, works, calls or chews or filters, and is taken back into the mat within reach of where it started. You hear them mostly as a texture under everything else — a gritting close to your ankle, a chewing that stops when you put your hand down, a drilling that resumes from another point in the moss a moment later, as if the work has simply moved its center.

Above them, but still tied to the floor, are the factories.

The ant colonies are set apart the way the trees are set apart, each one in its own open space, working a territory that is empty to your eye. You feel them before you see them: a vibration in the mat, a file moving under a liverwort leaf, a column surfacing for one second at a green knuckle — a stone wearing moss — and vanishing. They carry fragments of lichen, spores, dead midges, bits of bark that have fallen from eleven trees away. They do not build upward. Their nests are horizontal, spread out under the mat like a lung under a rib, and over a century they will have turned over the soil of their whole district without ever making a hill out of it. When a worker dies in the file, another steps over it. The file does not pause.

The termites are inside the trees. You cannot see them at all without breaking the tree, and nothing here breaks. From the outside a trunk is grey-brown, deeply furrowed, final. Inside it is a thoroughfare, a warm, damp, dark industry grinding the heart out slowly and carrying it down to the roots, where fungi take it and turn it back into mat. A tree supports its termites the way the mat supports its beetles: completely, and without change in how it looks. The tapping you hear, far off and regular — a stroke, a stroke, a stroke — may be a beetle larva in a dead limb, may be termites in the trunk, may be a woodpecker working a crown forty paces away. Here it does not matter which. It is the same rhythm at every scale.

In the water pockets live the frogs.

Each pocket — a leaf-cup, a hollow between moss and stone, a black still place where your fingertip finds a cold bottom — has its resident. It does not move to another pocket. It sits with only its eyes out, green and lidless-looking, and when it calls its throat inflates under the water and the sound goes out wet and pumping across the mat to the next pocket, and the next. They never sync. One begins, another answers half a beat late, a third overlaps it. Over distance their calls come from everywhere and from nowhere in particular, because there is no edge to the sound. A frog will call from the same pocket for what sounds like your whole stay, and when it stops another pocket twenty paces off takes up the phrase at the same pitch. Males, presumably. The females come in on nights or hours you cannot distinguish from day, lay a string of eggs on the underside of a floating leaf, and leave. The tadpoles eat algae off the mat from inside the pocket and never need to leave it. They grow a frog the size of a thumbnail and stay there.

Above the frogs, in the air that hangs in the bowl, are the sawing things. Ground crickets work the mat from below, invisible under the felt, each one married to a small warm patch of moss where the air is a fraction less cool. Their song is not music; it is a continuous high note produced somewhere under your boots, rising and falling as they move a centimeter. Higher, in the crowns, are the cicadas — or what passes for them in this cool damp — tree frogs that climb, insects that only live for the chorus. They make the factory tone you feel in your teeth. It does not come from one tree. It comes from all of them at once, so that when you turn your head the pitch does not change, only the direction of the emptiness it fills.

The birds are the only ones you will see regularly, and you will see them at a great distance, briefly.

Each tree has its pair. Small, dark, quick in the high small crown that is too small for the trunk beneath it. They flicker from limb to limb, they call in short mechanized phrases that carry across the open mat without anything to soften them, they fly — straight, unhurried — to the next tree, and to the next, and do not stop in between because there is nothing to stop for. They eat insects in sheets: when a midge swarm or a run of termite alates rises off the mat for ten minutes, a bird will drop, take three, and rise again. They carry seeds in their droppings and drop them onto the mat and onto the next crown and onto the next, and the seeds are eaten by ants or rot in the damp before they can do anything. The trees do not make more trees. The birds do not need them to. They are conveyors moving life from one finished place to another finished place.

Under the mat, in the set trails the description mentions, are the mammals. Shrews, probably. Small marsupial mice, perhaps. You will not see them on top because there is no top for them to be on; the mat is their roof. They run in tunnels in the single layer itself, eating beetle larvae, eating worms, eating the fallen leaves that the fungi have not yet taken. Their feet make the mat spring back. Their passages aerate the felt and let water sigh out somewhere else when you press. A shrew's whole world is a district of mat fifty paces across, and it will know every pocket, every ant trail, every place where a frog sits in the dark. It will never climb a tree, never cross the rim onto the drier common country outside, never meet a shrew from the next district except by calling through the mat, a thin squeak you hear more in your foot than in your ear.

The predators are as thin as everything else. Spiders hang in the single tall fern tuft and in the single shrub and in the low forks of the trees, in webs you find only when a bead of condensation runs into them and makes them briefly visible. Centipedes move under the moss felt, eating the same larvae the shrews eat, and are eaten by the shrews. A frog will take a beetle that has climbed out of its pocket. A bird will take a frog that has climbed too high. Nothing is large enough to be a hunter in the ordinary sense. Predation here is a small mouth in a small place, repeated everywhere, thousands of times a day, and it balances the eating of the mat so precisely that the mat never thins and never thickens.

And then there are the trees themselves, who are not scenery. They are residents who have finished.

Eleven of them, perhaps twelve. Each one stands alone in a volume of air out of all proportion to its living crown. Their trunks are wide and furrowed and cool to the hand with the same damp as the mat. Epiphytes coat their lower limbs — mosses, liverworts, lichens that look like grey-green paint worn away in the rain. In their crowns, high and small, microclimates of insects change with the condensation. A species of frog may live its whole life in the water held in a fork. A beetle may live its whole life in the bark of one limb. No new limb is made to replace a dead one; a dead limb is taken by fungi and termites and becomes, slowly, part of the trunk's interior. Growth up there is so slow it looks like stillness. It is stillness that is being eaten and remade at the same rate.

The shrub and the tall fern are residents on the same terms, only lonelier. The shrub holds a spider, some mites, a few beetles that will never leave its roots. The fern unfurls one frond a year and loses one, and the animals in its leaflets are born and die in sight of each other. They have no partners of their kind within reach except by sound.

No one here is alone, and no one here is close.

That is the arrangement. Every kind is spaced to the limit the bowl allows. Every individual can hear another of its kind across empty mat and empty air, and can call back, and that is the whole of its social life. The ants farm a district. The frogs sing to the next pocket. The birds fly tree to tree. The fungi connect what the mat has eaten to what the mat will eat next. The terms for young and old do not apply because nothing is visibly young and nothing is allowed to be visibly old; a beetle that has bored for a year looks like a beetle that has bored for a day, and a tree that has stood for a thousand years looks like a tree that stood yesterday.

There is no human inhabitant, no large animal, no builder, no one who marks a path or leaves a fire or comes back to check. The inhabitants do not need a witness. They work in the cool damp whether you are there or not, at the industrial pace of their chewing and sawing and calling, loud under the quiet light, and when you step back over the rim the sound does not follow you — not because it wants to stay, but because the next mat, the drier rat, the ordinary country beyond, has its own inhabitants doing the same work at a different pitch, and these ones have never had a reason to cross the edge where their damp ends.
