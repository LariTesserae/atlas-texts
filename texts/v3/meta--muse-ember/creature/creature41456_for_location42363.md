There is almost nothing to point to.

If you walk a long way — a kilometer, ten — under that flat light, you could believe the green you saw once was a trick of the eye, a damp seam darkening. It is not there for a long time. The mat goes on finished and dead and sun-baked, brick and thatch and polygon cracks, and it holds nothing.

Then in a hollow, where the weave dips a hand's breadth lower than the rest, you come on a working.

It does not announce itself. It is a frayed line, not a garden. You have to kneel to see it properly, and kneeling, your knees get hot very fast through the mat. Up close the line resolves, slowly, into pieces.

A stem, no thicker than a knitting needle, stiff as a dry reed but still alive — you can tell by the faint give at a joint, by the way the light catches the skin. It runs sideways across the surface for a span, hugging it. Where it touches, it puts down roots. Not one taproot driving down. Many. A small fan of them, level with the surface, threading themselves into the warp and weft of the older mat, into the seams where silt has collected. They do not dig. They spread.

At the node where it rooted, it leaves a rosette. Leaves are few, tough, waxy, no longer than a thumbnail, cupped upward a little as if to catch what falls. They are grey-green on top where the sun has found them, paler underneath. In the cool of dawn they beaded when you brushed them. By mid-morning they are too hot to hold your hand against for long, but they do not wilt. They simply close, a fraction, and wait.

Then from that rosette another stem goes out. And another. Always sideways. Always at the same shallow level, just above the dead work and just below the wind.

There is no upright part to them beyond that. No trunk, no branch reaching for light. Light is not scarce here. Water is, and water does not come from above for long. It collects in the basket of roots, in the little shadowed hollows under the living stems, in the damp seam behind the front. So the plant lives *in* the surface it is making, not on top of it.

This is how you can tell the living part from the dead. Behind the front — to the south or the north, wherever the patch has already been — the stems are paler, dry, locked in. They are no longer weather. They are ground. Silt has packed around them, rain has swollen and sun has baked them flat. They tick faintly at night as they contract. The living stems run ahead of that, green only in a thin, loosening way, with the warp ends still sticking up between them, unbound, casting small shadows.

The front is never more than a few stems wide. Everything else is memory.

What it does, all day in the heat, is push. One node at a time.

It finds a seam that stayed damp after the last rain — a crack that did not bake shut at noon, a pocket where dew dripped one by one until dawn — and it sends a root into it. Then it stops. It does not rush. The waxy leaves conserve. The stem hardens off behind the growing tip. On a dry day nothing happens that you could see, except the panels around it expanding with heat and the dust moving off the bare mat and settling, grain by grain, into its basket.

After a rain pulse, it wakes. You can see it if you come back in three days. Where there was a gap, there is now a new rosette, a little paler than the others. A stem has crossed the gap and rooted on the other side. The patch has locked to itself a little further.

It does not eat the dead mat. There is nothing here to rot it. When a stem behind the front dies — and they all die, one season or three later, out at the far end from the growing tip — it dries in place. It is not taken. The next season's silt presses it tighter. The plant that follows grows *over* it, not from it. So the living line leaves its own body behind as it goes, and in time cannot tell its body from the continent. The whole plain is that: front after front, for a very long time, all moving in no particular direction except into damp.

The flowers are an afterthought, and you might miss them. Where a rosette is well-rooted, a short stalk lifts just enough to clear the leaves — an inch, two — and opens. Small, beige-white, no scent you can catch, no nectar gloss. There is nothing to visit them. Nothing flies here, nothing walks here. The wind takes the pollen straight off the anthers and carries it, mostly, away to settle uselessly on brick-dry mat a field away.

The seeds are made for that distance. Light, papery, ridged so the wind will lift them and turn them. They fall everywhere. On the finished mat they do nothing. They bake. They blow again. Only if one lands precisely in a seam that stays damp for three days together — a rare seam, in a rare hollow — does it germinate. Then it is a single rosette, alone, hundreds of meters from the nearest other patch, with no parent to find it. It puts down its flat fan of roots. It thickens the mat by a fraction of an inch where it stands.

From there it begins again: one stem, then another, trapping dust, holding dew, building a little thickness that will in its turn hold water.

That is its whole life. No legs to carry it to that seam. No mouth to take the dead. No eyes to find the hollow. It lives only where the surface already holds moisture, and it makes the surface hold a little more moisture, and then it lives a little further in.

At dawn it drinks. This is the busiest hour, though you would not call it busy. The dew that has beaded on the upturned leaves runs down the stems and drips into the pockets. The air around the patch for an hour smells of wet fiber and warm clay. The roots in the seam swell tight against the mat. The plant takes it in through the shallow basket, quickly, before the heat returns.

At noon it waits. The leaves close a fraction. The stems are too hot to touch on the sunward side. The mat underneath radiates heat back through its feet, if it had feet. It stores it.

In the evening it ticks with the rest of the mat as it cools and contracts. A dry stem behind it clicks against a live one. The wind moves through the front with a soft shushing that the bare mat never makes.

Then night. Damp again in the seams for a few hours. If it is a night after rain, a new tip, somewhere invisible in the dark, pushes a root a centimeter into a gap that is still wet.

It is patient to a degree that stops being a virtue and becomes just its nature. There is no predator to outwait. There is no prey to chase. There is only the slow advance, patch by patch, into damp, and the building behind it, layer on layer, that will outlast any single rosette by years, by decades, until the patch as a whole has become a plain.

If you left it alone for a human lifetime, you might come back and find the front a meter further on, or ten. Or you might find it had stopped entirely, waiting out a dry year that is longer than its stems can span, green faded to grey, until a rain two years off finds the seam again.

It does not grow up because there is nowhere to grow up *to*. It grows sideways because sideways is where water remains. It lives, if you can call the thin green line living, only at the edge of the world it is making — the world behind it already dead and becoming ground, the world ahead of it still waiting to be made damp enough to begin.
