The oldest residents do not walk, and they do not sleep. They lie in the warm shallows in the hollows, grey and faintly green, a thin trembling skin over the water. Microbial mats spread from pool to pool, following the slow sheets of water through the lanes, filming the stones, crusting the edges of the channels. In the mineral water they precipitate more of the pale mud they live in. The ground itself is, in part, something they have made - sinter and silt laid down grain by grain, cracked into polygons, smoothed and re-smoothed. Where a mat is left undisturbed it thickens, ripples, smells faintly of heat and copper. Where people wade and bail and kick it, it tears and regrows within the day.

In damp corners, where the water only weeps, other lives take the second place. Thin fungal films grow over plaster and the undersides of roof slabs, over the inside of clay pots left wet, over the backs of stones in tool sheds. Grey, lichen-like patches spread silently on walls that never dry, on the shaded side of a compound wall, on leather left too long by a hearth. They are not tended. No one harvests them, no one cleans them away entirely. They belong to the dampness as much as the people do, and people learn to scrape them from a doorway or a workbench with a thumbnail and think nothing of it.

Over the pools, in the low steam under the grey ceiling, the next layer hangs in the air. Midges and small flies rise in clouds thick enough to breathe through, to walk through with your eyes streaming. They do not die off. The warmth and the constant water keep them breeding without pause. They settle on forearms, on the wet stone of bathing platforms, on the faces of children splashing in the ankle-deep flow that runs through a lane. People wave them away out of habit, as you would wave at weather. They are food, first and most reliably. On the surface of the water they fall and are taken. In the air the swifts take them.

The swifts wheel just under the mist, screaming, never high, never far. Swifts and other aerial birds nest in the town as much as in any cliff, in wall cavities and under eaves and in the half-finished upper courses of a house that has been waiting a year for its roof. Pigeons are there in the thousands, cooing without break from the cavities, fluttering down in short white bursts to drink from a channel, whitening the ledges under their roosts. Herons stand motionless in the channels for hours at a time, grey on grey, ignoring the hammering and the shouting, their heads twitching at a frog.

Frogs and toads sit in the shallows of the lanes themselves, in the warm trickle between stone paving. A step will make a dozen of them hop, plop, settle again. Children pick them up, turn them over in wet hands, put them back in the same inch of water. Newts slip under stones at the edges of pools. Small fish dart only in the cooler feeder channels, where water comes in from outside the hot basin, and in the narrower runs that have been dug and re-dug straight through the settlement. There they are netted, scooped, taken by hand by boys who know which channel runs cool in the morning - though morning is no cooler than midday, only mist-thicker at the ankle. Turtles haul themselves onto warm mineral ledges and stay there, still, basking not in sun, for there is no sun to speak of, but in heat rising from stone.

None of this would hold without the animals that live directly off people. Ducks paddle through the thoroughfares, in family lines, in loose flocks that know the shortcuts. They go into houses. They come out again. They quack all day over the roar of the vents, lay eggs on damp stone platforms inside, in storage yards, in corners of workshops. Chickens scratch at mineral ground that has never known a worm, picking at insects, at fallen midges, at grain spilled by a porter, at scraps. Dogs lie half-submerged in warm shallows to find a temperature that suits them, shifting a body-length at a time, barking at a stranger, at a pack animal, at nothing. They are everywhere and they are fed from the town and they feed on the town's waste in return.

Goats and pigs are tethered in small stone pens wedged between houses, on terraces laid a hand's breadth above the water. They get no forage here, for there is nothing green to eat. They get grain brought in from the drier rim, they get scraps, dried fish, the offal of a turtle, the gut of a duck, whatever the lanes wash to them. They grow fat on waste and on standing in warmth. They give back little milk, a little meat when a family needs it, dung for the small hearths. Their dung is fuel, dried on hot slabs beside the roof, along with husks and straw carried in with the grain and, sparingly, splinters of the treasured wood.

And then the people, who are less the rulers of this than its most laborious residents.

The town does not end at any one pool. It spreads from cluster to cluster across the basin, lane after lane of near-touching houses, compound walls abutting compound walls, workshops shoulder to shoulder with bathing platforms, storage yards wedged between homes. It is crowded because the wet flat ground is scarce, and because a channel and a warm pool can be shared. A family lives in a house that is half-built and has always been half-built: a wall stopped at waist height, courses of stone waiting beside it, a corner re-raised because it sank a finger's width last year, a roof of slabs weighted with more slabs. They live in it anyway. Their threshold is raised against the water. Their walls sweat. Inside, the floor is damp stone, the beds are raised platforms with mats and leather that never fully dry, the air is warm and close with hearth smoke and wet wool and the smell of ducks coming and going.

They are never dry. Clothes - leather kilts and wraps, undyed woven cloth brought in or worked from imported fiber, shawls thrown over shoulders against the weeping walls - stay damp and are wrung out and worn again. Skin stays damp, soft at the fingertips and toes from constant wading, nails peeling, feet wrinkled, forearms beaded with condensation that drips and runs. Hair is crusted faintly with mineral. Eyes water in the steam. Everyone learns to shout, because the ground itself is shouting under everything: the hiss and rush of vents out on the flats like a distant waterfall, water sheeting down every channel, dripping from every ledge, hammer on stone, dogs, ducks, people calling names across a lane. A child is born into that noise and sleeps through it, is soothed by it. Silence, to them, would be the wrong kind of quiet.

Work is manual and close to the body, because there are no engines and very little metal. All day the clack of stone hammer on stone. Men and women and older children cut stone from the flats, shape mud brick and bake it hard in the warm air, plaster, raise a wall a course, stop, re-dig a silted channel with a bucket and a leather sack. Pots are shaped by hand, beaten smooth, set to warm by a hearth. Hides are beaten at tanning troughs by the warm channels, people standing knee-deep, forearms in water, working leather that will become shoes, straps, a roof cover, a bucket. Stone hammers and copper chisels are worn smooth. A wooden handle is worth more than a pot. A roof beam, carried in on the back of a porter or a pack animal from beyond the basin, is inherited, repaired, never wasted. Most houses have only the one.

They live on what comes in and what the warm water gives them. Grain from the rim is ground on stone querns, made into porridge and flatbread baked on hot slabs and over small fires of dung and husks. Dried fish comes in with the grain, and small fresh fish come from the feeder channels. Eggs from ducks and chickens and pigeons are a constant protein. Frogs, turtles, pigeon squabs, the odd goat or pig, are taken when the town's waste and the pools' insects have fattened them. Children scrape dried microbial scum from pool edges and eat it, bitter and mineral, as a supplement they are taught to prize. Nothing is seasonal because nothing in the basin obeys a season. The pools are at the same level as last year. The vents roar at the same pitch. The mist hangs at the same height at midnight and at what would be noon. Breeding does not stop. Growth does not stop. Work does not stop for weather, because there is no weather to stop it.

Daily life is therefore not marked by light or season but by activity and by water. People bathe constantly, communally, on platforms over the pools, naked children splashing in the lanes, older people sitting with their legs in the warmth, talking in loud, hoarse voices across the steam. They wade to a neighbor's house through ankle-deep water. They bail their thresholds. They listen - unconsciously, the way you listen to your own breathing - for any change in the roar of a vent, though it does not change. Porters and pack animals arrive from the rim with wood, grain, dried fish, fiber, salt, take back stone, pots, leather, brick, dried guano from the pigeon ledges. Most people never make that trip. Traders bring words for things no one here has seen grow: leaf, forest, harvest, winter, snow. Children handle a traded piece of wood as if it were a small animal, save shavings, carve a handle and keep it for a lifetime.

Their bodies adapt as surely as the ducks' and the frogs'. Rashes from the damp, fungal patches on damp skin, joints that ache - or that find relief soaking in the pools. Lungs accustomed to mineral steam. Ears accustomed to hammering and roar, voices that carry. There is no dry place to put a thing to keep it safe from mildew, so they keep nothing that mildew destroys if they can help it: stone, clay, leather, bone, shell. A thing made of fiber is for daily use and daily replacement, carried in again by the next porter.

It is a crowded, loud, unfinished life, held perfectly steady by a crowded, loud, unfinished ecology. The microbes build the ground. The midges eat the microbes. The frogs and fish and birds eat the midges. The people eat the fish and the birds and the frogs, and feed the pigs and goats and dogs from beyond the basin and from their own waste, and the waste feeds the microbes again. The stone town builds itself and settles and is rebuilt in the same plots, generation after generation, on ground that is still laying itself down. No one finishes a house and calls it done. No one expects the mist to lift. They are born, they work in warm water up to their ankles, they shout over steam, they sleep to the sound of vents and pigeons and ducks in the next room, and they are replaced by their children in the same half-built houses, in the same perpetual damp and dimness, listening to water and to each other, and never quite finishing, and never decaying either.
