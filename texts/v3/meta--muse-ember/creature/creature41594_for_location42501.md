If you stand still long enough for the dust to settle on your shoulders, the living comes to you. Not as individuals. As pressure.

First there is the air, because the air is crowded first. In the shafts of filtered daylight that fall sideways through the fissures overhead, the glittering dust is not only dust. It is shed skins and seed-fluff and the evaporated oil of a thousand leaves, and moving in it, sideways and vertical and everywhere between, are the midges and mites that no one here ever names because they are the first condition of everything else. They live by the thousands in a single breath of warm air, scraping lichen-colonies off the undersides of the leaf-roofs, drinking resin-mist, laying their eggs on the dampest thing this place allows — the underside of a leaf that has just unfurled, still warm from its own unfolding. Under them the roar builds and falls. You feel it in your teeth before you understand it as sound: millions of wings, millions of legs, a surf of chittering that moves across a chamber like weather.

It has to move. Nothing here can be left alone for an hour.

Go down — or rather, press your foot down — and the second layer announces itself by giving way. The mat under you sighs. It is not soil, it is tissue, and it is inhabited the way a forest floor in a wet world would be inhabited, only here the tunnels are inside living stem. Root-moles work through it blind, pale, hairless, their forefeet huge and shovel-flat, their noses constantly tasting mineral dust. They do not eat the mat so much as unweave it, loosening the weave so the roots beneath can breathe the warm air and exude their soft stone more quickly. Blind grubs follow them, fat and translucent, feeding on the crushed pulp their passage leaves, then pupating inside a hollow root where the sap runs thickest. Smaller still, sap-suckers with needle mouths tap the creeping stems and swell until they burst, and the burst is food for the next layer up. The mat is their body and their road and their graveyard. When a grub dies it does not rot. It dries in place, white and clean, and is picked apart by the mites in the air.

On the surface of the mat — you learn not to call it a surface — the herds move. Six-legged, bark-skinned, slow as hills deciding to walk. Their skins are ridged and resinous, warm to the touch, and on some of them the back is translucent. Photosynthesis happens there, green-gold under the skin, in animals that also chew. They mill. That is their entire strategy and their entire social life: to mill in vast, near-touching masses that cover a hollow between plant-hills entirely, heads down, pruning. A vine that would seal a doorway in a week lasts an afternoon under a herd. A bud the size of a house, furled and trembling, will be stripped to its skeleton before it can open. They are the reason the chambers stay chambers at all.

They eat no water. They strip leaves and pulp for the oil-sap in them, they lick resin from wounded bark, they mine the chalk dust that settles on their own backs with broad mineral tongues. What they excrete is not dung in any wet sense, but dry, hard pellets, rich in the limestone they have over-mined, and those pellets precipitate, slowly, into the fresh ribs of stone that rise in thin places on the rises. A herd resting overnight can leave a ridge. A herd passing a chamber leaves it, in a week, a hand's breadth higher than it was. Their young are born late in the milling, dropped onto the mat among the legs, already dry, already barked, already trying to prune. They are born unfinished, like everything else, their legs still soft, their backs still clear enough to see the green working inside them.

And some of the things they prune refuse to be pruned. The walking plants are not the slowest of the grazers so much as the slowest of the herds. They haul themselves on cord-like roots, deliberatively, one root after another, feeling for a hollow where the light has shifted, leaving a furrow behind them in the mat that closes within hours. You can watch one for a whole morning and think you are watching stillness. It is not. A tendril has lifted, tested, anchored. The main trunk has leaned a degree toward warmth. By evening it will be in another chamber, and the fronds it half-opened on the way will be the roof of no place at all. Their boundary with the herds is not a metaphor down here. A translucent-backed grazer that dies in the wrong hollow will sprout. A walking plant that is eaten hollow by beetles will crawl, for a while, on its remaining roots. You learn not to step on shape.

The towers are their anchors and their enemies. Trunks the size of towers, leaning into each other, fusing, forming tunnels you walk through with your shoulders almost brushing. Inside the hollow ones lives the third great population, the one Ornith? No — the trunk-dwellers, semi-sapient, wholly interior. Some are born, live, and die inside a single hollow stem without ever seeing the dappled light. They tap the inner wall, they nest in the chewed-fiber hives they build in the curve of the trunk, they maintain the tapping channels with their own teeth, they emerge only to swarm — when a grazer's back is thick with parasites, when a leaf-roof seals too fast, when a trunk is about to be eaten hollow by a passing horde and needs emptying. Their language is vibration through wood and a high bell-like ringing that the canopy birds above them learn to copy. They remember which trunks fell last year, and where the mat folded over the roots, and which chambers have been born since. They trade that memory.

They trade it with the small stocky people, the warm-blooded ones, who are the only inhabitants who generate their own heat and keep it. They are knee-high to a fallen trunk, broad through the chest, furred short and dust-colored, with large hands for reading the mat. Because the roar is too loud for voice and the mat is too alive for footsteps to be heard, they communicate by touch and tremor: a palm flat on a root, a stomp that sends a pattern through the weave, a whistle a few inches from an ear. They carry the whole of their built world on their backs — stone blades knapped from the fresh mineral, bone needles cut from old grazers' leg-bones, baskets woven from stem-fiber, pots of sealed resin for carrying sap and pulp. They suck sap directly from cut stems, they chew fruit and seed-pods for pulp, they lick salt-resin from bark with the same tongues the herds use for chalk. Their bodies stay dry. They plaster themselves in resin-dust in the heat of midday and peel it off at shift of light.

They do not build largely because the place does not permit it. A hut of woven root-fiber lashed into a hollow trunk will have a single great leaf grow over its roof in three days and a bud push through its floor in five. So they build small and portable and expect to lose it: a cairn of knapped stone half-swallowed by the mat to mark a safe hollow, a low wall three stones high abandoned mid-course — not abandoned, outpaced — a scatter of tools left on a ledge while their owners follow a herd. They never sleep twice in the same posture if they can help it, because the floor might grow up through the shelter in the night, because a walking plant might decide their hollow is the right light. They move with the growth fronts. They move with the herds, because following a herd is following a cleared road and a source of pulp and dry bones for needles and a curtain of insects thinned by grazing. Their children are carried in hollow stems lent by the trunk-dwellers, born in warmth, already able to read tremor before they can walk.

Over all of it, between the leaf-roofs and the towers, the flying layer makes its own light worse and better. Beetle-clouds, iridescent, scrape the algae off the translucent edges of leaves so the light can percolate again. Moth-swarms, so thick the air moves, fan the oil vapor out of the chambers in waves. Pollinators the size of hands and the size of cats hover at flowers that open like mouths, drinking resin and dust, carrying seed in the fur of their bellies and dropping it finger's breadth from where they land. And the canopy birds, screeching, whistling, ringing like bells in chorus, nest in the umbrellas of leaves that are halfway to being roofs and crack seed-pods with beaks built for stone. When a tower-trunk is eaten hollow in an hour by a ground-horde of beetles and falls with a crack like thunder, they rise all at once and the chamber goes suddenly, violently bright, and shoots are already thrusting through the punctured mat before the dust settles.

All of them live by the same chemistry and the same pace. Sap is oil, volatile, sweet and sharp, beading and evaporating. Air is food. Mineral dust is food. Death is drying. A grazer that collapses in the mill is stripped by mites and beetles and trunk-dweller swarms and left as a white, clean skeleton that the people will collect for a needle or a handle, the mat will weave under, the mineral will exude over. A trunk that falls is eaten, hollowed, composted not by rot but by consumption, and becomes a furrow, a ridge, a new doorway.

Stand on a plant-hill at midday, when the insect surf dips for a second and a leaf-roof opens like an eyelid, and the scale is not of a cave or a garden but of a region that is being born and unborn continuously: hills of woven root rolling to a horizon of fused trunks, valleys folded into breathing tunnels, a new mineral spine rising across a chamber that did not exist last week, movement at every level, color and texture and noise layered to saturation. The herds are halfway to being something else. The walking plants are halfway across. The buds are halfway open. The people are halfway through their camp. The trunk-dwellers are singing through a wall that is still growing over them.

Nothing has water. Nothing has stopped.
