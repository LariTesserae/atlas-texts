The chewing begins before you see anything, and it does not tell you who is chewing.

Somewhere in the sedge, close to the water, a stem is being cut through methodically, bite after bite, with pauses to reposition. It could be a water vole at work on a lawn. It could be a muskrat on its feeding platform. It could be a beaver at the edge of the willows, felling something much larger. Here the sounds stack on top of each other until you cannot separate them, and the lives stack in the same way.

If you stay still the water shows you its smallest inhabitants first. The clear beads that rise unbidden through the moss are not just water. They are cold, oxygenated, and already tenanted — the surface skin of every still pool holds a film of algae and duckweed, grazed from below by pond snails whose shells ride up into the light and whose trails are visible for a few minutes before they fade. Under the floating mats, where light is green and dim, the water is crowded and dark: freshwater shrimp work the rotting stems in slow, sideways shuffles, picking at detritus; caddis larvae carry their houses of grit and root-fragment down the channels they have cleaned, building in miniature the same kind of woven architecture that holds the dams together above them; leeches lie anchored under leaves, waiting; water beetles hang at the surface to breathe, silvered at the belly, then dive with a faint plink when your shadow crosses them.

In the mud of the channels, where your boot would sink, the life is thicker. Mayfly nymphs cling to the undersides of stones the beavers have packed into the dam walls. Dragonfly nymphs lie buried to the eyes in peat, feeling the vibrations of fish above them. The peat itself is alive in a way you cannot see, a black, acidic sponge of bacteria and fungi breaking down sphagnum and sedge root and bone into more peat, sweetly rotting, feeding the green that feeds everything else. Nothing falls without being eaten first by something small.

The fish are the next layer up. Sticklebacks and minnows move in loose, nervous schools through the shallows, flashing in and out of the sedge roots. They are almost invisible until they turn, and then there are dozens of them. In the deep pools behind each beaver dam, where the water holds still and dark and a little warmer, pike hold station. One pike can occupy the same shadowed lie for hours, gills moving slowly, eye fixed on the mouth of a muskrat run. It does not patrol. It waits. The pressure of its stillness is what keeps the smaller fish tight to the margins. Newts crawl in these same margins in the cold shallows, their skin damp and matte, their plumed tails fanning slowly when they court in spring. Frogs are everywhere in damp profusion — moor frogs in the sedge tussocks, common frogs at the pool edges — laying spawn in jelly masses that cloud the water and hatch into tadpoles that blacken the shallows in season. The tadpoles do not hurry. The cold slows them, and the cold is also what protects them, because almost nothing here can afford to be loud or fast for long.

The banks are not banks. They are terraces built and re-built by water voles, and the voles are the street-level population of the place. You see their work before you see them: lawns of sedge and cotton grass clipped to a few centimeters, as precise as if mown, radiating out from hundreds of holes. Each hole is a door, black and round, opening just above the waterline, often with a small ramp of mud worn smooth by feet. Inside, under the hummock, are tunnels and a nest chamber lined with cut grass. Outside are runs so well used that the vegetation dies back to a bare line across the causeways. A vole emerges, whiskers working, sniffs the air, snatches a stem, and vanishes. Another crosses the mud causeway in front of you without looking up, pausing only to check the water with its nose. They are building, eating, and watching all at once, and their clipped lawns are not waste — they are pasture, maintained for the tender regrowth, and they in turn feed the ducks and the deer of the rim and the insects that feed the birds.

On top of the vole terraces, in the water itself, stand the muskrat lodges, dome on dome in rows along the channel. From a distance they look like part of the marsh. Up close they are ragged cities of chewed reed, mud, and root, packed so tight that rain runs off them. Each has a central chamber above the waterline and two or more underwater entrances, ramps leading down into the dark where the water stays just above freezing. Through the roof pokes a small chimney of hollow stems, carefully kept open, for ventilation. Muskrats come and go through this dark with a soft splash, more often at dusk and dawn, their bodies low, their dark eyes bright, their tails trailing like rudders. They haul cut stems to floating feeding platforms nearby — rafts of vegetation lashed together just offshore — and sit there to eat, leaving a signature scatter of chewed ends. The sweet, musky smell of their lodges is the smell of the whole place on a still day. They are plump, unhurried, and utterly single-minded: eat, build, maintain the chimney, replace the platform when it sinks. When a lodge is abandoned it slumps within a week into black peat, and other muskrats build on the slump, raising the ground a millimeter at a time. In a year a marsh that was water is soil, because a muskrat lived there.

The beavers work on a larger scale, and more slowly. Their terraces cross the main channel in stepped dams, each one a woven wall of willow, alder, and mud, so tight that water holds behind it in a perfect sheet. A family lives at the center of each system — a pair, last year's kits, this year's kits — in a lodge that is entered only from below. At night they emerge to work, and the sound of their rebuilding after a flood is the sound of the place restoring itself: a branch dragged, a thump of mud, the slap of a tail on the water as a warning. They do not build for comfort. They build for depth, for an underwater pantry of branches that stays immersed through the skim of ice that forms on the side pools at night, and for a moat that keeps mink and otter from walking straight into the nursery chamber. The kits stay close, learning the smell of the dam, the right kind of mud, the way a willow sapling on the bank will grow into a root bridge if it is bent and held by other stems across a narrow channel. The causeways that lick between lodges are not beaver work alone but are worn and maintained by all traffic, never quite dry, grooved by beaver feet and muskrat feet and vole feet into a single continuous road that no one animal owns and every animal uses.

The water highways belong to things that do not build. An otter moves through the main channel as a dark line under the surface, breaking it only with its nose and a faint ring of water. It travels the whole length of the basin in a night, checking every lodge, marking its latrines on prominent hummocks with a fishy, tarry scent that the voles know to fear. Its body is an answer to the cold, sleek and insulated, and its passage is almost silent — a heron will not even lift its head for it. Mink slip between the muskrat lodges on the same routes, smaller, quicker, heads up, tasting the air for a vole at a burrow mouth. Stoats hunt above the waterline along the vole runs. None of them live here in the way beavers and muskrats and voles live here. They pass through, leaving droppings and scent and eaten remains on the feeding platforms, part of the noise the marsh makes without ever raising its voice.

Above the water, the air is as densely occupied as the mud. The reed stands hold woven towers — nests lashed to living stems above the water, swaying together in the channel's undertow, many-chambered and carefully braced. Reed warblers and sedge warblers and reed buntings sing from the tops of the stems all morning, their songs drowned a little by the wet air. Grey wagtails work the dams, bobbing on the still pools, flicking at midges. Teal and mallard dabble at the margins, their feet stirring up the tadpoles and the shrimp. Snipe probe the saturated peat with bills as long as their bodies, invisible until they flush with a soft croak. At the far waterfall, where the river comes over the rim white for a moment, a dipper walks under the surface on the rocks, head bobbing, hunting caddis in the current. Grey herons stand in the shallows for an hour without moving, neck folded, eye on a frog or a stickleback, then strike so cleanly you see the ripple after the fish is gone. In the willow thickets that rustle without wind, wrens and long-tailed tits nest in the dense fork of dwarf birch and alder, and at dusk bats cut low over the pools, Daubenton's skimming the surface for midges and leaving no sound but the faintest tick of water where a wing tip touches.

Even the stillness is alive. At night cold pools in the bowl and hangs as mist that burns off only when the sun is high. In that white, silent hour the marsh disappears under fog, and the inhabitants are present only as sounds: drips, a frog croak muffled by water, a duck's wing brushing a stem, chewing in the reeds. In the afternoon the water rises a finger's breadth with melt and rain that has seeped for days through limestone, and dams hold, and spillways run. In the morning a skin of frost still rims the shaded peat and the side pools hold their skim of ice at the edges, and beaver work done in the dark is already set hard by the light.

Nothing here is wasted because nothing can afford to be. A dead reed becomes a lodge wall. A dead beaver branch becomes a dam. A dead fish becomes pike food, then otter food, then peat. Peat becomes a hummock. A hummock becomes a vole nest, then a muskrat feeding platform, then a heron's perch. The green that looks so impossibly dense is not scenery. It is sphagnum growing, slowly, in thick spongy carpets that hold water like a wick; sedges rooting; cotton grass seeding; dwarf willow being eaten back and resprouting; ferns uncurling in the drip shade. Every plant is a house, a pantry, or a road for something else.

If you stand still long enough your footprints fill. A water vole crosses in front of you and goes on. A muskrat chews on its platform without noticing you. Under the surface film a fish eye watches. Above you the sky is an oval of ordinary light, and below it the city keeps rebuilding itself — building and sinking, growing and rotting — by tooth and paw and bill and body, at low volume, in damp cold, with no tools and no rest, because in this place building is not an activity. It is how everything lives.
