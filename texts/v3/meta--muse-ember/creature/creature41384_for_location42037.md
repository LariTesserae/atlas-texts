The smallest lives are the ones that make the light you read by.

First, on the pocket-waters, there is a skin. Not a skin of water, exactly, but a skin *on* it — a film of tiny floating plants, rootless, drifting, shouldering each other in a warm soup that never runs anywhere. They are paler at the edges, and there a faint glow catches and holds, as if the film were thinking about being visible. They multiply by splitting, by swelling, by letting go of a daughter that floats off a finger's width and begins again. When the air warms above a vent, the whole film brightens a fraction, all at once, and dims when the steam thins. Frogs sit in it up to their eyes, and they too are part of the film — grey-green heads, throats working, each exhale making a small ring. Their blinking is slow. Their calling is not. They click and croak from the pockets in a chorus that rises in waves, band after band, until your teeth ache, and then subsides, and then rises again from a different pocket, as if a single throat were being passed around the basin.

That is where many of them begin: in the damp that never quite becomes water. Mosses beaded on stone, algae films on the pockets, the pale root-hairs feeling out of a crack that is still warm enough to hiss. These are not decorations on the rock. They are the rock, living. They come within days of a crack opening, with shoots no longer than an eyelash, and they stay to cool it, to hold it, to be eaten and to eat the heat. On rotting trunks, fungus shelves stack in tiers, growing while the trunk is still standing, growing faster after it gives up and falls — indeed, something is already living on the part that will land before it lands. A fallen frond is eaten from below while it is still falling, tunnelled, lifted again as a mushroom cap, eaten again. Death here is not a later chapter. A body steams, and the clearing it makes fills before the steaming stops.

What light there is, they make, and it costs them. That is the law they live under. The low glow that comes up from the ground — from the fungus shelves, from the algae films, from the bellies of grub-like things half-buried in leaf litter, from the veins of the great fronds — is not given. It is labored out of warmth and rot and whatever mineral sweetness rises with the steam. When the air warms, the veins pulse amber, or bruised blue. When a grub burrows deeper into cool litter, its belly dims. When a swarm passes overhead and darkens what little glow there is, everything below seems to hold its breath, then flare slightly as the swarm moves on, as if relieved to be seen again. Far off, where a rim is lowest, the sum of a million of these small labors hangs as a faint amber haze under a ceiling of absolute dark. If you could rise you would see no stars. You would see ground, shining.

Because the world is inverted, they live facing down. The plants are not green in the way of sunlit things. They are translucent, waxy, paler than skin, and they hold their leaves up not to catch light — there is none to catch — but to catch condensation. Shield-herbs unfurl a leaf the size of a shield and hold it over the damp, and the leaf sweats, and drips, and feeds the thing below it. Ferns do not grow in cathedrals here; they grow everywhere, in cracks, on the backs of stones, on the bark of other ferns, on the backs of leaves. Three, four canopies of them, one above the other, never quite touching, never quite ceasing. A leaf, once opened, is not one life. It is a raft for mites, a perch for a frog, a drip-line, a shelter for something chewing its underside with a sound so fine you feel it in your jaw before you hear it. The mites move in the amber pulse of the veins, grazing the glow itself, perhaps, or the film the glow encourages. They are white, or clear, or black in that underlight, and they are so numerous that a leaf seems to shiver even when the air is still.

The ground, too, is a crowd. Under the leaf mat is a worm-carpet, moving in pulses, not in one direction. If you stand long enough you feel it through your boots — a slow heave, then another, as if the basin were breathing through the animals in it. Ants braid their columns around fresh basalt, carrying fragments larger than themselves, re-routing when a vent sighs and the scent-currents shift. Their columns unbraid and braid again, following heat as much as scent, moving a colony a stone's width to the left because the ground has shrugged a stone off a root and opened a new crack that steams. Beetles click against each other in the dark, a constant, nervous percussion, testing, mating, colliding, turning their glassy backs to the glow. Midges hang in the air above the warm pockets in clouds so still they look like smoke, until a jet of vapor sighs out of a seam and they scatter like ash, and settle again, and are snagged, some of them, by the webs.

The spiders have webbed the glades shut. Their sheets are so fine and so numerous that mist snags on them and hangs, and the cloth is alive. Small white things move on it — spiderlings, mites, trapped midges still kicking. The spiders themselves are pale and unhurried, rebuilding what the vines tear, what a swarm washes through, what a falling branch rips in a single afternoon. A web here is not a trap set and left; it is a lung, constantly re-spun, constantly dissolving back into the canopy a week after it is abandoned. Everything woven and tunnelled and alive is provisional. The termite mounds rise like smoking chimneys, and inside them other lives are stacked — beetles, mites, fungi, worms — living in the warmth the termites make and the termites live in, each using the other's architecture.

And the plants, themselves, live fast in the way of things that do not have to wait for the sun. In warm air, in fresh mineral soil that no rain has washed thin, a shoot will extend while you watch it. A bud swells and bursts with a pop like resin. On one wrist-thick vine, climbing a sharp rock and then climbing another vine, you can find the whole life at once: a flower opening at the tip, fruit ripening below it, leaves yellowing and dropping at the base, all within arm's reach. A bloom opens on a thousand plants at once because the warm air changed, not because a season turned. For a day the whole bowl smells sweet and fetid, and the flies come thick enough to blot the light they themselves make. Then the bloom collapses, and the flies, and the sweet smell turns, and something else opens higher up, in a different bowl, and the sound of it moves ridge to ridge. There is no normal state for them to be turbulent against. There is only pulse.

Above the low glow, in the canopies, are the louder lives. Insects stridulate in bands that start and stop and start again a half-beat later from a different patch of dark, so that the sound seems to crawl. Frogs answer from below. And from the canopy come the bird-shaped things — birds, or things that do what birds do — screaming and answering themselves, the answer delayed by the sharp ridges and arriving distorted, a second voice that is not quite the first. They are rarely seen whole, if at all. A shadow with too much weight for the branch it crosses. Eyes that shine amber and green where your light catches them — their own glow reflected, or thrown back — and then gone. They navigate, perhaps, by echo off fresh rock, by the warm updraft between bowls, by the sound of the crowd they are part of. When they lift, or when a swarm lifts off a rotting trunk, the whine is a single chord that fills your chest, and the condensation shakes loose in sheets, and there is a brief, startling rain from everything above you, a drumming patter that lasts a minute and leaves the air thick and then stops, and the insects are still hanging there, beaded, unswept.

The bigger presences move through all of it without parting it. A long pale limb uncurls from the canopy, feeling for the warm pocket-water, and hangs there, feeling, while frogs call up at it and mites cross its skin. It does not drink in any way you can see. It touches, and withdraws, and the branch it left seems lighter for a long time after. Farther off, a herd — no, a flock, though they walk — passes through a glade in a body so continuous that the far side is blocked for minutes. Hooves and feet and tails stir the leaf litter into brief weather. Ferns bend and spring back with grubs still in them. Ants scatter their columns and re-form behind the last tail. Their eyes shine where the glow catches them, amber and green, row after row, and then they are gone and the sound of them rolls away off the ridges, braiding with the sounds of the bowls they enter next.

They are, like everything here, temporary in form. The ground heaves a little every so often, shrugging, opening cracks that steam and fill with green within days. A sharp spire too thin to hold moisture stands bare and black and hissing in its own heat while plants crowd its base, waiting for it to cool enough to live on. A tree rots so fast at the base that it simply gives up one afternoon, and before it hits the ground, things are already living on the part that will land. Nothing persists in the form you found it, and so nothing here can afford to be solitary or still. They shelter and feed in the low glow, overlapping presence on presence, near-touching, in layers, hotter and wetter and louder than you expected.

Under a stand of broad-leaved trees, where the glow is steadiest and the litter is thinnest, there is a scatter of smooth stones, heel-sized, cracked shells all around them, piles of husks in a meticulous, repeated pattern on a flat slab. Something here has learned to use a tool and has not learned, or has not needed, to build anything to keep the tool in. The makers are only glimpsed: a hand-like shape, small and quick, fingers black with damp, a face turned toward the glow to see by it. They sit, or crouch, on the slab, select a stone, fit a shell into the cup of the rock, strike — not hard, precisely — until it gives. They eat what is inside, or carry it off, or leave it, and leave the husks sorted, shell half-shell, in a pattern that repeats from visit to visit. The stones lie where they were left, wet, already greening at the edges. The makers themselves do not stay to be seen staying. A vine reaches your shoulder, a swarm decides to move, the ground shifts under you, and they are gone, and the slab keeps its pattern without them, and the glow from the fern veins above it keeps pulsing amber when the air warms, as if the work were still going on in the light they made for themselves.

That is who lives there: the film on the water and the frogs in it, the grubs with lit bellies and the fungi that raise the dead fronds again, the mites on the underside of a single leaf and the spider who re-weaves the glade shut each morning-that-is-not-a-morning, the worm-carpet and the ant columns and the clicking beetles, the vines that are flower and fruit and dying leaf at once, the screaming canopy things and the pale limb that feels for water, the walking flock whose eyes shine green in the glow, the swarms that pass like fronts and shake the rain out of the leaves, and the small hand-like makers at their flat slab, cracking shells by underlight, leaving their stones to green.

Stand in one place a long time — if you can stand without touching something alive doing something to you — and they resolve, briefly, into their larger scale: bowl after bowl of them, ridge after ridge, each a little different in which swarm is passing, which vent is sighing, which plants are in bloom, which frogs are calling loudest. The air moves from bowl to bowl carrying spores and midges and the smell of crushed green things. The sounds braid together over the ridges into one continuous, breathing noise. And when you finally turn to go, because the place has changed its shape in the minutes you were there, the lives behind you do not pause. The films brighten and dim. The webs snag new mist. A shoot extends, just a little, while you look at it. The hand-like shape returns to the slab, face toward the glow, and strikes again.
