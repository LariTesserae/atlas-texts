If you come looking for whoever lives here, you will look first in the wrong places. You will check the houses — roofed, doors hung. You will check the stalls in the covered markets, carved ready for goods that never arrive. You will check the bathhouses, the granaries, the shrine-platforms on their terraces. All of it is in order, all of it is finished, and none of it is occupied in the way a house is occupied.

The first sign that anything is living at all is not a track or a face. It is a noise that comes from high up and does not stop. In the still, thick air, broad leaves rub against each other — *shhk-shhk*, endlessly, as if a crowd were shifting its weight and never moving. Somewhere in the canopy a seed pod pops with a sharp crack. A few seconds later, somewhere else, another. That is the closest this place gets to a clock, and to a voice.

Follow the sound up and you find the elders.

They are the emergent trees. Their trunks are black-green and so dense they ring like stone, and in places a trunk is not beside a house, it *is* a house. A wall has thickened around a hollow, a hollow has been smoothed by growth until it is a room, a room has locked its shape. The crowns tower over the basin roofs and merge into a broken canopy, so that from the rim the seven terraced towns look less like towns than like clearings in a forest that has learned geometry. These trees do not hurry. A plant here can live for as long as the city has been settled, because there is nothing to browse it, no pollinator to hurry it, no winter to end it. It grows at the edges, constantly, and dies at the edges, constantly, and the total green never changes by so much as a leaf. They are that old, and that young, at once.

One in the market square has made a home of its own hollow and expects you to understand it as a home. Inside the trunk is a small courtyard. There is a well in the middle, and a bench carved from the wood itself, and moss in the joints where the living walls meet the stone, and a door that opens onto the square again, on the other side of the tree. The tree does not contain the courtyard the way a pot contains a plant. The tree *is* the courtyard, grown around a void and then finished around it. Ferns live at the damp edge of the well. Roots form the bench's supports. Everything inside is cool, wet, and perfectly still, and the light that falls in has the same white-yellow as the light outside and never moves. It is a dwelling with one resident, and the resident is the dwelling.

Beneath the canopy, in the shade that never deepens because the sun never moves, are the middle dwellers. Palms. Tree ferns. Shrubs and giant herbaceous leaves that would curl in this heat if they were not drenched. They are drenched. Condensation is their weather. Every surface is a watershed, and so every leaf is beaded, every frond drips, and the dripping is part of their living. They drink from the air that thickens around them, from the steam that meets the thinness that should be here and refuses to be thin. They live under the elders the way tenants live under a roof, but there is no season to thin them out, and so the strata only thicken. Emergent, then palm, then shrub, then giant leaf, then — at the very bottom of the green — moss.

The moss is the floor. It lives on everything flat and vertical and in-between: on plaza stones where your foot leaves a dark bloom that does not go away, on the worn centers of the grand stairs, on temple stone where it has settled into green patina, on basalt columns, on red iron clay, on white sinter soft as chalk. It gleams like oil because it is never dry. It is the patina, the green in every joint, the thing that proves erosion here is not decay but a finish. If the trees are the elders and the canopy is their talk, the moss is the population that lives on the skin of the city itself. It does not root. It inhabits surfaces.

And between the trees, in the air where there should be soil, live the hanging ones. Orchids and bromeliads and fronds fall from every bridge, from aqueduct arches, from lintels and eaves, trailing ten meters toward the water below. They do not grow out of the ground. They grow out of stone, out of wood, out of the drip-lines themselves. A vine will bridge from roof to roof and make a green tunnel over a street that is also a canal, so that the water below is ankle-deep, knee-deep, always skirting a step, and you must wade and duck at once. That vine is a resident of two houses and one street and the canal between them. It belongs to none of them entirely. Its life is the crossing.

Roots do the same work underground, more slowly. A root heads down through a bathhouse floor and comes up through a granary slab on the other side of the square, lifting nothing, cracking nothing. It has negotiated its shape here long ago. This is how individuals live in this city: not contained by walls, but threaded through them. A single plant can be in the hot place and the cold place, in the public square and inside the hollow tree, in the stone and in the water, because the stone and the water and the plant were never really separated. The city and the jungle are not competing here. They are the same dense tissue. Stone gives the plants something firm to hold in ground that would otherwise be soup. Plants give the stone shade and a reason not to slump.

On the water itself lives another population. The aquatic mats skin the canals in green felt. They are not floating free and carried away, because the level never rises or falls and the current, such as it is, has nowhere new to go. They live on the surface, in the sheeting water over worn steps, in the pools where aqueduct mouths spill and every eave drips. Fallen leaves do not rot into leafmold here. They dissolve in hot, mineral water, feeding the mats downstream. The mats are the skin of the canals, and they are eaten by no one, and they grow at their edges and dissolve at their edges, and the green felt is exactly as thick tomorrow as it is today.

You will almost not see the fungi, and that is where most of the living that matters is done.

They are pale, thread-like growths, and they live on the undersides of leaves and in the black mud of the canals. They are the ones who eat, in a place with no mouths. With no animals, the work of eating and being eaten is done by water and heat and fungi, blooming and dissolving in the same steady state. Their spores drift in the steam, always in the same currents, always settling on the same surfaces. They have territories in the air over the central lake, in the covered streets where drips fall faster, on the open terraces where they fall slower. They live in the damp underside of everything — under the great leaves, under the travertine crust where hot ground exhales through wet seams, under the stones where your foot makes a bloom. They are the undertakers and the neighbours and the quiet population that never makes the *shhk-shhk* or the pop. If the plants are the city you can see, the fungi are the city that does the work of unmaking it, leaf by dissolved leaf, and remaking it again at the edge.

At the center, in the lowest terrace, the lake is divided and the two populations that live at it have learned to live across the division. On the west half the water is hot enough to scald, and white mineral crust grows in rings where it cools. On the east half it is cold and black and still. The line between them is sharp as a cut and does not waver. Plants grow right up to it from both sides, leaves leaning toward whichever half they prefer — the heat-lovers leaning west, the cold-lovers leaning east — and roots intertwined under the line, in water that should not be both. They are the only residents who can cross that border, because they cross it underground, where the line is a negotiation rather than a wall. Their leaves are separate. Their lives are not.

Further out, the impossible places have their own specialists, because nothing here can afford to be surprised by them. Where one of the main rivers splits at a bridge, the right branch flows *up* the cliff face in a smooth sheet, thirty meters to a ledge, and enters the rock. The plants along its upward course grow sideways, then up, then sideways again at the top, as if agreeing with it. They live oriented to a gravity that runs both ways. Where the grand stair rises for two hundred steps and brings you back to the level you left — the fourth terrace looking down on the third, the third looking up at the fourth, both correct — ferns and mosses live in the water-rills worn into the center of the steps, in the channel where unimaginable traffic once passed and now only water passes, forever, in a perfect line. Where shadows fall in two directions from the same noon light, the leaves accept both. Where a basin shows in reflection one extra tower that has no counterpart, the mats on the water skin the false tower as faithfully as the real one. A drip in a covered market is heard a fraction of a second before you see it strike the pool; the epiphytes on the stalls below are watered by the sound first, and by the water second, and they do not seem to mind the order.

Even the cliffs have residents. Black basalt faces sweat in vertical tears that gather into threads, threads into falls no thicker than a wrist, falling six meters, then vanishing sideways into a crack exactly the right size for them. Mosses and small ferns cling to that route, living in the weeping itself. They are not near the water. They are *in* its path, and the path has not moved in longer than they have lived.

To live here, then, is not to move, or to gather, or to leave. It is to return. Push a vine aside with your shoulder and it swings back to the same trembling position. Step through a puddle and it is a puddle again a breath later. The residents have a posture, and the place holds them in it. They grow at the edge and die at the edge, and the edge is always in the same place. The light does not change its angle, so they have no morning and no evening to keep. The heat does not build toward a night that is not coming. The roar under the city does not swell or fade. They have no predators to flee and no winter to survive, and so their whole life is extension: another leaf rubbed against another, another pod swelling somewhere in the canopy to pop, another thread of fungus blooming under a leaf and dissolving before it has finished blooming, another tenth of a meter of hanging frond trailing toward water it will never quite reach because the water is already everywhere.

After a while you understand that you are not looking at many populations. You are looking at one tissue that has learned to be a city. The trees are its roof-beams and its walls. The vines are its bridges. The roots are its passages between rooms. The moss is its weathering. The mats are its skin on the water. The fungi are its stomach. The spores in the steam are its breath, going round and round in currents that never leave the basins. The hot ground exhaling through travertine is its chest, beating under everything, felt in your teeth on the stair. Stone is its skeleton, and it does not need repair because it has finished being shaped, and the plants grow around the finished shape and hold it in shade.

It is inhabited to saturation and empty of anyone who could be visited. No birds to cross the canopy. No insects in the rot. No frogs in the canals, no fish in the lake, hot side or cold. Nothing with a face will ever answer the *shhk-shhk* of the leaves or the crack of the pods. And yet nothing here is dead. Everything that can live without moving, without season, without being eaten, lives here, exactly as it lived a thousand years from your footprints — because tomorrow, in the sense the rest of the world uses, has never arrived, and the residents have all the time there is, which is none at all, and it is enough.
