The census fails at once, and that is the first thing to learn about who lives here.

You can count the large things easily enough. Eight trees, perhaps nine if you count the one so fused with the rusted frame that it is hard to tell where trunk ends and iron begins. A few great boulders. The low crescent of stones in the northern silt. The pale disc near the center. The shallow central pool, and the rare long shape in it. If you stood on the rim and tried to list the inhabitants, you would be finished in five minutes, and completely wrong.

You have to kneel.

The mud between the green patches is not mud. It is a skin, and it is moving. Covering the silt, packed so densely in places that the color of the ground is their color, are the paddlers. Millions of them, pale and translucent, no longer than your thumbnail, blind, soft, with six small legs that do not so much step as paddle. They have no eyes and do not need them; they read the world through moisture and through the faint chemical film of algae and through the pressure of the bodies ahead of them. Their whole body is mostly gut — you can see it through the translucent skin, a dark thread of leaf-mulch and black-brown sponge moving slowly inside them, proof that they are eating even as they move.

They do not move as individuals. They move as a weather. A loose wave crosses a patch of silt over the course of an hour, thinning the algae film to a paler tone where it has passed, and the wave behind follows the same path the next day, and the day after. Lift a handful and it squirms and settles as one body, then disperses again on the surface like water finding its level. They are in the shallow pools in equal numbers, swimming up in motes when you disturb the edge, furred thick on any leaf you lift from the water, clinging to the undersides of stones, climbing, in a slower arboreal form, into the moss that furs the tree trunks. Wherever something is wet and stays wet, a version of them lives there.

Beneath them, in the sponge of the soil itself, are their slower cousins — blind, tube-like turners that you will never see unless you dig, and even then you will mostly see the effect of them. The black-brown soil is full to the surface because they are continually kneading it, making it hold water the way a sponge holds it, excreting a fine stabilizing slime that keeps every curve soft and every surface from drying or cracking. That is why your footprints fill with clear water and then smooth themselves out by the time you turn back. You are not walking on ground that happens to be full of animals. The ground is the animals, held together by their bodies and their slime, and when you compress it they flow back.

A paddler lives, if it is not eaten, a long time by any small standard. Not because it grows fast — nothing here grows fast — but because it does not need to hurry. It eats the algae film and the fallen leaf-mulch, and the algae living symbiotically in its own gut can use the light that filters through its skin. When the wave meets another wave at the edge of a patch, the animals at the interface cling and separate and seem to bud new bodies from the collective mass. A colony is more real than an individual. The individual is just a place where the colony is momentarily thick enough to be counted.

And the colony is fed from above, by the second population, which is also without count.

In the sunbeams the air is not empty. It is full of sun-motes. Even smaller than the paddlers, winged specks that catch the white-gold light and hang in it, never seeming to land. They are so numerous on a bright day that the light itself looks solid, a warm, slow snow that does not fall. Close up, each mote has a pair of dark wings and a body that is mostly a parasol of silk and gut, a creature built to float on humid air and feed on light and on the fine exhalations of the paddlers below. They do not sing or whine. They make no sound at all. Their only interaction with the solid world is to die in it. When one dies, or when the evening mist thickens and they descend a little, they snow down onto the silt and the pools, and the paddlers move toward the fall the way a wave moves toward a new patch of algae. Air feeds ground. Ground feeds air. The motes thicken at midday when the air is brightest and heaviest, thin a little at dusk, and are always, always, present.

Between the skin and the snow, the trees hold their distances and keep their own populations. Each tree is old beyond reckoning, low and leaning, its roots spread wide and shallow in the sponge as if anchoring were the least of their concerns. Under each canopy the lushness is sudden — ferns, film-ferns, broad leaves that fold and unfold, hanging mosses like wet hair. Those are not just plants. They are the tree's outer fauna, a green thatch that traps condensation and then weeps it, drop by separate drop, in bright weather. Animals live in that thatch, paddlers adapted to bark and to the undersides of leaves, grazing the micro-algae that grow on the weeping surfaces. The leaves themselves are collectors; they condense the permanent humidity and drip it to the roots, and the roots pass it to the soil-turners, who pass it back to the surface. A tree does not drop its leaves seasonally. It drops them constantly, a slow rain, and a leaf that falls in the morning is black mulch and paddler food within days. Nothing here fully decays. Decay is too final a word for a process that never leaves anything behind.

Then there is the hunter.

In the main pool, where the water is a forearm deep and tea-colored, something long and blind rises with the patience of a reed. It is not a plant. It glides barely disturbing the surface, and the paddlers at the edge thin out where it passes and slowly close again behind it, as if the skin were being brushed by a finger. There are perhaps two or three of them in the basin, each longer than your arm, coiled sinuously in the shallows. They have no eyes, no sharp teeth, no mouth that you can pick out at a glance. Their skin is the same translucent pale as the paddlers', but filled with a slower darkness. They hunt by the thinning itself — by sensing, through touch and through the water, where the herd is moving away, and by rising, impossibly slowly, into that space.

Predator and prey here do not rush. That is not slowness as a failure of energy but as a whole physics. The hunter will rise for an hour. It will hang in the surface film, breathing through its skin, while paddlers swim up in motes around it, unhurried. It will take its prey not with a strike but with an engulfing, a soft closing of a body that is almost all mouth and almost no head. When it dies — and even these live a very long time — it does not leave a skeleton. It dissolves back into mulch on the pool bottom within days, feeding the algae patch that feeds the paddlers that feed the next hunter. What it does while it lives is prune. Without it, a paddler wave would graze a patch to the mineral silt and starve. With it, the waves learn the shape of their pools.

The stones weep because they are inhabited, too. The slowest inhabitants of all are the stone-weepers, colonies living in the pores of the boulders and in the half-sunk ring of the northern silt, in the softened corners where erosion has done its work. They store water and release it in beads and in slow, separate drops. To look at a great stone in shade is to see its underside beaded, a surface breathing out. They set the drip that is the only regular sound besides your own squelch. They have been there, in some form, since the basin settled into its final curve, and they are a large part of why nothing here is ever sharp. Their slime, and the slime of the soil-turners, and the slime of the paddlers, rounds everything down. Even the broken fence-post has gone soft at its break because paddlers and stone-weepers and moss have colonized the fracture and made it part of the skin.

And at the center, where the water is clearest and always a little warmer underfoot, is the disc. You will at first take it for the last built thing, a piece of pale material left by whoever left the swing frame and the stone ring. It is level, it is always dry in the rain, water rolls off its edges beaded, as if repelled a millimeter above the surface. Stand on it and it is warm through your damp feet. It does not move. It does not need to. It is, by far, the largest single animal in the basin — flat, pale, ancient, its upper surface a shell or a skin or a grown-over colony so fused that no one could say where one body ends — and it is the heart of the temperature. The warmth that never falls toward evening, the mist that is warm on your skin, the water chemistry that stays perfect though nothing feeds it but sky and seepage: the disc radiates a slow heat, filters the pool through itself, and keeps the clear patch around it clear. Sun-motes gather above it more thickly than anywhere else and never land on it. Paddlers graze its perimeter but do not cross it. It is the one place in the basin that is not skin, and therefore the place that controls the skin.

The other remains are inhabited in the same thorough way. The stone ring in the north is now a gathering place for stone-weepers and for paddler waves that seem to return to it deliberately, as if remembering a shape. The metal frame, its crossbar rusted to a copper-green sag, is a mineral reef: the rust is food for stone colonies, the legs are fused into the tree trunk, absorbed and overgrown, the tree's thatch now wallowing in it. The twig you snap and find reattached by morning was not healed. It was re-laid, most likely, by a paddler wave moving across the silt in the warm mist, carrying leaf litter ahead of it and setting your twig back into the current of the skin, because the basin corrects itself to the same soft curves and its inhabitants are the correction.

There is no other keeper. No separate engineer stands above this system to maintain it. The maintenance is the inhabitants themselves, in their orbits: the trees drop leaves, the leaves become mulch, the mulch becomes algae, the algae becomes paddlers, the paddlers become hunters, the hunters become mulch, the mulch becomes trees. The sun-motes fall and become algae. The stone-weepers drip and become the humidity the motes float in. The soil-turners knead and keep the sponge from ever drying or hardening. The disc warms and filters. Nothing is loud, nothing is dry for long, nothing is allowed to grow out of its allotted patch — not because a rule is enforced, but because the grazers will be there tomorrow to graze it back, and the hunters will be there to graze the grazers, and the slime will round whatever edge tries to form.

Stay until evening and the rhythm shows itself without ever breaking into day and night. The pools that vanished at midday in evaporation reopen, tea-colored and ankle-deep, where there was only mud at noon. The motes descend a little into the warm mist. The paddler waves cross the bare silt between the tree patches — those twenty paces of apparent emptiness — taking hours to do a journey you could walk in a minute, leaving the pale grazed trail behind them that will green again with algae in a day. A hunter rises in the central pool. The trees weep a little faster. The disc stays warm.

You, walking through, are not a predator or a prey or even, to them, a large animal. You are weather. You part the skin and it closes behind you. You humidify the air with your breathing for a moment and the motes thicken around your face. You leave skin and hair and footfall in the silt, and by the time you look back your prints have filled with clear water and smoothed out, absorbed into the collective body that was there before you and will be there, breathing at the same slow, thorough pace, after you have gone.
