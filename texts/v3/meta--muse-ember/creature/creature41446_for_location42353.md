At first Marrek does not resolve into people at all. It resolves into sound and movement and damp — too many small lives overlapping to pick one out — and only slowly, as you stay, do the human inhabitants come forward from within that density, not above it.

The air is the outermost layer. Rock pigeons are everywhere there is a ledge to claim, and a ledge is rarely unclaimed for long. They nest in unused chimneys and hollow brickwork and under the decks of canal bridges, in colonies large enough to whitewash whole facades with guano, so that buildings look painted from a distance. In the heat of the day swifts cut low over the canals, quick and constant, and gulls come up from the cliff face to scream over the reservoirs. At dusk the tunnels give up their other population: bats pour out in a silent, continuous river, the whirr of wings in the tunnel mouths replacing, for a while, the cooing that has gone on all day. On the electric lines the birds sit thick, sagging the wires they perch on.

Below that, in the water, the population is even less countable. The canals carry slow, brown-green water at knee-height in some districts and shoulder-height in others, and the water itself moves as if it were thinking about it, because carp and eels thread the current so densely in the smelter channels that they change its flow. Snails coat the cut stone in a living grey, mussels cling to the pipes, frogs call from the dark, damp corners underneath bridges where the water has been weeping over the stone for centuries. Springs break out of warehouse walls and keep the gutters running even when it hasn't rained, and everything that lives here has learned to live in that permanent wetness.

On the ground, in the seams where pavers meet and water lingers, the living cover is not green. It is fungus — black and ochre mats, orange crusts, pale films that spread and recede with the seasons like a slow tide across wet concrete, across the shaded side of brick, across the timbers under canal bridges. It is quiet, it does not move quickly, and it is the only rooted living thing the plateau allows. Basalt sheds what little dust falls, the builders paved over what soil there might have been, and in living memory nothing leafed has taken hold in a crack, a courtyard, a terrace corner. The eye keeps looking for it out of habit and finds stone and fungus instead.

And all around that, in the warmth the bricks give back at noon, are the animals that live directly off the city itself. Rats run the gutters in broad daylight. Lizards sun flattened on the radiating stone. Cockroaches and moths and flies thicken around every warm engine and every scrap heap. Bees and wasps work the fungus blooms and hover, patient and incessant, around the open food stalls in the markets. Spiders have webbed every rafter in the engine halls, where the pistons turn slow and massive in unison behind open walls. Cats and dogs — feral, sleek, entirely urban — patrol the residential terraces, slipping between laundry lines and cages hung over the street, between houses stacked on workshops stacked on warehouses stacked on the vaulted waterworks underneath.

The people of Marrek live inside this, not in spite of it. They are workers and residents of the smelter district where the air smells of hot metal and fish, the textile district where the mist is white with steam, the terraces where washing hangs over rooms-outdoors roofed half the time by awnings and bridges and pipes. Their shirts stick to their backs from the damp that never leaves the air, their ears popped on the way up to the plateau and popped again coming down, their steps have worn the main stairways into shallow bowls. They import their grain and their meat through the markets, and the scraps that fall are part of the city's economy as surely as coal or current.

They expect the animals. It is a practical expectation, worked into the day. They oil the gears where pigeons have pushed twigs into the housings. They lift the grates where eels have tried to climb out of the canals. They sweep guano from the tram tracks before it makes them slick. They step over the carp-thick channels and under the drip that falls fine and warm from every pipe and cornice in the afternoon, when rain here rarely falls as rain. The animals, in turn, live off what the city cannot help giving them: warmth from the engines and the stone, water everywhere near the surface, fungus mats that never dry out, waste from mills and kitchens, shelter in every layered crack. Neither side is a visitor. You cannot find an empty wall for long; a filter left clean in the morning will be furred with nesting material by evening.

That is why the sound never really stops. It is not bursts but superposition — water dripping and rushing, pumps thumping in the waterworks, the low hum of the tram, metal clanging in the workshops, and over it all the animal chorus: cooing, chirring, calling, splashing, claws scrabbling on stone, wings in the tunnel mouths. At noon the whole place seems to be breathing with its mouth open. At night, when the stars are sharp over the plateau, the bats take over the high air and the frogs take over the low corners, and the city goes on underneath them, damp and warm.

Nothing here hurries, human or otherwise. The old residents will tell you that if you leave a street for a year you will come back to find it almost identical — a little more worn, a little more stained, a colony a little larger, a pump a little slower. The cycles are long: the slow pulse of the water table weeping through basalt, the slow growth of the fungal mats, the slow movement of the bird flocks between districts, the slow polishing of handrails to a shine and limestone corners to softness by water and by hands.

So Marrek is inhabited, completely and insistently, but not by people alone, and not by plants at all. It is inhabited by a dense, slow, animal city built inside a stone and machine city, with humans living in the middle of it — oiling, sweeping, lifting, walking, sleeping above the waterworks — while pigeons shift on every ledge, eels thread the canals, fungus tides in the seams, and the whole plateau holds its heat and its damp and its noise, settled rather than abandoned, alive rather than mechanical.
