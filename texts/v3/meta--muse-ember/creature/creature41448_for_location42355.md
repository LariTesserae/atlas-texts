It is wet first, and life here begins that way too.

The first lives are not animals, and they are not on your feet. They are in the water itself, a thin skin over the warm pools where the gutters gather and the vents drip. It catches the light and throws it back in impossible colors, green and violet and peacock-blue, though there is no oil in it. That is bacterial film, feeding on the warmth and the waste water the place makes without pause, tightening and loosening with the temperature. You could put your hand through it and it would close behind your fingers.

From that film, in the dark inside the vents and the pipes, hang the black fungal mats. They do not spread. They do not reach outward for light, because there is nothing to reach for in the joints between slabs. They stay where the damp is constant and the heat is constant, soft and dusty at the edges, wet at the center, in a layer you can see only if you lean close to a grille. A little farther out, where the water is warmest and stillest, the midges rise from the film in clouds. They hang for a while, settle, rise again, thick enough to be a weather of their own over a pool. They are food, and that is most of what they are for. Everything here is food for something else at exactly the rate it is needed.

In the walls the scale changes but the principle does not. Behind iron plates and under eaves and in the cavities where a cable run meets a brick wall, beetles live in the dark, small and black and slow, moving as if through shallow water even on dry concrete. Spiders have strung their webs between artificial structures because only artificial structures are there to string between — rail to rail, pipe to lintel, gantry to gantry. The webs sag under collected water, beads strung on the threads, and the spiders sit in the middle of that damp geometry for hours, not weaving new ones so much as maintaining the same ones, repaired as they decay, the way everything else is repaired.

Below the main level, in the canals and culverts and straight, canal-straight channels, the fish live in such numbers that the surface breaks and breaks again. They do not leap. They do not dart. They swim their reaches back and forth, turn at the concrete end, and swim back, mouths sometimes at the surface, gills working in the warm water. From above you see only the interference of their bodies, a constant shivering of the reflections. They are fed by the same loop that feeds the film, and in return they feed the birds that come down to walk the canal edges, deliberate and unhurried, in the shallows among the puddles.

At the main level the animals are domestic, and they have gone past feral into something steadier than wild. You meet them first as a mass before you meet them as individuals. Cattle standing fetlock-deep in the wet sheeting of a yard, shoulder to shoulder, black and white and brown coats damp through to the skin. They breathe and shift a few inches and settle, a slow weather moving through the herd that takes minutes to cross it. Their eyes are half-lidded against the haze. They do not turn to look at you.

Sheep are closer packed, wool heavy with moisture, the smell of lanolin warm and close in the damp air. They keep their heads down when they are not moving, and when they do move it is all at once in the center of the flock and not at all at the edges, like something being pressed and released. Pigs lie in long concrete pens with their snouts against the wet floor, pink skin almost translucent in this humidity, breathing audibly but softly, the sound of them a low accompaniment to the dripping. Goats have taken the raised platforms and the lower containers, standing above the herds, beards wet, listening to nothing.

In the loading bays that have become habitats, the poultry are in their thousands. White and brown and speckled, they move in ripples rather than in flocks, a surface tension of bodies that parts around your ankles and closes behind you. There is no dust here, because nothing is ever dry enough to make dust. Their feathers are damp. Their feet are clean in a way feet on concrete should not be, washed constantly by the film of water they stand in.

Dogs sleep in doorways, by the hundreds in a long street, one against the warm wall and another curled in the lee of a bollard. They are of no particular breed and of all of them, long and short, rough-coated and smooth, and they do not bark at you. They lift their heads, judge the weight of your step, and put them down again. Cats have taken the warm ducts and the tops of electrical cabinets and the sun-warmed — no, the engine-warmed — ledges, stretched to the full length of themselves with their paws tucked, because warmth is the one thing the place provides in surplus and they have learned exactly where it pools. A cat will let you walk past within a hand's width and will not move. It will blink, once, slowly, in the late-morning light that never changes.

Above, the birds. Pigeons so thick on a ledge that the stone looks speckled, breast to breast, cooing occasionally in a sound so low it is more felt than heard. On the wires, birds in rows, black against the wet haze, feet gripping and releasing and gripping again. And higher and farther, the flocks that wheel over several blocks at once in one silent body, turning as if on a mechanism, casting no shadow you can trust on the slabs below. They come down to the pools for the midges, to the canal edges for the fish, to the feed that is always being made in the mills and never quite runs out. Nothing drives them. Nothing startens them. Hunger does not make them dramatic. They are fed from the same industrial loop that feeds everything else, and their numbers do not rise or crash. A flock circles the same blocks in the same late morning.

None of them grows outward. That is the thing you learn after the first hour, the way you learn not to look for green. A calf does not appear in a field and take its first steps in front of you. Animals are born in the warm lower levels, in the insulated cells below the yards, and arrive in the traffic of the place at their allotted size, as if they had always been there. They live their span in the shuffle and the press of bodies, breathing the same warm, damp air, panting lightly because there is nowhere cooler to move to, and then they are not there. You do not see a death. The gap closes. The herd shifts a few inches and settles. The mechanism that removes them is the same mechanism that repairs the rust at the rate it would rust, invisible in its completeness.

They are not hungry in any dramatic way. Lice live in the wool and the feathers and the coats, mites in the ears, flies settling on the eyes of the cattle in the afternoon that is always late morning — and they too are in equilibrium, never an outbreak, never quite gone, a small steady tax on the abundance. The beetles eat what falls. The spiders eat the midges that miss the birds. The birds eat the midges that miss the fish. It is a closed circuit drawn in living things, and everything in it is calm because nothing in it is allowed to peak.

Once in a long while, walking your loop, you meet another walker. Or perhaps it is the same one you met hours ago at a junction you cannot be sure you have passed before. They are damp at the hems like you are. They carry nothing, or they carry the same tools, and they do not hurry, and they do not ask you where you are going, because there is nowhere to be going to that is not the next identical junction. If people live here, they live as the animals live: housed by industry, fed by the loop, warmed by the engines, learning the slabs and the weeping walls by heart, leaving footprints in the shallow water between them that fill and level and are exactly as they were before.

That is what lives in the Flatworks. Not a wilderness, and not a farm, and not a zoo. A population that has been brought to the instant of its completion and held there, wet and warm, young and unaging, crowded to near-touching and completely still, breathing in and out and in again like the place itself.
