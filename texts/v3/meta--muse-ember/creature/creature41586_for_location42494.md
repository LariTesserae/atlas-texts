If you stand on a bridge over a canal and wait for something to swim past underneath, nothing does.

That is the first thing to understand about what lives in the Pocket, and it takes a while to become clear, because everything else works so hard to imitate it. The water is twelve to fourteen degrees and clear enough to read a sign painted on the canal floor. At noon the sun comes through the canopy with ordinary force and casts ordinary shadows on drowned doorways and empty parking bays. You can see all the way down. No fish turn in the current between the tower legs. No insects sit on the warm handrails. No rats work the docks, no birds use the slot of sky in the Lattice. The docks were built for them, the bridges are built for them, and the absence is not an oversight. It is the whole design.

What moves, then, is almost always something that was made to move in their place.

The only things that could be called alive in the older, biological sense are the things that are allowed to be soft, and even those are not quite wild. On the north-facing walls only, where the builders have decided a little softness is permissible, moss and biofilm green the composite concrete in thin, careful patches. Algae is born here — it is, with buildings, the only thing that is — but the water is kept sterile and the clarity is the proof of it: nothing clouds it, nothing swims in it to stir it. 

The kelp is more deliberate than that. Long dark strands of engineered kelp are braided into fences and moorings around the Docks, and they sway not in a wind but in the pump-currents, nodding in the quadruple thump that comes up through the basin floor and into your teeth. They do not root where they choose. They are placed where a mooring is needed. The same is true of the salt-grass mats on the floating planters in the Gardens, bobbing at the waterline where the light is kindest. They are not tended by gardeners. Trimmers move along rails above them, keeping them to a useful height, a useful green. It is vegetation as infrastructure, sparse and almost entirely useful, and it lives only because the city has made room for it to live.

Everything else that occupies a niche has been slipped into it by machine.

The builder-bots are the detritivores. They consume rubble and old print, nosing into berths that were godowns, clearing what a previous day built so that today can build over it. The repair bots move through walls like fungus, eating damage — a crack is not decay, it is an instruction, and they are the instruction followed. Sensor swarms travel in murmurs that look, from an upper gallery, like starlings, wheeling and turning together over the canals for no reason you can see, making the same sudden coordinated turns. Drone flocks make their shift-change circuits all day, whining, pausing for the tide bell — one clear note for the turn, answered by three — and then resuming. Submersible traffic uses the canals exactly as fish would, nosing in and out of dock faces, bolted through the basin in transit tubes without wakes, bending around corners at impossible angles. A hydrofoil route takes the place of pedestrians at high water. A ferry route crosses a pedestrian bridge at a different level, and both are used constantly, but what crosses is pods of parts, pods of glass on their way to a window that has not yet been built, pods moving inside held-air bubble-streets while a builder-bot swims past the transparent wall at eye level.

Nothing eats anything else. Workshops hunt for parts, parts hunt for workshops, builders hunt for empty water to fill. It is predator and prey translated into logistics, and the pace is biological even though there is no biology left to pace it.

They do not sleep, and they are not born. They are printed, deployed, recalled, reabsorbed. A tower ends abruptly mid-floor in a field of rebar and print-arms, its last storey not yet decided, while below it a bootstrap of shops and transit tubes hums, finished and working. The city is perpetually about eleven years old. Anything that cracks is reprinted that day. Rust is scraped before it can stain. A broken pane pales, shivers, and re-knits itself. Walls are phase-set — solid to pressure, yielding to a maintenance drone with the right resonance — and doors announce themselves a moment before a hand, or a manipulator, reaches them. Materials remember. That is as close to a life-cycle as anything here gets: to be built, to be busy building itself, to be taken apart and printed again.

That first unwritten rule is really the description of the population: everything submerged must be buoyant, sealed, or busy building itself. There are no gaps to hide in. Buildings share load-bearing walls, balconies interlock, a rooftop is the foundation of the next tower and the tram stop for the one after. Pipes run in braided bundles across facades, hung with signs and valves and cables as thick as masts. Heat shimmers off equipment. Water drips in predicted places. Condensation forms on the canopy and falls as a fine, cool rain that never reaches the water, evaporating halfway down on warm machinery. The air in the dry pockets, held like inverted hulls inside the water, tastes of ozone and wet stone and machine oil and minerals. It is a weather system made by the crowd of presences, even though there is no one in it to feel crowded.

At high water the ground floors vanish and the canals ring like a struck bowl filled to the brim, sound arriving late from its source, a clank from the lower docks reaching the upper Gardens a second after the arm has finished its swing. At low water the plaza mud appears, fresh and pale, and walkways emerge slick and steaming until the next tide takes them back. Water is the calendar. High water means docks, low water means streets, and the bells are allowed to argue with the sun about which matters more.

So what lives in the Pocket, if you let the answer accumulate rather than settle on the first swimming shape that isn’t there, is not a population at all. It is a set of occupants performing the functions of life without the life: kelp holding a fence, grass holding a waterline, bots eating rubble and damage, swarms wheeling like birds, traffic moving like fish through streets that drown and reappear twice a day.

The place sounds alive for that reason. Pumps thump, print-arms strike new floors into being with a sound like a distant weld, air hisses in and out of dry streets, bells answer bells. It is the sound of a city being alive without anything living in it — perpetually constructing, perpetually operating, and never, in any sense the water would recognize, alive at all.
