First there is a tap.

Three taps under a slab, pause, three taps again. It is not loud enough to carry. Nothing here carries. But at knee height, with a hand in the cool underneath the stone, you can feel it through the rock — a leg, a body shifting in damp cold that has held all night.

Under every slab is a small, crowded winter. Glass beetles, dark and translucent, crowded bell-to-bell in the mineral film. Iron beetles, duller, heavier, standing motionless until the stone shivers and then tapping back, a Morse of disturbance. Shed skins folded like tissue. Eggs tucked where the seam stays wettest. A fungus pale as bone-spittle eating the dust that the wind has packed in for years. At dawn they all drink. They turn to the underside of the stone, to the corrugated drip-line of a shed roof, to the inner curve of a rusted drum where rainwater has not quite run off, and lap at the condensation as if the stone itself were sweating. By mid-morning the slab is too hot on top to touch, and the whole population has withdrawn an inch deeper, into the one place that stays cold.

The lizards do the opposite. They are the shelf in the heat.

By the thousand, sun-baked and swift, scales translucent against the UV, they pour out of the cracks when the stone begins to give back the night. Morning is for mating in the cool — quick, formal encounters on the flat of a basalt polygon, a meter across, lifting slightly at its edges. Then mid-morning they are everywhere in a different way: not resting, but running. At this altitude a lizard cannot sit still on black rock at noon without cooking; it must run to cool its belly, run in short, frantic bursts from shadow to shadow, across ground that would blister a human hand. That is when the hawks time them.

The hawks do not call. They ride. All day the hot stone sends up thermals, silent elevators, and the hawks use them like a road. A constant traffic, high and patient, circling the caldera rim and the second mesa and the volcano that never gets closer. When a lizard is forced into the open to run, a shadow passes and the run ends. The rest of the shelf watches and keeps its hours: lizards mate in the cool, insects swarm in the heat, hawks hunt at noon.

Under the things that were built for heat, other lives keep to the warmth that never leaves.

Mice with enormous ears and almost no tail live their whole lives under concrete footings, in the warm hollow where a power house has sunk half into ash. They never surface at noon. They run at dawn and again after the thirty-degree fall at evening, in the brief country of cold stone, along paths worn into pumice by their own feet. Their nests are lined with cable strands, carried strand by strand into the cracks by the whole community — lizards too use the cables, threading them as nesting material, as shelter, as a bridge between machines that were never meant to be touched.

In the shade of a pipeline, under a solar condenser, where condensation collects all morning, the shelf suddenly goes green and packed. Cushion rosettes the size of dinner plates wedge themselves into cracks where a bird has nested and left guano; their leaves overlap, succulent and tight against a world that would dry them out in an hour. In between the leaves live insects, and inside the insects, almost invisibly, live spiders. Insects inside leaves, spiders inside insects — fertility here is not spread. It is imported in droppings, gathered in shelter, concentrated. Tussock grasses grow in lines following wind and water, their roots holding a whole cobble together. And on the north faces of machines — the one face the sun does not blaze — iron-blooming lichens spread orange as rust, very slowly, year after year, living on metal that is itself rusting.

Other plants appear only to sample the world. Small flowering things push out after rain, open for a few hours, and close again by nightfall as if they do not fully trust this place to stay wet. Rain comes violently for twenty minutes, hisses on hot rock, runs brown into the cracks, and is gone by sunset. Everything that lives here has learned to live on surfaces, not pools. In a footprint made by a boot in ash, there is water enough for a beetle for a day. In the hollow of a palm-sized basin, mineral-white water settles and crawls with tiny shrimp-like things, pale and translucent, turning over in water that boils at a temperature that feels cool to a human hand. You can put your hand near a steam vent and feel nothing like heat — at this pressure the physics is a trick — and the white plume races sideways along the ground instead of rising, and the steam moths come to it at dusk to feed on heat as much as on anything else, hundreds of them, wings beating in the first cold.

The swifts live shoulder to shoulder.

In the basalt columns hundreds of nests sit inches apart, their droppings whitewashing the rock below in a thick, permanent stain. At close range they are deafening, a rapid insectile chitter that fills the cliff face. Step ten meters back and the sound bends upward and dies in the thin air. That is the rule for almost everything here: noise dies unless it is metal on metal, or water on hot stone. The world is too busy down low to waste energy calling across the open. A pump clanking a hundred meters away is a vibration in your teeth before it is a sound. A condenser dripping onto pumice below makes a clear metallic tick, steady for an hour after dawn, like a slow clock for everyone who has gathered around it to drink.

Because the machines are the watering holes now. The solar arrays, cracked white with dust, tilt at the sun and never move. The heat exchangers hiss and radiate their pipes for kilometers in straight lines that cut across the natural polygons of the stone. The long metal hall near the rim that once smelted sulfur ticks in the sun, cooling and contracting, cooling and contracting, all day and all night, a slow pulse that the beetles and lizards have learned to ignore. Nothing is maintained for people. The machines run themselves on their schedules, attract shade-seekers, provide perches and cavities and drips. Fuel drums rusted to the color of the soil are rain traps for lizards. A shed with a collapsed roof is a colony. A weather station is a cliff.

Walk ten meters and you pass more evidence of life than you would expect from a whole valley below: nests, shells, bones collected by wind in the lee of a rock, shed skins, eggs in cable loops, the whitewash under the swifts, the orange bloom on the north side of a pump station, the green intensity in the lee of a condenser. Predator and prey keep their frantic, formal hours. Dust devils pick up seed and screws and beetle shells and redistribute them evenly, and the shelf accepts them all.

At evening the stone gives up its heat all at once. Dew forms instantly on every metal surface. The surface runners go under. The under-slab world wakes again, tapping. The mice come out. The moths come over the vents. The machines, which have been cooking silently all day, start a second cycle in the cold, coughing on at dawn when the temperature difference between night air and warm earth is enough to condense and to run.

It is not hospitable, not hostile. It is settled. A high, cold-air place that is hot. A dry place always damp in the cracks. A packed density that from a distance looks like nothing. Everything that lives here lives close to the ground, close to a seam, close to a drip — small, fast, and furiously, quietly alive.
