If you listen first, before you look, what is alive here does not announce itself the way living things do elsewhere.

It drips. Tick-tick from the gutters. The cluck of a pump starting and stopping. The soft rush in the runnels where water that has been intercepted off a fin, or condensed off a hot pipe, or sprayed over a garden forty minutes ago, is being taken that much closer to where it will be used again. Under the deck, through the soles of your boots, there is a thrum that is not quite sound — press shop, steam hiss, the big engines in their damp insulated rooms turning over and over. It is a low, wet breathing, and it never quite stops.

Then the green comes forward, because it is louder than anything else that lives here, and it is alive in a way that is difficult to separate from the building it covers.

It is not wild. Nothing here is allowed to be. The turf you walk on in the southern terraces is a module — pressed fibre and algae and mineral pellet — slumped a little where it is wettest, held by interlocking shells below it. The sedge and small bright ferns on the low factory roof were encouraged, in mats, to hold the rain. From the gutters hang curtains of fern and water hyacinth, roots dangling in the nutrient runnels, deliberately farming the runoff before it reaches the sumps. The trees are real, but each stands in a container-vat, its roots in a dark bubbling tank, its trunk laced to the frame with cables it is growing around and around. Fruit trees have been grafted flat, espaliered across the sides of air ducts. Rice grows in suspended troughs that travel two centimeters a day along a track, so no blade has to endure the same light too long.

They thrive, and in thriving they do what living things do despite the diagram. The vines find the bolt-holes. The algae chooses the warmest sump, not the one intended for it, and skins itself across it in a slow green. The condensation chooses the coldest rib of a roof and weeps there no matter what was planned. In the pipe-vents, where it is always warm and always wet, a phosphorescent moss was seeded years ago. It has escaped a little. At dusk it creeps in pale green seams along the ceiling of the underwalk and makes the shadows under the catwalks glow faintly, as if the place were phosphorescing to itself.

Under and between all of that are the smaller lives that make the loops work: the mats of algae being stirred by slow paddles to keep them from stagnating, the bacterial heat in the compost piles that must be turned and shredded and moved or they will rot, the thin biofilms inside every pipe joint where the water sweats. Much is in suspension, much is invisible, but the whole hamlet would sour in days without them. They are the true residents of the water, which is to say, of almost everything.

Animals, by any ordinary measure, are an absence. There are no insects to speak of — the fans and the perpetual mist keep them from establishing, and anything that arrives is treated as a contaminant in the nutrient loop. No pollinators, no grazers. The lushness has no fauna to match it, and so all the work that would be done by other mouths and wings has to be done by hands.

There are two exceptions, and because they are rare they are noted to the next shift like weather.

In the lower sumps and the pipes that feed them, a few amphibians brought up years ago have persisted. Pale and translucent, living on algae, they are not quite above the water and not quite of it. You see one occasionally in a utility pool that is supposed to be only a warm, shallow sump, still as a piece of wet glass, and then it is gone into the plumbing.

And in the warm vent of the high tower, a pair of white birds nests each season. Very clean birds. Quiet. They circle once in the morning over the parapet, over the cloud sea, and then they are gone into it for the rest of the day. They are the only wild song this wet green place has, and they do not sing.

The rest of the living that matter here belong to the third category: the people.

You see them first not as individuals but as movement at walking pace, in pairs. Two meters above a lane a pair on a gantry bolting a new water tank into place. At ground level, in yellow slicks, a pair carrying baskets of cuttings toward the compost shredder. In the rice troughs, their boots in warm ankle-deep water so clean you can see the skin of their feet, a pair harvesting the moving crop hand over hand, a blade at a time.

Up close they are damp all the time. The damp of the place gets into their clothes, their hair, the hems of the dormitory blankets. Their skin is soft from the permanent warm pocket of air — high, but not cold, the light thin and bright and diffuse through evaporation, the pressure in their ears long since adjusted. They have learned the quiet without being told. Shouting carries too far across the flat exposed plateau, and everyone knows everyone else’s shift. A voice, when it comes, is low and unhurried, a tool set down producing a clear clank that stands out precisely because it is rare. They do not rush because rushing in a one-meter lane where a crane is lifting a module into place, where nothing can fall, would be a kind of rudeness to gravity itself.

They live, mostly, in the south. The southern terraces are old by the standards of this place: fully overgrown, steel struts padded with vines, workshops occupied, roofs heavy with compost and crops. The dormitories are there too, small and layered and close, their walls weeping a little where new concrete is still curing, dark streaks running down like an old cliff seep though the walls are three weeks old. Their homes are not finished, because nothing here is allowed to be finished. A lane that was a lane last month is now a wall of beans. A terrace that was being built is now being tended, and will sink a little under its own lush weight, and have its water rerouted.

In the north, at the embryonic end, the same kind of people look different only because the landscape around them has not yet become landscape. Rebar rises like a thicket of winter twigs. A hillside that will one day be a garden is at present aggregate and polythene staked down against the wind. Scaffolding is the forest, and the ferns are already exploring its lattice, tasting it. The builders work in the same yellow slicks, saying less. At night they become the drainage shift, listening for a pump that has missed its cycle, walking the catwalks with torches while the phosphor-moss glows overhead.

They do the work of the absent animals. They pollinate by hand, moving from espaliered fruit tree to espaliered fruit tree along the air ducts. They prune, and carry away the cuttings so they will not rot. They thin the rice, stir the sumps, brush the algae off a sensor, unclog a fin where fog has been intercepted too thickly. Some of it is done by drones — slow, ungainly machines that bump gently off cables, that hover and sway over the gardens during a spray cycle — but drones are not trusted with anything delicate. The delicate work is human because it requires touch, and because touch is the only pollinator left.

Their rhythms are not day and night as much as cycle and shift. Every forty minutes the fans turn, the spray-bars sigh, and a fine warm mist goes over everything. Between cycles, condensation gathers and runs and gathers again. Harvest days, planting days, construction lifts, night-shift drainage — they overlap and cross each other vertically, a crew two meters above another crew, a conveyor of woven matting drying in the sun on a roof while beneath it the press shop thrums. To live here is to live inside several loops at once: irrigation, compost, nutrient, heat, and to keep them all moving, because stillness is rot.

And so they endure as the keystone species of a small world high above the weather, manufacturing its own landscape. They are imported, like the soil. They come up through the cloud sea with the pressure change real in their ears, they stay for seasons, they learn the pace, the low voice, the wet boots, the names of everyone else on shift, and eventually they go down again. No one is born here. The hamlet persists by keeping its people moving, as it keeps its water moving, as it keeps its rice moving two centimeters a day.

Look back from the parapet at the very top, where the newest frames are still bone-white and the old ferns lean out over the straight drop into nothing you can see. The people below you are small, and paired, and damp, and quiet. They are not separate from the green or the water or the moss glowing faintly under the walkway. They are the hands that tend it, which is to say, in this made place, they are part of what it is to be alive there at all.
