If you kneel, as you must, the first living thing is not a thing at all. It is the ground deciding to be alive, continuously.

The mat is one mass fed by millions of mouths. A hand's breadth down you are not in soil but in weave — rhizome over rhizome, fine grass-root and thick cable-root and the fleshy, pale storage roots that thrust themselves outward and upward in tight, white fingers. Between them, black-green leaf mould that never quite rots, and fungi in their thousands, stitching it all together with a mycelial net so dense that a footfall finds a spring and a release everywhere. This is plant as place and plant as animal: when something dies on top, the mat does not wait for decay. The white roots come. Grader beetles come. Flies come in a brief, iridescent weather. Nothing is allowed to lie long enough to touch stone.

In the hollows of that weave the permanent residents live because they cannot afford to leave. The choir-beetles are the size of your thumbnail and translucent amber when the dawn shine catches them, but you rarely see them. You hear them. Each lives in a little round chamber chewed in a rhizome, and rubs its wing-cases together with forelegs built like files. A single beetle is a tick. A hollow full of them is a hum. A whole hillside full of hollows is the sound you feel in your teeth. They do not sing to be loud; they sing because the mat carries vibration better than air up here, and because their world is dark and narrow and the only way to know where another beetle is, or if the temperature in your chamber is right for eggs, is to touch the weave and let it speak. When the sun warms the mat the note rises. When something heavy steps overhead it drops and restarts, like a choir catching its breath.

Around them, almost invisible in the filaments, the file-mites work. They are not bigger than a pinhead and they rasp in that same key, only higher, eating the fungal threads and the soft skin of roots. You would never find one unless a grazer's foot paused and the mat dimpled and a hundred of them scurried at once, a grey shiver. And lower still, in the slow blind tunnels no wider than your wrist, the ripple-burrowers go back and forth all day, eating their way through rhizome and leaving a chamber behind them, sending a slow wave ahead of their digging that you can see travel across the surface for a meter or two before it arrives under your boots. At night the plateau is full of these travelling ripples, as if something enormous were breathing just under the skin.

At dawn the whole country sweats, and everything that lives on the surface wakes to drink it. The condensation stands as a thin shine on every leaf and filament for perhaps an hour. The dew-runners — small, long-legged, hyaline insects with mouthparts like paintbrushes — surf the shine, licking it up as they go, so fast and so many that a fresh patch of mat can seem to sparkle and dry in minutes. Tiny grazers, springtail-like, hop from pore to pore. The choir-beetles come up to the surface chambers to sing louder into the damp air. It is the busiest hour, frantic and bright and wet, and then the sun takes the water back and the work narrows to endurance.

The giants are the only other plants that step out of the mat as individuals, and even they are not separate. A stalk-giant rises on a trunk of living wood, wound tight with vines which are themselves flowering, and spreads a crown of leaves each as wide as a small boat. From below, for a kilometer, you can see one. Up close, it is architecture and animal at once: the trunk is hollow at the center with a slow circulation of sap, the vines are host to gall-wasps and nectar beetles, the underside of each leaf is a farm — a condensation trap where water beads and falls in irregular drips, and a roost where the big flyers hang upside down with their wings folded like closed fans. The leaves lift and settle not simply by light but by the hum. When the subterranean chorus rises in the morning warmth, the crowns lift, opening their undersides. When the chorus falls to its baseline thrum in the dry afternoon, they settle, conserving damp. A field of giants, spaced a hundred paces apart, therefore seems to breathe in unison, very slowly, very far apart, but everywhere at once if you learn to watch with your feet as well as your eyes.

Between the giants, and because of them, the grazers move. They are the size of houses and move like walking architecture. Six legs, perhaps, each as long as a tree trunk is tall, jointed with a sideways shuffle, carrying a body that is less a body than a platform — broad, flat-backed, covered in a shaggy pelt of long, coarse hair that itself shelters mites and small beetles and a kind of hanging moss. Their heads are low and their necks short; they do not lift to browse, they lower their whole forequarters a fraction and eat the mat standing up, snipping and sucking and leaving behind them a clean, pale swath that is raw and mineral for a day, then greens again from the edges inward. They go slowly because the air is thin and the mat is deep and because there is nowhere to hurry to. Their breath steams faintly even at noon. Their dung is dark, steaming, rich, and it is gone within the hour, not because it rots but because things arrive to eat it from inside the mat.

They are not hidden animals. Almost nothing here is. A grazer is preyed upon openly, in full light, by the sail-stoopers. These are not birds — they do not have feathers — but they fly as if the sky were a sea, with wings the size of sails stretched on long finger bones, veined and translucent at the edges, beating once, twice, and then holding a glide that can cross a whole valley in view. From a kilometer away you can see the shadow start, can see the grazer go still, can see the long stately chase begin across the open sponge: the predator stoops not in a dive but in a slow, terrible tilt, the grazer turns its great platform body with its stilt legs in a wide, ponderous arc, the mat rippling under their combined weight. Sometimes the stooper misses and rises again to circle for an hour. Sometimes it succeeds, and then the sky fills — not with a frenzy, but with a stately descent of other stoopers and the screaming, sawing carrion flies that do the real work — and in half a day the white roots and the beetles have taken the carcass into the weave and there is no bone left exposed to the sun. The place eats its own dead where they fall, quickly and without ceremony, because to leave a body would be to leave a hole in the skin.

Twice a day, sometimes not for days, something else moves through. The dark layers. Swarming flyers in numbers that block the sun for minutes. They are not one species but a migrating community — winged ants as big as your hand, nectar moths, sawing midges, the small parasitic wasps that ride the grazers — traveling together across the tablelands from one mat to the next, following the weather fronts you can see far below. Their wings make a second wind. Their calls layer over the ground chorus until your ears ache with pressure. They bring with them a rain of dung and scales and dead that feeds the mat in a pulse, and they carry away pollen from the giant crowns and spores from the fungi. When they pass, the light returns, the ground hum resumes its dominance, and for a while after you can smell them — sweet, animal, faintly of hot iron.

In the dry afternoon everyone and everything is pressed down. The grazers stand in the shade of a giant if they can, or stand still with their legs tucked and let the sun harden their backs. The stoopers roost, wings folded, on the high rim of a crown, heads turned to the wind. The surface insects go under. The mat itself closes its filaments, conserving what damp remains, and the chorus narrows to wind across open green and a single, endless insect note, high and clear, from a hole no one ever finds. This is the empty-looking time. It is not empty. It is holding.

The builders live in the interstices of all this, and they are the hardest to see and the easiest to miss if you are listening only with your ears. They are small and dark, no taller than your knee, with skin the color of wet bark and feet broad and padded, built less for walking than for feeling. They live, as you have been told, largely inside the hollow trunks of dead giants — the dry, warm cylinders left when a crown falls and the trunk splits and dries but does not rot, because the mat has already claimed the outside and the inside is too dry for fungus — and under the lifted edge of the mat where it swells over rock, in low chambers woven shut with living vine and dead fiber. A family may occupy one dead giant for generations, plugging its base with stones polished smooth by handling, hanging woven cups from the living leaves above to catch the drips, keeping a fire-pit that is almost always cold.

They do not speak across distance. They cannot. The wind and the chorus would take any voice. Instead they move by vibration and signal, as the beetles do, but with hands. A foot pressed to a trunk tells you who is inside. A plucked vine, pulled at a certain rhythm, sends a message along the living wood to the next hollow a hundred paces away, and the giant's crown, already trembling with the hum, carries it farther. A tap on a polished stone, three short and one long, is a call for water. When the grazers pass, the builders come out and follow in their pale swath, not to hunt — they have no weapon that would matter to a house — but to gather the small things the grazer's feet have uncovered: chipped obsidian nodules from under the mat, bone awls shed from a stooper's kill, fiber for baskets and for the simple loom frames you find propped against a trunk. They plant nothing. They nudge the mat, cut a shoot here, weave a cup there, and leave the pale tracks to green again.

They are prey to the same sky, of course, and more so than the grazers. When a stooper's shadow crosses, a builder does not run — there is nowhere to run to that is not open mat — but goes down, into the mat's edge, into the hollow, under a giant's leaf, and waits with a hand on the weave to feel whether the chase is moving away. Children are taught first not the names of things but the feel of them: the note of a safe hollow, the ripple of a burrower, the heavy thrum of a grazer's step, the sudden quiet that means a stooper is overhead listening for movement. Their tools are what can be made from what the mat gives: stone hafted with cord, bone, fiber, a little hammered metal kept and re-hammered for a lifetime. No wheels, because nothing here needs to roll across a sponge that springs back. No pottery that lasts, because clay is scarce where plant is ground.

At night the cold comes in as a clean, mineral bite and the condensation begins again, and the chorus changes key entirely — deeper, slower, as if the plateau were dreaming in vibrations. The grazers settle, their platforms heaving, mites working their backs. The giants' leaves close and drip steadily in the dark, tick-tock against the mat. The builders emerge in numbers you would not believe from the day's emptiness, moving between dead trunks with their hands on the wood, sharing news pulled along vines, drinking from the woven cups, tending the quiet loom frames by feel. The stoopers sleep hanging from the crowns, wings like closed sails. The migrators are gone to the next tableland. Underneath, the beetles sing their night note, and the whole skin of the world resonates, low and insectile, in your sternum.

And underneath all of it, the mat keeps eating and being eaten, root drinking beetle drinking fungus drinking root, grazer drinking mat, stooper drinking grazer, builder drinking drip, fly drinking corpse, root drinking fly — nothing separate, nothing fallen and left, the country living not in bodies that stand up out of it, but through, in every layer at once.
