Nothing here stands apart to be pointed at.

The first lives are under your feet, where you are not walking on ground at all. The soil is a mat — roots, root-hairs, fallen bark, pulped leaf, rhizomes braided together by pressure — and it gives and springs when you step on it. Between the braids it cracks in clean, fresh lines where new roots have pried through bark-soil, and small steep hills of compressed leaf-mould lift up and steam faintly with sap and fermentation. It is young ground, freshly split, sharp-edged. When a ridge slumps it does not erode; it rots where it stands into loam, and growth tears it apart from inside. What looks like stone from a distance is hardened, layered trunk. Inorganic things are few, and quickly taken, and turned green again.

At ankle-height the mat thickens into a forest that has been flattened by its own weight, a low dense carpet that is not grass but the canopy of a forest too crowded to rise. Above that the understory rises wall upon wall — ferns unfurling, reeds, broad herbaceous stalks, stem against stem — so that you do not so much enter the place as get enclosed by it. And still it goes up. The midstory climbs as far as the light will let it, and the canopy is also a ceiling, and in the depths the ceiling closes and day turns to green twilight, until it opens again onto a plaza: not a clearing made by clearing, but bare living ground where a tower has just collapsed and dissolved.

That collapse is the way most things here end. There are no animals to live in these places as tenants. The vegetation is not on the place. It is the place, and so the inhabitants are towers and streets and roofs and roads, except that they are all growing and can therefore cease to be any of them.

In one district the towers are bundled trunks, lashed together at the base by root-cords, growing in straight ranks because that is how competition forces them — straight, then splitting at the top to hold leaves as high as possible. Eight storeys of leaves, ten, twenty, hanging balconies of epiphytes, curtains of vine squashed across the gaps for walls. They push up ten feet in an hour, leaves expanding wet and furrowed, sap booming in the trunk under pressure, and at the next growth-wave a neighbor may be starved out.

In another district the inhabitants layer themselves flat: fern-fronds window over window, a building that is only overlapping green. In another, broad-leaf plants spread their crowns until they meet and make a continuous roof, and beneath that roof streets form by negation — nothing can grow in the deep shade except the pale, thread-like stems that learn to crawl along the ground, feeling their way in the dark in search of a crack of light. The streets are not planned. They are where growth has refused to happen.

Elsewhere an avenue is a vine that, seeking light, stretched and thickened and hardened, died back at its core to leave a hollow tube, and then grew again around its own hollow. Bridges of living tendril cross between towers and swing in the warm air. Under the streets run the aqueducts: thick aqueduct-roots storing damp and distributing it in pulses, swelling after a shower, thinning in drought, carrying the only standing water in the valley inside themselves, from place to place. No river runs. No lake stays. The water lives inside the inhabitants and moves when they swell.

You do not hear them move like animals move. You hear them grow. Millions of leaves rubbing make a rustle that deepens into rhythm, into a roar. Stomata open with a click like rain on a roof that never stops. Trunks creak and boom. Branches snap and re-strap themselves. Seeds burst from pods with a crack like knuckles, or sigh out in clouds of fluff. Underground, roots tick against each other and pop the fresh soil apart in a way you feel in your teeth. On warm afternoons, when transpiration peaks, the whole basin breathes at once in a wave that travels for miles — a rising and falling hiss that drowns thought. To live here is to be, continually, in the middle of that vibration.

Because it competes, it builds, and because it builds, it competes. This is the whole of their social life. One plant feeds on another by shading it. A strangler vine climbs its neighbor, tightens, starves it of light and air, and the tower sags, folds, dissolves into mulch with a sigh of steam and spores. Another sends roots silently into a trunk to steal its stored water. Pollen is thrown like seed, stamens exploding in sudden pollen-winds because there is nothing else to carry them. Seeds travel by explosion, by wind, by being dropped from a height they have grown for the purpose. A thorn thickens until it can cut a rival stem. A leaf hardens until it pries. A trunk hollows itself to be a channel. There is no tool here separate from the body, because everything that might be made is first grown.

There are districts of thorn, sharp and defensive, where the young sharpness of the ground and the sharpness of the plants reinforce each other. There are slow, mossy quarters where growth has been so dense for so long that air itself seems to grow, through seep and vein. There are markets of bloom — sudden, local concentrations where flowers open and close in minutes, pistils thrusting, the whole place briefly sweet and sharp, and then gone. Some inhabitants live for a season. Some live for a minute. A seed in one pocket will sprout, flower, and seed again while its twin a foot away takes a full season, and the two are forever mismatched, reaching at different speeds. It is not clear that they are two. It is not clear that they are not the same one, copy and copy.

And beneath all of that, in the root-tunnels, are the oldest movements, though nothing here is old. Roots pass under a street and emerge in a clearing a mile away without passing through the intervening ground. Aqueduct-roots swell and thin. Damp runs upward along certain stems. Gravity pools around the thickest trunks, as if weight were local weather. And sometimes, deep underground, you can see a smear of sunlight on the wall of a tunnel, though there is no sky above you — light remembered by the bark, held there.

The only consumers of the dead are the fungi. They are neither plant nor animal in any clean way, and here they are allowed the one job no plant can do for itself: they eat what has collapsed, burned down to short shoots by a fire of decay that eats without heat, and return it to green. A plaza open in the morning is a thicket by evening; a thicket choked in the morning is open by evening, cleaned by them. Nothing else dies here, in the sense that nothing else needs to be disposed of. It rots in place, and the rotting is itself a living.

Whole quarters migrate. Over weeks they crawl, root by root, toward the light at the basin's edge, leaving behind them the fresh sharp ground they have just made and abandoned. Leaf-fall comes in storms spiraling up as well as down. Branches trade places. The city is turbulent, Solaris-like, creating and destroying in the same breath, and its inhabitants are not individuals in any countably stable sense. A single root-system may be a whole district. A whole district may be a single individual that has translated itself from one end of the valley to the other, leaf by leaf, root-step by root-step, leaving a copy of itself behind in the shape of where it has been.

Then, at the edges of what can be described, they cease to be quite describable.

Fallen leaves are sometimes green, sometimes already rotted, sometimes still on the branch they have fallen from, and no one can say which is first. Shadows lie on the ground that do not correspond to anything above them, because the canopy above them is not the canopy that cast them — it is a later canopy, grown a moment later, copying the first. A vine avenue followed straight will, if you do not hurry, loop back to where you started, not because it curves, but because the vine has grown longer behind you. A flower may open mouth-like and deep, and inside it, impossibly, is a miniature of the whole valley, with its own tiny weather inside the weather. A plant can be both its own ancestor and its own descendant, because its fallen leaves become new plants in its own image, in its own place, while it is still standing there.

Water is present the way breath is present. In the early morning the leaves are beaded so heavily that water runs from their tips in a constant soft dripping, cupped leaves holding small perfect pools that last minutes before the plant drinks them back. Then the beads vanish, the ground turning dusty and papery. A rain may come locally — a single street receiving a sudden heavy shower as warm air releases the moisture the canopy has been exhaling for hours. It drums on the roof-leaves, steams where it touches sun-warm bark, and is gone in ten minutes, reabsorbed. You can drink nothing here except by biting a leaf or a fruit, and the taste is always green and sweet and sharp, though nothing here has a mouth to bite with.

By late afternoon the warmth peaks and the valley is at its loudest, at its most itself, new growth tearing through old in waves you can watch cross the basin, a visible tide of green. The light, still ordinary white-yellow daylight, is broken and re-broken in moving lace — leaf-shadow and stem-shadow and quivering bars that sometimes move against the wind, sometimes after a delay, so that at noon the shade under a tree may be the shape of a tree that will grow there tomorrow.

The city of plants keeps building and unbuilding itself, street by street, tower by tower, district by district migrating slowly across the low, young, damp-and-dry ground. It has no animals to name it or map it, and its inhabitants have no names to give themselves. They are substrate, predator, prey, soil, sky, building, road, and weather, and they go on — larger than the sight that tries to take them in, region succeeding region, layer on layer, never finished, never still.
