At first, underfoot, they are not creatures at all. They are a paleness, a way the floor has learned to be white. Only when you press weight down do they resolve — not into steps or stones, but into edges, overlapping edges, thousands of them, each one no longer than a fingernail, laid shingle-wise from the hand-wide seam to the far curve so there is no earth left to see.

Each one is flat. Each one is translucent, almost unpigmented, with a faint milky rim where they overlap the next. And along that rim, almost too slender to notice, a slit. It opens. It closes. It opens again. That is the labor of the floor.

They pulse, all of them, in a slow, uncoordinated ripple that travels nowhere. When your boot comes down, the whole pavement shushes and parts, each tile curling slightly away from the pressure, showing its underside, soft and damp, and then flowing back behind you into precisely the pattern it had before. The slit-mouths work at the mineral film on the stone beneath them, scraping, scraping, so quietly you mainly hear it as that wet scraping shush under the other sounds. What they scrape they take in. What they shed — a skin, a flake, the pale dust of their own edges — they also take in, or their neighbors do. They eat algae at the margins. They eat each other’s shed skins. They do not travel. They do not seem to grow past the size they were found at. A tile that dies — if a tile can be said to die, here, where nothing changes — is simply re-covered by the next, and the pavement is whole again at the same density, the same whiteness, the same slow open-close along every edge.

If the floor is alive in a horizontal way, the walls are alive in a vertical one, but slower still.

Ringing the low curve of the pocket, crowded stalk by stalk, are the filter-cups. Cream-colored, feathery, each on a thin stalk that trembles more than it stands. They do not walk, though they wave, row after row, in a motion too small to call walking — a bending and recovering, a bending and recovering, as if they are forever trying to lean toward something in the damp air and forever stopping a fraction short.

From the air they take fine dust. Their feathered tips spread and close, spread and close, straining a dampness that has no weather in it, and now and then a grain falls from them. It falls straight. It does not drift. That falling is part of the ceiling-to-floor economy of the place, because below them, in the damp pockets where even the tiles thin out, something else is waiting to eat what falls.

The pinhead crabs.

They are not much larger than a pinhead, and they are crowded in those pockets as the tiles are crowded everywhere else. No eyes that you can find. No color to speak of. What they have instead are claws, and they click them, steadily, at one another, at the walls, at nothing in particular. Wet clicking, endless, unanswered in any way you can make into a language, though it has cadence, and the cadence never varies and never stops. They scuttle — or they seem to, one pinhead length at a time — gathering the dust the cups drop, gathering the shed flakes the bells let go, carrying them a short distance and then carrying them back, or not back, to the same damp corner. Their clicking is how they know where the edge of the crowd is. It is how they know where they are. They have been clicking in this same constellation of damp for as long as the pocket has been closed.

Above them all, mouth-down, hang the bells.

They crowd the sagging ceiling until the ceiling is another floor. Tiny, bell-shaped, translucent as the floor-tiles but upright in their hanging, each on a thread so fine it looks like condensation holding it up. They shiver. Continually. It is less a movement than a condition — they are of a size to shiver and they do — and from that shivering comes the high whine that fills the pocket and inflates in the small volume until it seems to come from inside your own head.

Condensation touches them, which is always. A bead swells on the ceiling in its fixed constellation, touches a bell, and the bell’s shiver sharpens for an instant and then is the same shiver again. The bead does not fall. It is whole again. The bells do not chime toward a note; they sustain the chord the room was making before you arrived, and will make after you leave.

That light — flat, colorless, indecently bright for so far below leaf litter — is not any one creature’s lamp. There is no source to point to because it is the multiplication of all of them. A thousand translucent shells, bells, tiles, cup-tips, reflecting and re-reflecting until the whole pocket is lit as evenly as noon inside a paper lantern, and gives off no heat at all. The stone stays cool under it. Your breath stays pale in it. The creatures themselves, gathered in such number and such closeness, are the brilliance. They are bright because they are translucent and crowded and forever refracting each other.

And greenness, barely, in one damp corner only.

A patch of algae, white-green, soft as felt under a fingertip if you could get a fingertip to it without displacing crabs, grows — or rather, persists — in the one place where the root can attend. The root came through a seam in the ceiling long ago, or has always been coming through. Pale, thread-like, it dangles with a dozen hair-thin offshoots that reach down and stop, each one the same length it was, each one not quite touching the floor, not quite touching the algae, but close enough that the algae knows it is there. A few stubby shoots have grown sideways from the patch, as if uncertain whether up or out counts as growing. They have stopped sideways, at the same stubbiness.

Nothing here thrives. The plants attend. The root drips nothing. The algae does not spread past its felt edge. It only stays green in that corner and nowhere else, and the floor-tiles at its edge periodically scrape at it, and it is periodically the same size again.

These are the residents in the present tense, but there are also residents in the past tense, built into the walls because nothing here can be finished and therefore nothing here can be removed.

Seven pebbles stacked two inches high, neatly, pointlessly, in a place where a stone should be left to the earth to finish settling. A ring woven of dried root, half the size of a plate, tucked into a niche and twice as neat as a root ought to be. A spiral scratched into the soft wall, over-scratched, line upon line in the same groove, as if something has begun it a thousand times and reabsorbed it a thousand times. Parallel scrape-lines by the seam, where the stone is always exactly hand-wide.

No tools made these. No metal. No wire. Only creatures with no hands to speak of, pressing at the unfinished stone because the stone allows a first act — a bulge, a bud, a crystal point three millimeters long, forever — and they are themselves made for first acts. The crabs have scratched the spiral with their pinhead claws, click by click, and the wall has smoothed it and they have scratched it again. The tiles have stacked the cairn one pebble at a time in a labor of weeks or centuries, and the pebbles have not fallen and have not been added to. Something has woven the root-ring around the thread of the living root and left it in its niche, unfinished as a plate, finished as a ring. The scrape-lines at the seam are where the floor-pavement has tried, in vain, to scrape an opening wider than a hand, and the seam has remained exactly hand-wide, and they have scraped again.

They are not builders in the way you are a builder. They are tenants who begin, because beginning is the only form of action the pocket permits.

When you are here, you are a tenant too, briefly. You place a boot and the tiles hiss and re-form. You shout and the whine and the clicking and the shush answer you with the same chord they had before you. You leave a half-print in the soft clay and the wall reabsorbs it, and when you look again the same half-print is there, being reabsorbed again. You are not new to them. You are not dangerous to them. You displace them, and they flow around the displacement and close it.

Movement without progress. Emergence without arrival. That is not a rule imposed on them from outside; it is what their combined living adds up to. The cups strain and drop dust that the crabs eat. The crabs click and carry dust to corners that feed the tiles’ scrapings. The tiles scrape film and shed skins that fall, in time, as dust for the cups to strain again. The bells shed and shiver and are touched by condensation that is always touching them. The root reaches and does not arrive, and the algae stays, and the tiles eat at its edge. The water pocket on the floor — clear, still, rimmed with a dark tideline, never rippling, never draining — holds a reflection of the ceiling bells and of no one who stands beside it. No creature lives in the water, except perhaps the reflection, which lives there as constantly as the bells live above.

No one is in charge. No one is new. The pocket closed around empty air while the earth was still soft, and what lives in it has been closing and opening, waving and stopping, shivering and sounding, clicking and carrying, reaching and attending, ever since — alive, abundant, deafening in a space no wider than a small bedroom, and at every moment exactly as alive and abundant as at the first moment, because here, to live is to repeat, and to repeat is to stay.
