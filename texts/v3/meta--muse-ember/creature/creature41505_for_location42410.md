First it is heard before it is seen.

What lives here does not announce itself with a call or a footstep. It announces itself with surf — rain on twenty layers of leaf, stacking and crashing down until rain stops being rain and becomes weather you are inside of. Under that, the popping. A trunk expanding a millimeter and cracking like a knuckle. A liana under tension groaning, deep and slow, like rigging. A seedpod drying and firing with a small, sharp report. At night, when the dim at the top becomes a green-grey twilight below that never quite darkens, a grinding you feel in your teeth as a pulse of growth migrates across the plateau, a peristaltic tightening, the whole mat inhaling.

That is because nothing here is separate from anything else. Everything is touching everything else, so everything can transmit. The inhabitants are, first of all, transmitters.

And then, slowly, the sea of leaves resolves.

At the bottom, where you would expect ground, there is a mat meters thick and it is alive all the way through. Moss cushions the size of a hand, saturated, soft, never lifting to flower but breathing when pressed by the weight above them and recovering. Fern fronds pressed flat by their own weight, overlapping like shingles, catching drip from the layer above and passing it, still moving, to the layer below. Threading through all of it, fungal threads like wet silk, binding moss to fern to root to root, not feeding on the mat so much as *being* its continuity. In the root-rivers that braid themselves through this mat — clear, very cold water traveling inside hollow stems and root conduits, never cutting a channel — bacteria live in the flow, turning fall into next growth in place, instantly. Nothing is allowed to die and leave. What falls becomes layer. What decays becomes air, becomes water, becomes the next leaf to uncurl a centimeter above where the old one rotted.

These bottom-dwellers have no soil to stand in. They stand in each other. Press your foot down and water rises around it, clear. Lift it and the mat gives with spongy resistance, and recovers, sealing the wound in hours with cambium flowing like warm wax. It will not be long before your footprint is softened, colonized, smoothed over. Whatever holds still long enough becomes substrate.

Above the mat, filling the next vertical inch, is another life, and another, and another, until naming a height is an arbitrary act. Low creepers angle their leaves to intercept what the canopy missed, then intercept it again for the layer beneath. Shrubs whose trunks are no thicker than a wrist lean, year after year, into the one gap in the light they can sense, thickening only where pressure tells them to thicken. Each stores water in stem, in leaf, in its own hollow — the whole plateau is a marsh lifted to altitude, and its inhabitants are sponges that have learned to stack.

Higher still the sea thickens into hills. Canopy trees spread their branches and then spread again, until branch becomes net and net becomes branch, and a hill is not a hill but a trunk that refused to stop dividing. Their leaves are many kinds of leaf. Some are broad and flat and furred with the condensation that beads on everything here. Some are veined with translucence, and inside those veins they do not only take the dim light — they focus it and carry it down, fiber by fiber, lighting their own deep tissues the way a buried signal travels. Light, here, is not simply intercepted; it is transported, negotiated down through seven, ten, twenty layers, each stealing a little more, until the light that reaches the moss is a rumor of the light that began at the top.

Between these trees loop the lianas. They loop and re-loop, not climbing so much as bridging, thickening over decades into grown arches across root-rivers, into a dome where two canopies have grown together and made a sheltered hollow. When water strikes a hollow stem it booms. When the whole continent-scale sponge heaves — with water, with its own immense metabolic breathing — the lianas take the strain and creak. They are the rigging, the suspension, the thing that keeps the saturation from tearing itself apart by its own weight.

And hanging down into the air itself are the aerial roots. They do not search for soil. There is, in most places, no soil to search for. They exude compounds into the mist, ionize it, coax water out of air that should be too thin to yield it. They liquefy their own weather. The fog that beads on your eyelashes, on the hairs of your arms, until you drip without knowing why — that is their country. Trunks weep. Leaves weep. The stone at the frontier weeps. The inhabitants breathe weather in and out: the plateau exhales cloud at its edges and inhales dew along its interior, and storms are born in the canopy, fed by evaporation from below, and crawl across the green sea for days, dropping rain that will be caught and caught and caught again before it ever reaches the bottom.

There are inhabitants you will never see from any height, because their size or their speed puts them outside the eye's separation of forms. Under the mat the mycelial nets exchange not just nutrients but structured signals, pulse-coded and error-corrected, kilometers — *kilometers* — solving distribution and repair in real time, deciding which frontier stone needs a spore, which gap needs a creeper, which trunk needs to thicken to become a bridge. The answer travels as a tremor in the silk, as a change in the chemical wetness of a root conduit.

Above them, drifting down in slow curtains through the dim, is spore snow. Each spore rides the condensing air like an instrument, its coat bending light and charge to steer itself toward wet stone and virgin substrate. Most will land on leaf already occupied, on mat already saturated, and will be taken, filtered, recycled. A few will find the newest places, where the high plateau is still emerging, where bare rock sweats black and slick in the cool air — and there they land, germinate, send a hair-thin root into a crack, and begin, very slowly, to un-stone the stone. Algae crusts it first, within days. Then moss. Then the hair root. Then the creeper. Then the wrist-thick shrub leaning into light. Colonization, here, is a succession of inhabitants each making room only for the next plant.

None of this is built. There is no tool, no wall, no path, no maker. A bridge is grown by pressure and response. A dome is a folded leaf. A shelter is two canopies agreeing. The technology is the biology, fabricating matter molecule by molecule from air and water and dim light, at nanoscale and continent-scale at once, with no factory and no blueprint outside the living tissue itself.

That is why there are no individuals in the tidy sense. Competition is plant against plant for a gap in the light, for a centimeter of wet stone at the frontier, for the right to be the trunk that bridges. There is no predator and no prey, because everything that moves, moves by growing. An inhabitant is born as a spore in the air, becomes a leaf in the light, becomes litter without ever becoming litter, becomes mat, becomes mist, becomes spore again. The hills are plants. The valleys are plants sagging between hills. Even the sound is a plant: sap rising, stem resonating, pod cracking, mat grinding as it expands.

And it is still emerging. At the edges and summits, the growth fronts advance across the newest stone at a pace you could almost watch if you stayed long enough. The sea of leaves heaves. Rain falls and is re-caught and falls again inside the canopy, a closed loop. Spore snow drifts. The pulses migrate.

Weeping, condensing, saturated, dim, cool, deafening — a world whose foundation is not underneath the plants, but *is* the plants, growing themselves into being, filling every space they make, and leaving, in the end, no room for anything else to live there besides themselves.
