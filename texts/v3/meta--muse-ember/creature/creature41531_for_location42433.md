The first lives are the ones you step on without knowing you have stepped on a life at all.

Kneel down — your knees wet at once — and put a hand flat on the stone. Under the film of water, under the cold that aches up through the boot, there is already a skin. It is thin and irregular and it does not root. Black sheets spread where the stone sweats longest, ochre where iron is coming up in the water, white-grey rings in the silt of the hollows, so pale they seem to give off their own light in this lightless grey. They have no leaf, no stem, no green to borrow color from. They are fungi in the broadest sense, and beneath them simpler films still, bacteria-thin sheets that live in the dusk between stone and water.

They eat the stone. That is not a metaphor. The water that has been wearing this plain for eons brings dissolved minerals up along invisible seams, and these crusts take them apart. They breathe out a soft, invisible gas and take in the cold air's moisture. In a wet patch you can watch — if you watch long enough — a flat place swell, just a fraction, inflate like a blister, and then sigh out a pale puff of spores. The mist takes them sideways and they are gone. The mat itself is alive in the slowest way: over a year it will creep downslope a fraction of an inch, black at the leading edge where it is new and hungry, grey behind it where it has aged and sloughed. A black flat from ten years ago is grey now. Nothing about it is sharp. The corners of everything have been taken off by water, including the living things.

The pale rings do something stranger. In the silt pans they expand and contract with the mist-tide, and when the sound rises — when the whole plain begins to drum louder — they brighten. Faintly, unmistakably. It is as if vibration shakes more mineral loose from the grit and they feed on the shaking. They are not listening in any way you would recognize. They are sonotrophic. The louder the place gets, the more they glow, and the more spores they puff. 

In the sheet-pans the films are different: translucent threads and slime that drift and cling, never quite settling. They wave when the pan ripples. They catch the tiny falls of spore and midge and mineral dust. They are the bottom and the top of the food here at once.

And they are already being eaten.

The slick-grazers are the first animals you will hear, even if you never see one. Flat, limpet-like, pale grey, blind — a thumb-sized disc of wet flesh with a rasping mouth underneath. They move over the fungal crusts at a pace that makes the crust seem fast. Their bodies are translucent at the edge, so they vanish except for the trail. Behind each one is a polished path, brighter than the surrounding mat, where the mouth has scraped the film clean and left the stone shining. Follow a trail and you will find a highway system older than any human mark. Grazer follows grazer. They are hermaphroditic, slow, and they carry spores in their gut and seed them behind them in their rasp-lines, so the mat and the animal migrate together like a herd and its grass, only at the speed of stone.

They do not look up. They have no eyes to look with. In this flat, shadowless grey there is nothing for sight to do that touch and sound cannot do better. A grazer knows the world by the texture under its foot and by the vibration coming up through the stone it is clinging to. When a predator taps near, a grazer will stop mid-rasp, seal itself to the rock, and wait. Its whole body is an ear pressed to the drum.

The tap-predators are the ones who make the plain answer back.

Six-legged, long-feelered, the color of wet bone. Their hind legs are for walking the pans; their forelegs are something else. Each foreleg ends in a hard, hollow club, cupped like a tiny ladle. A tap-predator patrols the edge of a pan on its toes, antennae trailing in the film, and every few steps it stops, lifts a foreleg, and taps the stone once. Hard. Clean. Then it waits.

The sound goes through the wet, smoothed, hollowed stone and comes back to it through its own feet and feelers. It is listening for how the stone answers: the difference between a hollow that is empty, a hollow with a grazer sealed inside it, a hollow with a worm turning in silt, a hollow with water moving uphill an inch. Different prey give back a different ring. The predator has learned the ringing stones by heart. Some cupped hollows sing a pure bell-note when a drip finds them, and a tap-predator will sit beside one of those for an hour or more, still as a stone itself, tapping now and then, waiting for something to come to the ringing — because the note mimics the sound of food dripping, and grazers and small filterers cannot help but move toward it.

When it finds its prey it does not chase. It taps harder, in a sudden stuttering roll, and the amplified vibration through the pan stuns what is in the shallow water. Filter-feeders retract, grazers freeze a fraction too long, midges shiver in the air above. That fraction is enough. The predator flicks out a feelered jaw-thread and takes them.

In the pans themselves, where the water is only a mirror of sky and a ripple of your face, life is crowded in a way the open stone never is.

The filter-feeders sit in the centre, sessile, half-buried in silt. They have no name in any human tongue, but they look like pale flowers made of gills — feathery, translucent fans that wave and chop and wave again, straining the water for spore, for mineral film, for the midges that fall. Their gills are the color of the film that feeds them. You can only see them when you are very close, because they are built to be felt. Each wave sends a tiny ripple to the rim of the pan, and the rim is scarred with drip-hollows, and the ripples tick the hollows like a piano. A pan full of filter-feeders is never still, even when the wind is.

Above each pan hangs their sky: the midges.

Not a swarm in the chaotic way of a summer evening elsewhere. A sheet. A dark, shivering skin of air just above the water, rising and falling together, humming at a pitch that matches the pan's drip. They are born in the translucent slime at the edge, blind, black, winged for hours only. They mate in the mist layer, they do not eat in the air — they live on what they carried from the film — and then, spent, they fall. Their fall is the filter-feeders' rain. Their hum, multiplied by thousands, shapes the air the way dripping shapes the stone. When the mist-tide comes in, the sheet thickens, the hum deepens, the pans swell a finger's width, and the whole column of life breathes in.

Under the silt, in the grey grit that never dries, are the silt-worms. White, eyeless, knot themselves and unknot themselves without pause, sucking and releasing, turning the silt over grain by grain. You hear them more than see them: a wet suck, a soft plop, felt through your boot-sole rather than your ear. They eat the dead of everything — dead fungus, dead grazer, dead midge, dead filterer — and they return it to the silt in pellets that then feed the rings. Without them the cycle would lock. They are the plain's stomach and memory. A hollow favored by worms will have silt that is looser, darker, and that rings duller when tapped. The predators know that too.

And then there are the ones who live in the sound itself.

In the weeping boulder-fields, where each rounded rock has a darker stain running from its crown and gives off water in a steady bead, a thread, a drip, there are small pale paddlers that live in the drip-hollows themselves. They are the size of a fingernail, translucent, all leg and drum. They do not hunt so much as they plink. They sit in a cupped stone and flick the rim with a hind foot, making the hollow sing its note, and the note carries for yards through wet stone. Other paddlers answer from the next hollow. It is how they find mates, how they warn, how they call prey close. The tap-predators have learned to tell the difference between a paddler plinking for love and a paddler plinking because a grazer has blundered into its cup. That is why they wait.

There are thread-dwellers, too, in seams you cannot see. The water comes up out of the stone everywhere along invisible lines, and in those cracks live things that are little more than a gut and a set of hairs, pushing water fractionally uphill — not against slope but along the line of a resonance in the stone. You will never see one. You will see only, on a still moment, a film of water on a flat stone running the wrong way for a stretch, thinning, and then closing behind it. Something down there is beating, very slowly, in time with the dripping above.

On a mist-tide, when the fog bank crawls across the plain like a tide coming in and the dripping doubles and the roar rises until you must shout to be heard a pace away, all of this becomes one animal. The fungal rings brighten. The grazers rasp faster, then freeze in waves. The tap-predators tap in counterpoint, a dozen answering each other across a pan. The paddlers sing louder to be heard over the drums. The worms surface and suck in unison. The midge sheet jerks and settles, jerks and settles. The pans themselves darken — not with shadow, but because the filter-feeders and grazers and worms gather below the surface in a solid sheet, turning the mirror black. Look down then and the pan reflects nothing at all. It is full.

Between pans there may be a quarter-mile of bare, slick stone where nothing moves. Nothing lives there in the dry sense, because there is no dry. The animals cross it only when the mist-tide wets it thick enough to carry sound. Then you will see — or rather hear — a tap-predator making the long crossing on tiptoe, tapping as it goes, antennae down, a grey spider of silence moving from one ringing pan to the next like a pilgrim following bells. The grazers cross more slowly, leaving a fresh polished trail that the fungi will follow for years.

The biggest of the tap-predators is the one you find last, because it does not patrol. It sits. For a year, maybe for many years, a single broad predator will hold one bell-stone at the rim of one pan. Its body is domed, grey, cold, the size of a forearm, its six legs tucked under it like a stool. Its forelegs rest on the stone. Every now and then it taps — once, a question — and waits for the pan to answer. Its tapping sets the tempo for its pan. The filter-feeders wave to it. The midges rise to it. The paddlers time their plinking to it. Prey that blunder into its ringing hollow are stunned and taken. It is not king of this place. It is only the steadiest listener.

People come, but they are not residents. They are weather.

They arrive wet to the bone, hunched against a wind that has nothing to rustle, their boots full of pan-water after an hour, and they do not stay. They find a windbreak crescent — three slabs, knee-high, already sinking and softening — and crouch in it for a while, out of the hiss. Their technology is hand-deep because the place allows nothing deeper: pounders of hard stone, scoops carved from bone — the thick, calcified foreleg of a large tap-predator, hollowed — and curved flakes for digging slight runnels in silt to lead water from one pan to another so it does not stagnate. They harvest by scraping and pounding, using the same stone for tool and for table. They scrape the fungal mats, they pound the grazers and the filter-feeders between two stones into a grey paste, and they eat it raw, cold, with the mineral tang still in it. Their hands are the only warm things on the plain, and the animals freeze at the heat and the irregular human tapping, then resume when the hands go away.

They leave caches: a hammerstone, a flake water-worn even in the hand, left beside a pan. The grazers will rasp them in time. The film of water that never leaves anything will polish them. In a season a slab will have sunk a finger's width, a runnel will have filled with silt. The plain takes them back quickly because it has only one way of taking things, which is to wet them and wear them down.

Their cycle, for all their strangeness, is the same as everything else's. Water lifts minerals out of the stone the animals have been living on for eons. The films break those minerals down and hold them. The grazers eat the films. The filterers strain the water. The predators eat the grazers. The worms eat the dead of them all and return it to the silt. The mist takes the water up again and sets it down on the cold stone. Nothing green ever enters, and nothing needs to.

When you leave, as you came, the stone will close behind your boot-print. The drums go on ticking over the worn, final, shining plain. Under the film, in the silt, in the pans, in the ringing hollows, in the shivering sheet of midges, the lives go on dripping and crawling and hunting and sporing and tapping — colorless, blind, grey and black, not needing to be seen at all, needing only, forever, to be heard by the stone that made them.
