If you stay still long enough for the roar to stop being weather and start being a room, the first inhabitant arrives without touching anything. You feel it before you hear it, a pressure change in the water that makes the low note in your sternum step aside for a moment, then resume. A whale.

She is not swimming so much as the water is being rearranged around her. A rorqual, throat grooved like a bellows, twenty metres of dark back that catches the pale daylight and returns it duller, and beneath, pleated and pale, a belly that swells and contracts with a patience that makes your own breathing feel frantic. She passes along the rim, not over the table precisely, but in that band where the current pours over the drop and the table's light gives out, and her sound does the thing the place does: it goes on for kilometres after she has gone. The groans are not a language to her the way words are to you. They are waypoints. She is calling into a volume almost empty of answer, and the answer, when it comes, is another whale, hours away, which is close enough here.

That is the first rule of living here, if you can call it living. No one is a local in the way a reef fish is a local. They are in transit, marked by a route that never changes, and the table is just a landmark they repeat. The whales run the rim like a road. They do not stop to feed on the glass, because there is nothing on the glass to feed on. The food arrives with the current, sifted from the open sea beyond the bright ceiling, and they take it in passing, mouths open in that slow, grooved expansion, and keep going. You could watch the same animal come back in a season, or a decade, and she would be taking the same line, at the same speed, making the same low argument with the turbines.

Then the sharks.

They do appear to live here, after a fashion, because they have found the only furniture the table offers: a crack. A sleeper shark will hang in a basalt fissure with his tail folded out of sight, head down into a standing eddy, mottled grey-brown against black glass, one eye milky with a parasite that has lived on him longer than you have been alive. He does not patrol. At two or three degrees, with a heart that beats perhaps once a minute, there is no reason to. Greenland water, Greenland tempo. For an hour he is indistinguishable from rock that has learned a shape. Then, without hurry and without signal you can read, he unfolds. The dorsal fin rises, the pectorals spread like slow wings, and he leaves the crack the way a thought leaves a mind, and goes away along the rim to another crack, which is to say, to another identical emptiness an hour's travel away. The crabs know him by the displacement. The octopuses know him by the brief darkness.

The octopuses are smaller than you expect for the scale of everything else, and whiter. No reds, no greens to borrow — there is nothing green in the world to be green against — so they wear the mineral frost. They live by the crack-edges too, arms tucked into a granite shard's diagonal break, siphon exhaling in a thin, sideways bubble-string that the current takes at once. They hunt and wait on the same principle: wait for the rim-current to deliver something alive that has missed the shoals, a silver fish, a crab startled out, a piece of whalefall drifting up from the dark below. An octopus here can sit in the same burrow for weeks, tasting the water with the tips of its arms, learning the exact square of turbulence that is always rough, that always brings luck. It will never build. There is no sand to excavate, no shell to steal and paint over. A crab will take a nodule, a bit of broken turbine housing, a pebble left in a straight sparse line by the old current, and carry it a few metres to a better crack, and set it down like a door. It is the only architecture anyone does here.

Above them, and never really with them, are the fish. A shoal comes over the glass like weather made of mirrors. Silver fish, herring-like and longer, arched in a loose formation with no centre you can find, a kilometre wide and a hundred metres deep, turning as one body because the turn began at the far end long before it reached the near. They are too high to cast a proper shadow, too far to touch, and they do not feed here either; they are feeding *while* passing, on the plankton the current carries where the light is unweakened. They will fill the sky under the sea-surface for ten minutes, a dapple that makes the white rippling above look grey, and then they are gone and the table is empty again, and you realize the emptiness was not an absence in between. It was the condition. The shoal was an event in it.

That is why the food web never roots. There are no grazers, so there are no grazed-upon, no forests to hide in, no nurseries in the glass. Everything that lives here is a thief of imports: filterers and predators and scavengers, animals whose work is to be at the rim when the sea delivers. The crabs clean a carcass and then wait, perfectly preserved in the cold, for the next whale to die somewhere beyond the rim and drift up. A carcass here does not get stripped to flowers of bone in a season. It lies, intact, flesh still holding shape, until a shark finds it, until a crab finds it, until the harvester, in its straight line, finds it and turns aside a fraction and then resumes. Nothing rots in any hurry, so death is also a slow, distributed thing.

And then, in among them, so completely that after a while you stop distinguishing the categories, are the other animals.

The turbines chained along the rim are sessile, by choice. Their blades turn at a speed that never varies because the current never varies, and they make that high whine you hear through your gloves. Their cables vanish over the edge, into the dark where the pressure would crush daylight, and inland in parallel lines to a power station that is a low, electrified hill of steel humming the base note. They do not migrate. They do not feed or breed. They drink current and exhale power, and the animals have learned to use them the way the octopus uses a crack: the lee side is calm, the turbulence downstream shakes loose food, the shark rests in the shadow of the housing. A pod of whales will pass a turbine for a hundred years, and the turbine will pass them back, unchanged.

The harvester is the only migrant that leaves a scar. It crawls — if that is the word for something on treads the size of houses — in a perfectly straight line at a speed that makes you doubt it is moving at all until you check a beacon and find you have moved. It scrapes nodules from the mineral floor and leaves behind a trail that never fades, a furrow in glass that stays fresh because nothing grows to fill it and no silt settles to soften it. It is a grazer in a world with no grass, a slow animal making the same sound year after year, and the crabs follow its trail at a respectful distance, picking at what it leaves exposed.

The factory module is flat on the rock, lit, humming, racks of containers in rows. One window lit. That is a home, of a sort. Someone lives there in rotation, or no one does for months and the machines tend themselves in the dark they keep for themselves. The human, when there is one, does not live so much as endure, at arm's length. You would have to shout to be heard over the roar, and your voice comes back flattened, as if the water had taken a vote against speech. The people who work the table — technicians, pilots, keepers who come down on apparatus and up again, or down in a pressure hull and stay in that one room for a season — learn not to talk much. They learn the squares of current the way the octopus does, they learn that this valley always carries a fast flow from the left and that square is always rough, they learn that the amber beacon on the half-buried housing will blink whether anyone is watching or not. They do not age faster here, or slower; they mostly do not stay long enough to find out. Some go a little deaf in the high frequencies, from the turbines, and a little blind to distance, from the hundreds of metres of nothing between one presence and the next. Their breath bubbles up in a long string and is taken sideways, and they watch it go, and that is often the most company they get for a day.

There is an old platform, tipped on its side, preserved exactly as it fell. Gulls would have nested on it in air. Here nothing nests. A colony of something — barnacle, anemone, a white frost of mineral — has tried to settle on its hull and given up, or succeeded only sparsely, because there is so little to settle *with*. It sits in the cold like a carcass that never quite became one. The sharks pass it. The fish arc over it. The harvester will, in its own time, come to it, go around it in a straight line that bends for one obstacle, and resume.

None of them mind each other. That is the last thing to understand. A whale does not acknowledge a turbine. A shark does not acknowledge a factory window. A crab does not acknowledge the human watching it from a few metres away, bubbles streaming sideways. They are not a community; they are a set of fixed patterns overlapping by coincidence, for a century, for a thousand years. Inflow equals outflow. Light in equals light out. Noise in equals noise out. The sleeper shark may live three hundred years and never once meet the same silver shoal twice. The turbine may turn for fifty years and never stop for a storm.

They live far apart on a mineral table under a bright sea, in a roar that is the sea itself, passing and waiting and turning, each in its own square of current, each so slow in the cold and so loud in the water that to be here is to be heard by everything and seen by almost nothing, and to go on, in the same way, after you have gone.
