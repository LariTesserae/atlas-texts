At first, standing on the main walk long enough for your eyes to give up on light and start reading outlines instead, you could think no one lives here at all.

It is only the hum, the ticking of the pipes under the floor as they expand, the drip falling into a tin basin with perfect regularity. The heat makes the air shimmer above the boiler wall in a way that looks, if you stare, like movement. And then — because the Underhearth does not hurry anything, including being seen — movement begins to separate itself from that shimmer.

The first lives are the smallest, and the ones most committed to not being noticed. On the cold main at the north end, where condensation runs in a film down the pipework, there are lines. If you lean close, close enough that the warmth of your own breath fogs the metal, you see them resolve: pale shrimp-like mites, blind, translucent as salt, grazing in single file along the wettest seam. They never cross a dry joint. They never hurry. They go out along the drip and back along the drip, a slow, patient procession that has worn its own faint track in the mineral scale. The dwellers know them. No one with a rag wipes them away. They are clean work, done without hands.

Higher up, where your eyes cannot quite reach, is a sound that is not machinery. A soft, stuttering coo, repeated and answered, and then a hush when the ventilation sighs. The pigeons. You will not see them at first either, only the faint grey drift of feathers caught in the amber light, and the nests made of wire, rope fiber, and dust packed into the trusses. They were born here — every bird in that flock, and this is important to understand about everything that lives in the Underhearth — and have never left. Their feathers are grey not from pigment but from brick-dust worked into them over a lifetime. They feed on spilled grain from the storage racks and on the pale fungi harvested below, snatched and dropped again. They nest in the heat the trusses retain. They rise, circle once in the cathedral dark beneath the ceiling that never ends, and settle again, as if the length of the vault were all the sky they needed.

Lower still, flattened against the boiler flank where the steel holds a steady fifty-something degrees, sleeps the heat-lizard. It is blind and translucent enough that the blue of a vein shows through its back, and it does not move for hours at a time. Its blood is being warmed by the steel, directly, without effort. The people who live near that boiler can tell the lizards apart by the faint pattern of scale on their tails, much as they can tell pipes apart by their tick. A lizard will let a hand pass near it to feel the warmth of its own flank, and then go still again. It eats the mites when they wander too far down, or the smaller mites that live in the dust of the boiler room itself. It is not a pet. It is a neighbor, in the strict sense.

In the canal, the black water barely moves, but something in it does. Every so often the surface breaks with a soft, wet ring, and the eel-like fish comes up to breathe the steam directly. It is blind, wrinkled like a pipe joint, and it turns in the forty-centimeter trough with a slow, undulant patience. A small turbine wheel turns near it for no reason except to keep the belt from seizing, and the fish turns with it, patiently, for days. Children sit on the concrete lip and watch for the ring. They have names for the fish, in the way people name weather — not to own it, but to mark that it has returned.

And then the plants, which here are animals in all but movement. You must learn to see them as inhabitants and not as decoration, because no one planted them as decoration and the place decides, very firmly, whether anything green survives.

The fern-and-bean ledge along the rail where the pipe has leaked for fifty years is tended the way a joint is tended. Roots live in mortar and ash. The beans have learned to grow not along the rail, as they were trained, but along the warmth of the rail, creeping toward the pipe. The ferns are etiolated, pale, their fronds thin as paper. The white fungi wall by the canal alcove is cultivated in boiler-shell planters filled with ground brick — each fruiting body a small, blind hand reaching out of the damp. It is harvested with a knife, left to regrow, harvested again. And the montbretia in the cinder pit, banded with iron, older than most of the machines, leans away from the amber bulbs and toward the boiler wall, its shoots bent by heat the way a tree at the surface would be bent by wind. Its leaves are pale and unmistakably thirsty. People prune it. People also leave it alone, because it was there before their grandparents, and it has advantages they do not.

It is only after you have registered all of this — the lines of mites, the cooing above, the lizard’s slow warming, the fish’s ring, the green things fingering the mortar — that the dwellings begin to make sense as dwellings, and not storage.

They are terraced against the warm wall, one stacked upon another, sharing flues. Curtains move. A stovepipe ticks. A flowerless window box holds ash and a single fern. The doors are sheet steel and wood and brick, layered like the walls themselves. And the people come out, not all at once, not on a morning, because there is no morning, but as the boiler cycle turns.

The Underhearth breathes over weeks. When the great engines turn over, the heat inches up, the ticking speeds, the ventilation sighs harder and lifts the dust, and that is the signal. A curtain is pulled back. A kettle is set. A shaft of movement works its way down the terraces. If you watch long enough you understand that this is waking. There is also a sleeping, in the long cool turn, when they withdraw and the place settles back into its hum and only the animals and the mites and the fish keep their slow patrol.

They are, like everything here, unfinished in the way the surface world would call finished, and finished in the way this world requires. About a hundred and forty years of heat have lived in their bodies. Their skin is perpetually damp and never quite dry, salt drawing at the edges of their shirts. Their sweat does not cool them — they know this as a fact of weather, not a hardship — and so they carry rags, and they move slowly, and they breathe slowly, and they have learned to conserve the few breaths the thick air allows. Their eyes are dark-adapted to amber pools and red pinpoints; in the glare of a caged bulb they squint and turn away, but in the dark between bulbs they see the heat shimmer off a pipe as clearly as you might see a face. Their hands are hardened at the palms from rope, metal, hot brick. Their hair is thin. Their clothes are layered and patched and never quite clean, because water must be caught and what is caught is drunk or used for the gardens, not for washing away heat.

You will not hear them loudly. Sound carries in the vault and returns, and loudness would be wasteful anyway. Their speech is low, unhurried, and often conducted around a basin or a boiler door, shoulder to shoulder, because warmth is shared currency. The shift bells that sound like struck cooking pots are enough to mark work. A knock on a pipe can carry three levels of dwellings. They murmur more than they talk, and their children learn the language of the place by listening to the ticking and the drip and the sighing, long before they learn much else.

Their work is tending. It is not a factory that was abandoned and then occupied; it is a body that went to sleep warm and stayed warm, and the people are its caretakers in the same way mites are caretakers of pipes. Boiler tenders know the scale on a joint by feel, know when a coral of mineral will hold and when it will flake. Water catchers watch the thin rain under the cold main and the weeping of the intake wall and keep the basins, the hollowed engine blocks, the tin cups in their patient round. Garden tenders — often the same women who tend the dwellings — feed the fungi wall, untangle the beans from the rail toward the heat, water the montbretia from a leak so it does not have to be watered from a store. Someone tends the turbine wheel in the canal, though it turns nothing, because a still belt will seize and a seized belt will be a loss the world cannot afford. Someone watches the pigeons, not to keep them, but to keep their nests from the walkway and their grain from the damp.

And between the work, there is the rest of living, compressed into the warm terraces. Cooking over shared flues, so a dwelling warms another. Eating beans and fungi and the occasional pigeon egg, and, rarely and with ceremony, a fish taken from the canal — black, hot, smelling of steam and minerals — though most of the time the fish is left to turn. Children play in the narrow trough of the canal and on the catwalks, counting drips, counting the seventeen minutes between sighs, learning where the floor is hot and where it gives. They have never seen the sun, and so the boiler wall in its banked glow is, for them, a kind of sun: steady, low, to the south end of their world. The ceiling lost in darkness above them is night, permanent and high and studded, on clear heat cycles, with the red pinpoints of pilot lights. Some of them, the very old, still remember stories told by grandparents of a grandmother who came down as a girl, of stairs that went up twelve stories and out into weather that changed by itself. The children hear it the way the dwellers hear the pigeons: interesting, far away, not quite to be believed.

The old ones sit. That is their work now. They sit by the boiler wall with their backs to the warmth that has lived in the brick for a century, and they watch the turbine, and the lizards, and the children, and they know the scale of things. Five generations, perhaps. No one keeps exact count, because time is marked by the thickening of scale and the creep of a vine and the slow cycle of the engines, not by years. When someone dies — and they do, quietly, in a warm room, in the cool turn — the body is not taken away, because there is no away. It is dried near the boilers and then reduced, patiently, and the ash goes into the brick planters, into the cinder pit, into the soil that is only brick-dust and iron filings. It is not a grim practice to them. It is the loop, complete. Fungi eat the damp, mites eat the fungi, lizards and fish eat the mites, people eat the fungi, and everything feeds the heat in return. A person, in time, feeds the beans.

If you stay long enough, you begin to understand what they grow toward, as the plants do. Not light — light is rationed here, pooled and amber, and they keep to its edges. Heat. They arrange their dwellings against it, their chairs to it, their hands to it, their stories to it. They move toward warmth the way the montbretia leans, without thinking about it, as if the world’s usual signals had been quietly inverted and they had learned the new ones perfectly. The sweat on their necks, the slow shuffle worn into the floor channel, the salt ferns across the lower brick that have stopped growing because the place has balanced itself — all of it is the same accommodation.

They are not waiting to go up. A few, in a season, might climb the switchback stairs with a message or a part or a repair, and come back down with the heat as a wall hitting them again at the fourth landing. Most never do. They are born here, they learn the drip and the sigh and the tick, they tend what must be tended, they warm themselves, and the pigeons coo above them and the fish turns below them and the mites make their slow lines along the wet metal, and the place, hot and dark and dense and tenderly repaired from what is already here, holds together around them without further change.

It is a small world with its walls close. It hums. It ticks as it warms. And the lives inside it go on, in that settled, weathered, patient state, breathing the thick air, growing toward the heat, content to remain.
