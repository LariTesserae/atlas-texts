You do not see them at first. You hear them first, because everything here carries.

A shuffle on aggregate, too slow to be a machine. A cough muffled into a coat. Two lights coming through the fog across a terrace, orange and swinging, and then gone again behind a press. It is a long time before you understand that the lights are not staying. They cross, and cross again an hour later in the opposite direction, and you realize the crossing is the living. There are not many. One terrace can hold a whole shift walking single file and still look empty to anyone standing on the next one down.

They are the most obvious animals in the Basin, and the least at home. The workers come down the shaft in heavy coats because the cold never leaves, and they go up again when the roster says so, and the ones who mean it call that living here. Most do not. They sleep in heated boxes along the service corridors where the green-white tubes burn, in rooms too small for a window, and they eat from ration packs warmed on a motor. Their hands go numb on iron railings and stay numb. Their eyes adjust to sodium orange and then ache in the green-white, and ache again out in the dark between lamps where nothing quite reaches. You learn to pick them out by their warmth: a coat steaming where a pipe crosses, a breath fogging and hanging a moment longer than it should, a place where the frost on the concrete has melted off in a boot-print and not refrozen yet.

They follow the same paths every shift because the paths are made. A man will walk an hour to check a gate he already knows is closed. Another will patrol a channel where there is nothing to patrol except the water. They are solitary even when they work together, each one in his own weather. When the fans cycle and the fog thickens, you see only their lamps, bobbing, and the lamps become a small herd moving through a much larger emptiness.

Anything that can live by following warmth will follow them.

It does not follow them into the open terrace, where the winter air cuts through and Skins the puddles with ice. It follows them into the seams. Where a duct sweats and drips steadily onto dusty concrete, where a joint has loosened and lets out a warmish thread of steam in the interior rain, where an engine idles and heats the air for ten meters around it — there, life gathers in thin lines. It maps exactly onto the made things.

In the service corridors the lines are planted. Long planters hold the engineered grasses and ferns they put there for air, pale under the tubes, yellowing where the cold touches the tips. Men and women in the corridor crews water them with cans, though the rain from the bridges already wets them, because tending is part of the schedule. The moss that has taken to the lamp tubes and to the edges of the planters is the same color as the ferns, only softer. Where a drip falls in the same place all winter, a felted patch of lichen-moss spreads, pale green-grey, eating cement dust and a little lamplight, nothing else. It is the only thing that looks like it grew on its own. Even the mold that comes behind it was probably seeded in the mix.

The mold comes in black tides over the rubber seals. It loves the warm, wet corners where two pipes meet, and it eats patiently, seal by seal, until a joint weeps and a new drip is born and a new patch starts below it. Spiders live in the high duct corners above the steam, pale as the moss, building webs heavy with condensation beads that catch the orange light. Moths live around the lamps themselves in a slow, stupid orbit, dropping into the fog and dying there, and the spiders eat them. Pigeons — someone must have let them in long ago, or they rode down in a crate of feed — roost in grey rows on the pipe bridges, drinking the interior rain as it falls, pecking at frost-melt on the steel, flying short, startled circuits from one terrace to the next and landing again where the pipes are warmest. Rats live deeper in that warmth, in the grease traps and the warm ducts above the vats. You rarely see one whole; you see an orange eye-shine in a grate, a tail vanishing under a machine, a greasy run along the edge of a walkway where many feet, human and otherwise, have worn the same line clean.

Below all of that, where the terraces give up and the reservoir takes over, there are people who do not go up.

They are the Gate folk, if anyone still calls them that on the rosters. They live on the lowest ring in huts built from salvaged panels, on stilts above the black water, where the rain that falls from the bridges is thickest and the fog rolls in and never quite leaves. Their lanterns reflect in the sump and do not go out when the shift changes. They tend the gates that send the water up and down a hand’s breadth, and the water is farmed above them for coolant and they farm it for nothing, and so they stay to watch it rise and fall. The cold down there is steadier, the air tastes of mildew and hot oil, and their bodies have learned it too well: skin chapped raw, lungs that cough out metal dust in the morning, eyes that water in any light brighter than their own. If a child is born down there, it has never felt wind except fan wind, never seen a sky because there is none to see, only a ribbed ceiling that sweats. They fish, unofficially, with nets from the stilts.

What they catch is what lives in the reservoir. Pale fish that never surface, blind and slow, kept alive by the warmth of the outflow pipes. They move like thoughts under the black water. When the interior rain falls hard from a bridge, they rise a little, mouths gaping at the surface film where oil and insects and fallen moths gather. They taste of copper and coolant when they are cooked on engine heat, and the Gate folk eat them anyway. They do not see the lanterns. They have no use for eyes in water that is never light, only reflections of lamps.

In the coolant tanks above, in the warmer rings, the eels do the same work without a surface. Eyeless, translucent, they knot themselves around the heating elements and stay there for days, warm, breathing slowly, eating the bacterial film that grows on the metal. The film is orange where iron bacteria have taken hold, waving in the flow like hair. Men in the tank crews scrape it off with rods and the eels slip away into the dark between pipes, and then come back when the heat settles. Nothing much eats the eels. When one dies it sinks and feeds the film, and the film feeds the next eel, and the cycle goes on in a tank sealed from the world.

Small things like that are the true population: the bacteria in the coolant loops, the slime in the pipes, the rust that starts bright and quick on any cut edge and spreads like a slow fire. Predation here is not tooth and claw. It is water finding a seam, frost prying a joint, a schedule denying a body sleep, a press stamping seven seconds away while a man tries to rest in the noise. The Basin eats its own made things, and the small living things help it, and are helped by it. The made earth is channeled by its own water. The pipes have grown icicle trees in winter air. The concrete walls that were poured last season are already being softened at the drip lines, learning where the water wants to go.

And then there are the machines, which behave more like animals than any of this does.

The little maintenance crawlers patrol the channels all night, low and patient, following their rails, turning where the rails turn. When they idle they huddle near a warm pipe like things seeking heat. The overhead cranes cross the dim on their rails carrying a single beam, groaning, their work-lamps swinging across fog, turning their heads — you cannot help but think of them as heads — toward a sound. The presses in the middle ring are parked with their arms folded, sleeping quadrupeds. The conveyor belts move empty, loop after loop, blind worms that have forgotten what they carried. The vats sigh steam from their pressure valves in a regular rhythm, breathing. If you stand long enough in one place, you begin to count them as you would a herd: so many crawlers on this channel, so many cranes on that rail, all moving, all following lines laid down for them, all using the heat and light and water as they find it.

That is what lives in the Low Basin. Not a community, not a wilderness. A thin film, placed and then adapted, spread over made ground. Humans in coats following their hour-long paths. Ferns in planters following the green-white tubes. Moss following a drip. Mold following a seal. Rats and pigeons and spiders following warmth. Fish following the warm outflow. Eels following a heating element. Crawlers following a channel. Nothing builds its own shelter; everything borrows the Basin’s. Nothing makes its own soil; everything lives on slag and aggregate and steel.

When the shift bell rings, muffled by fog, the lamps move out in a line toward the shaft, and the crawlers keep patrolling, and the fish rise to meet the rain that falls from a bridge for no one, and the frost forms again on the far wall where no one is looking.
