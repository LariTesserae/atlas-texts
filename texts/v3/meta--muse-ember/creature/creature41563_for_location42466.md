The first thing that lives here is the thing you walk on.

You do not notice it as life at first, because it does not look alive. It is only the slickness under your boot, the quality that makes you walk more carefully on the submerged cobbles than on the dryish stone near the rim. Every stone below the film of water wears a skin, translucent and faintly grey, a few cells thick if you could see them, much thicker in the hollows. It is not moss. It is not algae. There is no green in it at all. It is a biofilm — bacteria and other microbes of the warm, mineral water, living in the water itself and holding on to the rock because there is nowhere else to hold on.

They are the place's true population, in numbers no one would count. They live on what the water brings up from below: dissolved minerals, heat, the faint chemistry that leaves the buff crust around the pool. In return they make the water more itself. Where they are thickest, at the edge of the main pool, they precipitate calcium and silica out of solution in thin layers, and those layers harden. That is the porcelain edge you can crack with your weight — white and ochre, a few centimeters thick in places, built up layer on layer, polygonal plates sealing over again as soon as you disturb them. Rock is growing here because living things are precipitating it, and living things are growing because the rock gives them a warm, wet, unchanging surface to grow on. The two are indistinguishable after a while. Disturb the crust and you disturb the colony; leave it and the colony makes more crust.

They do not bloom. They do not die back. The water is always the same warmth, the same saturation, the same mineral load, so they stay at the same thin thickness, covering the submerged stones from rim to center, connecting pool to film to film without a break. If there is a food web here, it starts and largely ends with them.

Only a step above them, in every sense, are the animals that graze them.

In the main pool, if you stand still long enough for your breathing to quiet, you may see the newt. Or — it is difficult to say whether it is the same one you saw last month, or another very like it in the same place. One small newt, dark against dark basalt, hanging a hand's breadth below the surface near the center, where the water is warmest and stillest. It does not swim. It does not hunt in any way you would recognize. It hangs, gills feathery and almost still, taking in what little oxygen warm water holds and what its skin can take from the damp air above. Its body is in the equilibrium, too: slow, unhurried, cold-blooded in water that never gets cold. It will shift, now and then, a body-length to the left, and hang again. It presumably eats the microscopic life in the film and the water — ostracods, larval specks, the biofilm itself scraped slowly at the pool margin — but it does so at a rate that does not disturb the rings from the drips, and does not deplete anything that needs replenishing. There is no shoal for it, no mate visible, no chorus. If there are others, they are as solitary and as still as this one, tucked under a ledge or in a crevice between cobbles, hanging in the same warm, dim water. A population reduced to the minimum that warmth and food will support, persisting because nothing here asks it to do anything else.

The beetle works the other side of the same bargain. A single beetle, as far as anyone who has sat here can tell — shell black and polished by constant wetness, making its slow way across a flat, warmer slab near the southern rim. It cannot fly, or does not. There is no wind to fly in, and no reason to leave a stone that is always exactly damp enough. Its feet find the biofilm on the high stone where the water is only a shine, and it pauses, and drinks the film, and moves on. Where its larvae live is not obvious; presumably in the wet seams between cobbles, feeding on the same microbial skin, growing as slowly as everything else here. It and the newt never meet. One lives in the pool, one on the stone beside it, and the inches between them are a whole ecology. It leaves no track except the faint polish of its shell over years of crossing the same warm basalt.

And then there are the shells. Not living now. Tiny, white, empty, wedged in the crevices between the cobbles where the gaps are almost closed, collected in pockets like gravel. They are too small for the beetle, wrong for the newt. In life they were probably small gastropods — snails that could live on biofilm alone, without a leaf or a root anywhere in their world, grazing the mineral film at the pool's edge and sealing themselves against the saturated air when they rested. They needed calcium, and the water provides it in excess. They died, as things do, and nothing here broke them down. No scavenger swarm, no fungus, no acid soil. The mineral water that made their shells in the first place preserves them, bleaches them white, and sets them in the stone like fossils in formation. You can read the place's history in them: a few snails lived here, over a few years, perhaps a few generations at an unhurried pace, and their houses outlasted them by a long margin because the place does not recycle quickly and does not need to.

That is almost the whole census. No fish in the pool — too warm, too still, too mineral, and nothing to eat that a fish would bother with. No frogs — nowhere to lay eggs that would not be stewed, no plants to hide in. No insects in the air; the only winged thing ever to arrive would find nothing to land on that was not already wet. Even the air is inhabited in a way: the vapor that lifts off the pool, condenses on the overhang and on your shoulders, and falls again as drips, carrying microbes with it, seeding the film on stone that was disturbed yesterday. The place breathes its inhabitants around in a circle a few meters wide.

People do not live here. They are the only other animals who have ever come, and they have only ever visited. The low arc of dry-laid stone on the windward side, the three scooped basins by the pool filled to the same level as the pool, the pair of hammerstones left side by side, the soft shards of pottery half-buried in the clay film — these are not a camp. They are the traces of someone sitting down in the warm shallows for a while, perhaps soaking feet, perhaps just resting in the quiet, and then getting up and leaving the stones as they found them. A human body is too warm, too active, too large to be a resident of an equilibrium this tight; it would sweat, and add to the condensation, and disturb the crust, and then it would leave, and the film would seal over. The place remembers a footprint for an hour by the cracking of mineral plates and then forgets it.

So what lives here lives by not doing much. The microbes build rock. The newt hangs. The beetle crosses its slab. The snails, mostly, are gone and leave their houses behind. Nothing eats anything else in any dramatic proportion, nothing migrates in or out, nothing breeds in a season and starves in another, because there is no season. The light does not change, the temperature does not change, the drip does not change its interval. Life here has adapted not by becoming clever or fast, but by becoming settled — by joining the stones in being exactly what they are, warm and wet and packed tight, for a very long time.
