At first there is not a population. There is a pressure.

You do not so much walk through it as negotiate with it, because the floor is rarely empty for more than a few steps, and the walls are never empty at all, and the air is not empty either. Wherever you stand still long enough for your eyes to adjust, something crosses in front of them, something clicks, something chews, something drips and falls and runs. The life here is not rare and hidden, the way life usually is underground. It is dense to the point of being a weather system. You hear it before you separate it: the amplified dripping in the vaults, the dry rain of clicking from the rock faces, the hissing of vents, the splash and scramble in the pools, the low booms of settling stone, wings tearing, and underneath it all, a constant coughing and snuffling, as if a whole landscape were breathing through its nose.

The base of it is not green. There is no grass, no root, no leaf, no moss as you know it. What clothes the place instead is felt and paint.

Vast fungal mats spread wherever the damp is constant. They skin the walls from the floor to halfway up the ceiling in blue-grey velvet, they hang in curtains from ledges to catch the condensation as it beads and runs, and they run underground in pale cords that surface without warning as sudden, fleshy mushrooms with no cap at all — only a folded, brain-like fruiting body, pinkish and pulsing, that releases a puff of spores when you brush it. They eat the dead and the mineral stain, they sew rubble back together after a slab falls, they shoot new threads across fresh fall within a day. Between them, in the rivulets, bacteria have painted the stones themselves: rust, ink, brown and clear films, and a startling, almost metallic orange that catches the shaft-light and makes a stream look lacquered. Even the driest stone sweats when you press your palm to it, and this low, non-green carpet is what lives on that sweat.

On that carpet, the herds move.

They are segmented and armoured, dog-sized, pink-grey, half-blind, with kneeling legs that fold them low to the ground and scraping mouths that work sideways. They are grazers of fungus, and they chew it down to mineral, leaving a clean, wet path behind them that shines in the sun-shafts. The mat regrows in days. They return. So the whole basin seems to breathe in a slow, synchronized advance — not a scattered crowd of individuals, but a river on land, flowing from chamber to chamber through the broad, low arches, dust rising where they bolt, snuffling where they settle to chew. Where they have passed, the felt is cropped short. Where they have not, it grows thick and velvety.

The walls are inhabited layer upon layer, in tens of thousands.

Clinging in clusters so tight that at a distance they read as a change in the colour of the rock are thumb-length amphibians with sucker feet and flickering tongues. They particularly favour the sun-warmed faces of pillars, where a thin blade of noon moves very slowly across the floor and the stone is wet clay and ochre. Near-touching, they cover whole faces like a living wallpaper, constantly clicking. Every few minutes a section ripples: they detach, drop a short way, land on a lower ledge, reattach, and the ripple runs across the wall like wind across a field. They are not still camouflage. They are a surface in motion, shedding and resettling, climbing back up again after.

In the shafts, the air itself is textured.

Flocks of swift-like fliers — leathery-winged, sparrow to hawk-sized — spiral up and down the light, nesting in the cracks of the high, irregular ceiling in screaming colonies. They use the warm up-draughts from the vents as elevators; when a vent sighs steam and the air rises, they spin a little faster, lifted. When they take off together it is a weather event: a vortex of bodies, a tearing roar of wings, a momentary eclipse of the punctures in the roof. They fall again out of the light, screaming, to land in the cracks, and the chamber, half a second before, deafening, is suddenly only differently loud.

Below them, in the rivulets and pockets of warm water that appear, disappear into porous travertine, and reappear foaming white a hundred metres on, eel-like swimmers work the shallows in shoals. The water is rarely still for long — something swims through and leaves a wake that persists. And some of them are the strangest of the common things here, because they do not stay in the water. They climb. Hauling themselves up wet walls on fin-limbs, out of water entirely for hours, they hunt the wall-clingers on their own wallpaper, leaving glistening tracks on vertical stone where no swimmer should be able to go.

Under the floor, the engineers never stop.

Blind, pale, mole-like but bigger through the shoulders, burrowers push up the still-unhardened clay in endless, intersecting tunnels. The ground is never still under your feet. It bulges, subsides, is riven by a crack that parallel-walks you for ten metres and then closes as the animal beneath passes. Their spoil is laid in ridges that are instantly colonized — by things with too many legs, by fungal threads, by the bacteria that stain everything they touch. A boot-print in the sun-shaft, half-filled by falling dust, is already a small landscape for them.

And rarely, and felt more than seen, there are the large ones.

Solitary, low, long-legged predators. You will mostly find their tracks in the wet clay — three-toed, human-hand-sized, fresher than the rubble, older than the fungal regrowth. They do not announce themselves. The grazers fear them as a change in the air. When one passes through a roost, the wall-clingers ripple all at once, the fliers go up in one confused thunder, the grazers bolt in a single dust-raising surge, and for thirty seconds the whole chamber is nothing but noise and motion. Then it settles, not quieter, but differently loud, and the predator is already elsewhere, low and alone, a current moving through other currents.

In the midst of all that animal abundance, scattered small and easily lost, are signs of something that makes and uses things.

Not much of it. A windbreak, half-collapsed, of large flat stones lashed together with gut cord — there is no plant fibre here, no wood, cordage is sinew and hair. A shallow fire-pit, blackened, its ash long since washed into a rivulet. A flaked stone scraper still hafted to a bone handle, left on a ledge. A needle of polished bone. And now and then, in a little clearing where the mat has been deliberately kept cropped, low domed huts of interwoven shed exoskeletons and haematite, roofed with fungal felt, big enough for a crouching family. Whatever lives in them now is hand-tool primitive: stone knapped, bone worked, things mended and remended. They are not the landscape. They are a small, persistent noise inside it, and the place reclaims them fast — leave a camp for a season and burrowers will have run a tunnel under the windbreak, wall-clingers will be piston-crawling on the hut roof, a new shaft of calcite will be growing up through the fire-pit.

Because nothing here behaves as an individual for long. A migration of grazers is a flood. A flight of the shaft birds is a storm cell, complete with wind and shadow. A wall of clingers shedding at once is a landslide that then climbs back up. The herds are eaten, or outbreed their food and crash, and the walls thin, and then flare again. A vent opens, warm air rises, the fliers spin faster, condensation breaks loose from the top of a pillar and rains down in a fine, brief shower, the rivulet swells and carries a fungal leaf, and a shoal of swimmers turns to follow it.

It smells of iron and ammonia and wet stone and mushroom, because that is what a country made largely of animals smells like at 25, 28 degrees, close and liquid. They are its weather, its erosion, its builders and its demolition crew, running the place turbulently, densely, loudly, while the stone underneath them is still trying, slowly, to become a landscape.
