You hear them before you see them taking up the whole place.

Not a footfall exactly. A click. *Tik-tik-tik*, rapid and dry, under something snout-like, and then a heavier click on concrete, hoof on stone, hoof on rail. The sound carries cleanly here because everything is hard and damp, and nothing softens it. Then the bellow comes, from no one place in particular — a deep, hollow note that does not so much sound as resonate, that makes the corrugated walls of the nearest warehouse hum in answer, as if the building itself were continuing the note for them.

In the mill yard, one is already crossing the gutter line at a pace that seems less like walking than like being slowly, inevitably moved.

It is the size of a large hog, low-slung, barrel-bodied, its six legs short and set wide. The hide is grey and wet and wrinkled, and it is not just grey — it is grey with the place worked into it. Grit, brick-dust, cinder, ground into the skin like aggregate in concrete, so that at a distance it looks less like an animal than like a piece of the yard that has learned to move. Along its back, overlapping in segments, are plates of mineral slag, grown up out of the skin itself, dull and pitted, fitting one over the other like crude roof tiles. Its head hangs low, a blunt, arched snout working slowly over the concrete, its neck thick folds.

All six feet end in the same thing: black, iron-hard hooves, neat and polished, that click against the polished grooves in the concrete. When it walks, the barrel of it rolls very slightly from side to side, and the six legs find their places under it without hurry. It does not stumble. There are no stumbles here.

Another comes around the boiler house. And another. A loose procession, following the gutter, bumping gently against each other and making way with a sigh — a long, damp exhalation through the chest that fogs the air at their own ankle height. They are fat. Sleek-fat, despite what they are doing, which is nothing so much as grazing.

Up close there are no eyes to find. Where eyes should be, in the flat sides of the head, there are only small milky pits, skin-covered, faintly paler than the rest. They do not open. In the turbine hall, when a piston clangs and the whole floor rings, their heads lift all at once, snouts up, and you understand they were listening with the whole of themselves. They navigate by the clicking under the snout — a needle of sound they send out and take back — and by the vibration that comes up through their legs. The concrete carries it. The iron carries it better. Put your own hand flat on a pipe in Kell Hollow and you can feel, very faintly, that they are all standing on the same instrument.

Their mouths are seldom seen. The snout works the damp concrete, the north sides of walls, the inside of drains, scraping the grey-green fungal felt that crusts every damp surface. The felt comes away in a clean streak, and then, over hours, begins to creep back to the same edge. They take it in slowly, steadily, with a thick tongue you only glimpse, and they drop pellets as they go — grey, mineral, neat. The pellets do not accumulate. The fungus spreads over them almost at once. On the yard, grey flakes curl up from their backs where a plate is shedding, curl like dry leaves, fall, and are colonized in the same way. Later, the animals come back along their circuit and eat the flakes with the fungus on them, and the fungus eats what they have passed, and the plates grow again from the minerals they have taken in. It is a fully closed appetite. Nothing is wasted because nothing is ever finished.

They drink by licking. Condensation beads on pipes, on the undersides of eaves, on their own plated backs, and they lift their heads and lap it up, or turn and lick the plates of the animal ahead of them. In this way the water that sweats off the cold metal at night is taken back into them, breathed out as damp musky breath — faintly metallic, like the smell of the gutters — and condenses again. Their breath fogs your ankles when one has settled its bulk directly across a doorway and will not be hurried. Within an hour of being here you will have to climb carefully over a back sleeping in a wheel-rut, or wait for a slow bulk to heave itself out of your path, hindered, unbothered, and impossibly heavy for how quietly it moves.

Because for every one of them in the yard there are others up on the walls.

You look up and the place comes into focus all at once. Under every eave, in every window frame, pressed flat against brick and corrugated iron with all six legs splayed, they cling. Hundreds of them, smaller only because they have ironed themselves out against the vertical, their barrels flattened, their snouts tucked down into the damp. The roofs are shingled with them. The underside of the water tower is a beard of them, hanging head-down in a mass that does not fall. They are motionless, except for a constant fine twitching of all six legs, like a shivering, like a net of small vibrations constantly being plucked and re-plucked at the surface of the building.

They are not young. They are not growing. They are doing the same thing, in the vertical, that the others do on the horizontal: listening, licking where the wall weeps, grazing the felt where it crusts the brick. If one loses its grip — if one ever does — the ones near it nudge it back toward the wall with their blunt heads, patiently, until the legs find purchase again.

The ground ones, too, walk in circuits. Along the canal, in single slow file that is never really a file. Turn at the station platform where the rails end in buffers. Follow the rail line to the substation. Loop through the mill yard. Begin again. No leader. No destination. The procession has worn its own channels into the concrete — those long polished grooves you first took for erosion — and the river of them is always at the same height, always moving slowly enough that the landscape as a whole does not seem to move at all. A scarred one with a torn plate on its left flank, exposing paler hide beneath, is in the same doorway every time you pass, at the same point in its own smaller loop. It seems less that they are traveling than that they are tending the circuit, keeping it open, keeping it true.

When they call, the sound comes from the hollow of the chest, a deep resonant bellow that you feel as much in your teeth as in your ears, and it is always answered — not by another animal, necessarily, but by the buildings. A corrugated wall will hum for a second after. A pipe will ping. In a place this loud with machines, their voices do not rise above the turbine and the conveyor and the fans. They become part of the current, a low note in it, a second factory whistle sounding from inside the animals themselves.

They do not fear you. They step around your boots with the same indifference they give a pipe or a dropped plate. They have no interest in what you are, because nothing here has an interest in anything outside its loop. There is no hunting, no fleeing, no birth you can witness, no death you can find. The number does not change. Thousands, always thousands, fat and sleek and apparently ageless, their scars persisting, their plates shedding and regrowing in the same segments.

By the end of an hour you stop counting them and start counting on them: the click under your feet, the weight settled across a threshold, the grey shingle of bodies on every wall, the slow river turning the corner of the boiler house and turning again. They are not in Kell Hollow the way animals are in a field. They are the way the hum is in the turbine hall, the way the damp is in the concrete. The place finished itself, and they are what finished it — six-legged, eyeless, iron-hooved, listening to the buildings through their feet, eating the film that eats them, drinking the water the cold metal gives up at night, and walking, and walking, in a circuit that does not go anywhere because it has already arrived.
