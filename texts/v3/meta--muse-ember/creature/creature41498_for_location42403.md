They do not live *on* the plateau so much as in its breathing.

You feel them first before you can claim to see them. Through the soles of your feet, through the wet basalt, a warmth and a pressure that rises and falls, a slow pumping no greater than a few centimeters but spread over a kilometer of rock. It is not an earthquake. It is not weather. It is movement everywhere at once, near-touching movement, and it makes your teeth ache and your ears pop on a summit that is not getting any higher.

That is where they are. Not in a burrow or a nest. In the seams, in the damp, in the warm gravel a meter beyond where a braided thread of water vanishes. The air tastes of metal and rain and warm earth, and they taste the air, constantly, because tasting is how they navigate a world without true light and without sound.

At the dimming — for there is no dusk, only a further thickening of the grey-green ceiling — the air itself seems to gather.

The swarmers come down or up, there is no difference. They were already there. Blind, translucent, no larger than a thumb joint and no larger than a hand, they hang and drift in curtains so dense they blot what little light remains. They have no mouths in any useful sense. Their whole bodies are filters, fringed with hairs that catch spores and metal ions from the saturated, thin air, that comb the updrafts for what the piston-fields lift from below. They exhale what they cannot use, and that exhalation is also a seed. In the sunless dim they cast no proper shadow; they cast a pool, a smear, a temporary lessening in a hollow between stones, updraft carrying them onward.

You learn not to look for individuals. A swarmer today is weather tomorrow, and soil the day after, carried up on the same columns that carry moisture to the high ridges to condense there. Their generations do not follow each other. They loop.

Below them, and under your feet, is the green.

It is not a forest in rows. It is saturation, layered until it becomes a fabric. Fern-mats, broad-leaved things with leaves like wet black paper, moss falling in curtains thirty meters from a ledge and then, where it ends, another kind beginning. The roots interlace into a single springy skin you can walk on, humming faintly with contained moisture. The plants are bottled-green to drink the rumor of a sun behind damp grey, but they do not live by light alone. They open at night as much as day. Many feed on heat directly, on the deep warm rising through cracks, on the chemical fire of things eating stone. Their few flowers are not colored to be seen. They are shaped to release spores into the warm currents, upward, into the engines of the sky.

And the green is grazed, ceaselessly, because nothing here is allowed to grow undisturbed long enough to become old in any one place.

The grazers move in near-touching ranks across the mats, low to the ground, many-legged, plodding in step dictated not by their own hearts but by the piston-fields beneath them. Their bodies are already half-mineral. Basalt dust and ironstone have been incorporated into their shells, quartz has veined them where they have stood still too long. They cannot stand still. To stop for long is to begin to fossilize — to be left behind as a layer in a fresh cliff, fern-fossils over basalt that should be a million years younger, a stone both newborn and ancient. So they walk. Walking is how the species stays an animal.

Their teeth are millstones, turning with a soundless, grinding motion. Inside them are chambers — acid, bacteria that eat stone, fungi that came with the grazers as passengers and now live as organs — where rock is unzipped into minerals and sugars. They eat the fern-mat, they eat the soil the mat will become, they eat the crystal-turbine dust that falls on them like a fine rain. One kind does nothing else but walk the great conveyor bands of lichen across whole mountain flanks, its hooves tamping soil into place as it goes, a living piston matching the rock pistons it crosses. It has no other work. It does not hunt, it does not rest where the ground wants it to rest. It keeps the band moving.

Above the mats, on the updrafts, are the drifters.

One of them is vast as a house and translucent as a lung, slow, without legs or wings, pulsing itself higher on heat the way a piston-field pulses. It does not chase the swarmers. It travels through them. As it goes it rains down a fine rust-red dust, waste and seed at once, onto the fern-mats below where the dust is both fertilizer and beginning. When it dies, or when it simply grows too heavy with minerals to stay aloft, it settles and becomes a terrace, a new slope, a fresh step in a fault scarp that will be green within the week. On the scale of days a valley can decide to become a mountain. The drifter is part of that decision.

There are predators, but they do not pursue. Pursuit would be wasteful in a place that wastes nothing. A predator is a connector further down the line: it finds a grazer whose mill-teeth are worn, whose mineral shell is cracked, whose movement has slowed toward stone. It merges with it — no bite, no roar, only a slow dissolving, a taking-in. The dissolved animal's minerals are returned to the updrafts in the predator's own body, exhaled as aerosol, caught again by the swarmers' filters, dropped again as dust by the drifters. Nothing builds. Everything is recycled into motion. They do not call to each other. The thin, saturated air swallows sound and the fern-mats absorb what little is made. They recognize each other by vibration through the soles, by pressure on the skin, by the changing warmth of a neighboring body.

And the industry of the place is not separate from them. It *is* them, grown large.

The crystal-turbine plants stand where the wind from the updrafts is constant, each one a lattice of iron-moss and quartz spar turning in the warm currents. They are not driven by wind the way a machine is driven. They grow toward heat, they turn toward it, their edges rasping soundlessly against cliff faces, grinding rock into the fertile dust that feeds the mats. The conveyor-lichen crawls in bands, mechanized in its stateliness, moving nutrients across a flank for days, depositing them in fan-shaped deltas where the bands meet. The fungal mills in the damp troughs unzip fallen plant matter into sugars, into acids, into more lichen, at a relentless scaled pace. The piston-fields themselves are colonized by small, heat-loving things that ride the rise and sink like passengers in slow elevators, pumping cold air down and warm air up with their bodies, lifting moisture to where it will condense on the high ridges in thick wool on the northern faces of rocks.

To live here is to be, in sequence, a filter, a mill, a conveyor, a seed, and soil. A swarmer may filter the spores of a fern that grew from a grazer that ate a lichen that unzipped a drifter that ate the swarmer's ancestor. Their tracks are sometimes found fully formed in mud that has not yet been rained on, because the herd that will make them is already walking the path in a loop of the thermal seam, one step ahead of its own feet.

They have no noon to mark a life by. Their lives are measured in breaths of the plateau: an inhale of cold air down a seam, an exhale of heat up a canyon, three minutes of rain that falls upward and gathers in a pocket on the underside of an overhang, a fault sighing upward a hand's breadth. A fern-forest can grow to maturity, collapse under its own lush weight, and be ground by the mill-animals into soil that lifts into a new terrace, all in hours. They are born into that speed and they do not hurry. The frantic energy is not in their limbs. It is in the system, total and perpetual, building and breaking the same things faster and faster without ever stopping to rest.

They do not see the sun because there is nothing to see it with. They see heat as shape, shadow pooling at odd angles, the detached darkness under a ridge at an hour when the ridge itself is still in light. They do not hear each other because hearing would be lost in damp. They feel the plateau working through their bodies, the low pressure, the tick of cooling crystal, the drip of condensation, the soft boom, arriving long after the dust, of a valley deciding to become a mountain somewhere far away.

If something made and left, it would be eaten. That is why there are no buildings, no roads, no ruins, no names carved. Anything built would be taken as feedstock, ground by turbine plants, unzipped by fungi, tamped by hooves, dissolved by connectors. So they build nothing. They continue.

Stand long enough on a young ridge where the basalt is still warm enough to make the air shimmer, and you stop trying to count them. The swarmers are weather. The grazers are the turning of the soil. The drifters are rain that has learned to travel upward. The turbines are plants that have learned to grind. The pistons are rock that has learned to breathe.

It is almost completely silent, and the silence is dense, the silence of a system running at full capacity in a vacuum-sealed room. They are not quiet because they are hidden. They are quiet because they are everywhere, and the place that made them wants only to be continued.
