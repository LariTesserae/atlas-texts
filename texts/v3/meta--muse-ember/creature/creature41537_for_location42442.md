At first, no one.

That is the honest field note, and if you wrote it in pencil the dust would take it. There are no animals here. Nothing moves under its own power, no insects in the powder, no burrowers under the stone, no birds to cross the dim sky and make it brief for a third thing. You walk the plateau for an hour — a long hour, in a place where an hour feels borrowed — and the only motion you catalogue is the one the place itself keeps insisting on: dust settling, crystal fronds lengthening by a fraction you will never catch, a shadow going the wrong way across a black shard.

So you have to learn to watch differently. Not for movement. For duration.

The first resident to declare itself is not a creature at all, if creature implies appetite and escape. It is the fern-work.

Up close the mineral dendrites are hard and cold, white filaments branching from a point across the face of a rock in perfect, unhurried symmetry. They look like frost ferns, until you put a gloved finger near them and feel no frost, only stone. They spread a few millimetres in a century, feeding on the dust and on the faint thermal gradient of the ground. They grow toward the warmth that leaks from the built things and away from it, in the same gesture, because the field around those things is symmetrical and the fronds are symmetrical answers to it. Where two fronds meet an obstacle they stop, not because they are blocked but because the accretion and the dissolution arrive at the same number. A whole rock can be furred white, a whole pan of dust can be laced, and it is not a forest in the way a forest moves. It is a forest that has agreed to be still.

And yet it is the only thing here that visibly increases. Everything else is finished. The ferns are still finishing, at a pace that makes geological time look brisk. To them, a dome's slow cycle is a heartbeat. A crystal colony that started when the pocket was sealed will live until the pocket seals tighter, and it will never have hurried in its life. If they have a society, it is this: growth toward heat, arrest at contact, a garden whose shape is a map of the field it cannot see.

Above them, and around them, is their sea.

The dust is not dirt. It never gets wet, never clumps, never forms frost. It is pale grey-brown mineral flour fine as ash and gritty as ground glass, faintly metallic on the tongue. Because there is nowhere for it to go, it does not disperse. It lifts in the still, thin air toward the ceiling — that close, unreachable underside where the horizon folds — hangs there for years in a light too dim to stir it, and then settles again in exactly the same pattern. Footprints you made minutes ago settle. A pan you smoothed with your boot will re-smooth itself to the same low undulation.

Watch it long enough and it stops being weather. It is a tide with a memory. The same grains ride the same slow column, the same eddies form where the ring's gravity is misfiled, the same drifts accumulate where a dome breathes heat. It is the medium everything else lives in, and it carries the taste of the place: dry, metallic, high. When you breathe — and your breathing is the loudest thing in this landscape, harsh and thin in the low pressure — you are taking in the herd. It gets into the gaps in your clothing and stays there, because that is what it does. It settles on whatever is warm.

The built things are warm.

There are three domes, no larger than a room, scattered so far apart in a room-sized pocket that you lose them and find them again in the wrong direction. Their ceramic-metal is warm to the touch despite the cold that gets into your seams. It does not rust, crack, or dull. Their seams are seamless. They have no doors.

They are not houses. They are sleepers.

You can tell by the heat. It does not radiate like a stove; it persists, the way an animal's flank persists in winter. A dome inhales field through its walls — matter that conducts nothing and everything — and exhales it a fraction warmer, a fraction older. Around each one the dust forms a slightly different halo, the ferns lean in a slightly different geometry. One of them has been abraded by a thousand years of dust into a perfect hemisphere, as if erosion took up a tool and decided to stop, and that one is warmest at the rim, where the work was done. The other two keep their full low curve, unbroken, patient as eggs that will never hatch because hatching is not the point.

They do not communicate in any way you can hear. The sound you do get is eaten mid-air by the thinness and the fold. Kick a stone and the clatter dies before it lands. What you get instead, at intervals so long you might think you imagined them, is first the tick — dry, like a clock in another room, when thermal contraction works through the spire — and then, rarer, a deeper ping that you feel in your teeth more than in your ears. That is likely the domes, turning over in their slow math. One full cycle of whatever they are doing takes about seven hundred years. In human terms nothing is happening. In the pocket's terms, that is a breath.

Half-buried at a shallow angle is the fourth, and the loneliest.

The needle spire is black as polished obsidian, but it bends light around its shaft so you can never quite see its tip. It does not point up. It points past the ceiling, in a direction that should not exist inside a mountain, and where it enters the ground the dust makes a perfect, untouched circle, as if cleared and then consecrated by inattention. It is not a tower. It is an ear, or a tongue, or a root pressing into something outside the pocket and listening back through the rock.

It is the source of the tick. Cold contracts it; the contraction travels; the tick is the sound of a thought arriving late. It does not move toward anything. Everything that lives near it — dust, fern, stone — orients to it and then orients away. Its shadow does not behave. Black shards near it cast shadows that fall toward the light, slightly darker approximations of the already dim illumination; near the spire the approximation fails entirely. If the domes are sleepers, the spire is the manifest one, the one that stays awake by being pointed elsewhere.

Across a shallow pan lies the ring.

Three metres of white metal, warm, and when you brush it your fingertips feel a faint lift, as if gravity has been locally misfiled and not yet corrected. The stone around it has grown up and over its lower curve as though the rock wanted to swallow it, and then thought better of the idea and stopped for good. Half in, half taken. The dust near it hovers a fraction longer before settling; the ferns here grow thinner, more translucent, as if they are being stretched by the lift.

The ring is the only built thing that seems to have a mouth. Not an opening — there is none you can find — but a negotiation. Stone approaches, metal repels, equilibrium is reached mid-bite and held for centuries. It is warmer than the ground and older than the ground and younger than the ground depending on where it is in its faint cycle, because time here runs at different rates on different materials and the ring is the fastest of them, hurrying through its seven hundred years while the plateau barely ages around it. To stand near it is to feel the pocket's rules fray: uphill remains uphill if you keep walking, left and right exchange at the survey lines, elevation is not height but distance from the centre of original engineering.

Those lines are the fifth resident, and the one you cannot touch.

If you look at the dust at the right angle to the silvery light, you see them everywhere: concentric scoring, grids of microscopic perforations in stone, lines that map distances that do not correspond to the distances you can see. Someone measured this place with instruments whose mathematics we do not possess, and the measurements are still measuring. They are not trails — no animal made them — but they behave like trails. Cross them and direction renegotiates. Footprints appear ahead of you. The domes that were near are suddenly beyond the folded horizon. The spire you just left is ahead of you again, unchanged.

Whatever lives in the marks lives in geometry, not space. It is a nervous system laid over the plateau, a set of instructions that the ground still obeys. Anything that would try to live here by walking would have to follow them or be folded. The ferns obey them without knowing. The dust obeys them by settling in pattern. You, conspicuously, do not. That is why the wrongness accumulates the longer you walk.

And there is the light itself, and what it does when it cannot leave.

The dim blue-grey twilight loops. A distant source — a sun through too much thin air, or through a narrow aperture somewhere high — casts only directionless illumination, and the stars prick through even in the brightest part of the cycle. The light does not fall; it circulates, reflecting off white stone, being trapped in amber, being denied by black. Shadows are not black here; they are depressions in the light, and they migrate, almost imperceptibly, toward their source. If you watch a shadow long enough it seems to graze. It is the shyest thing in the pocket, and the only one that appears to move of its own accord, and it moves the way the pocket wants it to.

Nothing new arrives. Nothing leaves. That is not emptiness. That is a closed ecology whose members have replaced appetite with maintenance.

The domes breathe field and shed the heat the ferns use. The ferns grow toward that heat and seal the stone against abrasion, slowing the dust that would otherwise scour the domes and the ring. The dust carries mineral from the stones to the fronds and back to the pans, lifting to the ceiling in the same tide, settling in the same pattern the survey lines dictate. The ring lifts the dust a fraction too long, feeding the thinner fronds that grow in its pan, while the stone that tried to swallow it holds the whole negotiation in balance. The spire ticks its contraction through all of them, a slow metronome felt in teeth and stone, timing the contraction and expansion that drives the dust column. The marks decide where left is left. The light loops through it all, never becoming day, never quite night, thin and silvery and patient.

And around all of it, holding it, is the largest inhabitant, which is the pocket itself.

It is hidden because it is contained — no wider in absolute space than a large room, yet within it the ground still rises to a small, true peak, high in its own frame, thin-aired under a sky that is the inside of the same rock. It treats its centre of original engineering as a centre of gravity, its folds as horizons, its instruments as organs. It is sealing itself tighter around its contents, the way a wound might heal if the wound were made of stone. Erosion continues only at the microscopic scale. Crystal growth balances crystal dissolution. Everything has been worn to balance: edges rounded, slopes at the angle where dust stops sliding, hollows where finer material collects and stays.

That sealing is its life. It does not eat. It does not reproduce as dust or fern do, by accretion and pattern. It simply, very slowly, becomes more itself — more closed, more finished, more at peace in its breakdown. The builders are gone, but they did not leave a ruin. They left a laboratory in which a few laws could be suspended at a time, and the laboratory learned to keep the suspension going without them, the way ants keep a trail after the queen is dead, the way a heart keeps beating in a body that no longer wakes.

If you stand for a long time on the highest point the pocket gives you — a rise no taller than a person, but high in the only way that matters here — you can see the whole of it at once, if you know how not to look directly. The plateau glittering with fronds spreading like time-lapse ferns. The three warm domes, one worn to a hemisphere. The needle pointing past a ceiling that is also a floor. The ring half-swallowed by stone that changed its mind. The dust that has never known water. The shadows falling the wrong way. The survey lines catching the light.

They are not looking at you. They have been looking at each other, in their different times, for thousands of years, and your presence registers only as a temporary centre of gravity, a warm shape that breathes too loudly and makes footprints that appear ahead of it. The pocket will let you walk its fold until you are tired, then it will settle dust over your tracks and go on sealing. The tick will come. The ping will come, in your teeth. The light will loop.

Whatever lives here lives by remaining.
