The first lives are not animals. They are the film.

Before anything with legs, there is a dampness with a texture — olive-green where it stays wet on the concrete, iron-red where it has dried, velvet-black where it has taken the rubber gasket for a hill. This is biofilm, bacteria and fungi and yeast that fell from the hall above and found the one thing the pit offers reliably: warmth, and a thin, retreating line of wet. They do not travel. They bloom where the wet front has lingered, they are eaten back to a clean ring, they bloom again. When the filler machine cycles hard and the pit fogs, the film swells and softens. When the shift ends and the air drinks it, the film shrinks and hardens into a crust that will crackle under a foot too large to be here.

Everything else eats this, or eats what eats this, or eats the dead of both.

Look closer and the film begins to move. They are mostly less than a grain of rice long, translucent as water, six-legged, eyeless or nearly so, and they come in herds so dense their trails look like crawling lace. Springtail-like grazers, if you want a human word for them, though they do not spring so much as shudder and reorient in a space too low to jump. Their bellies are their autobiographies: you can see the green of the last meal through their skin, and the pale knot of unborn young beside it, and sometimes a darker spot of disease or a swallowed grit that will never pass. They move the way tides move, not toward food in any simple line but along the edge of damp, following the creeping film up the side of a rust ridge, around the rim of a washer, into the head of a screw. An hour after a bead falls, they are a wall. Behind them the biofilm is scraped clean; ahead of them it is still green. When the line retreats into a crack to dry, the wall dissolves.

They pause constantly to test. Antennae, palps, the hairs on their legs — all are wetness-detectors, vibration-detectors, chemical readers laid down by the animal ahead of them in the film. The lace trails are not just paths; they are messages. A grazer can read whether the animal before it was hungry, or frightened, or laying eggs. In a world 40 centimeters long, memory has to be externalized.

On the salt field, another kind patrols. These are mites, armoured, slow, deliberate. The carbonate efflorescence has made their country uninhabitable to soft feet — white needles, tiny swords, stillBattling upward overnight in dry weather, sharpening the terrain every week. The hard-soles step between them, testing each placement, distributing their weight on thickened pads. They are not grazing on the film so much as mining it. Stationed under the warm pipe where iron bleeds fresh, they scrape rust. To them the pipe is not a pipe; it is a cliff face weeping a mineral garden, and they grow plated shells from what they eat, layering iron into their own backs until they clink faintly against the salt when they turn.

They lay their eggs wherever damp has last been, in mortar pores, under the faint instruction tag, inside the hollow of a washer. When the bead lake dries, the eggs do not die. Neither do the adults, not all of them. They fold. Legs tuck, bodies curl, and they seal themselves into cysts no bigger than sand, packed shoulder to shoulder in cracks, waiting in the mortar for the next beading. A life here is measured less in days than in wet cycles: open, feed, migrate, lay, fold, wait. Some, when wetness returns, do stranger accounting with their bodies. They molt and eat the molt. They split a leg in a fall across the salt and, in the damp, grow a new mite from it — a small, whole animal budding from a discarded part, as if the population had decided that waste was too expensive to afford.

Above the floor, and inverted, is another population that may never touch the ground at all.

Under the pipe — their sky, their sun, their warm ceiling — a meadow clings belly-up. Tiny animals hang in their own threads, legs curled in a loose basket, waiting for rain that falls upward: condensation beading on the coldest metal and running in quick rivulets when the machine gusts. They catch the beads in their legs, drink upside-down, sway when the fan above bangs the doors in the hall and the whole pit shudders with a muffled thrum in the concrete. Their architecture is not built; it accumulates. Shed skins, dead kin, frass, spores from the mold below flung up in a gust — all woven into papery nests on the underside of the pipe and the underside of the corrugated lean-to. When the air moves, it hums. If one falls, it is not death; it is, for them, a birth onto the floor. Many simply climb back, thread by thread, rejoining the meadow. Some do not. They become floor animals instead, and learn the salt field.

Scavengers work both levels, though they are hard to separate from the grazers. Everything shed here is eaten. The zip-tie arch, the glove finger under the plate, the bottle cap crater — these are not just wreckage to them but landmarks, shelters, hunting blinds. A damp hollow under a ridge will hold, for a day, a continent of a lake: nematodes wheeling in the bead that fills the bottle cap, protozoa blooming and crashing, grazers converging from all directions not for company but because every animal in the pit is following the same moving line of damp. For an hour the floor is a single moving wall of life. Then the lake is drunk by heat and air — first at the edges, then inward — and the wall breaks into individuals hurrying for cracks.

And because there is prey, and movement, and heat, there are hunters.

Pale, blind, pseudoscorpion-shaped, with pincers too large for their bodies, they do not chase by sight. They chase by vibration, and by the heat of a crowd. The heartbeat of the filler machine comes through the northern wall as a steady pulse in the concrete, and they read that, and the stir of a thousand tiny feet in the film, and the click of their own pincers once in a while, like a fingernail on glass, when they strike at nothing or at something that was there a fraction of a second ago. In a room this size a chase is a loop. Forty centimeters of circuit and the predator is behind you again. So the grazers are never merely grazing; they are in perpetual slow migration, a tide forever being reorganized. The predator does not clear a territory. It follows a migration and, in following, creates it.

There is also the plant, if you can call it that, and it lives here in the animal way.

In the corner where a new crack leads up toward the thin line of light between panels, a single pale shoot has forced itself through, blind and stringy, root hairs hanging into air instead of soil, drinking only the fog. It never flowers. When conditions are right it makes more of itself along a line — tiny pale clones, each one etiolated, each one reaching for a light it will never have enough of and a soil it will never have. It is the only green that is not film, and it does not feed the crowd. It is fed by the same damp, and it offers them, at most, a shade that is no shade and a thread to climb.

None of them live alone, and none of them live for long in any one form. Bacteria bloom and are eaten and bloom again. Mold sends up a black velvet on the gasket and then, in a fan gust that is a storm here, releases a cloud of spores that fall like snow onto threads and salt and grazers. A screw that rolled here in spring is still a screw, but its threads now hold a bead, then a colony, then a clutch of eggs. A bead lake opens today in the bottle cap, tomorrow under the washer, and the entire packed population — floor herds, salt miners, ceiling meadow, hunters, cysts in the mortar — remakes its map accordingly.

When the machine idles, the pit cools by two degrees. The film shrinks. The herds fold themselves into cracks. The ceiling hangs motionless. The predator sits, pincers open, listening to a quiet that is not empty, only waiting. When warmth returns and the cold pipe starts beading again, they unfold. It is not a season. It is a pulse. Cloud is heat rising. Rain is condensation. Wind is the hall doors opening. Night is the idling and the drying. Morning is the return of wet.

If you crouch behind the conduits long enough, you stop seeing an infestation in a service pit. You see a young, low country that formed inside a machine — ground still being made by salt and crack and rust, water a visitor, green scarce and life not scarce at all, nothing empty, and nothing yet old enough to be gentle.
