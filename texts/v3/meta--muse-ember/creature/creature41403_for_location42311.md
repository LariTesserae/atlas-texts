Almost nothing lives on the plain itself.

The open ground between the islands is too new and too sharp to be inhabited in any way you can look at directly. What lives there first is under it, and on it, in a layer you would miss if you walked fast. On the polygon plates, in the orange and acid-green crust, a thin lichen and moss skin holds against the tilted clay. It does not grow so much as endure, waiting for the hours when the fog thickens and beads on every ridge.

A few centimeters down, where your palm finds it cool and damp, is the real country. Pores, capillary threads, the damp seam between dry crust and warm earth. Microbial mats spread there, iron-smelling and slow, drinking condensation as it travels down plant stems and building walls and vanishes into the soil. They are the source of the smell of wet stone and warm earth. They have no flowers and no sound of their own, except that everything else depends on them to make the damp usable. The plain feeds them not by rain but by habit — saturate, condense, drip, evaporate, saturate again, on a cycle of hours.

In the fog itself, above the clay, hangs another population. Minute, translucent insects, smaller than the drops they live in. They do not nest on the ground and they do not land on leaves. They live suspended in the saturated air at dawn and dusk, feeding on moisture and on the mineral dust the buildings leach out, their wings a blur that makes a blanket of static. At dusk their chorus swells to white noise so thick you have to shout to be heard a meter away, and then the air steals the shout. They are food for everything larger, and weather for everything smaller, and for most of the year they are simply present — a living humidity.

Where the ground funnels what little water falls, in the hip-sized hollows sunk in the clay, are the pools. Green-black, warm, stagnant. In them, in their thousands, are animals that are barely animals: shrimp-like, blind, translucent to the point of being almost only the water they are made of. They come to drink and breed and die within a day. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see in that dim light that their bodies need to find. They rise and fall with the dripping from the roofs above, which feed the pools trough by trough. If you watch long enough you see the surface shiver not from wind but from them, turning over in the water they carry inside themselves.

The islands are where all of this becomes visible as weight.

A thicket at a node is a wall of green so dense it bends the sound around it. Ferns the size of doors unroll in pulses during the condensation hours and pause in the dry, their fronds beaded and heavy. Vines spill from the hollow spires — there are no windows in the ordinary sense, only openings the buildings decide to make — and run across the ground to link structure to structure, rooting in damp pockets of soil the buildings themselves precipitate and hold. Moss and lichen bloom in acid greens and oranges on every damp face. Flowers open without any pollinator you can find nearby, or open and close as if drinking, thickening toward a spire that is singing a particular chord, growing toward sound as much as toward the dull coin of sun.

That is the thing here: plants grow toward resonance. Where the wind moves through a hollow tower and makes it sigh in fifths, a vine will find the wall and climb it more thickly than the lit side. The gardens inside the spires do the same, growing up the inner walls toward the hazy light at the top, occupying a cathedral void and then, when the node contracts, folding inside the lattice and being carried with it.

In the thickets things dart. Small nesting things that live in the hollow of the buildings and sing from inside them, making the spire resonate more than wind alone would. Scavengers that take the dung and urine of larger fauna and turn the gardens richer. Pollinators that may not be needed by the flowers, which have learned to open on their own schedule, but which carry spores and seed from node to node along the glowing paths. They live in the interstice between the built and the grown, because here those are not separate layers.

Below them, burrowers churn the young ground. They open the raw vertical-walled hollows and close them again, letting air into the damp layer and bringing the capillary soil up to the crust where condensation can reach it. They are the plain's slow editors. They dig up the buried cables between nodes without meaning to, surfacing only in the saturated hours to breathe warm air. Their work is why the ground never quite settles, why a new sharp crack can sigh open with damp air from below on a dry afternoon.

And then, at continental scale, the travelers.

The herds move across the emptiness in lines so long you see only the nearest edge. Barrel-bodied, long-legged grazers with necks like periscopes, their hides thick with lichen so that from a distance they look like moving clay. They step from polygon to polygon, testing each plate for damp with a foot, testing the fog with a slow rotation of the neck. They are not fast and do not need to be. Their steps are audible across the flat, a low percussion that raises the bottom register of the plain's noise for as long as they pass. Their contact calls are not calls so much as foghorns made of resonating throat sacs, traveling ten kilometers without losing power, overlapping and interfering until the horizon hums.

Far behind them, or far above the line, the predators pace at the same unhurried tempo. They do not chase. They match. The whole system moves like a tide sideways, prey and predator waves taking weeks to cross a hundred kilometers of valley floor. A predator is most dangerous not in the open but at a node, where a herd may alter course not for food but for water dripping from a building and for the resonance of its spires. There it can wait in the thicket, in the garden air the buildings exhale, and let the spacing do the hunting for it.

Above both, the sky is intermittently inhabited by flocks that are less flocks than weather — a darkening of the bruised haze in one place, then, kilometers later, in another, part of the same migration. They travel node to node, feeding on the fog insects, never landing on the bare clay. To walk under one is to feel a brief, cool shadow that the dim sun should not cast, and to hear the air change pressure.

The buildings themselves are inhabited most intimately of all.

Their walls are not stone or metal or glass. They are a lattice, translucent amber in places and pearl-white in others, warm and seamless, maintained from within by swarms at the nanoscale that still live in the material and decide, moment by moment, whether a wall will be solid or phase-thin to your hand. Rooms open where they are needed and close when they are not. The structures breathe very slowly through pores at their bases, exhaling the warm damp air that feeds the vines. Their outer surfaces are a forest of microscopic capillaries harvesting fog and dew, storing it, mineralizing it, releasing it to soil and leaf on a schedule no one fully controls. In the driest hour they are still dripping. On their roofs condensation runs in threads to the pools below.

To live inside one is to live in extravagant hollowness. A void the size of a cathedral, a garden climbing the inner wall, and then nothing — air, and room for sound to resonate. The density of life is low even inside its own architecture. What lives in the spire, besides the swarm and the garden and the nesting fauna, is rarely crowded and rarely seen.

If there are people here, they are not a population in any ordinary sense. A node may hold one keeper, or a family spaced across its twenty to eighty structures, and then five kilometers of nothing, and then ten. They do not build; the buildings grow themselves. They tend — pruning vines from condensation trays, opening a trough, listening to which spire has gone thin and which has closed its garden inside itself. They are adapted to the infrasonic wash that lives in the teeth and sternum, to thoughts that feel slowed by it, to a life where another of their kind is a landmark you approach by sound and by the faint glow of the path underfoot, not by sight. They eat vine fruit and fern spores and the mineral-rich film the buildings exhale onto leaves. They cross the emptiness rarely, and when they do, the herds and predators and winged weather pass them as weather passes.

Everything is spaced the way stars are spaced. Abundant, but rarely close. Connected not by touch or by contiguous ground, but by the sound that has nowhere to be absorbed on a flat, hollow, damp plain: dripping on clay amplified into a hollow *tonk* from everywhere at once, wind turning spires into chords, insects raising static until dusk, throat-sac foghorns from ten kilometers away, steps you can hear before you see the animal making them, and under it all the low hum of the lattice, constant enough that your own body seems to join it.

That is who lives there. Not in cities as crowds, not in fields as herds standing still, but in islands and in air and in pores and in walls, each of them so far from the next that the sound is the only thing that ever truly connects them, and the emptiness between them is not absence at all — it is the architecture that makes the living possible.
