You may look at one for some time before you know it is alive.

Along the terraces, among the bone-white struts and the clusters of chimney tubes, there are pale, bent shapes that seem to belong to the architecture. They hold the same stooped angle as the supports. Their wraps are the color of wet clay, their skin is the color of stone that has been handled for a very long time. If you watch the stillness long enough, a hand shifts against a wall. A shoulder turns sideways. A head, kept low beneath a leaking aqueduct, lifts a fraction, then settles again. They are not many — perhaps a few dozen in the whole pocket, perhaps fewer; the terraces hide them, and the echoes return the same voice from two places at once. They are solitary within the crowd, each bent over a narrow piece of work, each making almost no unnecessary movement.

They are short beside you, though it is hard to say how short, because they never straighten. Hunched to fit their doorways, they would perhaps reach your shoulder if they stood upright, but they do not stand upright. Their backs curve permanently forward, their heads bowed, their necks thick. On the steep stairs they move sideways, one shoulder leading, the other trailing, a hand kept lightly on the wall for guidance. Their legs are short and strong, their feet broad and bare. The soles are thick and pale, the toes splayed for the slick stone. You see them press a foot flat and hold it there, feeling the cool of the water table through the skin, judging a channel by the temperature underfoot before they look at it.

Their skin is damp with a mineral paleness, matte where the air is cold, faintly ruddy where a kiln has worked on it for years. Salt crusts gather in the creases of the joints. Hair is sparse — dark, wet strands plastered to the skull, a thin down on the forearms, in some of them a short beard that holds the condensation. The face is formed for looking down: brow heavy, nose broad, mouth small, teeth worn low by grit and by the food they eat. Their eyes are pale grey, almost the blue-white of the veined walls, and they rarely meet yours. The lids stay half-closed against the drips, and the gaze seems unfocused, as if the face were listening rather than seeing. They navigate, in large part, by palm and sole. A hand slides over a worn carving and reads it better than an eye could. Fingers are long, broad at the tips, nails thick and split from copper work and bone tools. When they pass one another on a stair — and passing is a negotiation of turned shoulders — the greeting is not a lifted face. It is a palm pressed briefly to another’s forearm, a shoulder brushed, and then they are sideways again and gone.

There is no cloth as you would know it, because there is nothing green to make it from. They wrap themselves in mats of dried fungal felt, stiffened until they shed water, held with copper wire and bone pins. Kiln-keepers wear gloves of old hide; gate-keepers have forearms corded from rope-work; those who scrape the troughs have one shoulder carried forward and nails split from the flat bone tools. Their bead-lines — bone, copper, black glass — mark their stairs and sometimes their waists or wrists, and when they climb, the beads click against the stone in a pattern the others know. A person in this pocket may be recognized by that click long before they are seen.

Their voices are low and brief, shaped by the roar that never stops. They do not shout across the chamber; the chamber does that for them, and badly, sending the phrase back a second later from a terrace above or below. A builder will say a word — *gate*, *felt*, *lamp* — and wait for its return, as if the echo were part of the sentence. Perhaps it is. Sound here does not leave, so a voice arrives both close and distant, and each of them keeps a small acoustic territory: one knows the drip in his cistern, one the creak of a sluice rope, one the ring of copper on a particular kiln stone. They seem less to converse than to confirm that the water is still at the same level, in the same channel, at the same pace.

Their work is the town’s metabolism, and it never finishes because it is all manual. Lamp-tenders crouch at the shallow clay cups, trimming the copper wick with thumb and forefinger, faces emerging in the warm pool and receding into the blue gloom. Gate-keepers sit for long stretches beside the sluices, hands on the hide ropes, listening for a change in the water’s pitch. Channel-menders re-lay a wall with the same re-worn stone, re-line a drain with the same clay, and the new work takes on the old patina at once. Nothing is built outward. A house is repaired where it always was. A stair is worn smoother where it always was. The builders are not so much the authors of the place as its maintenance — and the place, in return, maintains them.

The only crop is the pale fungal felt in the shadowed troughs, and it is tended the way everything here is tended: by hand, by scraping. They crouch over the troughs and work the felt loose with flat bone tools, eat some of it fresh — sour, iron-tasting, faintly warm from the air — and dry the rest into stiff cakes on cold shelves or at the edge of a kiln. The felt grows back in the same patch, in the same shape, as if the trough had a template. It is food, and clothing, and lamp-fuel in part; rendered fat from felt and from the rare fish goes into the clay cups. It is the one greenless garden, and they farm it without ceremony, the way they do everything, as a rhythm rather than an event.

The other animals are presences more than a population. The blind fish turns up in the distribution channels after a gate is lifted — translucent, finger-long, its organs pulsing faintly under the skin, no eyes at all. A gate-keeper will scoop it in a hand-net, not swiftly but with the certainty of someone who has done it before, and eat it there at the channel’s edge, or render it. It is not farmed. It is a gift of the water, and rare enough to be noticed.

The worms live in the damp mortar, which the builders leave unsealed in certain patches on purpose. They are pale and thumb-thick, slow as thought, moving by peristalsis through the clay and eating what felt falls to them. You know one has passed only by the displaced grit, or by a faint tremor under a palm laid flat on the wall. The builders work around them without comment, re-laying stone above and below, as if the wall were shared tenancy.

High on the back wall, in the blue-white veining, there is sometimes a cave cricket. It is grey and translucent, eyeless, silent, perfectly still, larger than you expect. Its antennae find the drip; its body does not jump when a lamp is brought near. No one disturbs it. It occupies its acoustic niche the way a builder occupies a stair, and the only sign that it lives is the slow re-angling of those antennae toward the water.

The young, when you see them at all, are smaller versions of the same bent shape, heads and hands too large for their bodies, learning to move sideways before they learn to walk straight. They stay low, near the sluices and troughs, watching hands. The old become listening fixtures: seated at a gate, salt crusted at the joints, a drip beading on the shoulder, breathing so slowly that you take them for part of the wall until an eyelid moves. You are never shown a death. A bead-line remains on a stair; a tool rack stays in order; after a while, a smaller figure is using the same stair, in the same sideways way, and the town has not changed its shape.

That, in the end, is what they are: a few slow, hunched lives fitted so exactly into the pocket’s balance that they seem to be remembered by it. When you turn to squeeze back through the crack, they do not follow you. The backs bend again over the troughs. A bead clicks on a stair. A voice says a word and waits for it to come back.
