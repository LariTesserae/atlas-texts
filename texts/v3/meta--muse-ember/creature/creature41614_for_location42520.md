At ground level, everything is alive and nothing is looking at you.

That is the first thing to adjust to. A resident is expected to startle, to call, to turn a head, to move aside. These do not. They are crowded past the point of courtesy, layered past the point of privacy, and they stay where they were born. Your eye keeps looking for the animal that lives *in* the green. There isn't one. The green *is* the one that lives here.

Start with what you are standing on, because that is where most of the life is, and most of it is not visible as a plant at all.

The floor, where it is flat enough to hold anything, is a mattress of moss. Not a crust, not a dusting. On rock, on fallen wood, on the sides of mounds, it has built up thick and spongy and black-green, holding water long after the rain has gone sideways out of the bowl. It lives slowly compared to everything else here, spreading by touch, by fragment, by the torn piece that lands a hand's breadth away and takes hold. In the shafts of light that find it, it steams faintly. Otherwise it does what it has always done in the dark and damp: it drinks. Mist combs through it and leaves its moisture behind. A drop runs off a leaf above and is caught and kept. A brief freeze rimes its tips at dawn and releases them by mid-morning. It is the bowl's reservoir and its first collector, a million small hands held up to a sky that only sometimes rains.

Threaded through it and under it, less visible still, are the ones that run the place from below. Fungi send out white fans through the litter, through the black spongy soil, through the softening bark of anything that has fallen. You do not see their work so much as its result: leaf after leaf, unpierced, unchewed, lying whole for exactly as long as it takes a fungus to find it, then thinning, darkening, sinking into humus at a pace that would shame a lowland forest. They are the conveyor. They take what the wind and the fall deliver and convert it, quietly, in the dark, into the thin black earth that everything else stands in.

And under and within even that, invisible entirely, are the bacteria in the peat, on the leaf surfaces, in the pores of the rock, in the dark wet stains where water seeps out and disappears. Their lives are measured in hours and generations you will never see, but their cumulative work is what makes the soil more than crushed leaves — what loosens iron in the stone and paints fresh rockfall orange, what holds the spongy ground together while the wind tries to undermine it. If the moss is the bowl's skin and the fungi are its hands, the bacteria are its metabolism. No one eats them. No one needs to. They eat the place itself, in the chemical sense, and leave it richer for the plants that eat light and mist.

Out of that dark, working floor, the ferns deploy.

A tree fern does not so much grow as it launches. The fiddlehead sits spiral-tight at the top of its trunk, holding its whole future curled in on itself, and then in a matter of days it unrolls — hydraulic, unstoppable, frond after frond the size of a door, opening into the wind that will flatten it and let it spring back. Ground ferns do the same at ankle height, again and again, a fountain of green that never gets browsed, never gets pierced, never gets pruned. When a gust hits, they whip. When a lull comes, they hold the shape they have won. When a shower of pebbles or a sudden slide of scree buries one, it does not struggle back out. It dies under the stone, whole and green for a while, and within weeks the stone itself is green, colonized from above by the next fern, the next moss, the next root that can find a crack no thicker than a finger.

That is the tempo of the underlayer: fast, unprotected, and entirely unlamented. No grazer has ever taken a bite out of it, so it has grown as if abundance were a duty. Every surface is occupied because every surface has been left alone long enough to be.

Above the ferns, the climbers and the hangers have solved the problem of light by refusing to stay on the ground.

Vines haul themselves up the cliff faces hand over hand, rootlet by rootlet, gripping fault planes that are still clean and sharp, wedging into cracks, jacking them fractionally wider with the slow press of a growing root. What looks from below like a vertical garden is in fact thousands of individual ascents, each one a thin green rope pulling itself toward the rim where the light, such as it is, is less completely stolen. They swing in the pulses of wind like cranes. They are flattened and released. They never touch anything they are not climbing.

The epiphytes do the opposite. Wedged into the forks of trees, into the crooks of branches, into any pocket where bark and moss have made a shallow bowl, they live their whole lives in the air. Their roots hang down and drink the mist directly, never reaching soil, never needing it. They are, in a way, the purest residents of this place: animals cannot live on mist, and these plants can. Each rain that tears sideways through the bowl for a minute is a meal caught by a thousand small collectors that do not have to go anywhere to get it. Each hour of frozen shade that beads on a leaf and then melts is stored, briefly, in a root waiting in the air.

In the middle air, where the wind has room to make noise, the bamboo-like stems live in stands and make the sound of the place. They clatter together, deep and hollow when a gust forces them, silent in the thirty-second calms. They bend as one, spring as one, knock and knock again. Their leaves are long and narrow and built for exactly this violence — to lie flat under the jet through the notch and then spring up when it passes. They do not flower for anyone. They do not need to. Their seeds, like everything else's here, are made to travel on wind, to cross a ridge in a gust and land, by chance, on pale fresh rock or in black soil or in the crook of a tree, and start again.

And over it all, holding it all up and being held up in return, are the trees.

Broadleaf trees with smooth, pale trunks sheathed in moss rise from that congested floor and interlock overhead until the canopy is not a collection of crowns but a roof, a continuous, heaving plane that bends in the wind as a single organism. Beneath them, a second tier of smaller trees and tall shrubs leans and reaches toward whatever leaks through — a tilted shaft, a break in the cloud, the pale light on the rim — and holds its leaves in the saturated emerald that the stray light catches. Their trunks are pale because the light is weak and the moss is constant; their bark does not need to be thick against sun or teeth. Nothing has ever bitten them. Nothing has ever needed to bake them.

Their lives are not long by tree standards, because the ground will not hold them. The soil is thin, spongy, constantly undermined, and the wind arrives in pulses that test every root. Trees are levered out, whole, and fall — often. When one falls, it does not disappear. It becomes the most crowded address in the bowl. Within a season moss colonizes its trunk. Vines climb over its branches. Ferns rise through them. Fungi thread its bark and soften it from within. Decades later its shape is still there under the green, a log-shaped hill, a nursery, a route for roots, a reservoir for mist. The tree's death is not an ending here; it is a change of tenancy. Everything that fed on it in a lowland forest would have arrived by now. Here, only plants and fungi arrive, and they arrive to live on it, not to eat it away quickly.

That is the whole economy. Light comes in thin and is used completely, leaf on leaf, layer on tier. Water comes in as mist and sideways rain and is caught by everything — moss, hanging roots, broad lamina that answer each fat drop with an audible *tok*. Rock breaks, daily, in ticks and avalanches, and what breaks is pale and clean for a very short time before roots find it and orange water streaks it and green covers it. Seeds and spores come over the rim on the same wind that roars through the notch, land on scree, on cliff, on a fallen trunk, and begin. No pollinator is needed beyond that wind. No predator prunes the excess. So the excess remains, and thickens, and layers, until nothing touches nothing.

In the lulls you can hear a tree creak and settle. You can hear condensation fall from the cliff in steady metallic drips. You can hear your own breath, thin in the thin air. And around you, all of it — moss drinking, fungi threading, bacteria turning, ferns unrolling, vines hauling, epiphytes hanging in the air drinking mist, bamboo knocking, trees holding up a roof of green against a lid of cloud — all of it continues to produce, at a pace that has nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with freedom.

They will never be eaten. They will never be still. They were not built to be finished, only to be deployed, again and again, in a small dark room at the top of the world that the wind entered a doorway ago and has never left.
