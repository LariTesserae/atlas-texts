There is no one plant here, and there is no empty place for one to be in.

What lives in the casing lives all at once, packed until the pocket is saturated. If you try to take a single stem in your hand and name it, the stem leads you into another stem, and that into a cable, and that into an iron rib, and that back into a leaf that is pressed flat and dry against the iron. Root is holdfast, holdfast is leaf, leaf is shingle, shingle is wall. It is a mat before it is a garden, and a garden before it is a forest, and all of it room-sized and still.

Overhead, where there is no sky, is the canopy. Dust-ferns have grown across the whole ceiling of the casing, broad lath-leaves overlapping like shingles, dark green gone almost black in the dim grey-green light that slips through the seam. They do not reach for that light — there is no up here to reach toward, only a diffuse twilight that does not brighten or fade. They have spread sideways instead, until every square of iron above is roofed. Under them the air hangs in still strata. You could watch for an hour and the strata would not drift.

Below the canopy the walls climb. Soot-fern and copper-moss have taken the curved iron in a single felt. The fern is blackened as if by the soot it feeds on, its fronds thin and papery, layered frond upon frond. The moss is coppery where it has taken up the salts from the wiring, a dull verdigris-green that holds the faint amber glow from the dead instrument panel without ever responding to it. It grows not inward from soil but outward from surface — from grease film, from dust-film, from the elbows of pipes that have filled with collected dust, from a piston locked mid-stroke. Where there is a new surface, there is already a plant on it, waiting to be noticed.

On the floor the grease-vine owns everything. Its stems are thick as cables and in places braided with actual cables, green with age, woven through bolts and gauges and the dead control box until vine and wire cannot be told apart. It has lifted nothing, displaced nothing. It has grown around and through. A fern has unfurled straight through a fan blade as if the blade were a slit of light. A vine has laced itself around a single gear tooth and stopped there, perfectly fitted. Leaves lie pressed flat against a turbine fin, overlapping the metal grain, as if listening to it.

They are listening, in the only way they can. Nothing here grows toward light, because the light is everywhere and nowhere and always the same. They grow toward vibration. Stems curl, almost imperceptibly — and then, having arrived, never move again — toward the loudest joint. Toward the fan deep in the machine that makes the low rumble. Toward the belt that makes the high whine. Toward the place where the dull clank repeats in its exact loop. The note that fills the casing passes through them, and they have shaped themselves to take it.

Leaflets in some of them have lined up in narrow slats, louvers mimicking the louvers of the machine. Spikes on others mimic its cooling fins. It is not camouflage. The machine was here first, and it is the only weather here, the only rain of sound. The plants that survived the sealing were the ones that answered its shape.

They live without water. There is no drip, no damp on the iron, no ingress at the seam but cold, dry air. Everything to the touch is dry, papery, dusty, though the whole pocket is shamelessly lush. These are sealed plants. They fatten their stems with their own internal stores and never spend them. What they spend, they take from the mountain and the machine in endless, unchanging exchange: mineral dust for salts, soot and grease for carbon, the carbon dioxide the casing exhales in its unchanging cycle, and that dim constant light for the rest. No pollinator comes. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. If pollen is made, it is shaken free by the perpetual vibration of the casing itself and falls — or rather, hangs — exactly where it was made, into the still strata, onto the leaves below.

Growth and fall happen at exactly the same rate, because both have stopped.

Under the living mat there is an underlayer, and that is the oldest generation. The first leaves to fall did not rot. They mineralized in place, silvered, turned to a sort of stone-litter that is still leaf-shaped, still lath-like, still legible as a leaf a thousand years later. They are not soil. They are a record of fullness. Once the garden filled every square there was to fill, density became law. Nothing can grow further because there is no space to grow into. Nothing can decay further because there is no water to decay with, no animal to eat, no air moving to carry anything away.

The machine is the other inhabitant, and they are locked in a single metabolism. It runs at the same speed it ran when the last leaf filled the last open square. It does not rise, it does not fall, it does not vary by a fraction. It hums through the ribs of the plants as leaves hum through it. That is their season, their day and night, their wind. To be alive here is to be tuned to that note and to hold it in tissue.

So they do not move. They do not tremble. A leaf does not lift. A shadow does not shift. And yet they are not dead, not abandoned, not a ruin like the hut with its door gone, the cable line stapled to the rock below. The rust outside stopped blooming. The plants inside stopped reaching. Everything has been weathered to its final form and then, inside this sealed pocket, allowed to remain in that form indefinitely.

Long-existed. Packed. Dry. Loud in the ribs and perfectly still. If you slipped back out through the seam, the sound would drop to a faint hum under your boots, and behind you the dust-ferns would still be shingled across the ceiling, the grease-vine still braided through the gauges, the moss still coppery against the iron, listening toward the fan — exactly as you left them, and exactly as they were long before you came.
