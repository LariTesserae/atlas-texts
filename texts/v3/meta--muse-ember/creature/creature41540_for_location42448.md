First you hear them, because looking will not sort it out for you.

The bowl is loud in a way that makes the loudness itself seem alive. The wind is doing most of it, roaring straight across the pale dish, scraping dust ahead of itself. Under that, the floor ticks and pops as it cools and warms, pebbles dancing in their shallow basins, slabs settling with small irritated sighs. And over that again, moving slowly, is a clattering and rustling, a whistling that tunes itself and untunes itself, as if something large were shifting its weight and changing its mind about where to stand.

It is the groves, making all of it at once.

No one of them is large. Taken together there are only four, five, six in the whole hour-across bowl, and between them there is nothing but cracked mineral, salt in the seams, pumice-light under your boots. That emptiness is not a lack. It is the land they are living in, the space they need. Each grove is a clump standing alone, dark against the pale, dense within itself and utterly solitary without. You can see the next one a quarter mile off. You can see its shade. Nothing connects them except the air that moves between them and the animals that run it.

A grove is a resident more than a plant. Its trunks are not wood but bundles of hollow fibrous stems bound tight together, creaking against each other, tuned by the wind until a whole crown will sing a low fluting chord you feel in your teeth. Its leaves are broad and overlapping, fan-shaped, waxy, dark purple-green above and grey beneath, finely porous and always shivering. Its roots go nowhere deep. They spread sideways as a mat of breathing filaments that mine the cracks for salts and powders, crinkling the crust as they go.

Above them the veil never lifts. Amber-brown, high and permanent, it holds the warmth in and the vapour down. The ground exhales through hairline cracks — a warm, faint breath that is not steam and not smoke, carrying hydrogen and carbon and the smell of hot stone. Where a crack breathes steadily, a grove grows. Where a crack goes cold, the grove thins and dies back. Over weeks the whole clump walks, very slowly, hard toward fresh breath, dying away from the old, leaving withered husks behind it and seedlings ahead of it like footprints. It is sealed in here, this high warm dish, and so the breath rises and is caught and falls again as drip, and never leaves.

The leaf does the drinking that roots cannot. All day the sponges are out, shivering in the wind, condensing that thin vapour into a clear, slightly oily fluid that is not water. At night it thickens and the leaves drip. Animals come to lick it. You learn to press your mouth to a cool leaf in the morning and taste something clean and metallic and sweet. It never pools. It is carried, instead — in sap, in hollow stems, in bodies. Everything that lives here has given up water and learned to live inside this other fluid, and so the bowl can be lush and salt-dry at once.

You understand the pace of things by watching the flowers. They open and close in minutes, releasing yellow clouds that drift and make you sneeze. A shoot pushes up through the crust in a tight furled fist and unrolls by afternoon to the size of a shield. A trunk swells with trapped gas and pops like a distant gun, raining seeds down steaming. Creation here is not seasonal. It is hourly, overlapping, wasteful and immediate.

The air gets up first.

When a grove is about to move its pollen on, thousands of glass-winged things lift from the canopy in a column, no bigger than moths, humming so hard the wind seems to pause to listen. They are almost invisible until the dim light catches them, then the sky blacks out for a moment at the edge of sight. They scatter grove to grove, to grove again, pollinating and stripping and seeding as they go, riding the very wind that is trying to tune the trees. Where they have been, leaves are chewed to lace and new buds are already furled and waiting. They do not stay. They boil up, they vanish, they boil up elsewhere.

Then the plain comes alive with running.

The runners arrive in herds that do not match the sparseness of the stone. Six-legged, long-necked, with sail-like ears that flap constantly to cool them in the still warm air under the veil. Standing still they are the colour of cracked stone, mottled pale and grey, and you could walk past a whole herd without knowing they were animals. When they move, they move as a frenzy. They do not graze. They strip a whole side of a breath-tree in an hour, leave it shivering and bare, calling to each other across the emptiness with weird echoing cries, and run on to the next grove. Within days, from basal shoots at the base, the tree throws out a new crown denser than the one that was eaten. The runners' feet stir the dust, their bodies heat the cracks, their droppings are vapour and salt again. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be stored.

And then, in the shade where the light goes darker still, are the ones that make the place feel wrong for a long time after you have named the others.

Burrowers that look like lumps of fallen leaf, grey-green and damp-looking in a place that has no damp, until they shift and reveal a fringe of small legs and walk. They mine the crust patiently, loosening polygons, opening new seams for breath to find. Their movement is the ticking under the ticking of the stone.

Clingers that hang under the leaves and are buds until they blink. Eye-slits open along the bud-seam. A tongue comes out, thin and cool, to lick the drip before the swarm can. They are still for hours, indistinguishable, then suddenly very animal — snapping at a glass-winged insect that blundered too close, retracting, becoming a bud again for the wind to shiver.

And the slow plant-animal that sits on a trunk for a whole day with its broad back to the dim light, photosynthesizing through its skin, drinking vapour off its shoulders. At dusk — which here is indistinguishable from noon — it stands on root-feet, stiff and deliberate, and walks to another tree, and sits again. It does not hurry. The grove itself does not hurry. The runners are the only ones in a hurry, and they are in a hurry because everything else is so slow and so certain to grow back.

Under all of it, in the dust between groves, seedlings are waiting. Hundreds of them, silent, sealed against the dry. When a tremor finds a new crack and a pebble-field settles and the ground breathes a little stronger in one place, something wakes. A root-filament tastes salt. A leaf unrolls. If no herd comes, if no grove migrates over it, it may become the nucleus of a new clump that will travel, in its own slow way, across the plain for the rest of its life.

There are larger rhythms still, and people-shaped ones at the edges of them.

The huts down below have no cisterns, no jars, no channels. The very idea of storing a drink is foreign here. The dwellers who use them — and they are scattered, never clustered, as if the place will not let anyone stay in numbers — live by the same rule as the animals. They sit in a grove's shade and wait for the leaves to drip. They shelter in circular windbreaks of stacked slabs, knee-high, open to the amber sky, with no roof left because a roof would fight the wind and lose. They wear woven mats of dry fibre, stiff and grey, fur or fibre against the hot stone. They cut with a single obsidian blade, beautifully made, left on a flat stone when its work is done. In the shallow kiln of fused ash and pumice they soften fibre, or warm their hands, or do nothing that lasts, and let the plain take it back.

Their paths are not roads. They are places where feet have worn a slightly smoother line between groves, stepping around the wide cracks. They come now and then, do a little — shelter, cut, drink, sit — and move on. They have learned the sounds: which pop in a trunk is a seed-burst to keep clear of, which singing means the wind is high and the drip will be thin, which ticking means a crack has just opened and the air there will smell sweetest for an hour. Their bodies, like everything else's, carry that thin oily fluid instead of water. Their skin tastes metallic when they sweat in the heat that the stone holds through the day.

No metal. No pottery for liquid. No wheels for a plain that is crossed on foot and by runners and by migrating groves. What they leave is what the bowl can absorb: a wall, a mat, a blade, a worn line in the dust.

Stand in the middle at that perpetual late afternoon, when noon and dusk look the same under the veil, and you can take the whole population in at once, which is how you know it is a population and not a collection. Wind roaring over empty stone. A grove a hundred paces away clattering and singing. A herd calling across the emptiness. A swarm humming overhead, visible only where the light catches a wing. The ground ticking like cooling metal under your boots. A trunk popping somewhere, steaming. A burrower shifting under a leaf that is not a leaf. A clinger blinking, becoming an animal, becoming a bud again. A slow sitter adjusting its back to the light.

It should not work — lush, crowded, noisy life with no water, high and warm and dim, sealed in a small dish of salt. And yet it hums along in balance, stubbornly, wastefully. Everything expends itself quickly and grows back quickly. The green world rushes across the stone like weather. And underneath, patient and settled, the terraces and polished channels and smooth rim hold the pattern together, as they have for a very long time, forgetting to be dramatic about it.
