At first there is nothing to see.

The middle of the room is empty, the floor is pale silt in old ripples, the light comes from everywhere and from nowhere in particular, and for a long time you will think you are the only living thing under the plateau. Then, slowly, the paleness separates.

Against the wall, where the stone sweats in dark patches that spread and recede over hours, there is a skin. Not rock, not shadow. A felt of pale jade and cream, thin and clinging, flat to the scalloped wall as if it were growing in the shape of the damp itself. It does not rise. It does not flower. It covers only where moisture lingers and where the fine cracks in the ceiling above line up to give it the most of that diffused, permanent noon, and even there it stays sparse, in isolated islands, metres apart. It grows so slowly that to watch it is to watch it not grow. That is its life: to wait, damp at the edge, bright at the surface, taking in light and air through a mat of threads too fine to count.

Above, where you would not think to look for a root, there are other plants doing the opposite of growing. From the cracks they hang ribbons a metre or more down into the empty air, translucent and pale green, swaying almost not at all. The roots are invisible above, lost in the white crust. Their tips are damp, they drink from the air and the condensation, they have no need to reach the floor. The world here is not only below. Some of what lives here has let go of the ground entirely and lives suspended, head toward the light, feet toward nothing.

And then the eye catches, on the wall, a darkness that should not be there. Small. Flat. Dark eyes, impossibly large for so small and white a body, looking out from white stone.

They are scrapers.

A thumbnail-sized hexapod, pressed flush to the wall in the damp zones, its body the colour of calcite, its six legs spread wide to hold itself to the curve of the stone. If you count along a whole wall there are hundreds of them, but you will never find two close together. Each has its own square of wall, perhaps two metres across, its own patch of felt-mat to graze, and it respects that distance as if the distance were a physical thing, a boundary you can feel in the air.

It moves a body-length at a time. It scrapes. It stops for hours.

In its forelegs, held up against its face like a tool held in hands, is its whole technology: a single chipped flake of dark, flintlike stone, rough on one side, worn smooth and pale on the other from a lifetime of use. It will make one in its life, or be given one by whatever slow birth made it, and it will carry it through every hour it lives. With it it works the mat away from the wall, bite by slow bite, the edge making that faint tap you sometimes hear from far away across the room — once — when the flake strikes stone and the sound is swallowed almost at once by the still air.

It builds nothing. No nest, no den, no burrow, no cache. The flake is not a weapon, not a house, not a mark. It is carried, not left. When the animal dies where it lived, flush and solitary against its own patch of wall, the forelegs loosen and the flake falls. That is why the floor has its solitary stones, ten paces apart, worn perfectly smooth and never in a heap. Some are geology. Many are a generation of tools, dropped.

If you stay long enough, your eyes will learn the wall. The white bodies against white stone resolve into a pattern of presences, each eye a still dark point, each body motionless for such a long time that you begin to doubt it was ever alive until a leg shifts, a body-length, and it settles again. They are abundant only in the way this room is abundant: counted over the whole wall, over the whole slow year, never crowded in any one moment. Their solitude is not loneliness. It is the spacing that allows the mat to regrow, the damp to return, the light to fall unobstructed.

Look up, then, past the ceiling ribbons, and the room fills a second time. It was not empty above you. It was only too still to notice.

Thread by almost invisible thread, they hang from the cracks in the white rock. A single filament, so fine and translucent that you see it only when it catches the light at a certain angle, holds a thread-bodied animal head-down in the air, metres from the next, spaced like stars in a sky that has no night. There are dozens of them. Perhaps more than a hundred. The volume between them is enormous and unoccupied and clean.

They do not climb. They do not fly. They hang and filter. Their long, ribbed bodies sift spores and condensation from the air as the chamber breathes, drawing in warm salt-smelling air through the cracks and exhaling it again over many hours. That is the only time they move on their own accord: when the room breathes, they all sway together, in unison, a slow pendulum on a filament, and then they are still again, head-down, waiting for the next breath. The dust motes that hang motionless in the light are not dust. They are what is being caught.

They never touch. They never need to. A filament from one will never brush another, because the spacing was established long before you arrived and will be maintained long after your footprints in the silt have slumped back into ripples.

And then, on a cycle too slow to predict from a single visit, the room is changed by something that does move across the middle, where nothing else lives.

A crosser comes down the slit in the plateau. It is large — dog-sized — and it enters the light the way the others never do: blind, pale, and jointed, its legs thick and folded like a settling dune made flesh. It does not hurry. It crosses the floor in a straight line from one wall to the other, eating the mats as it goes where the walls meet the floor, and it takes about an hour to make the crossing. Its footfalls are wide and polished in the fine silt, a track that remains for days, slowly slumping, the ripples remembering and then forgetting the weight.

It does not stay. It leaves by the way it came, and for days after, its absence is part of the density of the room. The emptiness feels emptier for knowing how vast it is between footfalls — a whole chamber of slow presences, and then this one great, slow passage that proves the middle was never meant to be permanently empty, only mostly.

That is all of them, and it is enough. The mats grow where they can, unhurried. The scrapers graze them a body-length at a time, each in its own square, each with its one carried stone. The hangers hold the air clean above, swaying only when the stone breathes. The ribbons hang from the ceiling, drinking light without touching ground. Now and then, rarely, the crosser crosses, cropping and polishing, and goes.

Nothing crowds. Nothing hurries. Nothing builds a home that would have to be defended. What lives here has learned to live by leaving almost no trace except a spacing that will not be crossed, a fallen flake on an empty floor, a filament catching light, and a track already softening back into silt.
