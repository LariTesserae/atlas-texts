If you stay in one place long enough, the emptiness resolves into residents.

Not all at once. First there is only what you cannot see, and that is most of them.

Plankton drift through the grid streets like snow that does not fall, thin and everywhere, turning slowly with the current. They are the true population here, in numbers no one tries to count. A warm plume from a factory cluster can rise and bend for kilometers and make a local summer, and downstream of it the plankton thicken until the water looks faintly milky in the lamp-light. Then a school arrives and grazes them down to clear water in an afternoon, and downstream again they regrow. Shrimp click and snap in that cloud, too small to see, a constant dry static under the hum of the pumps. Fish click somewhere farther off. It is not silence. It is a low, busy chatter at the edge of hearing.

The first visible lives are the ones that have claimed a surface. On the welds, where the paint is still bright, there is a new, thin crust at the waterline — barnacles, tube worms, the first bacterial film, beginning to soften the sharpness. Under the platforms, where humans rarely look except on maintenance rounds, filter feeders hang in rows. They open and close, open and close, patient as valves. They do not move. The current does the moving for them, bringing the plankton snow past their mouths. A few years from now they will choke the works a little. For now they are only a first, tentative skin on the city, learning where the flow is steady and where it reverses around the pillars.

On land — which is not land — the crabs have already taken over the edges. They walk the sharp, poured terraces and the graded spoil heaps in dark armies, overturning the manufactured sediment with their legs, turning up the experimental plantings before they can root. They scavenge what falls from the work decks: dropped food, bits of insulation, weld slag. At the dimming of the lamps, when night is only a lowering to a softer white, they come out in larger numbers, a slow tide of shells moving along the retaining walls. They are the gardeners here, against anyone's intention. Whatever tries to grow wild, they mow.

The eels have chosen the warmest pipes. In the outflow culverts where the factories bleed heat into the water, they coil in the bends and stay. A pipe that runs warm all winter is a home that does not migrate. They are there month after month, heads out, waiting for fry or a careless crab to pass close enough. The heat that makes the plume dangerous for a human in a suit — no one crosses without permission, it is hot and full of effluent — is for them a room turned up too warm to leave. They have learned the rhythm of the foundries: when a furnace ramps up, the plume thickens, and the eels grow sluggish and spread out along the pipe to cool their tails.

The jellyfish follow the same warmth outward, but they do not stay in one place. In enormous, slow bells they gather around the discharge where the water is a few degrees warmer and rich with nutrients, pulsing in unison because they are all answering the same temperature gradient. From a distance they look like traffic lights hung in the water, red and white in the lamp pools, opening and closing together. They sting anything that blunders through the plume, and that includes fish, and occasionally a maintenance diver who misjudges the current. They are beautiful and indifferent and, in their slow synchronization, the most communal thing in the trench for miles.

The fish come and go like weather. Mackerel and herring turn inside the avenues between lamp masts, silver flashes in the steady white light, using the straight streets the way birds use a river. They arrive in a school of thousands and leave in an hour, and the water seems emptier for their going. Larger fish stay longer. They have learned the grid as a wolf learns a forest track: which avenue is sheltered from the surge, which lamp pool gathers prey in winter, which junction the school passes at shift change when the submersibles stir the light. Children who have never seen a natural reef know these hunting lanes by heart, and time their own swimming commutes to avoid them.

The plantings are lives too, though they are not allowed to be wild. In concrete troughs bolted to the sides of structures, in imported soil that has to be held in place by mesh, algae makes a thin film, seagrass puts out sparse green blades, kelp is tried in neat, husbanded rows. The low-twenties white light is enough for a thin photosynthesis, no more. The kelp is cropped before it can thicken, the algae is scraped from the lamps, the seagrass is grazed as soon as it lifts. The crabs overturn the rest. Vegetation here is auxiliary, tended, eaten — a crop, not a jungle. It cannot root in the sterile made ground, and the animals ensure it never ripens into a thicket.

And then, inside the dry volumes, the makers live.

They live in pressurized hulls and concrete shells, in islands of air that smell of oil, ozone, and rubber seals. Surface-born workers still arrive by tugs hauling containers through the transit tubes, still flinch at the pressure on their teeth when they stay still too long in the current, still are surprised by how ordinary the rules are: the hum changes and a pump is failing, the loading shifts and a mast groans, the plume is hot, do not cross. They weld at midnight under work-lights because the shift doesn't stop. They crawl along handrails in bright suits. They tend the generators whose collective drone you feel in your molars. They eat, in part, what the troughs grow.

Their children have never drawn breath outside that imported air. Born below, they think concrete is a kind of rock and a retaining wall is a kind of cliff. They learn to swim not for sport but for commuting between buildings, the way surface children learn to ride a bicycle. They measure distance in current-hours. They can tell a pump failure by the change in the hum the way you can tell a kettle is about to whistle. They know which lamp-avenues the predators use in winter and cross them quickly, close to the wall, in pairs.

No one lives here alone, and no one lives here in a crowd. The tugs pulse through the tubes with their crews riding in the warm, close dark, timing their arrivals to the surge. The maintenance crews talk on short, crackling radio while they hang upside down under a platform, scattering the filter feeders, who close and wait and reopen when they are gone. A school of fish will follow a container tug for the scraps and the stirred plankton, flashing in its wake. A crab will steal a glove left on a terrace and carry it for a week.

Once in a while, something passes that does not belong to the grid at all. A whale, or a great shark, far off and too large for the streets, a silhouette that makes the masts look like toy poles. At its passing, the small life goes quiet for a minute — the clicking stops, the school tightens, the crabs freeze on the edges — and then, after it is gone, resumes exactly as before. It has not come to feed here. It has only passed through a city that happens to be at the bottom of the sea, and the city, being young, had not yet learned to expect it.

That is who lives in the built ocean: the invisible drifters and the crust on the welds, the armies on the terraces and the eels in the warm pipes, the bells pulsing in the plumes and the silver schools in the lamp streets, the sparse green rows cropped before they thicken, and inside the air pockets, the people who made it all and the children who think it was always there. Sparsely, abundantly, over a volume so vast that even all of them together leave most of the water empty — and feel, in the emptiness between them, the current turn again.
