At first, from the square, it seems as if no one lives there at all.

That is because no one is in a hurry to be seen. The houses are set well apart and the yards between them are kept empty on purpose, so a person crossing one is not going to a market or a field, only to the next necessary place. You hear them before you properly see them: not voices at first, but the small sounds the town was built to carry. A shutter lifted and let fall. Rope over a windlass. A bucket set down against a cistern rim, not struck, set down. The bellows sighing once, the hammer striking once and then stopping, as if the work has been measured to that one blow.

Then a door opens — not wide, just a hand's width — and someone comes out into the lane to tend a drain that is already clear. That is the day beginning for most of them.

There are thirty or forty houses, and maybe a hundred and thirty people in them all, never quite all visible at once. They live in the layered houses the way the houses themselves are layered: a family in a ground floor of older stone, a son's family added above when there was room, a kitchen lean-to tacked on with a different slate. You can tell a house by who lives in it now, because the notch for its number on the post has been smoothed by that family's hands pushing past it for a hundred years. No one builds a new house if an old one can be kept. A house is not owned so much as continued.

They are not large people, and they move like people who live with thin air. Short, deliberate steps on the schist track above the town, and in the lanes even shorter, unhurried. Their clothes are wool and undyed linen in the colours of the walls — grey, brown, faded black — cut loose and layered against the cool that does not leave even in full light. Aprons for the women at the looms and the pots, caps for the men at the forge, boots resoled and resoled until the leather is mostly stitching. Their hands are the most telling thing: chapped from water, nicked from slate, blackened at the knuckles for the smith, pale and calloused for the weaver and the potter. They lift jugs by every door in the morning and carry them to the rock basin at the spring or to their own roof-water cistern under the square, each house for itself, and they do not talk while they do it. The work is known.

There is no single trade among them, because there is no surplus to trade. The granary stands on stone posts against damp and rats, and the person who keeps it — it rotates by season, by family, by a quiet agreement no one bothers to write down — knows the grain by weight in the hand. The smith works the foot bellows and simple iron, hammering a hinge, mending a tool, riveting a beam that is slightly too short with copper and pegs because that is what fits. The potter turns the wheel by hand and fires in the shared kiln, making pots thick enough to survive a drop on stone. In the row of weaving sheds a loom stands in each one, and the sound there is not the clatter of a factory but a regular, patient thump, thump, stop while a shuttle is inspected.

They feed themselves mainly from storage, as the place intends. Potatoes dug from pits under the floors, kept dark and cool. Dried meat hung in the granary's upper hall. Grain brought up the ridge in sacks once a season by two or three men who go down and come back heavier and quieter, their shoulders tired. Porridge, potato mash with a little salt, sour mountain ash berries dried from the stunted row by the granary — kept alive more than grown, pruned small like everything else that tries to live here. What grows in the fallow plot grows thin and is taken thin, and no one resents the ground for it. They know stone when they step on it.

Children learn the town by being given its maintenance in small pieces. A girl of seven will be sent with a brush to clear moss from a drip line so it does not spread. A boy of twelve will be told to watch the gutter during a passing shower and tell his father if any water is cutting a new course. By sixteen they have a loom or a hammer or a wheel they are trusted with, and they work alongside a parent without much instruction because the work has been the same for generations and watching is instruction enough. The old people sit in the hall on the benches below the granary, not because they are idle but because they remember. They remember where a doorway was moved six inches to follow a drain, which slate came from which part of the old quarry, which spring ran lower in the year of the long dry. Their memory is a kind of pointing: *that* wall was repointed in the spring it was always repointed.

They govern themselves in that same hall, in the only room that is larger than hand-and-arm scale. The vault is high and cool and echoing, and condensation beads and falls in a slow, regular drip onto the floor near the cistern mouth. When they meet — it is not often, and it is never loud — they sit and speak as if not wanting to disturb the drip. Decisions are made by not making them too large. No one expands a yard. No one stores above a neighbor's wall. No one takes more than his house's catchment of rain. If a dispute comes up, it is usually about a drain running where it should not, and it is settled by going out and looking at the drain together.

The other lives in the town are kept in the same managed, close way, part of a household rather than separate from it.

The dog asleep in the lee of the smithy belongs to no one in particular and to the smith in practice. It is old, deaf in one ear, not tied, and it moves its head when the hammer stops. The two sheep in the pen by the wall are small and calm, shorn twice a year for the wool that becomes all those grey layers. The pigeons live in the granary eaves — tolerate is the right word; they steal a little grain and are left to it. A cat will appear where sun finds a slate warm, one cat for every few houses, and it will stay until the sun moves and then vanish through a gap no one has bothered to close. High and far off, the pair of ravens circle without a sound over the ridge. They are not theirs. They are just above, watching the hollow the way the town watches itself.

Lower down, in the cracks, are lives that do not need tending because they are already exactly where they can survive. A single stem of grass in a paving crack where soil has settled. Lichen in orange and black on the south walls, slow and crusty, eating sun and stone. Moss, but only directly under a drip line, never spreading farther, because the wall tells it where it may be. Hardy shrubs in the yards, pruned small by hand. In the troughs along the smithy lane a faint green film that the smith rinses without thinking. In the cool, no flies. In the lanes, no birdsong. The quiet is not empty; it is pruned.

No one locks a door. No one hurries past an empty yard to fill it. When it rains briefly — darkening the slate, making the gutters run for an hour, smelling of cold mineral — they come out afterward with a bucket and a cloth to check their own cistern, their own channel, their own threshold dished in the middle by feet, and then they go back in.

They do not look like people who are waiting for anything to happen. They look like people who have arrived, a long time ago, at a form of life and have agreed, without ever quite saying so, to keep it in that form. They repair before decay can outrun them. They leave the empty spaces empty because empty is what allows the town to breathe and the water to run where it should. Night comes as ordinary night comes, woodsmoke hanging thin and cold over the roofs, the wind moving over the ridge outside the wall but hardly entering the hollow. In the morning the north-facing walls will sweat again, the hall will drip again, and tomorrow, carefully and deliberately, will look exactly like today — and that, for the people who live there, is not a repetition. It is the work, finished and being kept.
