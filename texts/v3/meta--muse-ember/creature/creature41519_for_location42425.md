Nothing lives here continuously, and yet everything lives here.

You hear the residents long before you see them, which is how it should be. The bowl holds sound the way the mat holds water — in pockets, for a while, then letting it go. A rustle in a tower wall that never synchronizes. A low organ chord in the crown when the wind has the right angle. The slow thump of sap pumping up a stem, a heartbeat you feel through the soles of your feet when you stand still on a root-avenue. The inhabitants announce themselves as pressure, as vibration, and only later, if at all, as bodies. They are crowded in time more than in space. A call answers itself a minute later from a tower no animal has reached yet. That is not an echo of a call. That is the call, arriving.

The smallest residents appear first, because they have the shortest lives to live.

In the puddles that gather in the hollows of the mat — brown with sap and red dust — chorus frogs inflate. They blow up their throats until the skin goes clear and balloon-bright and then they sing, and sing, and sing, tuned whether they mean to be or not to the same key as the insects. A puddle can live only from morning to afternoon before it cracks into a skin and a white ring, so a frog must do everything in that warm shallowness: sing, mate, scatter eggs into water that will vanish by the time they could hatch. Their whole population is the puddle. When the puddle goes, they go with it — not walking away, but subsiding, drying, being taken back into the damp mat they came from. What remains is the ring, and for a little while the place in the air where their note was.

Nearby, unnamed beetles are doing the opposite work. A fallen leaf is eaten in hours here. A dead bird is unmade by nightfall. These are the residents no one notices because they are busy erasing evidence. They ride the mat's slow rot and accelerate it, turning a leaf-platform, a carcass, a shed skin into food for the mat underneath. Nothing dead keeps its shape, which means these beetles are the true builders of the town's emptiness. They clear the plaza so completely that it looks as if nothing ever landed there.

On the ground, in the polished places where feet and bellies have passed for ages, run the mat-runners. Small, quick, low animals that dart through the creeping leaves and root-avenues, their passage written into the floor as a faint green scar that closes behind them. They are in a hurry the way everything here is in a hurry at ground level. A runner may polish a stretch of mat smooth in a season of crossings, and yet you will rarely see two at once. The plaza is theirs by use, not by occupation.

And they carry passengers who live even faster. Parasite flowers bloom suddenly on a runner's back — a bright, impossible blossom opening in the fur, already wilting — and pollinators find it before the runner has gone ten steps and strip it clean. That flower's entire life, from seed to pollination to death, is contained in ten steps of another animal's errand. It is born, it is noticed, it is eaten, all while the runner keeps running. The runner does not stop. The flower does not ask it to.

Use a longer timescale and you get the stalk-grazers.

They stride the root-avenues on legs longer than the towers are wide, slow enough that their shadow seems to stay in one place. They are the pruning crew of the town. A grazer will come to a leaf-gallery halfway up a tower, pull down a whole waxy platform with a mouth built for leverage, and then eat it for a season. A season. One platform, one animal, mouthful by mouthful, while the tower goes on breathing around them and the swallows rustle in the walls above their heads. When the grazer finally moves on, the gallery is gone, the tower is lighter, the avenue below is suddenly sunlit in a new pattern. The grazer has reshaped the architecture by eating it, and the town has been rebuilt by being consumed. Nothing else here builds so deliberately.

The towers themselves are inhabited in a way that makes the grazers look temporary.

Inside each hollow stem, by the thousands, nest the hollow swallows. You do not see most of them. You hear their wings, a constant unsynchronized rustle in the spongy inner wall, thousands of birds pressing their warm bodies into the warm, faintly translucent fiber where the sun touches it. They nest in the same hollow generation after generation, and each generation adds one soft layer of debris — feathers, droppings, warm detritus — so the hollow gets smaller and, paradoxically, the tower gets taller from the inside. The swallows are not living in the building. They are building it inward, using their own accumulated lives as material. Their heat and their noise and their weight are part of the stem's structure now. A pod that looks like an egg from outside, a bedroom or nursery or storehouse defined only by a membrane of leaf, will hold a chamber where you can stand upright and turn with your arms out, a few degrees warmer than the sky, quietly raining from its own ceiling. The swallows know these interior weathers the way we know rooms. They sleep in one dawn while outside it is noon.

Between the towers, another population moves that does not nest or graze or sing, but weathers.

Veil-beetles ride the warm updraughts in clouds so dense they darken the light for a second at a time as they pass between towers. They are not migrating to anywhere in particular. They are following the breath. When a tower opens its pores in the heat and inhales, pulling a cool draft across a plaza, the beetle cloud is drawn with it. When at night the towers exhale their slow warm sighs, the clouds drift out again and settle. Their whole geography is the eleven towers and the air between them, and their map changes every few hours with the cycle of inhale and exhale. They are a living shadow that teaches you where the wind is thinking.

At dusk, a different eruption.

Spore-bats pour out of blossoms at the crowns, shrieking, in a rush that seems too many for the flower that held them. Some towers, in a wet year, grow a blossom large as a room; in the dry year, the bats emerge from it and fly away, leaving the tower hollow again. It is wrong to call this birth and it is wrong to call it departure. The blossom is a roost, a womb, a vehicle. The bats are the blossom's way of being elsewhere for a night. They hunt in the dark over the mat, their spores dusting the leaves they brush, and they return before dawn to fold themselves back into a flower that will close and open with the heat, as if nothing had left. Their lives are timed to a single night, repeated.

Over all of it, the raptors wheel above the ring. They are the only residents who regularly see the whole bowl at once — the eleven towers leaning politely inward, the empty plazas, the root-avenues branching and rejoining, the boundary stalks closing the world. Their shadow passes over a beetle cloud and a frog chorus and a night's worth of bats still sleeping in a blossom, and connects them. From the ground you cannot hold the town in your eye. From the air, the raptor can. They do not live in the towers or on the mat. They live in the outline of the place, in its boundary, and they come back to that outline season after season.

But the deepest residents are the ones who do not keep a species shape long enough for you to name them twice.

Given enough time and moisture, the same living stuff will take whichever shape the current echo requires. A ground-plant, in a slow season, will thicken — roots rooting and unrooting — into something with legs, something runner-like, and run for a while, and then settle back into the mat and be plant again. A tower that grew a room-sized blossom will grow bats out of it. The mat itself is a population: roots and creeping stems and ground-hugging leaves woven so long they have forgotten soil, furling and rotting sweetly in one place while thickening from beneath in another, polishing where runners pass, lifting into causeways where grazers stride. Seeds drift in the updrafts by the thousands, fall into a new plaza, and thicken the mat there. That is migration, reproduction, and urban planning all at once.

And inside the stems, inside the pods, there is a further, unseen tier. The hollow trunks drip with condensation from high above, falling a long way to a soft spongy floor. In a pod, it is quietly raining, and no one above will ever know. Something lives in that interior rain — in the damp walls, in the warm water that never reaches the sky, in the layers of swallow debris that are composting into new fiber. They live entire lives in a night that never ends, in a dawn that is always damp, in a space larger inside than the egg outside promises. You could stand in the chamber, feel the rain on your skin, hear the sap heartbeat diaphanous through the wall, and never see them. They are not hidden. Their world is simply out of step with yours, a few degrees warmer, a few hours delayed, like each tower itself, which is the tower before it, a season delayed.

This is why the town can be ninety percent empty and deafening at the same time. The residents are not filling it with bodies. They are filling it with repetition. The same tower again. The same plaza again. The same swarm, the same chorus, the same rustle, coming back a minute later from somewhere else, a season later from the same hollow, a breath later on the wind. To live here is to be part of that repetition — to erupt in a puddle for an afternoon, to carry a flower for ten steps, to eat a gallery for a season, to nest in a wall until the wall is made of your ancestors, to pour out of a blossom at dusk and return before dawn, to ride a breath from one identical tower to the next and darken the light for a second.

Walk a plaza for a long minute and see nothing. Stay another minute and the air will be full. Stay an hour and the mat under your feet will have pulsed, the towers will have inhaled and exhaled, a local rain will have fallen only on the undersides of leaves, somewhere in a pod a whole unseen generation will have been born in the rain, and a call you made on entering will be made again, altered, by a tower you have not yet reached — as if the town itself, crowded with its own time, had learned your voice and was trying it out.

No one lives here the way a person lives in a house. Everything lives here the way weather lives in a bowl: arriving in patches no larger than a room, vanishing, returning from a different direction, wearing the same shape again.
