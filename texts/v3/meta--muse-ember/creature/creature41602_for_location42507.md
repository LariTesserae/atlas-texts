At first it can look empty, if you measure emptiness by the things that move on their own.

There is no swimming. There is no flight. There is no crawling, no calling, no interruption of any kind. What moves here moves because something else has pushed it, and it moves all together, violently, in unison. A whole forest bends double in one direction, then straightens, then begins to grow the other way. You hear the water before you see why it has moved. You hear a gate clang a half-second before the steam bursts.

And underneath the roar — the deep, chest-deep roar of pumps and turbines working against water, the hiss where steam vents bubble under the surface like a kettle, the crash and slap of surges, the groan of metal cooling in wet air — there is a second sound, softer and nearer: ripping and snapping, as if something thick is tearing itself free and regrowing in the same breath.

That is where whatever lives here begins to announce itself. Not as individuals. As a pressure to continue.

Wade in to the knee, then to the waist, in the ponded water that is warm and opaque and thick with silt and pollen and spores and fine industrial powder, and the first living things you meet are not in the water so much as through it. Translucent kelp rises from the bottom, but it is not rooted. It is anchored — to pipes, to wreckage, to other kelp. Its blades unspool in slow spirals, up and down at once, in places emerging from the bottom and from the hanging rain and meeting in the middle of nowhere and continuing through each other. In the dim light, which has no source you can find, they glow very faintly, a green that does not illuminate so much as make the darkness visible.

They lean. That is their behavior, if you can call it that. They lean toward vibration. When a turbine spins faster in its flooded pit, when a pump churns warm water from one bay to another for no reason except its timer has not stopped, the kelp inclines toward the source of the hum and holds there, trembling. When the floodgates clang open and shut on a schedule laid down decades ago, the whole forest is bent flat by the surge — not eaten, not grazed, not chased. Nothing eats this. Leaves are never bitten. The bending is the weather, and the weather is kept by the machines.

Closer to the surface the density becomes near-touching. Reed-bells cluster in thickets so dense you cannot push through without tearing them, and they tear and regrow audibly in the same place, a soft ripping under the roar. Mirror-lily and steam-fern knit themselves into floating gardens, mats interwoven so tightly they will bear a step, then give way all at once in a sink of green. Some mats repeat the same leaf pattern over an area the size of a town, as if the valley had been printed from one template and not finished. Whole plants die standing, bleach silver, and are not removed. They are folded down into the mats they helped make, and the mats thicken.

On every vertical surface the same pressure has found another way up. Moss grows in a skin, thick as fur, in pale chartreuse and rust-red, over rusted iron, over tilting cooling towers, over the sides of pump houses. Ferns grow out of cable bundles. Vines grow out of vine-tangles to make new vines. On a derrick whose legs are furred with algae, on a substation on stilts whose amber lights flicker in sequence, on a conductor's belt that still moves in a slow, pointless circuit carrying only wet leaves — the green is not decorating the structures. It is sealing them. Roots have found the warm pipes. Moss has sealed the leaks. Kelp has reached the intakes and keeps them from clogging.

It is only then, slowly, that the other residents become distinct from the background noise they make. They do not grow. They churn, spin, hum, clang, squeeze, and carry. A pump house with its wheelhouse door swinging on its hinge is still electrified inside. A half-sunk conveyor turns and turns. In low sheds, algae-presses squeeze the surface mats into dark pellets and drop them back into the water. Turbines generate electricity that lights lamps and then goes nowhere. Floodgates flood one channel and starve another, so that a dry square of cracked concrete that was an island at noon is a lake by dusk and an island again by night.

If you stay long enough, the separation between the two kinds stops holding. The machines are not being operated. They are being tended, almost, by the vegetation, and the vegetation is being fed, almost, by the machines. The plants thrive because the machines churn nutrients from dying leaves and warm the water and stir the silt. The machines hum because the plants need the vibration to grow. It is a closed, automated ecology of metal and leaf, and neither half of it can be described as alive without the other.

That is why their living, such as it is, does not look like life with births and deaths. It looks like kinks and reversals. Every few minutes the current turns in a bay and the kelp forest, already kinked from the last turn, straightens and begins to grow the other way, leaving a visible kink in every stalk like a record of the bend. A new island of woven vegetation will rise, steaming, under a turbine's outflow, hold for an hour, then founder and sink under its own weight, taking a length of pipe down with it. A fresh cone of black, glassy mud erupts near a foundation, cracks along its sharp, unweathered crest, and collapses back into the water with a grunt of displaced steam. Nothing erodes slowly here. Everything is either brand-new or suddenly gone, and the residents regrow into the gap in minutes, audibly, through the machinery that made the gap.

Even their flowers testify to this closure. They are pale, and they are closed, and they never open, because nothing needs pollinating. There are no seasons and no grazing. There is no outside to bring in difference. The water reflects things not present — a clear sky and a sharp horizon in the sheet-water over black mud and concrete foundations, while the real sky is grey and low — and the plants themselves seem to grow toward those reflections, down as often as up.

So what lives in the valley is not a population. It is a metabolism that has learned to build itself out of whatever is already running. Layer after layer, bottom mat, midwater kelp, surface mat, hanging moss, aerial fern unfurling into the steam, with pumps and turbines and gates humming inside the layers and plants humming back by leaning toward the sound. Warm, submerged, lush, young, dense, industrial, deafening, and coming apart — and in the coming apart, growing again, through the pipes, through the cables, through the water that hangs in curtains and drips from nothing and soaks you from below.
