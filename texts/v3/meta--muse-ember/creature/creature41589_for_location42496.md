It starts where the water slows down.

Not in the open. The broad poured floor is too flat, too cold, too loud, too regularly swept by a sheet of water running to the sump — anything trying to settle there is flushed or vibrated loose or frozen under a boot. Life here is pressed down, under, inside, and to the edges, in the places design intended to be sealed and is not quite.

Under the grated pit in the corner, at the bottom of the ladder, is dark water that never leaves. That is the oldest surface in the room, in biological terms. Bacteria live there in the sump in a thin, invisible thickness, and in the warmer return lines, and in the thin film that coats the inside of every housing, every length of rubber hosing, every joint that weeps. They are in the water that hammers in the wall, in the water that beads on the cold pipes and hangs a moment before it lets go, in the mist that the ceiling rains into the fluoroscent light. A droplet can be born clean on a pipe, run over a colonized surface, gather a cargo, and fall onto the top of a pump housing where it joins a puddle and dries to nothing but them. When a valve snaps open and steam and cold mist blast sideways for four seconds, they go with it — for a few meters, for a few seconds — and settle again on a handrail, a sleeve, a gauge face fogged from the inside. When the purge drops the pressure and your ears pop, they ride the change in air back into a vent and back down.

There is no outside for them to return to. The loop that moves the water moves them. Water in, water pressed, water cooled, water released, water collected, water sent again. Bacteria are pumped twenty meters from pump one to pump two in a dark, warm rush, are heated in an exchanger that breathes, are cooled against a tank wall and tick with its expansion, are left in a sight-glass to grow on the glass until the level is smeared, are carried through a trench under the floor plates into more concrete and then back. Generations are short in the warm lines, longer in the cold standing water between plinths where puddles reflect the lights in broken gold. They eat what the machines shed — heat, copper, oil that leaks past a seal that has not yet seated — and they die and are eaten and are flushed to the sump to begin again. The place smells metallic and mildewed because of them. They are the reason the floor is slick in more than water.

Where the drainage channel runs along the wall and the fluorescent reaches a little farther, the sheet of water is slowest and the bacteria have a partner. A thin green film of algae paints the concrete there, slick as paint, and it moves a fraction toward the sump every night and then re-grows where it was taken. It does not need soil. It needs light, however poor, and stillness, however slight, and the constant wetness the system cannot get rid of. In daylight from a caged tube — dim, yellowed, flickering where moisture has gotten into the fitting — it photosynthesizes in the patient, marginal way of things that live in parking garages. At the far end of the channel, where the light fails, it gives up and the concrete is only wet again.

Where the water is not just present but *steady*, something slower has taken hold. At the base of a column, where a drip falls from a joint between panels with metronome regularity, there is a patch of moss no bigger than a palm. It lives in the joint itself, in the crack the formwork left, in the place where the wall sweats and never dries. It is dark, cushioned, and it holds the water it is given as long as it can. It does not care about the roar — the pumps are twenty meters away, the floor vibrates, the bolts tick against their washers, and the moss receives all of it through concrete. It grows the way the room ages: imperceptibly, fractionally, one wet day at a time. That is its whole strategy. To be wettest where everything else is only wet, and to wait.

And then, far from the channels, by the lift door where the cold seeps through the metal and your boots squeak, there is the anomaly that proves the rule. In a bolt hole in the floor, where grit and dust have washed together over a long time and a lamp is directly overhead, a single pale weed has come up. Two leaves. Stunted, colorless from lack of sun, leaning a fraction toward the light. It did not get there on its own — nothing gets in here except down the shaft, in on a boot, in on a pallet, in on a coil of hose left by a wrench and a bucket. Someone carried the grit. Someone carried the seed on a sole or in a cuff. The humans who work here do not know it is there, or if they do, they step around it without thinking. It lives because people pass through, because the light is left on, because the bolt hole catches what the floor would carry away, because the altitude makes it thin and cold but the concrete holds a little heat from a pump twenty meters off. It is the only thing in the room that is trying to be a plant in the ordinary sense, and it is doing it as badly and as stubbornly as a plant can.

Those are the living things. The rest of the residents are not alive, but they behave as if they are, and everything alive here depends on their behavior.

The pumps are spaced too far apart to be social. Pump one and pump two stand on separate plinths with nothing between them but wet floor and echo, each spooling up on its own cycle, pitch climbing until the whole floor hums at a deeper note. The heat exchangers stand alone like patient animals, breathing — a fan kicking in and pushing a gust of high, cold air across a face, a needle climbing, a tank level rising. The row of tanks against the far wall beads with condensation and ticks as it expands, turns, contracts. The compressors, the bank of fans drawing in thin air that whistles at the louvered vents, the pressure system proving itself because it is new — they create the weather of the pocket: the heat bacteria bloom in, the cold that makes pipes rain, the vibration that keeps the air to itself, the mist that carries spores and cells from one surface to the next. They do not notice the moss. The moss notices them every time a seal weeps a little more or a little less.

Humans are the third kind of resident: tidal, not permanent. They arrive when the lift finally stops and the doors open onto sound before light. They stand for a moment in the roar that you feel in your teeth, breath puffing a little in the cool, clammy air, and then they go to work by headlamps in corners the fluorescents never reach. They read gauges, they log a rising sight-glass, they tighten a fitting and stop a drip that a moss patch has been counting on for a month. They leave a wrench, a bucket, a coil of hose on the wet floor, and the distances make the tools look abandoned. They bring in grit for the weed and microbes on their boots for the sump, and they take readings out again in the lift. Their voices die two feet from their mouths. They live higher up the tower, in thinner air still, and they come down to this made mountain of slab on steel on concrete to check that the loop is still a loop. They are not part of the ecology except as disturbance and supply, which, for a place this closed, is enough.

At night, when no one is there, the residents continue without witnesses. The water beads and drips and runs to the sump. The bacteria in the warm lines divide and are pumped through the cold and divide again. The algae in the channel thicken a fraction where the light reaches. The moss at the column holds its drop a fraction longer. The weed in the bolt hole leans a fraction more toward the flickering tube. The pumps spool and clash and spool again, twenty meters apart, breathing in the dark between the islands of concrete.

It is complete in itself because it has to be. No birds get in, no insects find a moth to find around a light, no rat finds a trench it can hidden in — the noise and the wet and the sealed layers keep it to bacteria, algae, moss, and one pale thing with two leaves, plus the great breathing machines that make all of it possible, plus the brief, boot-squeaking humans who descend through their own weather to see that nothing has stopped.
