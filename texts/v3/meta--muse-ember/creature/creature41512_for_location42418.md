The people of Wickhollow do not so much live in the cavern as settle into it, the way damp settles into brick.

You will not meet them all at once. They are rarely in a crowd, and never in a hurry. They move at the pace the heat allows — short, deliberate walks from the engine ward to the vat terraces to the kettle-stone houses at the far grotto, then a long pause in a doorway where the air is a degree cooler. Their shoes are scuffed thin. Their shirts stick and are un-stuck and stick again. Skin is perpetually damp, hair limp, eyes large-pupilled and slow to open all the way against the amber lamps, as if blinking costs more here than it should. They speak low not because they have been told to, but because the rock takes the sound anyway and the heat makes breath expensive. A conversation is a few words, a nod, a hand resting a moment on a warm pipe while the other speaks.

Some of them were born down here and have never seen the surface except as a story told about rain. For them the ceiling is the sky, and the steam cycle is weather. They know time the way the town does: not by the hour but by how long it takes the upper rock to bead again, by how long the Kettle takes to crust over, by the drip-clock in the hall filling grain by grain. Work is measured that way, too, and bodies have learned to work that way — the engine hands with their forearms polished by oil and sweat, moving around the turbines in a slow orbit, listening with their boots for a bearing that has gone rough; the vat tenders with green-black stains up to the elbow, skimming mats and turning them, tasting rarely, talking to the tanks in the shorthand of people who spend ten hours a shift leaning over something alive; the households in the cool grotto, the poorest because the stone there never dries, who carry buckets of condensate from gutter to planter and sleep in rooms that sweat all winter.

Children run in bright bursts and then sit, flushed and panting, under a fern. They are very good at finding the coolest cracks, at catching the blind fish after a surface rain flush before the fish find a crack again, at naming the drips. Old men, who can do no heavy work in the heat, become watchers. They sit in the quietest lane with a palm flat against the wall and feel the rock tremble with water finding its way through. They watch the Kettle on a rest-cycle when the steam thins, to see if the condensation on the low ledge really does run uphill again, and they watch it for a long time without needing it to mean anything. No one announces them. They are just there, damp and patient, until one day their chair in the doorway is empty and their share of the loop has gone back into the gutters.

But people are not the densest life in Wickhollow. They are not even the oldest. The plants were here, in a sense, before the houses — or the houses learned to make room for them very quickly.

In every gutter and planter and drip-line, ferns have rooted and come up through the paving as if the town were a soil they were slowly reclaiming. Some are umbrella-big, their fronds beaded and heavy, shading a doorway, a market stall, a sleeping dogless threshold where a family eats. Vines have bridged lanes twenty feet up, house to house, and people walk under them without looking up, used to the green rain falling from them after a machine has been running hard. In courtyard pots, small twisted trees live for decades, their roots having found a warm pipe and wrapped it finger by finger, drinking heat and condensation in equal measure, leafing out a little after every rest-cycle. On the ceiling, the hanging gardens grow down — roots pale and blind, leaves turned up toward a light too dim to matter — clustered in the stalactite fields like a forest seen from underneath. No one planted most of them deliberately, and no one can quite explain why they flower when an engine shuts down for its long rest and the air cools a fraction of a degree. They just do, and people notice, and that is enough.

At the middle terraces the life is more deliberate. The vats are shallow and stacked, and the algae mats in them grow thick as felt, green and rust-brown, breathing slowly in the lamplight. They are harvested for food and for the engines, scraped off in sheets, pressed into cakes, fed back as starter to the next trough. Around them, on walls and pipes and the cool north faces of cold engines, moss and algae give off their faint living glow. In the dark between lamps it is not black, but green-black, and you can tell where the wettest places are by that glow. On freshly blasted walls, where the rock is still raw and toothed, a green bloom appears within a year — first a film, then a fuzz, then, in ten years, a fern. The town is always being colonized by its own damp.

Animals, by contrast, are rumors. No birds, no swarms. In the coolest pools at the far end, pale blind fish no longer than a hand appear in the gutters after a rain flush miles above, slip bright through the silt for a day, and vanish into cracks so small you cannot see where they went. Children will tell you where they saw one, and adults will nod as if it were weather. Around the white crust of the Kettle, blind beetles work the edge of the heat, eating algae, moving slow, their bodies translucent when a furnace backlights them. And in the damp behind the houses, in walls that have never been dry, live a few salamanders — grey, ancient-looking, slow as cooling stone — that come out only when a new tunnel is opened and the rock is still fresh. To see one is an omen of a new street. Most people live their whole lives in Wickhollow without seeing any animal but each other, and it does not feel empty, because the town is alive in other ways that do not need eyes.

Underneath all of it, invisible work goes on. In the Kettle, too hot for anything you could touch, extremophile mats tint the mineral crust and turn heat and stone into something softer, more usable. In the vats, bacteria do the slow chemistry that makes algae into food and mineral slurry into cement. In the gutters, in the warm rills that run downhill steaming faintly toward the pool, spores and cells drift and settle and start again. Hair falls, skin flakes, sweat evaporates and condenses and runs down a wall behind a house and feeds a fern root. Nothing leaves. Nothing is wasted. The loop that feeds the engines feeds the plants that feed the people whose breath feeds the steam that feeds the plants.

That is what lives in Wickhollow, in the end: not separate kinds, stacked by size, but one breathing system that has learned to house itself. The ferns hold the streets the way roots hold soil. The algae feeds the engines and the people and the beetles. The salamander waits in the wall for the wall to break. The child sits under the fern and learns to listen for drips. And if you stand still long enough in a quiet lane, with your palm on the warm wall, you stop being able to tell where the inhabitant ends and the inhabited place begins — which is, perhaps, exactly how they prefer it.
