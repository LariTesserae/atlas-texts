What lives here first lives in the weather.

Between one rain wall and the next there is a brief, almost clear moment, and in that moment the air itself thickens. Midges. Not a swarm in the human sense — no cloud with a centre — but a darkening of the bog air over the tussocks, a thin trembling above still water, so that when you stop walking you are inside it. They rise off sphagnum, off sheep backs, off your own hood if you stand long enough, and they vanish again when the next wall arrives sideways. The birds live in that same air but differently. Ravens quarter the plateau high and low, riding the colliding systems, screaming as they drop into mist and up again, a pair that has nested for years on the same craggy shoulder and will find the same ledge even when the ledge is white with rain. Below them gulls and shearwaters work the inlets in constant traffic, black-and-white stitching, turning inland on a wind that smells of diesel from below. Oystercatchers pipe on shingle that was rearranged in the night, find a scrape above the new high-tide line, and settle again as if nothing had moved. Everything here has learned to move.

On the summit plain the ground is not ground. Sphagnum is the resident, and it is not a plant so much as a population — a handswidth thick, springy mattress of tiny lives, each holding water twenty times its weight, each exuding the acid that makes the pools black and tea-coloured and keeps almost everything else out. Under your knee it gives, cold through your trousers, and then gives way altogether. In that sponge live springtails and mites and cranefly larvae you will never see unless you turn a stone, and in the open pools among it live trout and char in numbers that are not credible for water so cold and so apparently empty. They hang under the mirror that reflects the sky, so you step toward a reflection and find a fish instead. They feed on what falls in — midges, beetles, the occasional oystercatcher feather — and they spawn in the braided side channels where the river has abandoned its course overnight and cut a new one across a meadow, leaving oval cobbles polished like bread rolls.

The bog blooms, after its fashion. Cotton grass lifts white heads that shake rain off and shake it back on again. Sundews open small red hands in the wet and wait for the midges to settle, and the frogs in the old peat working — where the conveyor rusts and the dewatering pump still sucks — sit in water that is warm only by comparison and sing through the mist at lengths that would be ridiculous in a drier place. Peat, below all of them, is alive in the opposite way: anaerobic, silent, preserving. Anything that slips in — a boot, a fence post taken by a bog slide, a sheep that did not get out — turns black and intact and stays. The bacteria down there do not decompose so much as hold, for centuries, an exact shape of a life that was brief above.

Follow the water down and it multiplies. The rivers do not run, they wander, they braid, they appear as thin white threads on slopes that were dry in the morning. In them, in the high lakes, more trout; in the slower runs, spawning gravel kept clean by the constant rearrangement. Waterfalls roar in stereo after heavy rain, and the fish below them face upstream into foam. Nothing stays where it was left, so the lives in the rivers are lives of negotiation — eggs tucked into a cobble that may be moved, fry hiding in the lee of a boulder that may be buried by silt and uncovered by morning, trees rooting in the rotting trunk of a fallen one because the soil is too thin and too wet to root in directly.

At the inlets the drowning continues under the surface, and down there the density becomes almost solid. Kelp forest twelve metres down moves in slow, dense curtains, frond after frond, anchoring to rock you will never see because pollack and wrasse and crabs and the small silver fish they feed on are always in the way. The kelp does not stop at the waterline. It continues up through intertidal rockpools, into wet meadow, into the low rainforest, and the animals follow it up as if the shore were an inconvenience. In the sheltered bays the fish farm rings — orange floats, automated feeders clicking out pellets — hold Atlantic salmon in cages, thousands of cold lives surging and turning in unison, fed on a timetable that hasn't changed in years, fed by a person in oilskins who arrives before light and feeds them in rain that needles the back of his neck. Beyond the cages the wild fish keep their own traffic, and far out, in winter, a whale blows once, a column of breath you hear more than see, and is gone.

On the outer rocks seals bark and grunt, haul out, slip back in, their lives a pendulum between stone and kelp. In the canopy of the low forest — wind-trained, near-touching, no light reaching the floor except in flickering patches — another kind of density crowds the trunks. Moss a handswidth thick on every north-facing stone, ferns unfurling in the lee of every boulder, epiphytes and lichens and fungi layered over greens so dark they look black beside a shaft of pale sun on a waterfall. There are small mammals in the leaf litter, voles and shrews moving under ferns where the floor is always damp, birds working the understory where you hear three species at once without trying to, a wren, a siskin, something you cannot name, all feeding, all nesting, all decomposing in their turn into the thin soil that feeds the next tree. A tree falls — the soil is thin, the wind is constant — and fungi take it from the top down, shelf by shelf, while its roots, exposed and then buried again by a shifting river, hold water for other lives to live in.

On the plateau itself the large, visible lives are the patient ones. Hardy sheep, soaked to the skin, their fleeces heavy with rain, eating whatever green is within reach and walking only on the tussocks, not the moss, because they have learned the same rule you learn. A few half-wild cattle stand in pools to their knees and do not move for hours, breath visible even at the height of an imagined summer when it is still 8 or 10 degrees and the wind strips heat from wet skin. Midges darken around their eyes. A sheepdog lives with them, mostly wet, mostly waiting, startled into work when a fence goes down in a bog slide or a river rises two metres in a night and rearranges silt, branches, a plastic buoy, a dead bird, and the track that is really a causeway disappears under a lake for two hundred metres.

Human lives here are scattered thinly over the density, and they are not so much residents as attendants. Two men on a trawler moored stern-to at the inlet, engines idling, exhaust ticking, drinking tea in a wheelhouse whose windows are streaked, watching the gulls and the weather mast on the summit plain turning frantically against a sky that will not clear. A man for the hydro intake high in the valley, who walks the buried pipe line through forest in boots full of water, checks the hum of the powerhouse no bigger than a barn at the inlet, and goes home to a boathouse with algae on the roof and gutters overflowing. A shepherd who lives below the mist in a cottage you will only see as a light, who knows which bog has sheared along a deep crack and will take the young trees with it, who moves sheep before it goes. A technician who climbs to the weather mast to unfreeze an instrument with numb fingers warmed inside his jacket. They are few, they are mostly machinery rather than buildings, and their work is constant repair against water — pumps running, generators humming, iron rusting from the outside in at a pace you can almost watch, wood rotting from the top down, paths re-found every season.

And beneath and between all of them, the ordinary, unshowy abundance that fills the volume: feeding, spawning, nesting, decomposing, ceaseless but not hurried. Fish under almost every stone you turn. Fungi under the bark. Bacteria holding the peat together. Ravens, trout, kelp, sphagnum, sheep, salmon in cages, seals, midges, sundews, frogs, songbirds you hear but do not see, the whale far out, the people in oilskins clicking pellets into cold water — all of them living in a place that has been worn for so long into its last form that it will not change in any human lifetime, and is still, every minute, being remade in its details. Nothing dries. Nothing stays where it was left. And everything, in its own cold, wet, turbulent way, stays.
