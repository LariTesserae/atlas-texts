It is easier to think of them not as individuals, but as agreements between layers.

The first inhabitants are the ones that make ground. Low in the water, between the submerged trunks and across the flats, a mat spreads root by root, woven so tightly you could stand on it and feel only a giving, wet spring underfoot. It is not soil settled from elsewhere. It is built, alive, in place. Roots cross and re-cross, trap the unsorted gravel and the raw red earth the young ridges are still shedding, trap silt, trap the leaf that fell an inch and stopped. A root here does not seek water — water is everywhere, standing at ankle, at waist, at neck in the dips. A root seeks a place to hold while the floor rises underneath it, leaf by leaf. So the mat does not grow down so much as it grows sideways, and up, a fraction at a time, keeping its face at the same bright depth while silt accumulates below.

On that mat, and climbing everything that rises from it, live the raft plants. Their stems are hollowed with air bladders hidden inside, not for floating away — nothing floats away in still water — but for holding a canopy out over the surface as if on a held breath. Canopy touches canopy because the holders touch. There is no need to compete for a place to stand. A seed that falls lands on a leaf, on a root-mat, on water that carries it an inch before it snags, and that is enough. Many germinate in mid-air. You can see the pale rootlet let down from a leaf in the upper green, feeling, descending by growth, until it finds a trunk, a vine, the surface itself, and then finishes opening underwater. The hand that holds it never moves fast. A whole life can be lived suspended between the two greens, with one end drinking rain that arrives as a fine wall, and the other end drinking the standing sheet.

The waterline is where most of them truly live. Above it, the leaves are thick and waxy and bright against the high equatorial sun, which falls hotter here on thin air. Below it, the same leaves are pale and translucent, thin enough to see the veinwork. This is not a boundary for them. A vine can root in air, cross the surface, root again under water, and thrive at both ends equally. A colony of shelf-leaves spreads flat at the interface, half in and half out, expanding and contracting almost imperceptibly as the sheet rises by a fraction after rain and stays. They are the ceiling of the underwater world. Under their undersides the oxygen made by submerged leaves does not race up as bubbles. It collects in slow silver films, trembling against the leaf, held there by surface tension, and is colonized.

Under those films live the grazers, and they are as close to an animal as this place allows, and they are not very close. Translucent, smaller than an eyelash, they spend their entire lives on the underside of a single leaf, eating the algae that grows rich in the captive oxygen, keeping the film from bursting, returning what they take to the leaf that made it. They never surface. They never need to. The world for them is a lens of breath, curved under green, reabsorbed before it can leave. The entire nutrient economy is like that: closed and local. A leaf dies, falls an inch, is worked on by films and mats, is taken up again by a root that is already there. Nothing runs off. Nothing has to. The rain brings almost nothing but water. The stone brings the rest.

For the trunks that go down and keep going, the surface is the same non-boundary. A tree here grows upward as the floor rises beneath it, leaving its own lower branches behind, still alive, still photosynthesizing in clear water. So one life looks like several forests stacked. At the top, a crown in white-gold light. A few feet down, the crown of last generation, broader, paler, a rosette turned flat by stillness. Deeper still, the crown before that, spread between roots like a carpet that never rises. From above you see leaves; from below you see the same leaves, mirrored in branching, made translucent by the water they live in. They are not two kinds of plant. They are one plant at different ages, all alive at once because nothing dies to make room, because the water preserves sharpness and wood alike under glass. As a ridge goes from emergent island to reef of crowns to a suggestion of crowns under bright water, the trees on it go the same way, vertically, without hurry.

On the bare peaks that punch up knife-edged above treetops, life is sparser and harder and even slower. Black stone with glassy faces, red earth torn open — these are inhabited only by crusts. Encrusting colonies, lichen-like in patience, spread in the cracks by millimeters, mining minerals out of rock that has not yet weathered, holding a skin of moisture after the rain-wall passes, brightening where the sun is unfiltered. They do not soften the ridge. They live in its sharpness and, by holding water in the crack a breath longer, they are perhaps the reason the sharpness lasts so long. Down the slopes, where gravel has not sorted itself, roots test and grip and dissolve, exactly as slowly.

There are almost no animals in the way you would recognize one, and there is reason for it. Food is not scarce, it is ambient. Light, oxygen, water, a leaf surface to sit on — these are everywhere and identical everywhere. Still water brings no current of nutrients to a mouth. No season demands migration or dormancy. No predator has found a profit in moving faster than growth. So almost everything that lives here has solved movement by growing into the next place instead of going there. The shelf colony climbs a stem a centimeter a year to stay at the surface as the mat rises. The hanging plant lets down a root to the water and then becomes a trunk for others. A fallen trunk, silted over, becomes a planter for thirty kinds of green, then a platform for water to stand on, then ground again, without ever becoming architecture.

Once, far below the surface, you see the exception. A single blind, frog-slow shape easing through the weeds. That is the whole of the mobile world, more or less. It is broad and leaf-like and translucent, with no eyes worth mentioning because in clear, bright, shallow water eyes would be an expense specimen after specimen has refused to pay. Its skin does the work of leaf and lung and root. It presses itself to the underside of a shelf-leaf or a submerged rosette and stays there for months, absorbing, grazing the film, letting the plant do the work of making the day. When that leaf sinks too deep into rising silt, when its light thins, it does what the plants do: it grows a new anchor forward, an inch, dissolves the old one behind, and settles again. To watch it move you must watch it by position, an hour apart. There may be only a handful in a basin in a lifetime, each basin sealed off by its sharp ridge, each with its own depth, its own shade, its own slightly different form of the same shelf and mat and rosette, diverging because a seed travels an inch and rain carries pollen no farther than touching.

That is who lives there. Colonies that blur into ground, canopies that blur into rafts, trees that blur into their own underwater pasts, films that blur into breath, and, rarely, a soft blind thing that moves by becoming plant long enough to be mistaken for one. They make the only sound the place allows: a drip that takes a long time to fall the last distance to a surface it was already almost touching, water sighing once over a submerged log, a leaf uncurling under water with a small, patient displacement. By growth, not by voice.

At midday the light is ordinary and falls straight down through clear water onto green that continues unchanged. Nothing you can see is doing anything you can recognize as doing. The mat is rising a fraction. The shelf is holding its film. The trunk-reef is photosynthesizing in three registers at once. The crust on the peak is drinking a crack. And that is a life, whole, anchored, and complete, made by being plant, or by being almost plant, at the speed of patience.
