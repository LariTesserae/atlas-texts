Nothing here lives by itself.

That is the first thing you learn, and it stays true at every scale. A plant is not beside a house, it is leaning on the house, and then through the house, and then the house is leaning back. A beast is not in a pen, it is in a street, and the street is not empty of people, it is empty of a herd for an hour and then full. A predator is not outside the settlement, it is at the far end of the street, where the herd is, moving at the speed the herd allows it. Even the ground is not under them, it is among them — pushing up through a floor, cracking a path, being packed down again by feet before noon.

So the living things cannot be described one by one. They arrive as a thickness, a single continuous skin over the plain, low and green and white and moving, and only after you have watched long enough do individuals separate out of it, and even then they do not stay separate for long.

Everything is low. Not only the houses. The people sit more than they stand, and when they stand they do not rise above the thorn-trees for long. The beasts are small and cropped short by the spiky mat they eat, their backs at a child's waist. The birds nest in the thatch rather than over it. The lizards run along the tops of walls rather than up them. The burrowers are underneath all of it. Whatever lives in the Ahrit Lowland has learned to keep its whole body within a hand's breadth of the ground or of something else's body, because there is nothing tall to climb and nothing far to go to.

In the morning they all drink.

Dawn is the loudest hour and the busiest, and still it is quiet. Before the sun takes the dew back, the broad leaves are beaded and cold, and the salt in the dust has drawn moisture out of the air and made it faintly damp underfoot. The plants open their pink-white flowers for a few minutes. The birds make their one rusty chorus, from the thatch and from the densest thickets, and then stop, as if they have spent their voice for the day.

Then the people come out barefoot, in wraps of woven fiber and flattened hide, their feet making only a soft *poof* in the powder. They move from leaf to leaf, bending, licking dew off the broadest and coldest leaves with the tip of the tongue, letting it run down in threads to the roots to be drunk there. Children are taught this before they are taught anything else: mouth closed against dust, tongue out only for dew, never to waste what has been gathered in a body. A man will cut a stem and drink from the cut, a woman will open a melon-gourd half-buried in dust and pass the pulp around, a grandmother will press a piece of fat into a traveler's palm and keep her own mouth shut around the memory of it. They store water the way the plants do — in fat, in pulp, in the slow keeping of the body — and they spend it slowly, and they are watchful of each other's stores as of their own. No one spits. No one leaves a cup half-drunk. What wetness there is never pools. It is held inside living things and guarded fiercely, passed from hand to hand.

They work in the cool hours, and the work is mostly maintenance, and mostly silent.

At dawn the streets are full of processions — families walking long distances to a market three days away that looks, from the roof, like the next street over, because the settlement does not end. They carry baskets on their heads, bundles of salt, strings of dried meat, herbs wrapped in fiber, live gourds still fat with moisture. They walk in single file between the thickets, feet feeling the ground through the dust for the tremor of a herd coming, or a burrower tunneling, or a neighbor stirring on the other side of a shared wall.

For most of the day their communication is not speech. A tap of the foot on packed clay, a brush of a flank in passing, a hand pressed flat against a wall to feel an answer from the other side, a scent released deliberately in a crowded clearing — these carry what a voice would elsewhere. Bargaining in the markets is done this way: a bundle of salt passed back and forth, a touch held a little longer, a step back. Children learn to read these signals through their feet before they learn to speak beyond dawn, and elders, who sit longest in the shade of the thorn-trees and move least, are the best readers. They remember where a street was before the salt pushed it a hand's breadth to the east, which houses were abandoned to the creepers and which were reclaimed, which family is now living three walls away from where they were last season. Their knowledge is not written down. It is kept in bodies and in tracks that fill in and must be learned again.

The houses are not so much built as kept. A family lives low and stepped, three and four houses thick against each other for shade, walls of packed earth and dung and woven fiber beaten flat by hands, roofs of bundled reeds and thatch and hide stretched over green wood. The creepers climb it anyway. In the hot season people sleep on the flat tops, drying gourds beside their heads, growing small creeper gardens in the dust of the roof, listening to climbers move in the thatch above them. When the ground swells and snaps a fence, or salt bubbles through a courtyard wall and flakes it, they repack it. When a family moves — and families move a little, constantly, following the creepers, following the grazing, following the shade — the house is left to slump and green over for a while, until another family needs shade and reclaims it. No one lives in a finished house. A house is a habit of a place, maintained by whoever is there now.

The beasts are part of the architecture in a more literal sense. The small hoofed grazers, neither wild nor tame in any clean way, move through the commons and between the houses in slow herds hundreds strong, eating the low spiky mat down to stubble in one district while the next district, a week's walk north where it is a fraction cooler, is knee-deep in regrowth after its dew season. The people who herd them walk with them, not driving so much as allowing — opening a living hedge here, closing it there, letting a herd pass a garden before it eats it. The grazers give milk and meat and hides and dung for walls, and in return they are moved before they strip the ground bare, because stripped ground is not replaced from outside.

At a respectful distance, at a silent distance, the predators follow. Lean, scent-driven, they hunt by the tremor of feet through the ground and by smell on the warm wind. They do not chase. They circle the migration the way the migration moves, taking a straggler, an old one, a young one separated by a thicket. The herders know they are there and do not fight them, nor thank them. It is understood that a herd without followers thins and sickens, and a herd with followers stays moving. Scavengers work the edges of the settlements for what the predators and people leave, and nothing, in the end, is left for long.

Underneath, the burrowers tunnel under streets and houses, their spoil heaps appearing overnight in a courtyard, their tunnels collapsing in the soft salt and being dug again a few paces away. A house slumps a little more on one side and the family props it and knows why. The burrowers are not pests to be cleared. They are residents of the lower story, feeling their way by vibration, and the people above them feel them in return through the soles of their feet. Above, the climbers live in the roofs — small, soft-footed things in the thatch and in the densest creepers, moving at night by touch through thatch that is also a nest, also a garden, also a roof. Their presence is a rustle, a tremor in the reeds, a body pressing briefly against a body sleeping below.

The smaller lives fill the remaining gaps because there are no gaps. Lizards warm themselves along the sunlit tops of walls and drop off soundlessly when a shadow passes. Insects work the dawn flowers in a constant soft hum that is less heard than felt in the air, a vibration in the teeth and in the leaves. Birds nest so close to houses that a roof and a thicket and a family are hard to tell apart at a distance, the chicks silent after dawn, the parents brushing in and out with beaks full of pulp. And the plants themselves are the oldest residents. A thorn-tree with a bulbous trunk is known by the families around it; it has stood through three generations of repacked walls, storing water in its trunk for decades, giving shade, then fruit, then fence, then frame, then firewood when it dies. A rosetted succulent the size of a barrel sits shoulder to shoulder with its kin along a path, its leaves tipped with soft spines, holding its moisture for months. The creepers are the restless ones, climbing anything that stands still long enough — a wall, a roof, a dead tree, each other — until house and plant must be pruned apart by hand, or left together until the family moves.

Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced. Salt is eaten in pinches, laid in causeways, packed in walls, carried in bundles as money, and feared as poison by children who are taught exactly how much a body can hold. A gourd is drunk, then dried on a roof, then woven, then burned. A beast is meat, and hide, and dung, and a herd that must be moved. A dead plant is firewood for the cool hours, used briefly and without ceremony. When a person dies — and people do die, quietly, in the heat or in sleep on a roof — the house does not become a tomb. The family moves a little, the creepers take the doorway, the neighbors feel the absence through the shared wall for a few days as a stillness, and then a new family comes to reclaim the shade. The ground will take what it can, push up what it budges, and the settlement does not argue.

By midday the work stops. The heat shimmers over the salt pans, especially in the west where the plain is whiter and sparser and the settlements are strung thinner along the causeways. People rest in the deepest shade they can find, pressed against walls, against each other, against the cool side of a succulent, conserving themselves. The herds rest. The predators rest at their distance. The dust devil crosses at noon, a thin pillar spinning a mile without a whisper, and no one looks up for long. In the east the thickets are darker and the houses are almost lost in green, and in the north the day's work lasts longer because it is a fraction cooler, and this is all one place, large enough to have its own weathers threaded through it.

In the evening the light goes amber and long, and the shadows between plants and houses lengthen and merge, and the people rise again for the second working — repacking, rethatching, cutting back a creeper, walking to a neighbor's wall to press a hand against it and listen for the stirring on the other side. Then properly dark comes, under ordinary stars, with no lamp burning, and they sleep by touch and smell and vibration, the sound of a herd passing in the street reduced to a soft percussion on dust, muffled and endless, like rain on a distant roof, except it never builds and never breaks.

It is never still. It is only silent, and slow, and everywhere at once — flowers opening and closing, salt growing through a floor, a wall slumping, a caravan coming in, a predator circling, prey listening through their feet, a child learning not to speak until dawn. The city is a field, the field is a desert, the desert is a construction site that has been going on for as long as anyone can remember. And the things that live there are not on top of it. They are of it, layer by layer, still coming into being.
