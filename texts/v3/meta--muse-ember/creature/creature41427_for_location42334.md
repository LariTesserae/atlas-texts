Nothing here lives in the way a lowland would recognize living. It does not run, or call, or migrate through water, or build a nest that will be there tomorrow. To live on the High Shelf is first to agree to be buried, and to wait for dust to fall in the right way.

The permanent residents are the crusts.

You find them not as fields but as decisions made in small shelter. In the lee of a basalt stone, where a dust wave has laid a centimeter of fine ash and stopped, a mat will grow. Dark olive, almost black, a few centimeters thick, no larger than a hand, sometimes red-brown with iron, sometimes pale and lichen-like. It is filaments interwoven and tiny stone-like leaves that never open, because there is nothing to open for. At night, in the long dry cold, it shuts down entirely. In the high white light it photosynthesizes. For the brief hour at dawn, when a fringe of frozen carbon dioxide forms on the rocks and then passes straight to vapor without melting, it wakes and seems to drink from the air alone.

It grows millimeters a year. A mat found whole is a century of quiet in that hollow. It can be buried by a dust avalanche — a slow silent slide of powder pouring through a dry channel for hours — and wait beneath a fresh skin of ash, and resume. Walk the plain for an hour and you may see three. Each one is older than anything human that will ever cross here. Each one is less an individual plant than a place that has learned to stay alive by not needing much: light, grit, and the air's thinning frost for an hour.

What lives on the crusts is almost nothing. That is the point. Nothing eats them quickly. They shed organics into the dust a grain at a time, and the dust carries those grains elsewhere, kilometers away, when the thermals boil up over the salt pans in clear columns.

The grazer comes for that.

It is low and flat and blind, no larger than a coin, pressed so close to the ash that you are more likely to find its trail than the animal itself: a shallow, braided groove in the fresh dust, and then nothing. After a storm has laid down new material, it moves — slowly, only then — filtering the mineral air through a frill and living on the organics trapped in the grit. It does not flee. There is nothing here that needs to run, and the grazer does not know how. When the dust settles again it flattens itself, vanishes, and waits under the next skin. Its whole geography is a line of disturbed ash a few meters long, rewritten each time the ground breathes.

Under the white salt pans, where the ground has cracked into polygonal plates, there are burrowers. They make tunnels in salt, and the tunnels collapse silently behind them. They are never in a home, only in the act of leaving one. A pan that looks undisturbed from above may be hollowed and re-hollowed below by pressure and crystal, by an animal that moves forward because stillness would mean being sealed in. You will never see one. You will see, perhaps, a plate that has sunk a fraction too evenly, and know something has passed without sound.

And above, not touching, is the soarer.

It is seen perhaps once a day if you watch the updrafts: long-winged, hollow-boned, riding the clear columns over the pans for weeks at a time. It never lands on the Shelf. It feeds on dust creatures it cannot see from that height — the spores and fragments and air-borne organics that the crusts release and the thermals lift — and its shadow, when it crosses the plain, may be the only moving shadow on a landscape that otherwise casts sharp, ordinary, unmoving ones. It is not a resident in the way the mat is. It is a visitor who has learned to live by never committing to the ground that rearranges itself beneath it. Where it nests, where it breeds, is far below the rim, in a world with weather. Here it only feeds, and the feeding leaves no trace but a brief darkening of the ash.

That is the whole fauna, most days. A mat waiting for dawn. A coin-flat filter waiting for a storm. A tunnel collapsing behind a burrower no one sees. A wing in a thermal. It is sparse to the point of rumor because the turbulence is constant and the food is only what the turbulence drops.

Then there are the built things, scattered so sparsely that a lifetime might find two or three, and perhaps a dozen exist on the whole continent, most of them buried at any given time.

They are not inhabited, and yet they behave as if they are trying to live here in the only way the Shelf allows: by enduring rearrangement. One is a black glass spindle, eighty meters high, in the middle of a plain, with no door and no window and no shadow that falls correctly. The ground around it is always a few degrees colder. Dust settles on its surface in spirals that never complete — as if something were continuously unmaking the pattern, repairing the disturbance slowly, by fields that will not hold still for instruments. Another is a line of faintly luminous stakes, four kilometers apart, running straight across the Shelf as far as you can track them: a survey, a fence, a boundary, a warning, whose function does not survive on the surface, only its spacing. Another is a flattened disc a kilometer across, where the ash is arranged in concentric rings and the air above it shivers though there is no heat. Another, uncovered because a dust wave took the ground away, was a hollow tower whose interior was larger than its exterior and whose walls were warm in winter and cold in summer, reversing with the seasons.

The ground's slow convection exhumes them and re-buries them over centuries. A dust avalanche striking one does not accumulate; it is deflected. A beacon stake half-covered means a dust wave passed within living memory. A spindle with fresh salt at its base means the churn found it, tried to take it, and failed. They do not smoke, they do not broadcast, they do not break. Whoever placed them understood the ancient uplift and the endless turning-over of hot and cold stone, and placed these few small anchors to watch, or to hold, or to measure a turbulence that has been running since before there was language for it.

The only other living things are the ones who do not live here. At the rim, in stations in the vast soft amphitheatres where the Shelf finally falls three thousand meters in exhausted steps, people wait in thicker air, then come up to cross in the thin light where the sky thins toward black at noon and the horizon curves. Their breathing sounds enormous inside a suit or a hood, and then is damped by pumice and ash. They pass through. They follow, perhaps, that line of luminous stakes for a while, walk a day to stand in the cold halo of the spindle, measure a rise that was a hollow last year. No one stays. The Shelf does not support permanence for anything larger than a crust.

Put together, it is not a community. It is a single slow system seen from different heights and speeds: the mat persisting for centuries in a lee; the burrower persisting by moving; the grazer persisting by waiting under ash; the soarer persisting by never landing; the black glass and the disc and the tower persisting by refusing to be buried. All of them are doing the same thing in different materials — taking the dust that the ground is always giving up and taking back, and turning it, grain by grain, into a reason to continue.

Stand in the middle of it at noon. The plain is empty. The hills are broad swells, worn to bone. Dust is moving somewhere, always, in silent waves. The crusts are waiting for the next fall of grit. The tower a day's walk north is standing colder than the air around it, doing its unknown work. The silence is not an absence of life. It is the sound of everything here agreeing not to resonate: stone, air, and the few living things that have learned, on an old uplift that finished eroding long ago and has never stopped moving, how to live by being rearranged.
