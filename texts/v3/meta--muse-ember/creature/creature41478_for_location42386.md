First, they are heard, not seen.

The chorus rises from the central basins so completely that it fills the Hollow the way water fills a bowl — hundreds of frogs calling from the shallows, from the woven perches that stick up out of the pools specifically for them, from the brick rims and the wet ridges between. It is not a steady song but a pulsing, percussive roar, swelling and breaking and swelling again, and under its weight the other sounds of the place become small. The drip from the roof, the rustle of reeds overhead, your own footfall on the hollow-sounding floor — all of it has to push through the frog-noise to be heard.

Below the calling adults, the basins are dark with tadpoles. They move not as individuals so much as weather, drifting clouds turning slowly in the clear shallow water, nosing at the algae film that greens the pool edges. In a few weeks some of them will be frogs themselves, climbing out by the little brick steps the builders left for small bodies in deep water, finding a ridge to sit on and join the noise. For now the toads do that work on the dry land.

They are the still points in all this motion. A toad sits on a knee-high walking ridge, perfectly camouflaged against the polished clay, and does not move when the chorus swells around it. Only when your shadow crosses it does it remember its other talent: a sudden, surprisingly fast hop, a few body-lengths away, and stillness again. Lizards do something similar on the drier ridges, flattening themselves in the pale ribbons of light that come through the thinned reeds, basking in a light too weak for plants but warm enough for a blood that likes to move.

Around and under and above them is the layer that never stops. Midges and moths work the light slits, outing and fluttering in the grey. Spiders have strung their webs across corners and channel mouths where the midges must pass, and they sit in the centre of those webs with a patience the frogs never need. Beetles and woodlice run the damp timber and the bases of walls. Slugs and snails work the weeping walls themselves, slow as a hand feeling its way, scraping the green film clean as they go.

It helps to think of the walls not as walls to them, but as a landscape that is slowly being eaten. Inside the straw-flecked bricks, where a finger left its mark in the clay a generation ago, beetle larvae are mining the straw, tunnel by tunnel, in the dark between one brick and the next. In the clay pillars that hold up the roof, termites and borers are hollowing out their own quiet architecture. They do not hurry. The house leans a fraction where the ground gave, the roof has sagged to accommodate it, and the insects go on consuming the place that sheltered them, as steadily as the water consumes its channels. The Hollow persists because its inhabitants are both living in it and unmaking it, at the same slow pace.

Above that eating, under the eaves, the swallows have made their own claim. Their mud nests are pressed against the pillars, built from the same wet clay that makes the floor, and they come and go in the dim light with a chatter that is almost drowned, for a moment, by the frogs. Higher still, in the roof voids where the light never reaches at all, the bats hang upside down all day, folded into the niches bored into the walls at bat height. At dusk they pour out in a flickering stream, out through the slits and into the misted water-land beyond, to hunt the moths that have been out all day. By day they are almost invisible. The Hollow has a ceiling full of sleeping mouths.

On the ground, along the wall bases where the clay is always coldest and dampest, mice and rats slip. You rarely see more than a movement, a whisker, a tail disappearing into a crack that was made for water and has been widened for them. They are the Hollow’s night-shift, tracing the perimeter in the small hours while the frog chorus goes on without them, cleaning up the detritus and the dropped insects and whatever the pools leave behind.

And once in a while, the whole system makes room for a predator. A small water snake crosses a basin, low and sinuous, and the frogs’ chorus breaks around its passage like a wave around a stone. Calling stops, tadpole clouds scatter, toads freeze on the ridges. Then, after it has gone, the roar reforms, as if nothing had happened, though everything has just been reminded of the order of things: frogs eat insects, bats eat moths, spiders take midges, lizards take beetles, snakes take frogs. It is a complete little world, and it runs, oddly, on almost no green.

The green that is there is itself an inhabitant, and a hesitant one. Moss cushions the joints of the bricks. Algae films the wettest rims and the edges of the pools, a dull green skin that the tadpoles graze and the slugs scrape. In the one deep basin, where the water stays longest, a thin stand of reeds and cattails has rooted itself not in earth but in the silt that has collected against the clay — pale, sparse, and persistent. A single creeper climbs beside a drainpipe from a large pot, its few leaves dusted grey. That is all. Everything else that lives here feeds on algae, on detritus washed in from the roof and the channels, on the ash and shell grit and dung that were trodden into the floor in the first place, and on the building itself.

The few humans who live in the Hollow are, by comparison, occasional giants.

They do not fill the central space. They move slowly through it, distant presences in a place that is mostly empty, and their footsteps are the loudest human thing in it — loud enough to make toads hop and surface tension break. They mend. When a wall cracks and water finds it and moss follows, a human kneels and presses fresh clay into the seam with a hand, leaving another finger-mark, another layer, another repair smeared over the last. They adjust a reed siphon between basins, clear a runnel, lift a basket, set down a pot. They are not farmers here, not owners in any noisy sense. They are maintainers of a counterfeit wetland, people who built nesting niches and swallow ledges and frog perches into the walls and then stepped back and let the animals accept the imitation so completely that it functions as the real thing.

To the frogs, they are weather. To the bats, a passing shadow. To the larvae in the bricks, perhaps, a distant tremor that means a new layer of clay has arrived overhead, a fresh season of food.

So the population is not one population but many, layered by height and dampness and light. At the bottom, in the water, life at its most numerous and most vulnerable. On the ridges, the still hunters. On the walls, the slow grazers. In the walls, the hidden eaters. Under the roof, the fliers. On the floor, the runners. And threading through all of it, barely touching it, the handful of humans whose work is to keep the whole handmade cycle turning: rain shed inward, groundwater seeping up, basins seeping down and returning as damp at the wall bases, the Hollow drinking its own water and sweating it back out again.

If you stand in the middle, in the long reaches of bare ridged floor between the pools, you can feel alone in a building full of life. The frogs are calling from the far basin as clearly as from your feet. Something is eating a pillar somewhere out of sight. A bat is breathing upside down above you. A snail is making its slow progress down a weeping seam. The reeds are dripping. And all of them — the loud, the still, the hidden, the green — are holding the place together by the simple, steady act of living off it, until the Hollow and its inhabitants are no longer two separate things, but one wet, hollow, made world, quietly consuming itself and quietly being remade.
