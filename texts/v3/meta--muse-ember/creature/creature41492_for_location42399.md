The first thing that lives here is under your feet.

You step off the scree, through the sideways gap in the thicket wall, and the ground does not hold you the way ground should. It gives. It springs back. It is an inch, then three inches thick of living felt — roots and rhizomes interlaced, moss over moss, fallen leaf half-spent and kept from rotting by the cold, all of it saturated and beading. Pressed into it with your boot you can feel the mineral soil and crushed rock below, but almost never see it. The mat has covered it like a skin.

And the mat is crowded, down at a scale you could miss if you were looking for houses.

White fungal threads run through it, finer than hair, stitching one root to another. Where a root is cut or bruised the threads thicken and pale it over. In the black, clear threads of spring water where they well up between the domes, a film of algae and bacteria makes the still surface look faintly filmed, and in that film, turning in place, are protozoa too small to see except as a shimmer when the light catches them. In the damp litter, springtails — pale, blind, salt-of-the-earth small — jump away from your footprint in a scatter you feel more than hear. A cold worm, no longer than the nail of your thumb, works a tunnel through the felt, eating its way and leaving the air pockets that keep the roots from drowning. Mites the size of pinheads walk the undersides of leaves. None of them are many, and none of them are fast, but together they are the first population: the ones who make the felt a felt and not just a pile of dead things.

Stand still long enough and the felt begins to move in other ways. A stem overhead ticks as it contracts in the cold. A root probes a crack and, while you watch, thickens a fraction. A drift of snow held on a roof-leaf sheers off in a hissing sheet. Everywhere the city is in motion the way a glacier is in motion — too slow to call activity if you expect a town to hustle, too constant to call stillness if you stay long enough to learn its pace.

Above the mat, the houses are alive in the same unspectacular way.

A bulb house is not a house that looks like a plant. It *is* a plant: a root-swollen cushion grown up and over until its crown meets its neighbor’s crown. Its wall yields a degree or two when you lean against it, and on the inside the bark is polished smooth by shoulders and hands that have pressed there for years. The door is a flap of tough leaf that rolls inward and unrolls behind you, self-closing. The window is a membrane stretched between stems, amber when the sap in it freezes, rippling in the draft. The chimney is a hollow reed, its top blackened not by soot but by cold.

You can see its age in cross-section if you look close at a worn place: dead stems frozen solid in the middle of the living wall, a scaffold of previous growth overgrown and strengthened by new growth weaving through it. That is the elder inhabitant of every dome. Nothing is discarded in this cold. Dead plant matter does not rot quickly here; it freezes, it persists, it becomes foundation. A house is therefore layered on its own husks — a lineage of growth, grafted root to root, sharing nutrients underground so that a wounded dome can be fed by its neighbors. To cut a wall is to make the wall die back to the cut, and the chamber lose its insulation. Even the plants understand that they cannot live alone. Isolation is death on the wind off the ridge.

Next to the domes stand other kinds of life, other kinds of persons if you let the word stretch.

The longhouses are built of culms grown to the thickness of a man’s thigh, hollow, stacked two or three high, joined by root ladders that are themselves still growing under your hand. They breathe more freely than the domes — they are the wind-catching, airy blocks, reed all the way down, channeling the cold over the ridge. The reservoir plants are great distended bases, water-filled, slick and algae-pale, swelling and shrinking with the melt. The granary husks are large seed-pods with thick walls, cool inside, frozen at the edges, holding dried fruit and pale root stores. And the blocks you learn to read by texture, not sign: a quarter of dense cushion shrubs, solid, insulated, holding heat; a quarter of tall hollow reeds, vertical and open to the draft; a quarter of creeping ferns and mosses where the ground stays marshy and filtered.

Each has its own interior climate, its own smell — crushed stem, wet moss, cold earth, frozen sap — its own particular way of creaking when the wind comes over the shelf.

It is only after you have been in a lane a while, brushing the arching stems with your hat, listening to water drip rather than run, that you notice the other animals. You notice them because they notice you first, and because they move quickly, as if aware that their rarity is a kind of exposure.

A bird flickers from one canopy layer to another and is gone. Pale, almost white, grouse-like, silent. In a place where sound is swallowed by wet vegetation and the air hangs with breath, it has no need to sing, and perhaps no throat that would carry. It does not come to the ground — the ground is too wet, too cold, too busy being foundation — it travels the roofs, that springy turf of moss and dwarf grass where you can stand and see the whole oval of the city at once.

Below it, clinging motionless to a stem for as long as you watch, is a single large insect, leaf-green and leaf-shaped, so perfectly leaf that the only sign it is alive is that it is not bent by the wind as the leaf beside it is. One stem may be its whole world. It does not pollinate much, nothing here does, reliably. Pollination here is mostly self-grafting, root to root.

In the spring channels, mirror-still, a salamander hangs in the current. Translucent, the length of your thumb, its gills feathery and barely moving. It lives in those black threads all year, growing at the cold’s pace. And down in the leaf litter a slug-like grazer, no longer than your finger, scrapes algae from a wall, leaving behind a clean pale track as broad as a bootlace — the only pruning anyone does down here, and far too little of it.

They are too few to prune the city. They are too few to be anything but witnesses to it.

The tenders are witnesses of another kind. You may walk an entire lane and see none of them, and that is not because they are hiding. It is because there are only a handful, and they spend their days in the upper chambers, where the packed biomass respires and the walls are warmer by a degree or two than the air outside — small, collective heat, enough to keep the membranes from freezing solid at night.

They do not look separate from the place at first. Their clothing is leaf and bark fiber woven tight, always damp, never dried, because nothing here can afford to dry. Their skin stays wet with condensation, their hair matted by the perpetual post-rain air. Their eyes have learned the flat grey-green twilight that passes for noon; they are large and unhurried. Their hands are never empty — bending a stem back and holding it with a living tie until it holds itself, thinning a lane where the overhead arches have met and sealed it into a tunnel and then a wall, tying, pressing, mouthing a graft closed. They have no tools of metal or glass or anything cut. The mouth is a tool here: teeth and tongue soften a tendril, saliva seals it, lips press sap into a wound until the plant takes. Shoulders are tools: the insides of the domes are polished by them.

They are small, in the way things that must turn sideways through the thicket wall and stoop under lane arches learn to be small over generations. They move slowly, like the plants. Cold slows everything, including them. A life here might be long, tied to a graft that outlives the hands that made it.

They live mostly alone, one or two to a bulb house, a few together in the upper levels of a longhouse where the root ladders still grow. There are no lights at night and no fire ever; sleep is in the green-dark interior, pressed close to a living wall, listening to it creak and tick and respire, your own breath condensing like the plants’ collective exhale. They eat what the granary husks store — dried fruit, bitter roots, the algae mats skimmed from the quiet channels — and what the dwarf grass turf and reservoir plants offer in season. Waste is not removed; it falls as litter to the mat and feeds the felt.

They communicate, when they do, by touch more than by voice. Sound carries poorly in the packed wet air; your own breathing seems too loud. A hand pressed to a shared wall can feel a neighbor dome’s slow pulse through the root connection. A foot on the root mat can feel a vibration three domes away. On the roof turf, where the whole oval can be seen at once and the sky is less of a rumor, they sometimes meet — not often, and not for long, because a lane left alone closes, and a vent reed blocked will sour a chamber and thin its membrane window, and the mat that is foundation will shrink and crack if it ever dries, even in the cold.

No one here can expand. Beyond the thicket wall the wind is unbroken and the rock is too new and too steep, and young shrubs planted out there lose their grafts in a single winter. So the city grows only inward and upward, denser and denser, layer on leaf-layer. The tenders’ work is therefore not building, as a city-builder would understand it, but maintaining a boundary: keeping lanes open, keeping water moving through woven culverts of hollow reed, keeping every surface occupied and then occupied again above it, as you saw from the ridge.

When a tender dies — and in this cold a body does not rot quickly either — it is not carried out through the gap. It is laid into the mat, or left in the chamber it warmed, and the felt takes it as it takes a fallen leaf. It freezes, it persists, it becomes foundation for the next growth weaving through. That is the older city you see in cross-section, and it happens to people as well as stems.

At dusk, which here is never quite dark and never quite light, leaves turn almost imperceptibly toward what light there is. Stems tick. A drop releases from overhead onto a leaf below and rings, very faintly, like a distant bell. You can hear water everywhere and see it almost nowhere deep — shallow channels, floating mats of tiny leaves, icicles that form on eaves of leaves where the wind catches them and melt an hour later into steady drip onto the mat below.

From the ridge, on your way back out through the sideways gap, all of that is invisible. The green mass gives no sign of lanes and domes and reservoirs inside it, only its too-regular, too-packed density filling its small shelf from cliff to cliff, mist rising from it, frost on its windward rim. Only those who have descended know that the forest has not been built in. That the town is the vegetation, and the vegetation is inhabited — by fungi and water and worms in the felt underfoot, by a few quick rare animals in the canopy and channels, by grafted houses that breathe and remember, and by a handful of damp-clothed tenders in the warm upper chambers, bending and tying and mouthing the city closed again, day after dim day, keeping it alive by keeping it wet, and connected, and dense.
