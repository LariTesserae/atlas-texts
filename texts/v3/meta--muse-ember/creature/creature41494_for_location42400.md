The first thing that lives here lives everywhere, and at first you do not see it as a thing at all.

It is the mat. A cool, damp carpet pressed
so tight across the whole plateau that there is no place to stand that is not standing on something alive. Dwarf shrub shoulder to shoulder with lichen, stalk-grass with moss, root with root, until the distinction blurs. From the ground it looks like a green-grey sea. From a tower it looks like nothing has changed for miles in any direction. It breathes in the fog cycle, swelling a little in the morning when the condensation comes in thick as milk, contracting in the afternoon sun. At night, when the light is still ordinary and the stars are sharp, it ticks and sighs as it grows, and the sound carries for miles because there is nothing to break it.

It is old. Older than the wards, older than the factories, older than the words for ancient. Near the root-heart of the plateau it grows slow and massive and grey, patient as geology, as if it had finished becoming and were only remembering. At the edges of new districts it grows fast and bright and artificial, fresh-planted and unashamed of it. You can walk from one age to the other and the only sign is a change in the color of the mat under your boots.

Within the mat, and partly of it, are the larger, shaped lives.

There are the pipe-trees, hollow and tuned by the wind, which sing when the wind roars across the flat. There are the chimney-plants, tall and forgiving, which the foundries are hollowed from, exhaling vapor into the cold air in a steady, industrial breath. There are the silica weeds, whose leaves can be harvested as glass, transparent and cool and still faintly green at the edge. There are the moss-peat beds that are cut and milled into brick, there are the broad leaves cupped like shallow bowls, each holding a lens of condensation for weeks. There are the stalk-walls and leaf-roofs and woven-root warehouses, which are not metaphors. A house here is a hollowed stem grafted in a mould and then left to finish growing on its own. Some lean. Some are still slowly arching toward the light years after they were first occupied. Some have merged at the corners with the neighboring house, so that a whole terrace is one organism with many doors, and the sap numbers of the ward — painted on the sides of the stalks — fade and must be repainted as the stalk thickens.

The avenues are lives too. Two facing rows of living posts with a pressed-moss lane between them. An unused avenue keeps growing longer, week by week, stretching its formal lines out into the wild mat until it ends abruptly where geometry gives up. A used avenue shrinks. The distance between the Fourth Ward and the Cistern Ring is not a fixed measurement. It depends on how many feet and wheels have pressed it lately. A factory that dies does not stay dead. It splits open and seeds its own replacement, rows of young chimney-plants pushing up through the old floor already aligned in the old grid, already hardening into sheds. A house that fruits drops pods in the lane that root, and in a season become another house, smaller, facing the wrong way. Nothing here stays finished, because everything that lives here is still finishing itself.

And then, far less numerous than any of this, moving slowly across it, are the people who tend both.

They are sparse to the point that you can walk six hours across the mat and see no one. The wards are isolated nodes — a low cluster of stalk-houses folded into a shallow depression, a cistern ring a mile distant, a smelter district with its dark stalks and its chimney-plants — with miles of breathing green between them. The people live as the mat allows: in the terraces that are half house and half plant, in damp clothes that never quite dry, their breath fogging even at midday, their skin always a little damp from the air that wrings itself out every night.

They do not build so much as graft, cut back, tie down, and coax. A roof that is cut regrows. A wall that is left untended will harden into a tool; a machine that is left untended will root. Their work is the keeping of that boundary, day after day, shift after shift: tending the steam engines that huff and sigh in a rhythm you feel in your molars, mending the belts that carry seed-pods between plant towers to be planted as the next quarter, tracing the root-cords that carry electricity humming faintly where they pass through damp stalks, watching the fermentation vats bubble warm in the cool air, smelling of yeast and wet iron and crushed lichen.

They work by the second sound. Here sound sometimes arrives before its cause — the slap of a belt across the mat a second before the piston catches the light, a tower echoing a tool not yet struck. The tenders do not remark on it. They have learned to wait for the true one, the one that comes after, when the vibration has traveled through the mat and through the stalks and through their own boots. They learn to hear growth at night, the cracking and creaking and settling as the whole surface expands in the cold. They learn to walk on plant, not ground, and to read the sap numbers, and to go out at dawn with hoses and buckets to harvest the night's condensation from the fields and the cold sides of boilers and the undersides of leaves, because water does not flow here. It gathers. It hangs. It is drunk where it stands.

They eat what the mat and the factory make together, in the slow exchange that has replaced predator and prey: the mat feeds the factory with stalk and peat and silica and lichen; the factory feeds the mat back with heat and ash and vibration. The people eat and drink from the fermentations, take the glass-leaves when they are ready, cut the moss-peat when it is ready, and plant what the belts bring them. They are feeder and fed as well, and they know it. An avenue that shrinks too much will be replanted. A district that ages too fast will be left to grow grey and slow near the old root-heart, where the oldest tenders live a pace that looks, from the outside, like stillness.

There is almost nothing else, and what there is is half plant.

High above the wards, riding the wind between them, are the things taken for birds: seed-pods with membranous wings. They travel, they burst when they land, and in bursting they become the mat again a little further from where they started. In the warm nooks of the smelters, where the cold air never quite reaches, live small cold-blooded beetles that feed on rust and yeast. That is the extent of the animal life the plateau will tolerate. There is nowhere in a flat, watched mat for an animal to hide, and almost nothing for an animal to eat that is not already being eaten by the city. The beetles tolerate the city because the city makes rust and yeast for them. The seed-pods tolerate the wind because the wind carries them to new ground.

And beneath all of it, in the damp and the warm vats and the cupped leaves, are the smallest inhabitants: the yeasts that bubble, the lichens that are crushed and crushed again, the root-tips pressing down to the bedrock through soil that has always looked like this. They are tended, too, carried from ward to ward in sealed stems, kept warm, kept fed. A machine left untended will root, a plant left untended will harden into a tool, and a culture left untended will simply stop, and then the whole delicate layering — biological pace over industrial pace over geological pace — will lose one of its threads.

So the population of this place is not a crowd. It is a surface. One vast, living skin that breathes and thickens and ticks in the night; within it, taller shaped plants that house and power and sing; within those, a few hundred — a few thousand, spread over miles — of damp, patient people who graft and harvest and wait for the second sound; and at the margins, in the heat and in the wind, the beetles and the winged seeds that belong to both worlds at once.

They do not conquer the plateau. They do not finish it. A house will lean, an avenue will wander, a dead factory will seed its children in a perfect grid through its own floor, and the mat will swell at dawn and shrink at noon and grow an inch thicker with a soft pop at dusk, while the light remains completely ordinary and the shadows lengthen normally to the horizon.

He has already become what he will be. He is still becoming it.
