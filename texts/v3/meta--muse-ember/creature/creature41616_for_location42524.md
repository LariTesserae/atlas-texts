What lives here does not at first announce itself as living at all.

If you stand on the rim and let your eyes adjust to the smallness of it — the bowl, the fresh scarps, the town built inward upon itself — the first residents you pick out are the grey-green things low to the ground. They look like lichen that has learned to build. Low rosettes in the dust-felt, creeping mats in the cracks, pale ferns that never quite uncurl. They are almost colourless in the dim light, and if you watch them they do not appear to move. Only later, when you have walked a plaza and turned back, do you notice that the edge of a mat is not where it was, that a doorway you stepped through an hour ago is a finger's width narrower, that the faint trail in the dust behind a rosette has lengthened by a hand's breadth in the time you were not looking.

These are the primary inhabitants. Everything else is a slower, rarer variation on them.

A mat is not a plant in any familiar sense. It has no leaf to speak of, no root that goes down looking for water — there is no water to find. It lives in the top skin of the dust and spreads sideways, a woven felt of fine threads that drink the cool air directly. The air here is laden: mineral dust, salt, the mica-fine haze that never clears. The mat filters it. It draws salts out of the air and out of the stone beneath it, and in return it lays down stone of its own. Not a shell, not a fruit. A wall.

It grows in rings, as sediment does, but deliberately. A thread puts down a thin secretion, hardens, the next thread builds upon it. Over a season a doorway can be sealed. Over a year a gallery wall can thicken by another strata. Over a century a mat that began in a crack on the plaza can be the tower — hollow, terraced, layered, its interior still the original crack made vertical. The buildings are not structures the mats live in. They are what the mats become when they have lived long enough in one place.

Not all mats are masons. That is the first differentiation, and it only becomes clear after you have seen the town from above and walked it at ground level both. In one street the wall is thickening, new rings pale and clean, almost white where the stone split fresh. In the next street a wall is foaming at its base, softening, slumping back into dust-felt without a sound. The same kind of organism, a different phase. Building and unbuilding proceed side by side, so the city never ages the way other cities do. It is never weathered. It is only ever re-grown, or quietly unmade. A building swells a fraction in the warmest thinning of the grey, and then heaves and splits where a plate beneath it has lifted, and the clean crack is filled again with new rings. There is no ruin here, because ruin would require rot or decay. There is only a mat deciding, in its chemical slowness, to become dust again.

The rosettes are the pioneers of this cycle. They settle first in a fresh crack, where the stone is exposed and unweathered. They are low, radial, grey-green discs no larger than your hand, and they move — not by walking, but by dying at one edge and growing at the other, so their centre migrates across a plaza over years, leaving a faint, felted trail. A rosette does not make a wall. It makes the ground ready for a mat. It softens plate, crumbles it to felt, holds the dust in place so a mat can take hold. Where you see a vast bare plaza with a single rosette in the middle, you are not seeing emptiness. You are seeing the beginning of a street that will take thirty years to reach the far side.

And everywhere, particularly in the depths between buildings where the light is always dusk, there are the stone-ferns. They grow along sill lines and on the north sides of walls, where the cold pools and stays. They are more upright than the mats, more recognizably plant-like, but their fronds are thin plates of mineral, translucent at the tip. They do not photosynthesize in any bright sense; there is no bright sense here. They catch the slight thinning of the grey and use it to precipitate salt. In the coolest hours salt frost blooms out from their edges in white feathers, and for a moment the shaded walls look silvered. Then, when the light thickens again, the feathers collapse back into dust and are reabsorbed. It is their way of breathing, and of talking. A bloom of salt is a chemical message laid down where any other mat, any rosette, will later feed on it.

If the mats are the city, the animals are its slow traffic — not separate from the plants so much as a later elaboration of the same slow, air-drinking, stone-making life.

The wall-crawlers are the only true grazers. You are unlikely to see one unless you happen to be watching a fresh crack. They are flat discs, pale as the dust, with many fine feet along their rim, and they live inside the cracks the plates open as they lift. A plate heaves a hand's breadth, a clean split appears, and within days the crack is occupied. They move along the inner walls, rasping the lichen — the surface film of mats and rosettes — with their feet. They do not eat the wall. They prune it. A crawler's passage can keep a mat from sealing a crack it would otherwise close, leaving a breathing channel through a wall that would otherwise grow shut. They congregate where new stone is exposed, because new stone is richest in salts. For a few weeks after a plate lifts, a crack will be thick with them, discs overlapping, feet ticking infinitely quietly against stone. Then the crack fills with dust-felt, the crawlers disperse, and the mat resumes.

They are prey, but predator and prey here move on a timescale you can miss. The pale walkers are taller than you would expect from their thinness: long legs, skin like stretched leather, grey as the plates, with a torso that hangs between the legs like a suspended sack. They cross plazas at the dimmest light, which is to say, always. Their steps make no sound — the dust-felt swallows it — and their feet are broad and soft, made to step over the small abrupt steps of heaved plates without jarring the surface. A walker can take three days to cross a plaza you could cross in a minute, pausing between buildings for hours at a time, its body still but its skin rippling faintly, listening.

They have no eyes that look outward in our sense. They have patches of paler skin along their spine that register the minute difference between the thinning and thickening of the grey, and they have a nervous feeling for vibration through their feet. The faintest tick of cooling stone, the shift of a plate a street away, the rasp of crawlers in a crack — that is their world. They are hunting crawlers, but not chasing them. A walker will stand over a fresh crack for a week, waiting for crawlers to come up to feed in the light, and then stoop, slow as a closing hinge, and take one. A single meal can sustain it for a month. A walker may take only a dozen crawlers in a year. It does not need more. Its territory is the whole bowl. It will walk the same looped avenues for decades, because the town itself loops back on itself, and it memorizes it by vibration and salt, not by sight.

Walkers are solitary, except when two meet — which may happen once in several years, when their routes cross in a plaza at the same dim hour. They do not call. They touch. A leg laid against a leg, a slow exchange of salt along the skin, and they part again. The exchange is what passes for reproduction. The walker does not give birth to a young walker. It buds a small, pale disc from its flank, no larger than a crawler, which falls to the dust-felt and lives there for years feeding on mat-felt before it grows legs and stands. Most such buds do not survive. The bowl is too big, the food too sparse, for a population to rise. What looks like emptiness is a requirement: each walker needs a town’s worth of cracks and crawlers to live, and each mat needs a tower’s worth of space to grow into.

The gliders are rarer still, and you can walk the whole town for a day and see none. They are cape-backed things that live high, on galleries and roof edges open to the dim sky. Their cape is not a wing but a membrane stretched between long forelimbs and hind limbs, translucent and grey. They do not fly. They leap. From one gallery to another across a dry channel, from a tower to a terraced house, and then they ride the cool air down a street, turning in a slow arc, landing on a sill with no sound. They rarely cross the same street twice in a day. When they do land, they settle for a long time, cape folded, and their presence dusts a wall with spores. Pollination here is not flowers. It is a glider carrying a dusting of mat-spore from a building that is thickening to one that is foaming, from a rosette that is migrating to a crack that is fresh. They are the city’s messengers, and their flight is so slow, so deliberate, that a single crossing may be the most dramatic movement you witness in a week.

And beneath all of this — beneath the walkers’ feet, beneath the dust-felt — there is a finer population you will never see directly. In the dust rivers that flow like water made of powder, sinuous and deliberate through the dry channels, there are filter-threads anchored to the banks. They do not swim. They stand in the flow of silt and catch the mica-fine particles, and the slow river carries their spores downstream to new bends, to new fans of silt at junctions, to places where a channel that was straight five years ago has meandered and opened a fresh cutbank. The rivers are not just erosion. They are circulation.

In the dust-felt itself there are threaders: translucent, hair-thin things that move through the felt as if swimming through water, eating and reworking it, never surfacing. They keep the felt soft, keep it from binding. Without them the silence would be harder, the stone would ring. With them everything lands without a tick.

In the deepest cracks, where the light never reaches at all, there are sealed colonies. A crack opens, a crawler enters, a mat follows, and then the crack fills with dust above them. They live in perpetual dusk, feeding on the air that percolates down through the porous stone, secreting rings in the dark until the crack is a hollow tube of new stone far below the plaza. Some of the buildings’ lower galleries are built this way — not from above, but from below, by inhabitants who never see the sky.

None of them make a sound you can hear. Their traffic is chemical and vibrational and tactile. A mat knows a walker has passed because the dust-felt is compressed in a long-legged stride. A crawler knows a plate is about to heave because the salt in the stone changes a fraction before it splits. A glider knows another glider has used a gallery because of the spore-dust left on the sill. It is a town where information travels as slowly as bodies, and bodies travel as slowly as stone.

They live for a very long time, most of them. A mat that is a tower may be three hundred years old, still growing a ring a season, still splitting and regrowing. A walker may live fifty years and cross its plaza perhaps a hundred times. A glider may live thirty and never enter the same street twice. To them, the bowl is not small. It is enormous. The miles of empty plate between buildings are not waste. They are pasture, and watershed, and road, and waiting room. The emptiness is how they fit.

When a mat dies, it does not fall. It foams, softens, and settles back into the felt it came from, and the dust rivers take its salts elsewhere. When a walker dies, it dries where it stands, its skin tightening until it is indistinguishable from a heaved plate, and crawlers rasp it clean over years until nothing is left but a clean break in the stone where it lay. When a glider dies, its cape collapses and is eaten by threaders in the dust. There are no graves here, no bones. Only fresh stone where something used to be growing.

Stand in the centre of a plaza long enough and you may see one of them cross. A crawler in a crack, a rosette shifting its centre by the width of a thumb, a walker pausing between buildings as if listening to something you cannot hear, a glider riding cool air down a street it has not used in weeks. It will not look like life as you know it. It will look like the town changing its mind, very slowly, about where its walls should be.

And then, after a while, it will look like what it is: a whole population, plant and mason and grazer and hunter and messenger, living in a bowl no more than a mile across, under a sky that is forever dim but never dark, moving without sound, building without tools, eating air and making architecture out of themselves, with all the time in the world to do it in — because here, they have it.
