They are not awake all at once. No one with any say in the matter ever is.

The first to move in the bowl are the ones who live in the stone. Ants come out of the cracks in their hundreds, following a highway worn smooth across a doorstep to a plum that fell yesterday. Geckos, who have spent the night flattened against, or under, the warm walls, flick their tails and begin to work their way upward, hunting. Down in the black soil trapped in a root-joint, a centipede trades places with a beetle. In the fig canopy, where the leaves are still holding last night's cold, parrots wake arguing, one voice first, then all of them.

The people wake last, and they wake in the order the shade lets them.

Blue shade is first. It pools ankle-deep in the lanes and makes the bowl feel larger than it is, cooler than the plateau above by ten degrees. On the lower slopes, where the mud-brick houses lean wall to wall and share roofs too, a shutter is unbarred and a woman leans out to shake dust from a blanket. That dust is someone else's late sleep, someone else's breakfast cook-smoke. There is no privacy from sound here; a baby crying two tiers up is a baby crying in your kitchen. The houses are close enough that you learn the coughs, the prayers, the pounding rhythms of your neighbors as well as you learn your own.

Pounding is the clock before the sun. In the cool, in the mist that hangs low and does not rise, woman after woman finds her mortar — stone set into earth by a grandmother — and begins. It is not work so much as announcement: I am up, my grain is ready, my door is open. The rhythm travels down the stepped lanes and children, barefoot, follow it the way they follow any loud steady thing.

Children are everywhere, and they are the first to truly exploit the bowl's one trick. The dew is heavy on every leaf at dawn though the dust below is dry. They go to the fig, to the fruit trees, to the flowering shrubs packed wall to wall, and lick it off, tilting broad waxy leaves so the drops run to their mouths. Larks do the same above them. It is the only free water anyone gets before the shafts. A child can tell you which vine leaf holds the most, which branch is shaded longest, which lizard has claimed a particular warm stone and will not move for a woman pounding grain three feet away.

By the time the sun disc touches the floor — a small, hard circle crawling over the lip — the town is fully populated and already overcrowded in the kind of way that feels intentional. The sun patch only lasts two hours and it moves. Life arranges itself around it like an animal around a warm rock.

The market square, which is the clearing under the fig, is too hot to stand on at noon. Stones there will burn soles. So nothing that can be done in shade is done there, and everything that needs heat is done there, quickly. Clay pots are set to harden. Fruit is laid to dry on roofs. The copper smith, and if the word is not too grand — one man, one hammer, one fire pit, one piece of traded copper he has been coaxing for a month — drags his anvil into the patch for the minutes he needs the extra heat and then drags it back. People take their turn in the sun the way they take turns at the shafts: briefly, and without complaint.

The shafts are the heart of the town and everyone knows their pulse. Three deep holes, sunk by hand-drilled stone and rope into the aquifer below, damp and cold and far down. Water comes up one bucket at a time, hand over hand, and one bucket at a time is carried in covered jars through lanes too narrow for a loaded donkey. The carriers — mostly older girls and women with strong shoulders, who have carried a lifetime and have arms to show for it — do not hurry. Water is not hurried. It is rationed not by a written law but by a memory everyone obeys: how many buckets per household, per garden, per young tree. No one washes in it. No one bathes in it unless they are sick. Sweat is wiped, not rinsed. The bowl gives back transpired water all morning in the mist between the houses, which smells of crushed leaf and warm stone, and the people breathe that almost as much as they drink.

Who lives here, then, is not a population but a set of arrangements, perfected over a hundred generations of never leaving.

There are the fig-keepers. The fig is wider than the clearing and older than any house, its roots lifting slabs, making hollows under the soil where cats sleep and jars of grain are kept cool. Three or four families consider themselves its people. They prune it, they argue over its fruit, they keep the vines from strangling it, they know which limb is hollow and who nests there. When a root cracks a wall, the wall is rebuilt around the root. When a root cracks a jar, the jar is moved. The tree is not sacred in a spoken way; it is simply immovable, like the rim, and everything else has learned to live around it.

There are the goat people — which is to say, almost everyone, in the morning. Goats are tethered everywhere: on roofs, in courtyards, along the low thick wall that runs around the usable floor not for war but to keep them out of the gardens. Bells clank in a constant, rambling rhythm as they shift and strip bark from any root they can reach. Boys lead strings of them up the tiers, stepping over sleeping cats. Goat milk is boiled, made into a hard cheese that keeps in the cool earth, and the animals themselves are the lawnmowers, the pruners, the compost-makers, and the thieves. If you do not want your seedlings eaten to the root, you do not leave them at goat height. Everyone knows this. The goats know it too.

There are the weavers and the potters, working from open fronts so the work can be watched and corrected by passersby. A loom clacks in a house where the walls share walls with a granary full of sealed clay jars sunk into cool earth, defended against rats by cats and against damp by being sealed. The potter works in a pit to keep the clay cool, turning a base by hand, smoothing it with a wet thumb. The cloth is coarse, for heat and dust, and woven from goat hair and whatever fiber the terraces yield. A shirt here lasts because it is mended by the woman who swears while she does it, loudly, so the whole lane hears.

There are the old, who live in the deepest shade and do not move for the sun patch at all. They sit against walls that store heat and give it back slowly, and they do the remembering. They remember when the rain was violent and vanished, when a shaft rope frayed, when a hawk took a chicken and the whole bowl went silent for a full minute. They are low-voiced and economical, because the cicadas are so loud in the still shade that conversation is raised-voice work, and they save their voices. Their skin is cracked and dusty. Their breath is short until they go still, because they have lived their whole lives in thin, high air and the bowl's small weather.

And living among them, not after them, are the others who cannot be separated from any description of who lives here.

Cats pad between houses, dozens of them, unowned and fully owned, sleeping in fig-root hollows by day and patrolling granaries by night against the rats and mice who come out in daylight because there are too many of them to be shy. Dogs live mostly at the rim, barking at nothing, at distance, at the wind bringing a scrap of dry paper or a seed from a plant that does not grow here.

Lizards by the hundred work the warm walls, motionless for hours, then flicking. Ants run their roads across doorsteps and over fallen plums and declare war on each other by morning. Bees and large slow flies work the flowering shrubs whose blossoms touch each other, in a hum so constant you stop hearing it until it stops. Cicadas saw in layers from every tree at once and make the midday shade thunder. Swifts shear screaming around the rim and nest in its cracks, invisible until they pour out. Parrots argue in the fig. At dusk the day birds settle screaming into the trees and something stirs in the stone: bats pour in a thin, rapid stream out of wall cracks to hunt the insect plume that rises over the fig like smoke. Their guano feeds the soil. Their presence is welcomed the way a cat's is.

Twice a day the hawk comes over the lip, very high. It does not land. It passes. And for a minute the whole bowl changes: lizards flatten, birds freeze mid-call, goats bunch, children under the fig stop mid-lick and stare up. Then it is gone and the noise rushes back louder, as if relieved. Nothing here is allowed to lie still long. A fallen leaf is eaten by ants before evening. A dead bird is found by rats by dark. Pollination, predation, scavenging, decay — fast and close and endless. The green that looks so settled is being chewed, eaten, hunted, and rebuilt every hour.

At night the population thins to the ones who own the dark. Crickets, soft wings, goats shifting against their tethers, a distant dog, the occasional crack of cooling stone. The human town goes amber with oil in clay dishes, the lanes lit just enough to find a door by. People sleep on roofs in summer, under vines that cross lanes at head height so you duck under living roofs on your way to bed. The sky is only a small circle far above the rim, intensely blue-black and crowded with stars. The stone is warm, always warm, even high up, and gives its heat back slowly to the cats, to the lizards, to the people lying on it.

You could walk its entire length in five minutes. From the notch you can see all of it, wall to wall, three hundred paces across. But everyone who lives there — the woman at her mortar, the boy with the goats, the girl with the water jar, the old man under the fig, the cat in the root hollow, the ant on the doorstep, the bat leaving the crack, the root going down into the dark where the water is — could spend a lifetime learning its lanes, and would, because there is nowhere else for any of them to be.
