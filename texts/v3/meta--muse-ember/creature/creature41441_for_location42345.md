If you stand still in the bowl long enough, the first thing to live in you is not a plant. It is the ground.

It yields under your boot, then rises back around your ankle, a skin of roots and rhizomes and leaf-mould pressed so tightly together that it has stopped being soil and become an animal's flank. It breathes, minutely, in the warm afternoon — expanding, tightening, making that long creak you feel in your molars — and at night it contracts again, by a hair. All day it is thickening by a hair. All day it is healing the place where your foot pried it open, weeping a little water into the wound and then closing it.

That is the innermost population, and it is the one everything else stands on. In the lowest two feet of the Works there is a separate, darker country of ferns and grasses and moss, so packed that no light worth mentioning gets there except what the bog throws back up. The individuals down there are not showy. They do not flower toward the sky. They hold. They hold the water at the surface, they hold the iron that has rusted down out of the machines, they hold each other upright by sheer lateral pressure. A leaf dies down there and is replaced in the same square inch by morning, not by seed falling from elsewhere but by its neighbor leaning over and deciding to be two. It is a community without migration. It defends itself by being impossible to enter.

Above it, standing stems. Above that, a hanging thicket of lianas. Above that, a canopy, and then a second canopy just beneath the first, so that daylight arrives only as a mottled, green-gold patchwork that never stays in one place because the leaves are always settling, shedding, putting out new ones already folded in right angles. Epiphytes coat every trunk in a felt of small, thirsty mouths. Fruits hang in the understory where flowers ought to be.

They are not flowering for show. There is almost nothing to show them to.

No birds. No insects in any number from which a population could be inferred. On the second day you may see one frog, very still, in a leaf-puddle no larger than a coin, or the single ripple where one salamander moves in a marsh-channel no wider than your hand. These are not residents so much as occasional witnesses the place allows. The frog will stay in that puddle for a very long time, its throat pulsing, domestically unconcerned. The salamander will not hurry. Their presence feels like an event because the Works does not need them. It has subcontracted their work to itself.

It pollinates by wind and by rubbing — stem against stem, leaf against the warm side of a pipe, tendril against tendril until something catches. It moves by seed and by growth, which is slower and more certain. It defends itself by thickness.

In the core of the bowl, where the water never decides to flow, lives the plate-bog. Sheet after sheet of a flat pressed-leaf plant lies overlapping on the surface like tin laid over water, so still that the duckweed between the plates and the algae beneath them do not stir except when the generator turns. From above, the plates catch the sun and double it. From below, the algae keep it. On overcast days the whole bog glows faintly, a sourceless phosphorescence warm on your face, and the plates are lit from underneath as often as from above. That glow is not a light the place makes to see by. It is light it has trapped and not yet finished using.

The plates are a roof for things you will never see directly: a film of green-and-rust life that feeds on the iron seeping out of the low wall, where mineral springs bead and run in slow bright lines down bricks softened to river stone. The stones themselves weep. Rust-orange bacteria and mineral crusts live in those wet lines and extend them, grain by grain, making the wall part wall, part garden, part slow animal licking the bowl clean.

From the mat the inhabitants rise into the built things, because the built things are no longer ruins. They are settled, like houses.

On the boiler, which is half-sunk and domed like a hill, lives boiler-moss, thick and coppery-green, and with it copper vine. The vine has learned the boiler's heat and does not ask for more. It spirals in the slow pattern the boiler's seams once dictated, rooting in perfect rows along the old concrete, following lines where men once laid foundations. If you watch it long enough you understand: the foundations are still instructing it. The shape of industry is in the soil like a remembered posture, and the plants repeat it without meaning to. Stems grow in straight runs and then clip themselves, deliberately, to keep the line. New leaves unfurl creased for a factory that closed before they were born.

Inside the boiler's hollow, water drips in a continuous metallic percussion and lives briefly as steam where a pipe cracked a hundred years ago and now is a hollow stem. That hiss is also a resident. It breathes out through the plant that has taken the pipe for a lung.

The chimney is a hollow trunk now. Its brick has softened to stone, and a creeper has grown up through the flue and out the top, where it ends in a plume of green that is the only part of the Works that gets unmediated sky. The creeper inside is blind and pale for most of its length, palely feeling its way up the curved interior, and only once it surfaces does it bother to color itself.

The inverted shed roof in the east holds rainwater like a great leaf and feeds it, rib by rib, into pipe-grass. The grass runs down the metal in a bright green vein and is perpetually drinking. It is the roof's mouth. Below it, the old conveyor belt is no longer rubber at all. Roots have woven over and under it until it is stronger as plant than as belt, and it now serves as a bridge for roots to cross the marsh-channel without getting their feet wet. They cross and recross, lashing it tighter every season.

In the marsh-channel itself lies the engine block on its side, its cylinders full of reeds. The reeds are iron-eaters. They take the rust that the water loosens from the block, and their leaf-tips blush russet and orange with it, so the dead engine has given them a color they would not otherwise have. The engine has not been broken, exactly. It has been eroded into balance. The reeds hold it in that balance the way the mat holds everything else — by settling on it and refusing to leave.

Wires live here too, though they are no longer electrical in any useful sense. They run through the vines as nerves. On humid afternoons the air prickles before you hear anything, and coppery roots spark almost imperceptibly where they cross a bolt or a tendon of liana. The lianas have grown around the bolts, have incorporated them, and now the spark is their way of noticing a change in pressure or damp. The Works feels that prickle through the mat the way you feel it on the back of your neck.

And under everything, under the root-mat where no eye is meant to go, the generator still turns.

It is the slowest inhabitant and the most central. A flywheel completing a revolution in a minute, in five minutes — so gradual you would swear it is not turning at all — producing a deep groan, a clunk, a shudder that travels up through wet ground and out through the soles of your feet. The roots have grown around its housing and feed on the minute heat it makes. They do not speed it up. They do not need it to hurry. It is a heart beating slowly enough that you have to believe in it to see it move, and the whole bowl is arranged around that belief. Water stands a finger's breadth higher after a week of condensation because the flywheel has nudged the mat. A root thickens. A fruit ripens and falls and pings, quietly lit, like a rivet dropping into a drum.

The fruits are another sort of life: small, hard, luminous bodies produced in the understory and dropped from a foot up onto metal — into the boiler, into the inverted roofs, into the engine cylinders — where they make the polyrhythm this place is famous for hearing, drip and ping and clatter never in time with each other, never stopping. They are not eaten. There is hardly anything here to eat them. They fall, they ring, they add their iron and sugar to the mat, and a new stem comes up where they land, in a row.

Above all this, condensation runs off leaf undersides in an irregular percussion that finds your shoulders if you stand still and runs, warm, down your back. A vine, finding your boot immobile, will put out a tendril and test the lace. A film of algae will begin on your sleeve. This is not aggression. It is incorporation. Stillness is a resource here. Anything still for long enough is taken to be part of the structure and is furnished accordingly.

That is what lives in the Weeping Works: not a garden gone wild, not a factory reclaimed, but a finished, saturated world in which plant has taken the work of animal, machine has taken the shape of animal, and water has taken the work of both. It is too complete to want anything from you. It holds all its water and all its green and all its slow iron inside a wall you could see across in a throw, and yet crossing it is hours of pushing and ducking and climbing, because inside there is no empty space left for a stranger to stand in.

It will be like this for a century more before anything shifts in a way a human could mark on a map. A leaf dies and is replaced. A root thickens by a hair. The generator turns one more slow revolution. The marsh rises and sinks. The inhabitants do not age in any way you can see. They settle, they repeat, they weep, they hum, they grow in rows, and they intend, patiently, to stay that way.
