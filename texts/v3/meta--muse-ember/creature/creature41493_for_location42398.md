If you crouch long enough, and let your eyes adjust to the green dimness, the first movement is not a single creature. It is a pulse.

Pale, soft, blind — a column of them coming up a packed trail from under the root plate, antennae tapping, tapping, against the mud ahead of them, each one touching the one in front with a brief brush of antennae as it passes. They vanish into a tower. Another pulse comes out, going the other way. They carry pellets larger than their heads, balanced in their mandibles, the pellets still soft, still shaped by the gut they came from.

Only after a while does the pulse separate into individuals. The smaller workers, creamy-white, translucent at the joints, with jaws that click when they set a pellet down. The larger ones, head-heavy, dark-mandibled, who do not carry so much as stand in a doorway and turn their heads toward a vibration. No one announces them. You hear them first, in a warm spell, as a soft ticking deep in the wall — not footsteps, but mandibles and heads being tapped against earth, testing firmness, warning, asking.

They build the way they eat. Chewed earth, digested plant fibre, saliva, faeces — everything is mouth-work. A worker arrives with a pellet, presses it into the older grey layer with its head, turns, nudges it, tastes it with its antennae, and leaves. The wall grows in strata: weathered, fresh, still fingerprint-pocked, though the fingerprints are headprints. Everything is layered like a cake because a life is lived in layers, and what is finished today is food, floor, or foundation tomorrow.

They do not see the town. Darkness is not something they escape; it is the medium they live in. They orient by dampness. A tower is not placed for a view, it is placed for a drip. Walls are cupped, broadened at the top, to catch condensation from the underside of a root. A shaft is opened on the shaded side, closed when the air above dries. In the tallest towers, workers station themselves like living plugs in the chimney, opening and closing with their bodies so the warm air from below — body heat, the slow heat of decomposition under the root plate, the heat held in the low ground — breathes out and in. You can feel it, if you hold your hand close, a faint warm exhalation that stops when a body seals it.

Under the root plate, where it is always darker and always damp, the pulses slow. This is the nursery. Here the workers are attendants more than builders. They keep the mud moist with their own bodies, lick larvae clean, feed them mouth-to-mouth, turn them in the loam so they do not stick. The larvae curl and uncurl, pale grubs with nothing to do but grow and be fed, carried a few millimetres and set down again if the chamber floods or dries. Deeper still, in the warmest pocket where the air is amber-brown with wet soil and mushroom and green sap, is the one who is never seen in the trails. She does not build or carry. Her abdomen pulses slowly with eggs, and workers crowd her, groom her, carry the eggs away to the cool chambers, and bring her digested food in return. The whole town is oriented toward her warmth without ever needing to approach it.

Next to the nursery, in the dark, are the granaries. They do not look like stores. They look like sponges, dark and fibrous and alive, growing on stored, chewed fibre. Fungal gardens. White threads race out overnight along a line of moisture and by morning have stitched two chambers together. The workers chew the garden, feed it, eat the parts the fungus has already digested for them, and carry fresh fibre in on their backs. It is the town's stomach, externalised. What the workers cannot digest, the fungus can. What the fungus produces, the workers can eat. Neither lives apart from the other, and the threads of fungus spun through the mud of the walls are not decoration — they are rebar, and also pantry, and also lung.

Grazing those gardens, moving like weather over the sponge, are mites. You will not see them unless you are looking for a shiver. Eight-legged, almost invisible, they farm the farm — eating stray spores, cleaning the comb, keeping wild moulds from taking over. The workers ignore them, or tolerate them, which in this place amounts to husbandry. When a nursery has too many mites, a beetle larva turns up there, soft-jawed and patient, and hunts them one by one in the dark where the builders themselves are blind but not defenceless.

That is the level of predation inside. Small and patient. A centipede waits motionless in a doorway of a ventilation shaft, antennae laid flat along the wall, legs feeling the vibration of feet that have not yet arrived. A rove beetle patrols the avenues, armoured, slow, turning its head into every blind trial column and hollow that was dug and never claimed, as if checking empty rooms. When the beetle passes, the pulse thins around it and reforms behind it.

At night the town changes shift, and the builders mostly stop. Slugs come out, slow and silver-trailed, and cross the towers, eating the architecture as they go. A slug's trail is a temporary road that erases a wall a millimetre at a time and replaces it with slime and gut. In the morning the workers will plaster over the silver line with a fresh redder stratum. Springtails, tiny and forked, gather in the rain-pans where water has pooled to a mirror and not yet soaked away. Touch the pan and they spring, all at once, and land and groom themselves and spring again, cleaning rot and fungal spores from the surface film. In that same wet film, invisible even to a crouching eye, nematodes wriggle through the moisture, eating bacteria, turning the damp itself into food.

They are all inhabitants, and they are all temporary. A broad leaf falls in the afternoon and crushes a district flat. The workers under it do not panic — panic is too fast for this pace. They tunnel up through the crush mouthful by mouthful, emerge on top of the leaf, and begin again on the new surface. A seedling thrusts up through the middle of a tower. The builders do not fight it. They layer mud around the green stem, incorporating it as a column, and the tower now has a living core that will, in a season, outgrow it. In a dry spell, materials harden, the outposts at the far drips are emptied — larvae carried back toward the centre in pulses, fungal pellets carried like seed — and the abandoned towers are left to mites and springtails and seedlings. In the rain, when the mud softens and a pan fills, the pulses return. Walls are raised, breaches sealed, new shafts bored. Fungal threads run. The town re-expands into space it had decided, for a dry week, not to need.

Larger lives pass at the edge and change the humidity of everything. A frog sits in a rain-pan for an hour, unmoving, its throat pulsing, holding the moisture in one place while the rest of the town dries a little more. A shrew noses at the margin, vibrations travelling through mud faster than through air, and the whole nursery freezes underground, workers pressed flat against larvae, until the nose withdraws. Then, after a silence that lasts a full minute — the time between drips — the ticking resumes.

No one leaves. There is no outside to carry waste to, so waste is built with, eaten, farmed on, laid over. A collapsed wall that was not rebuilt becomes a flat where springtails graze. A blind shaft becomes a nursery for mites. A dead worker becomes food for the fungus, which becomes food for the living workers. The roads are only memory, remade each time a foot passes; let rain soften them and they revert to raw mud, and the town must learn its own streets again.

It is only now, watching the pulses go in and out of the layered towers, the attendants in the warm dark, the grazers on the gardens, the night grazers eating the walls, the still hunters in the doorways, that the whole becomes itself: not a hive, not a city, but a digestion that has learned to live in place. Pale builders carrying pellets larger than their heads, blind and tapping, feeding a queen they rarely touch, feeding a fungus that feeds them, fed in turn by mites and slugs and the slow rot under the root — a town of raw earth on raw earth, still being put up, pellet by pellet, with most of its future yet to be built or to erode.
