The first inhabitants you hear are not the ones who built anything.

They are in the mat, everywhere, underfoot and underleaf — frogs by the hundreds, deep-throated and companionable, a body of sound that starts before dusk and doesn't quite stop. You hear them before you see a person, because sound carries over water here and the mat magnifies it. They live on every hummock, on lily pads broad as plates, in the woven joints of houses, in the hollows of the alder trunks that serve as pillars. Children grow up knowing the frogs by their hours the way other children know clocks. When the chorus falters, someone has stepped too close to a nest. When it surges, the wind has come off the ridge and the reed beds are hissing again.

Below the frogs, and below the water, there are older residents who never come into the light properly.

Silver fish move through Tessik the way pedestrians move through a street town, in shoals, flashing under houses and through doorways. They are not stock and not quite wild. The flooded ground floor of every house is their street-level, lit from above, and fish drift down the stairs and out the intakes of fish pens where split reed weaves a slow current through. Eels live in the drowned stonework of the causeway, in the smoothed joints where flat stones meet. Crayfish walk that causeway at night, green-algae-lined, feeling their way by antennae along a road no living person has ever walked dry. Algae forests sway below them, patient in the cold, and pondweed waves like slow hair in the open channels. If you pole a dugout you feel for the causeway with your pole the way you would feel for a familiar stair in the dark — it is still a map, even submerged.

The people live upstairs.

That is literally true, and it shapes everything. Stone first, sunk into the mat until only the tops show. Timber above that, pale where new-cut, silvered where weathered a hundred wet years. Woven houses above that, reed and fiber and living willow left to root in the walls. A house takes a childhood to stand on a root pillar thick enough to bear it. While it grows, the family lives in an older cluster, rents a dry room, helps lash thatch that is allowed to green and moss and sometimes take seed and flower. When the pillar is finally lashed to the posts until post and root become one thing, they move up. They will sink by inches in a decade and rise by inches when the mat swells in a wet year, and no one minds the lean. Nothing is collapsing. Tessik has found its form.

The people themselves are weathered the way their timber is weathered. Unhurried hands, stained dark from peat and reed dye and fish slime. Knees used to kneeling in dugouts, shoulders used to poling against a current you can barely feel — from the snowmelt seep on the north rim to the south, a drift of maybe a finger's width a day. Eyes that know the difference between white-gold noon glare off open water and the flat green-brown of evening, and the dim, amber light of a flooded room where furniture has stood underwater for a generation and fish move through it like slow thoughts.

They are dispersed, the way the houses are dispersed. Dozens, hundreds of structures, but with great empty water between them — whole meadows of lily where nothing stands except birds. A family may know its channel, its hummocks, its raft gardens, its root pillars by name, and shout to the next cluster across a lily meadow a hundred paces away, voices arriving whole because nothing absorbs them. Children shout across channels before they learn to whisper. They learn to pole before they learn to paddle, to swim before they are quite steady on land, to read the mat by the give under a pole — where it will hold, where it bubbles faintly and will not.

There are no crowds here, and no privacy either. Sounds travel. Work is visible. What you do in your dry upstairs room — mend a net, weave a wall, pare a paddle of willow — your neighbor across the water can hear the rhythm of it.

Their work is mostly tending.

Tending the woven fish pens where the current passes through. Tending floating gardens anchored by root, where ducks and grebes nest undisturbed and someone will come each morning to check, and leave most eggs. Tending reed beds, not cutting them to nothing but cutting and replanting, because the beds are roof and wall and mat and bedding. Tending the alder-like trees whose roots are locked into the mat and whose canopies interlace above the houses — one half-dead alder is also somebody's wall and a heronry at once, and everyone has agreed, without ever quite saying so, to leave that wall alone. Herons call from rooftops, startled. Swallows nest in the eaves and return each summer to gaps left on purpose. Owls live in hollow trees that are pillars, and are not disturbed. Otters slide down piles and bark and are treated less as pests than as loud cousins who steal fish and are forgiven on the days they don't.

Fire is the rarest and most tended thing. Nothing is allowed to dry out here; everything else is damp. So fires only burn on the hearth-hummocks — small stone islands raised deliberately above water, the only places where rock has not sunk and mat has not swallowed. There, pots simmer, smoke rises thin and straight in cool air, hands are warmed, nets are dried, rendered fat for lamps is worked. In the evening the hearth-hummocks are where news collects the way frogs collect at dusk: who has poled a new water-street open beside a channel choked to a meadow, whose house has sunk a notch lower, whose root pillar has finally thickened, which duck has hatched, which eel has taken a new stone in the causeway.

The oldest people remember when the causeway was more than a memory under boats, when some of the stone foundations were closer to the surface. They are the ones who know which flooded room was a nursery, which is now a planter as the mat thickened, which chair has been underwater longest. They move slowly between upstairs rooms and those drowned rooms on poles and ladders, not out of nostalgia but because a flooded room becomes a fish nursery without anyone deciding it should. A generation of fish can grow up in a doorway.

That slowness is not a style. It is the only way to live here. A reed roof that flowers is not left to flower out of sentiment; if you strip it too bare you lose insulation, you lose bird nests, you lose the green that holds damp. A channel choked with weed is allowed to become a meadow, and a new channel is poled beside it — the old one now feeds frogs and lilies. Peat forms where old reeds fall and are not eaten. Timber softens. Stone polishes. Change is measured in generations, and the inhabitants have the confidence of a place that expects to be exactly as it is, only greener, for a very long time.

They eat what the mat gives in step with what they plant: silver fish hooked on bone hooks and flint-edged lines, eel from the stonework, crayfish gathered at night from the causeway, duck eggs from the raft gardens, lily roots and reed shoots from the meadows they have let grow. Clay pots darkened by smoke sit on the hearth-hummocks, never in houses themselves. Boats are single logs hollowed by fire and adze, poled house to storehouse, house to garden, house to hearth. Storehouses sit on raft platforms in the middle of the water, visited by everyone, owned by no one in particular, filled with thatch and fiber and dried fish and spare poles.

At night Tessik is loud in a different way, and every inhabitant has a part. Frogs take over outside the walls. Wooden boats knock against piers, over and over. The mat drips in root hairs like distant rain inside the ground. Fish jump suddenly among the houses with a slap. Nets splash. Children shout last messages across channels before coming in. Upstairs, lamps of rendered fat are lit in the dry rooms. Below them, in the submerged rooms, fish drift past the windows of light. On the roofs, swallows sleep folded in the eaves, owls wake in their pillar trees, herons settle into the alder-wall.

Stone, timber, fiber, root, water — and threaded through both, so densely that the quietest hour still hums: human hands mending, frog throats, fish flicking, otter paws on wet wood, dragonfly wings stitching the air above the lilies in summer, crayfish beginning their slow walk along a road under the sea that is, after all, still their town.
