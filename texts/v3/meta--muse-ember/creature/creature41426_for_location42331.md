What lives in Kersmill is, first, almost nothing else that is supposed to live in a marsh. And then, in the space that absence leaves, a great many people, packed so close that you hear a household before you find it, and find it before you see who is inside.

You count the roofs and you have roughly counted the families. A roof is a household, and a household is a roof, for the thatch is double and living and needs constant tending, and the people under it are needed to tend it. There are perhaps three or four hundred of them in that quarter-mile, gate to gate. Second and sometimes third stories of bamboo and reed and plastered clay rise over the workshops, and the verandas are linked house to house, so that a whole life can be lived without ever putting a foot on the ground. People are born on the upper, drier floors, where the air is still damp but does not weep from the walls, and they learn to walk on boardwalks before they learn to be careful on the causeway. They sleep within a window's breadth of the next family, close enough to hear a kettle, a cough, a child, and the shutters are thin reed matting against the rain. In the morning they come down, one by one, in the same unhurried order, and the town fills from the top down like water returning.

They are not large people, or loud people, or people set apart by any fierce mark. They are what the place makes, slowly, over a generation the way the peat makes a foundation: settled, patched, and made to hold. Their boots are always wet. There is no pair that is ever quite dry on the rack by the door, because there is no door that shuts against the damp, and the pile floors float a little and the canals splash in wind, and the wash-water leads drip through the filter beds day and night. Their clothes, woven of the same fiber they beat all day, patched and re-patched with sap and clay, hang over the canals in the wash-houses to finish drying in air that is already saturated, and they go out in them still cool and faintly sour. You can smell them before you can pick them out of the workshops: hot oil, wet peat, fermenting green matter, woodsmoke, and clean sour fiber, worked into skin and hair and beard. Their hands are stained green-brown at the knuckles from hyacinth and palm leaf, cut fine by rattan, callused on the palms from poles and beaters and skeins. Their backs go early. Their knees go early. By middle age most of them have lost a good deal of hearing and speak in a low, even monotone, not because they are quiet by nature but because the engine has been speaking for them since birth, and a shout is a waste of breath against a flywheel that never pauses.

Everyone in Kersmill is born within earshot of the engine, and most never leave that earshot to go anywhere that matters. The causeway is not really a road out; it is a mat over black water, and it gives under boots and springs back, and beyond the far levee the marsh is the same flat green in every direction, worked looser and looser until it is just swamp again. A child can pole a boat at a walking pace before he is ten, because the canals are the streets and the poles are the way you go, and can tell by the sound of the belts whether the beaters are running true, and can find the nursery beds by smell. Work is learned by standing near it. No one is sent away to learn it; it is too slow and too particular to the substrate for that. You learn the pace first — the engine turning at the speed that everything turns at, the beaters, the looms, the dryers in the workshops that lean against the engine house on all sides — and then you learn the task that fits your size and strength and years.

The work divides the day out without dividing the people very much, because no one is far from anything else. The engine keeper is the still center of it, though he does not look like a center. He is attended by the vibration through the raft floors, he walks his rounds on the one true foundation of brick and iron, he tends the steam at its joints and the grease on the belts and the copper wire strung low between roofs. He is likely to be an older man or woman who has grown deaf in one ear and matter-of-fact in both, who knows the flywheel by its thump the way other people know a pulse, and who is already half the building, darkened by damp and smoke like the brick. Around him, and in three shifts that are more like three moods of the same slow day than sharp changes, the fiber is cut and soaked and beaten soft and woven into matting and rope and packaging and rough paper. Women and younger men tend the looms and the drying racks, where sheets turn slowly in damp air. Stronger backs haul wet baskets of reed and hyacinth from the pole-boats up the house piles to the beating floor, along boardwalks that sag an inch a year and are re-woven when they sag too much, not before. Others go out beyond the levee with sickles to cut bamboo in its different years of growth, to pull hyacinth locking the side channels, to dig mud from the canals, to graft new root into a rotting pile at the waterline, to pack a floor tighter so a house leaning a degree to the south steadies for another decade. The thatchers walk the roofs, which are gardens and insulation and rain-shedding at once, and weed and patch the sedge layer that roots in the outer thatch and gains an inch a year. No one hurries, because the peat will not bear hurry and the climate will not reward it, and after a while you do not hurry either; your gait becomes the town's gait, your speech becomes the town's speech, slow and unvaried and carrying on underneath everything else.

Domestic life is fitted into the same close, vertical space. The ground level is wet work, storage, fuel piles of waste fiber, nursery beds where new root-mat is grown for next year's repairs, filter beds of cattail and sedge where the wash-water leads slowly before it is released to the ring canal. You climb to live. A family of several generations may occupy the stacked plant rooms above a workshop, with a grandmother mending matting on the veranda where she can watch the canal and the market stall below, with children running the linked boardwalks from house to house, knocking at windows that open onto other windows. Food is cooked over the fires that burn the mill waste for steam, and it is marsh food in the most literal closed sense: greens and shoots from the beds and edges, eels and fish taken sparingly from the ring canal beyond the sluice, root vegetables from the kept ground, smoked and soured and salted against the damp, eaten hot because hot is one of the few dry things in Kersmill. No one keeps chickens scratching, for there is no mineral soil for them to scratch. No one keeps a dog, for the noise is unbroken and the ground is a raft and a dog would have nowhere to be a dog. It is a complete human population in a plant-built place, and the strangeness of that, if you stay, stops being strange. The plants do the sheltering that elsewhere animals would do.

Children are raised to the sound. A cradle hung in a thatched room rocks itself slightly with the thump from below, and an infant sleeps through the clatter of the beaters and the hiss of steam and the splash of a pole the way a town elsewhere sleeps through rain. They learn the canals the way other children learn lanes, and learn not to fall, and learn to haul and weave and graft, and by the time they are strong enough to go out beyond the levee with a sickle they are already part of the cutting-and-planting that has shaped the swamp for a long way out. Old people do not retire so much as they settle into lighter work, as a house settles: mending, sorting fiber, watching the drying lines, sitting in the damp white mornings on the veranda until the white lifts, telling very little, because they have heard it all before in the same tone from the machinery. Their bodies, like the buildings, show the negotiation plainly — water stains stopping at a consistent height, timber silvered, iron bloomed and darkened, fungal feet treated with ash and sap, lungs carrying the fine dust of beaten leaf, backs bent from baskets. They are jacked up a little, packed tighter, grafted with a new root of care by their children, and they steady for another decade.

The other living things in Kersmill live at the margins of that human press, and they are few enough that a newcomer feels the absence as a presence. In a warm wet marsh you would expect a crowd, and outside the levee there is one, in the reeds and pools. Inside the palisade of noise and smoke and managed water there is almost no crowd at all. A gecko takes the warm wall by the engine house at dusk and stays there, minute and still, eating the moths that come to the lamps, which are themselves the only uncountable animal residents, beating damp wings around iron and wire. An eel turns, now and then, in the ring canal below the sluice, below the pole-boats. A heron passes high over the clearing, not landing, its shadow moving across the roofs for a second and gone. That is nearly the whole of it. The plants that elsewhere would shelter frogs and birds and insects in clouds are here mostly structural, harvested, or too constantly worked — the bamboo thickets cut by year, the palm clumps pruned, the vines trained over pipe-bridges to shade them, the living fences of thorn and bamboo marking yards. They do not host much else, and the people, who are the real fauna of the place, have made a kind of peace with the quiet around them; they fill it with their own sound, continuous as the engine, and do not seem to notice it any more than they notice the smell.

And the plants themselves live there as residents, not decoration, which is to say they live there the way the people do: managed intensively in the middle, more loosely around, remade at the rate of decay. The sedges on the roofs are tenants who insulate; the cattail and sedge in the filter beds are tenants who clean; the woven root on the canal banks are tenants who hold the peat together until they rot and are woven over again; the nursery beds are children of a kind, grown for next year's repairs. The marsh feeds the mill and the mill feeds the marsh, the roofs feed the houses, and the people feed, clothe, house, and fuel themselves out of the same green matter, in a loop so closed it has less the air of an economy than of a single organism that happens to have voices and hands.

So Kersmill holds its people the way its houses hold their inhabitants: not by permanence but by continuity. They are born on rafts, they work at a walking pace, they grow deaf and bent and patched, they repair each other as they repair a pile or a roof, they go on at the same slow speed as the flywheel going on underneath it all. In the morning the damp lifts slowly off their shoulders as it lifts off the town. In the afternoon the warmth is a sweat on the back of the neck, and they move through it carrying baskets, tending steam, mending matting on a veranda that looks straight into another family's window. At night the human noise drops away and the machinery does not, and they climb to the upper floors and sleep to the hum in their sleep, a small loud warm wet population, entirely itself, remade out of its own marsh at exactly the rate at which it decays, and as it will, at the same pace, for a very long time more.
