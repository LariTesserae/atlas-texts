The first living thing you meet is not large enough to see.

Put your palm down on the mat — you have to, to keep from slipping on the wet stone — and it comes up to you. Not one, but dozens, swarming the warmth that spreads through the moss in a minute. Springtails, pale as frost, that jump in unison at a vibration and land in unison. Mites no bigger than the head of a pin, translucent, with legs that move too fast for the cold. Blind beetles that live upside-down beneath the curled leaves of the liverwort, clinging with hooked tarsi, their backs to the stone, their mouths to the damp underside where condensation gathers and drips, drip, drip, all day, whether it rains or not.

They do not flee. They investigate. Everything here investigates heat first, and light second, and thereafter touch. If you stay still for a minute something will climb your boot to lick the salt and the warmth from it. If you stay still for ten, the conveyor worms will decide you are an efficient route and take it.

The mat itself is the first animal, if you can call it that. A centimeter thick, twenty kilometers across, following the lifted plateau for as far as you can see without a break. Moss and liverwort, shoulder-to-shoulder, coppery-pale and luminous in the white light, always wet to the touch. It breathes, slowly. Under the noon glare the top layer dries to paper and curls to protect the green-black heart below; by evening the whole sheet exhales a fog of its own making that hangs knee-high, that wets your shoulders as you walk through it, that beads and runs into cupped rocks and stays there for days. The mat drinks by licking, as everything does here. Dew is not weather, it is exhalation.

And it is grazed to a uniform height, as if trimmed. Look closer and the trimmers are under it. Leave one square metre ungrazed for a season and it will rise unevenly, become ragged, begin to lift the stone. But the whole plain is ankle-deep and even, because under themat moves a second, slower ocean.

The train-worms. Millipede-like, but not millipedes — long, jointed chains of segments each the size of a knuckle, blind, without jaws, that link and unlink in slow ripples. When they link, they become a single creature twenty metres long that undulates across the moss in a tide, grazing as it goes. When they unlink, each segment becomes a small, patient grazer on its own. Along their backs run shallow grooves, and in those grooves they transport their lives: chewed leaf fragments, spores, grit, the polished beads dropped by greater mouths. A train-worm is a road and a herd and a gut at once. You can hear them if you put your ear to the moss — a faint, endless rustle, chewing made huge by numbers.

They have no soil to go to. They are the soil, moving.

Between the mats, where the rock cracks, grow the furnace-ferns. The fronds are translucent, almost membrane-thin, veined with iron that shows as red-brown lines in the bright light. In still air they open like hands, twenty, sixty, a hundred of them from a single crown, held flat to the 2.9 of sky. At the first gust they close. Not gradually — all the fronds on the plain close at once, with a soft shiver you can hear. Inside each closed frond is a smelting heart, a knot of tissue that is warm to the touch even when the stone is below freezing. The fern does not only photosynthesize. It secretes, from roots like pale fingers gripping quartzite, acids that etch the rock year after year, freeing iron and copper atom by atom and storing it in nodules at the root tips. The nodules swell season by season, dull bronze bumps under the moss. A storm may tear one free. A grazer will find it. Or the fern will keep it for a decade, growing rounder, heavier, until its own weight cracks the root and it can at last feed what eats it.

Their leaves are stone-cold until you press your palm to them. Then you feel the stored heat come up through the cold surface, as if the leaf had been holding its breath.

The pipe-reeds are the plain's nerves. Hollow, jointed stalks rising from cracks to the height of a person, each one a resonator sealed at the base, open at the top to the wind. Their leaves are broad and fleshy and warm. On a bright, still day, if you hold a reed lightly between two fingers, you will feel it. A tingling crawl up your wrist, static from millions of stems humming together. The wind plays them like an instrument, and the sound that comes out is not a whistle but a low, sustained sigh, pitched according to the height of the stalk. At night — a brief dimming, never true dark at this latitude — the mats flicker faintly in patches where the current leaks from the reeds into the wet moss, a slow, greenish-blue pulse that the beetles navigate by.

Because the reeds generate current, and the current warms the moss, and the moss feeds the worms, and the worms carry the spores of the reeds to new cracks.

Everything lives upside-down or underneath if it can. Under a flat stone, packed so tight there is no air, blind beetles cling in rows, waiting for a drip. Under the shed plates of the great beasts, mites farm a fungus that softens the horn. On the vertical cliffs where there is no moss at all, there are curtains — insects hanging by their legs in living sheets, lichens in copper and white, and the guano of the birds caked so thick it looks like snow that has decided to stay on a wall.

The birds are not flocks. They are a medium.

From a distance they blot the light in shifting veils, in lines that go on for an hour without thinning. Close, they are not beautiful. They are dense, armored, black and iron-gray, with wings that do not soar but beat fast and click at the joint — click-click-click, like a mechanism. Their calls are not songs. They are percussive chirps, struck like tuning forks, metallic, brief, echoed and answered a mile away and stamped back again. They nest on the cliff faces in rows, shoulder to shoulder, a million crannies filled, and their guano feeds the lichens, and the lichens feed the insects, and the insects feed the birds' young, who sit in the glare with their eyes half-closed, licking condensation from their parents' beaks.

They never land on the moss. They touch it only to take a mite, a spark, a bead. They are the plain's weather made animal.

The workers are the beetles, and there are kinds upon kinds. Some are red-bellies, whose abdomens glow dull red as they metabolize, little furnaces on legs that will walk up to a cold leaf, press themselves to it, and warm it enough for a fern to open. Some are welders, with electric organs that arc blue-white along their seams after a molt, sealing their new shells shut with a weld they make themselves; you can see them spark at dusk, a tiny storm on a beetle's back. Some, when their shell is spent, simply abandon it, and the empty housing — perfect, domed, indifferent to the cold — is colonized at once by a lichen that blooms coppery in the shell's shadow, and by mites who move in like tenants. Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to stay empty. A shell is a house, a house is a garden, a garden is a meal.

And then the stampers.

Low, armored, many-legged, the size of a small cart and then the size of a house and then, in the great herds, the size of a weather system. No one part of them is decorative. Their heads are mills — a rotating drum of teeth and plates that lowers into the moss and works, patiently, for hours, grinding a year's growth into feed and heat and absorbable metal. Their hips are bellows, visibly swelling and contracting with each grinding stroke, driving air over the furnace in their gut and venting it as warm breath that fogs the cold air in a plume you can see from half a kilometer. They walk in herds that fill the plain from horizon to horizon, one deliberate step, another, at a tempo so slow it seems to stop time, and yet the clank of a thousand plates striking stone, multiplied, becomes a continental sound that you feel in your teeth before you hear it with your ears.

A herd enters a patch of moss and does not leave until it is cropped to the stone. Then it moves on, leaving a bare, dark circle that will take a decade to close. The stampers remember. A great herd's route is not random; it is a migration that is a front, that will return in twenty years to the same scars, and find them healed to exactly the height they need. They do not trample. They cannot afford to. They are too heavy to be careless, and too slow to correct a mistake quickly.

They are warm. That is their entire ecology. The moss is lush because they are warm, because their bodies heat the mats and their breath makes the condensation that the mats drink. The ferns are lush because they are warm, because the stampers' bellows drive the air that sparks the seeds. They coat themselves in mites who clean their plates, in beetles who weld nicks in their armor, in sparks.

The sparks are what the ferns release to reproduce. Not seeds as you know them. Dry, charged grains that drift on the wind that the pipe-reeds make and break, that hang in the bright air until they find warmth. If a spark lands on a cold stone, it dies. If it lands on a stamper, on a great beast's flank, on a bird's wing, it roots. It grows there, a coppery patch crusted on fur and horn and plate, drinking the host's heat, making its own little furnace. When the host is grazed, or when the patch is heavy enough, or when a train-worm passes under and tears it free, the patch becomes a nodule again, and falls to the moss to be eaten or to root in earnest and become a new fern.

Mating looks like standing still together for a month. Two stampers, or two great ones, flank to flank, plates pressed until they fuse at the points of contact. Then, slowly, a current passes between them — you can see it in the shiver of the mites on their backs, in the flicker of the moss beneath them. They separate heavier than they came, brighter, their plates a little more bronze, a little more iron. They have exchanged metal, and heat, and the instructions to make more of themselves.

Because they also make more of themselves another way. A stamper will bud. A small, folded version of itself will swell on its flank, warm and sealed, for a season. Then it will unfold, like a tool from a case, legs first, then mill, then bellows, and walk away on its own while its parent does not stop walking. The great beasts do this rarely. A great beast lives three hundred years, is not mature until eighty, buds perhaps twice in its life, and each bud is the size of a house when it detaches.

The great beasts, the house-sized quadrupeds, are the slowest and oldest and rarest of the workers. One step per minute. A footfall that sends a shiver through the moss a hundred meters away, that the train-worms feel as an earthquake and answer by linking into longer trains to ride it out. Their flanks are plated in bronze and black iron that grows like horn, year by year, in ridges and overlapping sheets, and is shed like hair, plate by plate, to fall clattering onto the moss where it lands with a sound like a dropped tool.

From their chests, vents at the sides of their necks, steam sighs in slow rhythm — in, out, in, out — and inside those chests a crop-stone furnace turns constantly, stone-cold stone taken in with the moss, ground to powder against other stones, heated by the animal's own metabolism until the iron loosens and the copper loosens and the waste separates. What they excrete is not dung. It is sorted, polished components: beads, rods, small gear-like ossicles, thin sheets of metal, each one gleaming, each one perfect, dropped in a slow, deliberate stream onto the moss. The mites take the beads at once. The beetles take the rods. The train-worms load the sheets into their back grooves and haul them to the ferns, whose roots drink the acid wash of the metal and store it anew.

It is a closed loop grown, not built. The plants smelt. The beasts refine. The worms transport. The beetles weld and house and burn. The birds carry sparks and guano across the whole plain so that no patch starves for lack of a seed. The mites and springtails are the hands, the stomachs, the cleaners, too small to count, too numerous to be counted.

There is no silence here because there is no space for it to gather. The sound is slow sound made huge: the distant continental clank of herds, the constant grinding of mills and crop-stones from every direction at once, the click and whirr of wings that never quite stop, the hiss of steam, the sigh of wind through a million pipe-reeds — all of it at a tempo that leaves room between beats, but no room between sources. You do not hear an animal. You hear industry.

Decay is the slowest industry of all. A dead stamper does not rot. There is no soil to rot into. It freezes, it dries in the wind, and it becomes a white monument for a generation, its plates still gleaming, its mill still open. Then the neighbors take it apart. Beetles take the metal, grain by grain, and weld it into their own shells. Train-worms take the soft parts that remain and carry them away in their grooves. Birds take the spark-rooted patches. Mites take the water, licking it from the bones until the bones are dust and the dust is licked into the moss. A carcass can sit for thirty years, a landmark older than some of the great beasts who step around it, before it is entirely unmade.

That is how everything lives here: cold outside, warm inside, bright everywhere and seeing almost nothing, because the light is too white and most of them are blind or half-closed against it, navigating instead by heat and tremor and current and the smell of ozone from a welder beetle and the taste of condensation on a leaf. They are not primitive. They are not advanced. They are finished in a way that looks, to an eye used to huts and walls, like a factory that was never constructed — only grown, over a million years of slow accumulation, out of stone and moss and animal heat, still lifting, still forging itself, one patient cycle at a time, under a sky that gives 2.9 of light and takes, in return, all the warmth they can make.
