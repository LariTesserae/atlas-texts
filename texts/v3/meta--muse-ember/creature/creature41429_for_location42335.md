At foot level, before anything with a face appears, the town is already inhabited.

The puddles are the first houses. They do not evaporate, they only deepen and join each other, and in that warm standing water life begins as a film. In the slow corners of the canals, where the current forgets to move, a grey-green bacterial slime spreads itself in sheets across the concrete, holding the light. Mosquito larvae hang and wiggle just under the surface, water beetles dive from the skin of the water and rise again to breathe, and over every still sheet the midges hang in a visible cloud, lifting when you pass through them and settling again behind you.

On the walls the life is slower still, because it does not need to move to feed. Black mould blooms on the north sides of the warehouses, where what little sun there is never reaches, and fungal crusts spread over damp timber and weep-streaked brick in dull, matte patches. White salt flowers push up through mortar after a dry hour, and around them the mould takes hold again. Oxidised copper goes verdigris, glass is filmed, iron sweats and drips along its whole length inside and out — all of it food, or shelter, or a surface to hold moisture against, for something small and living that prefers the mineral palette of the town to anything green.

The canals are fuller. Twice a day a factory discharges a fresh surge and the whole water suddenly moves, and the fish answer as one. Air-breathing catfish come up to gulp, their backs breaking the surface in a constant, silent boil, and tilapia and carp thrash in the heat just below them, in water warm enough to poach in. They live on what the cannery and the rendering plant put into them — fish waste, grain dust, warm effluent — and they keep the water turned over with their bodies, ripple on ripple, reflecting nothing but cloud. At low tide at the culvert mouths crabs work the mud sideways, claws up, while frogs call briefly from storm drains and stop the moment a truck passes, and water snakes cruise the canal edges with only a head and a line of wake to show.

In the flooded lots, where the water lies brown and still over concrete, herons and egrets stand knee-deep and grey on grey. They are so still they become part of the reflection. They wait for the fish that have wandered out, or for frogs, or for nothing in particular, for long stretches, unhurried, because there is always something moving below them.

On land the richest seam is waste that falls dry. Around the grain silos, spilled kernels have worked their way into culverts, into cracks in the aprons, into the dusty joints of rail sidings, and there the rats live by the hundreds. They run in set lines through the dark, well-fed on grain and on fish scraps and on whatever the cold stores and truck depots drop, their eyes catching the sodium light. With the grain come the insects that live in grain, tiny weevils and moths in the spill itself, and with the insects come the geckos.

In the evening — and the lamps come on early here, in the permanent late-evening light — the warm walls are covered with them. House geckos and skinks plaster themselves flat to brick and corrugated iron under the yellow pools of light, translucent and mottled, perfectly still for minutes at a time, hunting midges and moths that blunder into the lamps. Then a flick of tongue, a sudden rush, and stillness again. They love the heat that comes up through the concrete long after rain, and the walls hold it for them.

Cats patrol the warehouses after the rats, and are known, in a way, to the humans who work there. Dogs and cats both are well-fed on scraps and sleep for most of the day in the shade of idle machinery, in container yards, under parked trucks, lifting their heads only to watch a rat pass or a stranger cross the yard. They do not hurry. The heat will not allow it, and there is no need.

Above all of this the air is worked over, constantly and without excitement. Feral pigeons occupy every ledge, every tank top, every crane arm that has stopped moving. Mynas and sparrows live in the rafters of the warehouses and under the eaves of the worker houses, their calls softened and swallowed by the damp air. Swifts stitch back and forth under the roofs in the last grey light. Over the harbour gulls and terns circle and settle and circle again. At dusk, which arrives slowly and stays, bats flicker out from under the bridges and from culverts, small and erratic against the steam plumes that never quite rise.

And through it, thinly spread as the buildings themselves, are the people.

You see them first at a distance, on the causeway, in trucks and on foot, coming into the basin from the sea-flat and going out again. Inside, they are few for so much space. Shift crews at the refinery and the cracker towers, at the cannery, the smelters, the power station, the cold stores. Watchmen who tend the pumps on their concrete plinths, because without the pumps the cellars and underpasses would fill and the whole sheet of water would return. Families in the scattering of two-storey worker houses, with laundry hung to dry in the damp air and not quite drying, with doors and windows kept shut against the midges as much as against the weather.

They live low and slow, like everything else here. Sweat stays on skin, breath is short in the heat that rises through the soles of their shoes, metal railings are warm even in shade. They cross the empty aprons between one factory fence and the next in five unshaded minutes, from one yellow pool of lamplight to another. They do not so much tame the animals as tolerate them as co-residents: the cats in the warehouse, the dogs in the yard, the pigeons on their own roofs, the geckos on the walls outside the kitchen window, where they hunt in the light.

The people discard a great deal, and the town lives on it, and in turn the people live, in part, on what the town makes — fish from the cannery, work from the plants, shelter from the spalled and patched concrete. But they are not the centre of the place. They move through it in small numbers, between fences and flooded lots and yards of drums and scrap, while the mould keeps its place on the north wall, the fish keep boiling in the canals, the rats keep their culverts, the geckos keep theirs.

It is an active place, and a quiet one. The activity is not loud or seasonal. It is water moving sideways to the sea, steam venting in soft plumes, a crane swinging slowly, a drip from a pipe, a ripple of fish, a gecko turning its head, a heron shifting its weight in the flooded lot. The factories throb at a distance, a train brakes squeal two streets away and are swallowed by damp air. Nothing here needs a season, and nothing here quite stops.

Rain comes and goes without changing the arrangement. The ground stays wet, the walls keep weeping, the empty lots stay empty and reflective, and everything that lives in Brinemouth goes on working through them, quietly, in the grey light, feeding on the industrial detritus of a wet, hot, low coast, taking shelter in its warmth and its water, and making the emptiness feel crowded at ground level.
