Nothing in Ilthmere lives upright, in the open. Not at first.

You look for animals the way you would anywhere else — perched, walking, flying — and the streets give you nothing but wet brick and your own breath showing. The cold has pooled down here and settled, and life, if there is any, has pooled with it. It is down low, in the damp, and it is thin.

In the shallow depressions in the raw stone, where water gathers and stays because there is no river to take it away, something moves the surface a fraction of an inch though there is no wind. You will not see it, only the water. A small, eyeless disturbance, translucent as the mist it lives in, pushing at the puddle from inside. It does not swim. It waits. The puddle takes a year to fill and then does not empty, and the thing in it has learned that timescale. It is not much of an animal — more a place where the water has learned to be restless. In the ankle-deep mist that never quite moves, there are others like it, traveling slowly from one pocket to another, leaving the surface slightly less smooth behind them. They eat nothing you can name. They live on the leak between humidity and stone, on the fact that the rock is still pre-formation and has not decided to be impermeable yet.

Higher up, but still not really up, are the moths.

Near dusk they come out of the condensation — from the undersides of awnings, from the inside of the glass, from the bellies of pipes where the water beads and crawls. Blind, translucent, their wings crusted with a fine mineral dust they have licked off walls and windowpanes all day. They settle on the shop windows, heavy for their size, and stay there until the light through the gratings goes. In the morning they are gone and only the crust remains, a pale smear that the rain does not wash away because the rain here starts at roof height and lands without sound. They are not fed by flowers. There are no flowers. They feed by settling where the lattice leaks a little heat, where moss has taken hold in a north-facing seam, where the grass along one wall stays blackened and green all winter because the cold never quite reaches the seam. That grass is a life too, the slowest one in the place: a leaf a season, never dying back, never finishing. Life here does not hurry to resolve, and the moths have taken the hint. They do not age in any way you can measure. They persist through damp the way the damp persists.

All of it lives on leaks. That is the first rule, and once you see it, the larger inhabitants make sense.

Go down the narrow passage between the three-storey grey house and the nine-storey wet-brick slab. It goes down six levels, past gratings where ordinary daylight filters, past doors that have been in place forty years and tick once a month in your teeth, to the street that is underneath the later city and still numbered. That is where the dwellers live.

There are not many of them. A dense city this small should hold thousands; it holds perhaps a few hundred, spread out through the layers so that no two ever seem to be on the same level at the same time. They are pale from the diffuse light, in clothes that never dry. Their fingers are clumsy from the cold that pools on their shoulders and never leaves, and they have learned to work slowly around it. Some live in the houses that lean corner-to-corner, overlapping by a meter that should not be possible; a woman dries clothes in an alley where a useless tram wire sags; a man keeps a kitchen that is also a lift shaft, and the lift, patiently, never comes. Others live in the shopfronts whose lettering is for businesses that have not yet opened — they work there in advance, at the speed of frost, setting out goods nobody has ordered for a future that is still being decided. Others still work, very quietly, in the shops that closed a decade ago and left their signs up, because the sign is still true in the layer they inhabit.

They know the streets in the way you know a thing that changes. A street walked twice is a little longer the second time, long enough that their pace alters without their noticing, and they do not correct it. They sit at bus stops with no buses because the stop is a held place in the grid, like the amber traffic lights blinking for no traffic. They sleep leaning in doorways where gravity pulls a fraction stronger, as if the building is cradling them. When they talk — and you will hear them, occasionally, from a closed doorway, a conversation in a language that might be yours, spoken very slowly and left unfinished — they prefer the pocket of air near the old market where sound arrives a half-second late. There, a sentence is heard in the future. It can be continued by the person who was not yet there to hear the beginning. They are careful not to finish things. A finished sentence, a finished wall, a finished building would close a seam, and they live in the seam the way the moths live in the window.

And they are incorporated, not removed, like everything else here. A building that was half-finished when you arrived will be half-finished in a slightly different way when you leave, and the dwellers who live on its exposed floors — rebar standing like iron trees, stairs arriving at open air and stopping — have changed with it, millimeters a year. They accrete. Moss obscures a sign; a dweller grows a little more fixed to the damp wall she leans against. Older infrastructure is not taken away in Ilthmere. Older people are not taken away either. They are built through.

If you watch them long enough, you begin to wonder how much of a person is a person. A panel in one of their walls heals a crack by sealing it with glass that was not transparent a moment before, and a dweller beside it does the same with a cut on her hand, slowly, at the same pace. A door ticks once a month; so, faintly, does a dweller’s chest, if you put your ear close enough in the cold. The shapes they keep are held the way the vacuum-annealed lattices under the streets hold a building’s shape — as a field might hold a pattern — and the pattern is strong enough that you mistake it for a life. Perhaps it is a life, of a different thickness.

Because there are lives thinner still, living not in the water or on the glass, but in the air where the condensation forms in straight lines. You have seen the lines: a seam running across empty air like a crack in glass. If you cross it, your shadow for three steps is cast the other way. Something lives on that line. Not on either side of it — on the border between a coherent block where physics holds and the block beside it where it does not, where time runs a little wrong and a staircase arrives at the same landing but the room beyond is different on Tuesdays. These are the thinnest inhabitants of all, flat as the line itself, moving with it at lichen speed, a few millimeters a year as the border patiently shifts. They are fraying made local, the way the water here is dampness made local. They feed — if feeding is the word — on the inconsistency packed into the hollow, on the space between the planned building and the one that was built. You feel them only as a pause, a cold with no source, a moment when the ticking under the street stops and then resumes.

And under everything, woven through walls and masquerading as scaffolding and cable trays, is the oldest resident. The crystalline lattice is not a machine in the way we mean machines. To the hand it is sometimes too warm, sometimes cold for no reason you can find. It carries power and signal by means that make *current* feel inadequate, and it lives by correcting, by stitching seams as they open. That is its metabolism: slow extrusion of a new wall, a panel sealing itself, a puddle taking a year to fill. It ticks as it cools. It holds the city in a shape that is still deciding itself, and in holding it, it holds the moths and the moss and the puddle-things and the dwellers, each of them a different density of the same unfinished process. Erosion in Ilthmere does not wear things away. It builds them up raw.

So what lives here is not a population. It is a succession, layered like the city itself: water that has learned to move, moths that have learned to wait on glass, grass that adds a leaf a season and never dies, people who have learned to live in the overlap and not finish their sentences, seam-things on the border of the real, and below them all the lattice holding the pattern of them all. None of them is separate. They are all embryonic, twice over — the geology becoming earth, the city becoming a city, and everything that lives in the hollow becoming itself, slowly, quietly, and never quite arriving.
