You won't see most of them at first. If you stand ankle-deep and look down - really look, past your own feet - the water is not clear. It has a skin.

A greenish, faintly iridescent film lies across the mud and across the quieter edges of the slow channels, and it is alive in a way that has no face. Cyanobacteria and diatoms and a hundred other microscopic things are bound together with the slime they secrete, a living paste that photosynthesises in the sunlight and respires at night. Where the sun hits a shallow pan it can turn the whole surface to a faint soup of oxygen bubbles. Where a crab has scraped the mud, where a terrapin has paddled, where rain has dimpled a pool, the film tears and reseeds within hours. It is the pasture. Everything else in this place is built, directly or at one remove, on eating it, or eating something that ate it, or eating the dead things it leaves behind.

It feeds, first, the grazers you can just barely see: clouds of small freshwater shrimp flicking through the weeds and over the bare mud. They are translucent, each one with a dark green gut showing through its shell, and they move in short, darting pulses, constantly filtering and scraping. A cloud will hang in a sunlit edge for a minute, then scatter at a shadow - yours, a bird's - and reform as if it had never broken. Their lives are short and prodigiously fast for this slow place. In a warm pool they can mature in weeks, lay eggs that become cysts in the mud when the water recedes, and wait there, perfectly still, for a season or a year until the pan fills again. When it does, they seem to appear from nothing. They have only been waiting, curled in the drying clay beside the old hexagonal cracks.

The tadpoles work the same pasture but as a sheet. In some of the peat hollows in the north, and in the warmer lagoons to the west where floating plants gather, a pool will darken all at once, not with shadow but with thousands of small dark bodies pressed shoulder to shoulder, mouths rasping algae from the mud and from each other's tails in a slow, communal grazing. They do not hurry. A school can work an area of mud as cleanly as a lawnmower, then drift, as one animal, to the next patch, leaving a pale track behind it in the green film. After a while the water pales again where they have been, and later, as they grow legs and shrink tails, they thin out and climb, one by one, into the sedges to sit half-transformed and breathing both ways.

The fish are next, and they are the noise-makers of the shallows. Minnows dart in tight, silver shoals over the sand, turning together when a wading bird's shadow crosses them, surfacing with a soft *plip* to gulp air or snatch a midge that has fallen in. Mudfish are lonelier and stranger. They work the channels and the edges of the pans alone, wriggling more like eels than fish, lifting their heads to breathe at the surface, and when the water drops and the clay begins to crack they do not die in the way other fish would. They burrow down into the softening mud, curl into a ball, seal themselves in a cocoon of dried mucus, and wait. You could step over the spot where one is sleeping and never know it. Their heartbeat slows to almost nothing. When the next rain comes, sometimes months later, they loosen, soften, and swim out again as if no time had passed. Their footprints - their fin-marks - in the peat are the kind that might harden and keep.

Above them, at the surface film, the frogs and toads have claimed the whole basin. By day they are almost invisible, sitting half-submerged in a slough or a tuft of sedge, only their eyes and the tops of their heads showing, throats pulsing slowly as they breathe through skin that must stay damp. They have learned the exact pattern of stillness that the open plain requires. There is nowhere to hide here, no thicket, no bank to vanish into; shade is scarce and sound carries for kilometres. So they freeze, and their mottled green-brown skin matches wet mud and algae so exactly that your eye passes over them again and again until one blinks.

At dusk they become the place. It starts singly, a note here and there from a peat hollow, from the far edge of a lagoon, from a puddle you didn't notice at your feet. Within minutes the whole plain is in chorus, layer upon layer, not random but timed - each male holding his note a fraction longer or starting a fraction later than his neighbour, so that thousands of voices can be heard at once without becoming mere noise. You can sometimes pick out an individual sitting a metre away, throat ballooning out to the size of his head, then collapsing, while another answers him from the next slough. They are singing in the dark mostly because they are calling mates to water that may only last a few weeks, and the race to breed before the pan cracks again is the one urgency this slow world allows itself. The eggs they lay are gelatinous clusters stuck to sedge stems just under the surface. Most will never become frogs. They will feed tadpoles, feed shrimp, feed the film. That abundance is not waste here; it is the budget.

The crabs work lower and quieter. Along the southern outcrops, where the ground lifts just enough to stay dry on top and weep at the sides, small colonies of mud crabs dig burrows in the soft bank - one crab to a burrow, each entrance plugged neatly with a ball of mud when the owner is inside. By day you would swear the mud was empty except for the pockmarks. At dusk a crab emerges sideways, legs moving in that uncanny, almost imperceptible way, and begins to walk the flat in search of fallen sedge, a dead minnow, algae scraped from stone. They are the plain's undertakers as much as its grazers, and they will drag a carcass many times their own weight back toward their burrow, tenacious and unhurried. Two neighbouring crabs will posture and wave claws at each other if their feeding paths cross, but mostly they keep to their own few square metres for years. If you return to the same outcrop next season, the same burrows will be there, re-plugged, re-opened, inhabited by the same patched shell or by a slightly larger successor.

The terrapins move through all of it on a still longer timescale. They paddle in slow procession along the braided channels, shells often filmed green with algae, heads poking up to breathe, following routes that are older than any individual among them. A channel twenty centimetres deep is a highway if you live your whole life at that scale. They haul themselves out on the warm southern stones to bask, sometimes three or four together, motionless for hours, shells steaming in the sun, eyes half-closed. One animal might use the same stone, the same channel loop, the same peat hollow for its entire life - sixty, eighty years, perhaps more - growing slowly, laying eggs in the dry top of a stone outcrop, returning to the water. When a young one hatches it is no bigger than a coin and utterly exposed on that open stone. Most don't survive the first weeks. The ones that do become part of the permanent furniture of the place, as fixed in their habits as the rocks themselves.

In the air above the water it is winged patrols. Dragonflies work at arm's length, iridescent and tireless, holding a stretch of channel or pan the way a crab holds a burrow. A male will chase off rivals, hover to inspect a ripple, and drop to mate in mid-air, then find a sedge tip to rest, wings out flat, huge eyes taking in everything. Females come down to the surface to dip their tails and lay eggs among the weeds, where the nymphs - fierce, gilled, underwater things - will live for months eating shrimp and tadpoles and each other until they climb a stem in the night and split their backs to emerge. For every dragonfly there seem to be twice as many biting midges hanging in the warm, damp air where you stand. The males swarm harmlessly in a thin, whining cloud; the females are the ones who find warm blood, yours, a bird's, a terrapin's neck where the shell doesn't cover. At dawn and dusk the cloud thickens. The frogs open their mouths and snap air. The birds shake their heads. It is a cost of living near still water that everyone pays and no one can escape.

The great spaced hunters are the wading birds. Long-legged, grey and white, they pick their way far out on the pans, each one alone in its own stretch of water, spaced hundreds of paces from the next. Their cries, when they give them, are like torn cloth - harsh, ripped sounds that carry across the flat because there is nothing to stop them. They walk with a terrible slowness, one foot lifted, placed, lifted, eyes fixed on the mud ahead, and then strike - a spear of a beak - into water that to you looks empty. They take fish, frogs, shrimp, crab young. When they have fed they stand, sometimes for a long time, knee-deep and perfectly still, as if listening to the plain below them. They are not social in the way the ducks are. Their solitude is a kind of territoriality made possible by abundance spread thin: there is food for one bird in every pan, but not for two, and so they have agreed, over whatever deep time made them, to keep their distances. At night they roost on the dry tops of the southern stones or in the sparse trees, folded and grey as stones themselves.

The ducks and geese are the opposite - loose, noisy, and nomadic. They come in flocks that drop onto the western lagoons without apparent plan, honking and cackling as they land in sheets of water, paddling in ragged groups, diving for plants in water too shallow to dive in properly. A flock will stay a few days, then lift and move east to a clay pan that filled after rain, then north to the peat hollows, following the water's breath. Ducklings in their wake are bright and absurdly vulnerable, trailing in a line behind a mother who will hiss at any wader that comes too close. They nest, when they nest here, in trampled hollows among the sedge tufts, lined with down and reed, and the nest is abandoned as quickly as it is made. Their presence is episodic, a weather system of honking that passes, but each visit imports nutrients from elsewhere - seeds in their droppings, feathers, the occasional dead bird - and leaves them in the leached mud.

And then there are the plants, who are lives too, though they do not move to meet you. Each tuft of sedge or reed is a colony, rooted in water that would drown a forest tree, sending up trembling stems that can photosynthesise in the hard, hazy light and breathe through hollow tubes to roots buried in airless mud. Low salt-tolerant shrubs with thick, waxy leaves cling to the slight rises, conserving water in a place that is always wet but never rich. Small rushes flower inconspicuously and set seeds that float. Between tufts, for metres at a time, there is only mud and water reflecting the sky, and that bareness is not failure - it is the earth being too old, too leached, its minerals washed out into the lagoons long ago. A few solitary trees stand far apart, gnarled, hollow, roots in black peat, canopies thin enough that light falls through. They are old individuals in a young-looking world. Woodpeckers or small birds may nest in a hollow. Ants may farm the dry bark. Fungi rot the heartwood from inside, turning a dead limb soft until it falls and becomes peat again, layer upon slow layer, the black ground that stores the whole place's ages.

Even the dead wood and the dead animals are inhabited. Where a limb falls it is colonised in weeks by beetles, by fungal mats that spread invisibly through the mud, by the same shrimp and tadpoles that will scrape it clean. Decay here is not waste; it is the main road by which minerals return to the algae film, and algae to animals, and animals to mud. In the peat hollows the process slows, layering, compressing, so that a footprint made today - a crab's track, a wader's three-toed print, a terrapin's drag - softens at the edges, fills half with water, and may stay legible long enough for the peat to close over it. The inhabitants are, without knowing it, writing the record of themselves into the ground.

If you stay still long enough, the plain forgets you are there. The frogs resume their throat-pulsing beside your ankle. A shrimp cloud reforms around your reflection. A crab emerges from a burrow you have been standing a metre from and walks, sideways, past your foot as if you were a stone. A dragonfly lands on a sedge you are holding. The wading bird far out on the pan lifts one leg, places it, lifts the other, and continues its patient search. Nothing flees far, because there is nowhere far to flee to, and nothing needs to. The water is always near, the food is always thin but always renewed, the temperature is always warm and damp, the light is always hazy and ordinary.

It is a community that has finished becoming, in the same way the landscape has. No one here is new. No one here is in a hurry. The lives are small and spaced and loud and half-hidden in water, and they keep each other going in a slow, closed, singing circuit: mud to film to shrimp to fish to frog to bird to dead wood to mud again.
