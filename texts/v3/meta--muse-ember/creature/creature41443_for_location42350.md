At first there is only the floor, and the floor is alive.

You do not see it move. If you stand still in the dull red light from the fissures, with the heat already drying your teeth, the place looks like a pavement of stone worked into stone - pillow-basalt and frozen obsidian waves, dusted white with ash. Everything is grey-brown and grey-white and smoky amber. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is wet, or green, or soft. And then, very slowly, a section of it breathes.

A ripple goes out from the warm centre toward the walls, as if a hand had passed under the surface. Individual hexagons, each no larger than your palm, lift and settle and lift again, in a wave too slow to track with the eye, faster than the heat-tide itself. They are not stone. They are the stone's eaters, and the stone's skin.

The pavers cover everything. Floor, lower walls, the vaulted ceiling in broad hanging sheets, the leaning slabs of the old half-ring, the outside of the domes. Thousands upon thousands of them, edge to edge, interlocked so tightly no wind could pass between two. Up close one is a flat, dull hexagon with a faintly raised centre and a fringe of tiny pressure-feet around its rim, gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, in a slow peristaltic shuffle that never quite gets anywhere. Its underside is doing the work.

With a hot film along its belly - not wet, never wet, but a vapoury enzyme-sea that lives only as a shimmer of chemical heat - it dissolves the basalt it stands on. Grain by grain the rock softens, slurries, is taken in. What it cannot use it bakes and excretes upward as the flour-fine ash that coats your boots. Paver eats rock and makes ash; ash settles, is eaten again by a neighbour. When a paver has fed long enough and grown too large for its place in the tiling, it does something very quiet and very final: it splits down the middle. Two smaller hexagons where there was one, and instantly the new seam is colonised, the new edges begin to dissolve. The whole chamber is a slow, tessellating growth, a living pavement that reproduces by dividing, grazing its own house down to powder and paving again with itself.

It is against the seams, in the narrow living cracks between pavers, that the next life finds its place.

The glints are specks no larger than a grain of rice, bright as polished metal in a world that has had colour cooked out of it. They do not walk so much as dart - slowly, thermally, one burst of heat in a sealed sac and they are a hand's breadth away, then still again, gleaming. They live in the interstices, in the grooves, in the shed-scale and the waste that pavers cast off as they shift. A paving paver molts; a plate curls and flakes; a glint is there, eating the flake before it can fall. When the ceiling pavers molt, there is a dry, silent snowfall of their shed plates that never melts, and midway down, in mid-air, the glints swarm to it like sparks, cleaning it out of the dark before it lands. They are the chamber's cleaners, its scavengers, its bright restless punctuation. If you watch one long enough you realize it is not moving toward food. It is moving away from cold. Heat is its compass, its hunger, its home.

On top of the pavers, riding them as a turtle rides a slow sea, are the hearthbacks.

A hearthback is a dome up to a metre across, a fused carapace of translucent glass segments in grey and smoky amber, each segment thick, each junction sealed. It moves, if that is the word, once in an hour, if that often, from the cooler rim of the Vault toward the warmer centre, and then, after the heat-tide turns, back again. It does not step. It eases. Its many small feet - or its fringe, or its belly - presses and releases in a wave that the pavers beneath it feel and answer, rippling reluctantly away around its weight.

Inside the dome are slag-organs, dry chambers of salt and hot trapped gas, that soak up the heat of its passage. As it rests the glass cools, and it ticks. A faint, contented ticking, like a cooling stove, like knuckles settling. That is the only sound it ever makes. It has no eyes. It has no mouth you would recognize. It breathes, if breathing is the word, through slits along its sides that take in the thin sulphurous vapour rising perpetually from the fissures. Everything in the Vault breathes that vapour. Nothing drinks. Nothing sweats. Nothing can afford to be soft.

And a hearthback is itself a world. Its back is a landscape of grooves and ridges between glass plates, and in those grooves live things that never touch the floor: glints by the dozen, juvenile pavers no bigger than a thumbnail splitting and re-splitting, and tiny grey mites that scuttle nowhere, only shift from the warm side of a groove to the cool side as the dome warms and cools. The hearthback carries its own pavement, its own tide. Young ones, newly split from a parent's shadow, sleep clustered inside the seven waist-high domes scattered through the Vault - domes built long ago from pellets excreted by hearthbacks themselves, baked and stacked by a chimney-builder that seems to have vanished, or merged, or been absorbed into the general press of life. The domes are hollow and cool inside, nurseries in the dark, while outside them the adults make their endless slow pilgrimages to the centre and back.

They are not the top of the food chain here. There is no top. There is only a cycle, and the cycle has hunters.

The ribbons live in the cracks between the floor-plates. Two or three paces long, translucent yellowish, flat as a poured ribbon of heat-haze, legless, without a head at all - only a tip at one end that is warmer than the rest, that feels for warmth the way a blind thing feels for a wall. They flow. They do not run, they do not swim, they pour themselves through fissures too narrow for anything else, feeling ahead for the particular glow of a hearthback's glass dome as it warms.

When the tip finds it, the ribbon rises, wraps. First one coil, then another, around the dome, tightening with a patience that has no urgency in it because there is nowhere else to go. The glass, for all its thickness, is brittle. It cracks, eventually, with a sound like dry knuckles breaking - the only violence in the Vault, and it is very quiet. The ribbon then reduces the hearthback, entire, to powder and slag. That powder filters down through the pavers, is dissolved and eaten again. Rock to paver to hearthback to ribbon to rock. Nothing leaves the chamber. Nothing is wasted. The two mouths of the Vault - the throat you entered by and the high grey fissure at the far end - are not exits for life. They are only for the thin wind of the summit to die in, and for heat to breathe.

Ribbons withdraw when you come near. They do not flee. They sigh, a soft displacement of hot air, and pour themselves back into the cracks, tip first, and are gone, leaving only a ripple in the pavers where they slid.

That is everything, and it is overwhelming, because there is almost no bare stone to be seen. Life is arranged here not by water and light, as in other worlds, but by temperature. The centre is too hot to stand on barefoot. The walls and roof are only uncomfortably warm. The pavers tile both, but the glints favour the seams where warmth gathers, the hearthbacks commute between rim and centre with the heat-tide, and the ribbons follow the hearthbacks, and the whole living floor exhales every few hours as the fissures brighten and the red seep-light pulses like a sleeping heart. Then it dims, and the migration reverses, a handsbreadth at a time.

They speak to each other, after a fashion, but not in sound. A paver warms a patch of its own shell a fraction of a degree; a hearthback settles its weight a little more heavily on the stone; a ribbon presses its flank against a plate. The signals travel through the floor, felt in the stone, not heard in the air. The Vault is mostly, profoundly quiet. The basalt swallows sound. What you hear, if you stand still long enough, is the faint dry shingle of pavers shifting against each other, the dull knock of a ribbon's kill far off, the hiss of gas escaping a pore, and through the high fissure, very distant, the wind.

Above you, upside down in the dark, hangs a second pavement, grazing on stone that has never seen a sky. Its shed plates rain down and never melt. Its glints swarm in midair. Every few hours its ripples answer the floor's ripples, and the whole chamber - floor, walls, ceiling, domes, the ancient leaning half-ring of slabs too smooth and regular to be natural and now too weathered to read, paved over at the base by patient generations - breathes as one packed, hot, dry, finished world.

Nothing in it is green. Nothing in it is wet. Everything in it can be cooked, and dried, and cooked again. Everything in it is eating, or being eaten, or waiting for the next tide of warmth to move it a handsbreadth toward the centre, or away from it.
