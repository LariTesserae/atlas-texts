You do not find them where you look.

You came in looking up — for a bird crossing that black underside of sky, for an insect lifting from the standing water, for a fish flickering under the film on a square. You look in doorways, sensing the dark has weight there, as if something might be pooling with it. There is nothing with eyes. There is nothing with wings, nothing that rises to bite, nothing that calls. Your own breathing is loud in comparison, and the walls give it back to you late and dampened, as if they have been breathing for a long time and are only now allowing you to catch up.

So you lower your gaze, because everything here is low. Not low the way a roof is low. Low the way life, if there is any, would have to be to stay wet and warm.

The first sign is not a shape. It is that the water in the lower streets is never still, and never in the way water is still.

Kneel, if you can bear the heat coming up through your knees, and put your hand near the surface without touching. The film that mirrors nothing and everything moves. It creeps against the thresholds it should obey. In one square it rises and in the next square, joined to it by an open channel, it falls, and the difference holds for hours. That is not only the city slipping. That is a skin.

It lives in the top millimeter of the water, where heat from below meets condensation from above. It is colorless in the thin dirty light, until you shift and the light catches it in a slow, oily sheen, like a lung expanding. It eats what the ground gives off: dissolved basalt, sulfur from the deep vent that makes the basin breathe, salt brought up from the stone and left on ledges overnight. It does not photosynthesize. There is no light to steal. It chemosynthesizes off the warmth itself, fixing heat into slime, and it pulses in time with the ground's heave. When a threshold lifts a finger's width, the skin tightens over a new shape. When the heat wave thickens the condensation on the east walls, it thickens here too, and the whole square seems to exhale.

It has no mouth you could point to. It absorbs. It spreads by the same creep it causes. A footprint you make fills, and the skin closes over it, remembering the step a fraction late, the way the splash does. That delayed sound is not the water remembering you. It is them, displaced, reforming.

And because two cisterns hold water at different levels and no looking will make them agree, they are not one population, though the channel between them is open. Each vessel holds its own skin, its own timing, its own thickness. They have never mingled. A drip falls and makes a ripple outward from a point before the drop has arrived because the skin was already trembling in the place where the drop would land, waiting for heat, for weight, for a change in the air. They live in the discrepancy. The disagreement between levels is their habitat.

From the water, your attention goes, inevitably, to the walls, because the walls are weeping and the weep has tracks.

Follow a bright track down from a high damp patch to the black base where it vanishes. Where the track pauses, where it fans into a crack, where it darkens the mortar to near-black, something is growing sideways.

It is not your pale reeds, though the reeds have learned the same rule. It is lower and slower than moss, more animal than plant and less than either, a mat of fungi and algae and bacteria living together on damp lime and fired mudbrick. Their veins are the lichen you saw, the black algae, the fungal mats. They grow toward moisture, toward sound, toward the heat in the stone, rather than upward, because upward is only more dark.

In the day, or what passes for day under that thin light, they bloom white. The salt that blooms overnight on statue shoulders is not only salt. It is their fruiting, their exhalation, a crust they throw off when the heat thickens and then dissolve at noon into brine that finds the drains. The facade that sighs and leans a degree further into the street over a year is not falling. It is being eaten from within, selectively, at the exact angle where water seals it again. The ticks you hear in the stone when it expands are not only stone. They are hyphae splitting, spores, tiny ruptures as the mat drinks a cold sheet of condensation and swells.

They do not move in any way you would call moving. If you stay long enough in one courtyard — long enough that you cannot say if it is a minute or an hour, because the light has not changed and your sweat has not dried — you will see that a patch that was at the crack this morning is at the crack and a little beyond it this evening, having consumed the mortar there and left it grittier between your fingers. The doorway blocked by the floor of the room above it, the staircase that ends at a ceiling born later, the pavement under pavement: the city is layered because they are layering it, from the inside out, with their own patience.

Underneath the pavement, in the saturated ground a few centimeters down, where every footprint fills, is the next thickness.

You feel them before you see them, which is to say you never see them. As you walk, because there is no other way to move, and the city permits walking and forbids anything faster, the heat radiates up through the soles of your feet. In places it pulses irregularly, not like a heart but like a digestion. That is them, turning in the warm mud under basalt slabs. Pale, blind, translucent worms and woodlice-like things, elongated to the drag of water, having no eyes because there was no light before you arrived and there will be none after you turn around. They navigate by wetness and by gradient. They swallow heated silt and pass it, and in passing it they open and close the minute channels that let water rise in one square while it falls in another.

They are the reason the ground heaves in tiny pulses while you are still on it. A threshold that was level in the morning is higher by evening because a loose congregation has pushed up beneath it to feed on the film that condenses on the cool side of the wall. They are slow beyond animal time. One of them may cross a causeway in the interval between your southward walk in the morning and your northward walk in the evening, and arrive at the same plaza by a route that is not the causeway, because the causeway, like the water, is not reliable in length for anything that lives by slip rather than by distance.

When you step, they withdraw a fraction late. That late splash, that sound arriving after the sole has lifted — you disturb them, and the disturbed water, and the disturbed skin of living things, settle back out of order.

In the drains — cut stone, grown with salt, ticking against thresholds — they accumulate into larger forms. Not large in your scale. The largest you might ever sense is as long as your thumb, pill-shaped, soft as wet paper, living permanently curled in the dark where the water comes to an ankle and then, impossibly, does not come further. They filter the drip. They live in one cistern each, sealed by a difference in level no eye can reconcile, and though a channel links their homes, they have never met. Each population ripples at its own tempo, brisk and musical in one district, honey-slow in the next, a single drop every few minutes in the next.

They reproduce by letting go. A drifting piece of mat, a cyst of skin, rides the sheet of water that finds a new crack, evaporates where the ground heat touches it, condenses again somewhere else. The cycle is their migration. Rain, when it comes and sometimes falls upward out of the puddles in a fine mist, is their weather, and also their mixing. The mist beads on your upturned face and vanishes before it reaches the dark above because it is being taken back, cell by cell, by the air that is not air but a population of suspended things too small to see, waiting to fall — or rise — into a place where the level is right.

In the courtyard where the puddles have frozen in their ripples, where surface tension holds a single ring intact for as long as you watch, the oldest of them live. They are not frozen. They are in stasis by choice of conditions, their metabolism timed to the basin's slow breathing rather than to any clock. Heat comes in waves that are not weather, and with each breath the condensation thickens on the east and thins on the west, and they open and close like a tide with no sea. You cannot tell whether you have watched for a minute or an hour because they have made that unknowability a place to live. A sagging roof in the morning, intact in the evening, dry after wet — the same slab of basalt registers differently depending on whether the mat under it is feeding or resting. Time does not pass evenly here because for them, it does not need to. They have slowed to the speed of stone crumbling in balance with water sealing it.

There are no predators because there is no chase. There are no sounds because sound would be wasteful. What lives here lives by being indistinguishable from the processes you were told were only processes. Weeping is feeding. Ticking is growing. Salt blooming is breathing. The heave of the ground is digestion. The leaning facade is a slow animal turning its face toward moisture.

And if you stay longer still, if you let your eyes adjust to the dirty light and your hand learn the grit of mortar crumbling black between your fingers, the separate layers stop being separate. The skin on the water, the mat on the wall, the worms under the pavement, the curled cleaners in the disconnected cisterns, the suspended mist that falls upward: they are one regional thing, spread across a valley, a low basin, many districts thinning at the edges into marsh and mud and water again, far more roofs and arches across a distance the dark will not let you measure. From the air you cannot see, they must look like circulation. Streets are not streets to them, but conveyances their bodies have widened over generations, cutting and recutting the channels as surely as any mason. Buildings are not buildings, but warm, damp cliffs they have softened at the corners, rounded, slumped, sealed.

They have no name. There is no language here, and no need. They recognize you only as a brief change in heat, a disturbance in the film, a new source of salt from your sweat, a warm shape that moves too fast to feed on and therefore must be waited out. Your footprints fill with water and lose their shape. They close over you behind your fingers when you push your hand through the dark in a doorway.

You leave by the way you came, which is also a way you did not come, and the dark closes behind you as heavily as it closed before you. Behind you, a wall ticks. A reed bows under water. A puddle reflects a building that is not behind you, and leans with you a breath too late, and shows your face older, or younger, or with the hot sweat already dried — because for an instant you have been part of the skin, displaced and reformed.

It will go on. Not waiting. Not watching. Living at the speed of the city settling into itself, which is the speed at which, here, living happens.
