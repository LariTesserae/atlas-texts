They live first in the light, because there is almost nowhere else.

Not spread, not wandering, not hidden in any particular way that you could learn and then expect. Whatever lives in the pocket lives in relation to a crystal, and the distances between the lights are not empty the way the empty places between stars are empty. They are pauses. A broad panel of black stone with nothing on it is not uninhabited. It is the space a centipede is crossing, the space a seed has not yet reached, the space between two breaths. The islands look lush because everything that can be lush has gathered itself into them, and left the stone bare around them like a floor swept deliberately clean.

The plants are the oldest inhabitants, or they are the ones that act most like it. They do not so much grow as persist in a shape.

The star-rosettes are the first you touch. Each one is no bigger than your thumb, mauve and felted, and they overlap until the whole mat is as thick as a quilt and faintly warm from underneath, as if something were breathing slowly under it. The rosettes do not open for you. They do not close. Their edges are fringed with hairs so fine they catch the dust in the air and hold it there, a pale powder on every leaf, and when you brush one with a knuckle it gives off that candle smell, crushed leaf and hot wax and a sweetness like burnt almond. Underneath, running between them, are the pale fingers of the root mat, spread flat across the stone, dissolving it grain by grain. That is how they feed. Not soil. Stone. And the vapor in the warm air, combed out by feathery edges and waxy surfaces, and the faint light of the crystal they ring. What falls from them — a leaf, crisp and whole — does not rot where it lies. It is taken up again, leaf to root to fungi to leaf, in a loop so complete and so slow that from where you stand nothing seems to happen at all.

The curtain is another kind of inhabitant, and it is harder to tell where the plant ends and the animal begins. It hangs from the dome in sheets, vines and hair-fine strands falling straight down without swaying, dotted with hard pods like beads. None of them move in the still air. But every few moments one of the pods ticks, or pops, a small knuckle-crack inside the drum of the room, and a fine glitter of spores or dust or something smaller than dust sifts out and settles. The pods are sleeping places, and storage, and perhaps nursery. You can see, in some of the translucent ones, a darker shape curled inside that neither wakes nor dies.

The great stalks are fewer, and they are the tallest things in the room after the crystals themselves. Their bases are translucent, like wax left in the sun, and they lift fronds as wide as ears up toward the ceiling until the fronds touch the stone and stop. They grew until they could grow no farther and then they remained. On a still day, if there were days, you would say they were listening. The thin whistle that runs without a breath seems to come from them — air, pushed gently up and down by the breathing of everything else, passing through their hollow stalks and across their taut edges, making a note that never starts and never ends. The fronds themselves do not flutter. They hold their shape. It is the air that moves, and it moves the same way, forever.

There are shelves of amber fungi stacked like steps against the stone, round bulbs in tight bunches, and ferns whose plume-heads are feathery and dry to the brush of your hand. The fungi are the warmest to the touch and the sweetest in smell. They sit between the plants and the stone, taking the fallen leaf that will not rot and turning it, patiently, back into vapor and mineral dust. The ferns stand apart, plumes lifted, filtering. Everywhere the roots are not roots but mats of pale fingers, drinking stone, drinking light, drinking air.

Only the single fern standing alone far from any crystal breaks the rule, and it proves it. It is no bigger than your forearm, its plume dry, its roots spread thin across a polished panel. It has no lamp of its own. It lives on what the room itself exhales. It is the one that has wandered, or been dropped, and found that it could stay.

The animals are not separate from this. They are the other half of the same staying.

On the stone, the pale centipedes move. You hear them long before you pick them out — scrape-scrape-scrape, thousands of tiny brushes on rock, in a rhythm you cannot follow and also cannot escape. Up close they are slow enough to seem part of the floor. One foot, then another, placed with terrible deliberation, a segmented body the color of warm dust carrying more dust on its back. They are crossing the empty panels, always. Not to get somewhere. A circuit of the pocket might take them a lifetime, and a lifetime here has no visible edge. They carry mineral grain from the bare stone to the edges of the root mats, they carry a fallen leaf to the fungi, they carry their own waste back to a place where a pale finger will dissolve it. Their passage is the only work that looks like work. When one passes over your boot you feel the scrape through the leather and it goes on, unhurried, into the dark between the islands.

Around each island hangs a faint cloud of tiny flying things, a glitter of wings. They rise a little, settle a little, rise again in the same place. There is no wind to carry them from island to island, so their flight is a short vertical commute between leaf and air, leaf and air, perhaps carrying pollen, perhaps carrying spores, perhaps only stirring the vapor a fraction closer to a leaf that can drink it. Their wings make the smallest part of the ticking. 

The hoppers sit on the broad leaves and do not move for hours at a time. They are translucent, so you see the workings inside them, a slow churn of wax and resin, and a small light burning in each belly. The light is not their own, exactly. It matches the color of the crystal beside which they sit — pale blue-violet here, greenish there, weak amber by the fungi shelves — as if they had borrowed it and would lend it back only by moving. When one does hop, it is a sudden, soundless arc to another leaf on the same island, impossible small, and it lands and becomes still again, its belly light unchanged. They look like glass animals. They are perhaps the clearest sign that nothing here is made of water. The light is held in wax. The gut works in wax. The hop is a decision made in wax, very slowly.

The ceiling is alive in a different way. The hanging things cluster in the crystal light, high on the dome, where the warm air rises — if air can rise in a room with no draught — from everything breathing below. Blind, moth-like wings folded tight, long-legged things with too many joints clinging head-down, their legs interlocked like latches, thorn-like creatures that at first are only part of the rock until one antenna twitches. That twitch may be the only movement you see from one of them for a long time. They hang there drinking the vapor that the floor exhales, breathing it back down again. They are the roof population, the ones who never touch the stone.

And then there are the ones you cannot place, and perhaps that is the point. A fern turns out to have a row of eyes along its stem, small dark beads that do not blink, and they follow your shadow as you pass, not with any alarm but with the same fixed attention the crystal gives. A moss that looked like a cushion lifts on a hundred hair legs and walks away, slowly and completely, when your shadow falls across it, leaving a clean print on the stone that will not fade. Some animals look like plants and some plants look like animals, and after a while you stop trying to decide which of you is the intruder and which is the furniture. The moss wanted shadow. The eye-fern wanted to watch. The thorn wanted to be mistaken. Each choice is a way of living in a place where nothing changes and everything is already in its place.

Because they all breathe together.

That is the boom you feel in your teeth and in the soles of your feet. Not perfectly, not like a machine, but with a slow shared swell — in, and out, and in, and out — a tide of warm air pushed against the dome again and again, making the crystals hum and the hollow throw every scrape and click back at you louder than it was made. The rosettes breathe through their felted hairs. The centipedes breathe through the soft places between their segments. The hoppers breathe through their translucent skins, and their belly lights pulse, if they pulse at all, with that same swell. The ceiling things breathe down into the islands. The fungi breathe out the vapor that the leaves comb back in. The air is breathed and rebreathed, second-hand, thousandth-hand, until the smell of crushed leaf and hot wax and burnt almond is not a smell of any one creature but of the room itself, inhabited.

When you hold your breath to listen, the breathing goes on without you. When you move, the sound does not follow you. It is simply there, at the same volume, everywhere, because the inhabitants are everywhere the light is, and also in the dark between the lights, and they have made a single breath out of many.

In the shadow between two islands, where the crystal light falls off and the room returns to bruised violet dark, is evidence of the one inhabitant who does not fit the loop neatly. A low dome woven of plant fiber and resin, no higher than your knee, with a narrow opening. A perfect spiral trench cut into the dust. A neat circle of small stones. A cairn of pebbles stacked patiently. Half-buried in powder near a crystal island is a tool: a stone flake struck only a few times, a bone awl, a length of cord twisted from fiber, a cracked clay cup no bigger than an egg. And on a crystal face beside it, cut with the same patient blows, a row of tally marks, identical, side by side, dozens of them, none different from the next.

Someone primitive was here once, or something that used primitive means. Hand tools. Simple craft. A shelter. A mark to count a thing.

You do not see it whole. You see where warmth would have kept the dust from settling, a handprint in resin on the low dome, a place on the stone worn smooth where something small sat for a very long time facing the islands. It may be warm-blooded, in a way the others are not. It may have eyes made for this violet dark. It may have been the one who brought the tools down through the crack, or the one who learned to twist the curtain-vines into cord and to fire the cup from the amber clay near the fungi. It eats, perhaps, the warm resin of the rosettes, and drinks from the cup, and sleeps in the woven dome with its knees drawn up, and wakes to count the breaths.

It counts them because they are all there are to count. A season does not turn. A leaf does not rot. A crystal does not fade. The only event that repeats is the swell of breathing, and the scrape of the centipedes coming round again, and the tick of a pod, and the whistle through the stalks. So the tally marks are identical. Not from carelessness. From accuracy. Each mark is a breath, and each breath is the same as the last, and the maker cut them side by side in a row that goes on and on, trying to make the invisible arithmetic of the room visible, crafting a calendar out of sameness.

Whether it is still there, in the shadow, holding its breath to see if you will notice it, or whether it left its tools and went back up through the crack and never returned, or whether it stayed long enough to become part of the stillness itself — slow, wax-skinned, crystal-lighted in the belly like the hoppers — you cannot tell. The room takes its work exactly as it takes everything else and alters nothing. The cup does not fill with dust. The cord does not fray. The tally marks do not weather. They wait, as the fallen leaf waits, as the dust mote waits in the crystal light exactly where it hung a minute ago and exactly where it will hang an hour from now.

That is who lives there, in the end. Not a species. A gathering. The rosettes and the curtain and the great stalks and the fungi and the ferns, drinking air and stone. The centipedes scouring the panels. The hoppers borrowing light. The flying glitter rising and settling. The blind roof-hangers drinking vapor. The eye-fern watching. The walking moss retreating. The counter in the shadow, making marks for a time that will not come.

None of them grows in a way you can see. None of them dies in a way you can see. One rosette fades beneath another. One centipede lays its dust down and another takes it up. One pod ticks and another is ready to tick. The form remains. The pocket remains, hidden and complete, fifteen paces across, loud as a held chord, warm as a body, lush with islands in the dark between them, and full — crowded, breathing, scraping, ticking, whistling — with life that has learned to run without water, on wax and vapor and stone, in a loop with no beginning that you can find, and no end that you can see.
