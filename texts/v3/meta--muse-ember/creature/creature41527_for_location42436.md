You meet them first with your ankle.

Not a single plant, not a flower to lean over and look at. A mat. Moss, ankle-deep in places, knee-deep where it has piled longest over a seam or a tile still doing its slow work, springy and damp and reluctant underfoot. It takes your weight and gives it back over minutes, as if it were considering whether to. It is grey-green, the green of a thing that has never been in sun and has forgotten to try, pale and translucent at the growing edges. It holds cold. It holds damp. It holds, under its surface, all the rest of them.

For they are not individuals in the way anything above ground is an individual. They are presences, layered. Push through the moss and you find the next layer already there, hundreds of presences in a single square meter, overlapping, sheltering each other, leaning on each other because leaning is cheaper than standing alone.

The low ferns come first in height, if height is the word. A frond unrolls no wider than a hand, then another beside it, then another in the exact same spot a season later. They are soft, almost translucent, turning up not toward light — there is none to turn toward — but toward a gradient. Toward a degree less cold. Toward the faint unreliable warmth of the conduit along the wall, or the tray, or a place where the air above the moss is a fraction warmer than the air among the roots below. They do not move quickly enough to see. If you sat with one, you would notice it only in retrospect, a month later, a year later: slightly more open than it was.

Beside them, and through them, the clubmosses stand upright like miniature towers, soft green spikes no taller than your thumb, each one a small architecture of packed leaves. They do not branch the way a tree does. They multiply by staying. A tower thickens at the base, a new shoot rises from a rhizome bulging like a knuckle under the moss, and the old tower remains, leaning, still alive. It is how they store what they can coax from stone: not in a trunk, in a knuckle, in a root.

And then, slower than both, the broad-leaved shoots. Waxy leaves, cupped enough to hold a bead of water that swells and does not fall. A leaf folds, a leaf unfolds, over timescales that belong to the place rather than to a year. A leaf unfurling now might not be fully open until a child born above ground is old enough to be told, in a low voice, that this pocket exists. Leaves fall straight onto leaves below and do not rot quickly, because there is nothing here whose job is to rot them. They become black, sweet humus in place, and the next generation roots directly into its mother.

That is their family structure. There are no flowers that open for an audience. Occasionally a pale stalk rises and bears a small blind bud that opens to nothing and closes again, self-fertile, clonal, lean. Not bitter, not thorny, not fast — there has never been anything to eat them, and nothing has learned to defend itself against a mouth that does not exist. Without pollinators, without herbivores, without worms turning the soil, without any animal at all since the place was sealed, they have pared themselves down to the essentials: damp, cold, warmth in the right proportion, and mineral persuaded out of rock by filaments at their roots.

Seeds fall straight down. There is no wind to carry them. They land on the mother plant, or on the moss immediately below it, and wait. Some wait a very long time. Condensation comes one year in three to the right spot, a drip falls once in an hour or once in a day from the ceiling where warmer air meets cold rock, and the seed that has waited finds it. When it germinates, it does so exactly there. So a colony forms: a tight circle, generation upon generation, old rhizomes still alive under young ferns, a family that is also a single body spread thin across a square meter and then another. You cannot point to where one plant ends and the next begins. That is the point. They do not disperse, because there is nowhere to disperse to, and they do not need to.

Above you, the elders are the roots from the ceiling. Ropes and curtains, some as thick as a wrist, some as fine as hair, descending with great deliberation. One has grown under a crate and lifted its corner a millimeter over a decade. One has sealed a hatch it was never meant to keep. One has wedged a tile wider, and moss has sealed the crack again, and water, only damp, has polished it smooth. They are alive and reaching, imperceptibly, toward the drip and the warmth. If the moss is the carpet of the place, these roots are its chandeliers, its slow stalactites — patient, hanging, gathering.

Lower down, among the roots, it is colder still, and frost feathers itself across metal and rock in the farthest corners and melts without ever quite becoming water. The plants know this geography even if no one has ever written it down. They thicken where the trickle is strongest, where a seam in the wall is mending itself with nanolattice too fine to see, where a floor tile yields under your boot and then, over minutes, reasserts itself and sheds silt. They lean toward the conduit that should not be warm and is, faintly, persistently, warm. The current physics, consulted politely, gives up. The plants do not care. They have taken the warmth as a condition, like cold, like damp. The fed pulse in the soil — mineral filaments coaxing nutrients out of rock at a rate that should not be possible — is taken up by white root filaments that run bright along the surface before they dive. That, and damp, and time, is what leaf by patient leaf they fix carbon from. Never light. Warmth and mineral and damp alone.

Their days are not days. There is no dawn to open to. There is, over hours, a bead swelling under a leaf; over a winter, frost across a panel; over a lifetime, a colony expanding by a hand's width and then being still for another. The sound they make is almost not a sound: a leaf unfolding somewhere in the dense mat, too soft to be a sound, that the quiet presses in and then absorbs again.

And threaded through all of it, as ordinary as furniture now, is the other resident. It does not look like technology and does not behave like anything dramatic. It ticks, warms, filters, holds. It moves a little heat from nowhere in particular, holds the moisture in a careful damp equilibrium, mends the seams when a frost lens splits them. The plants grow densest around its edges, because that is where life is marginally easier. It is not alive, exactly. But it metabolizes, at the lowest possible rate, and has for a very long time. A trickle, sustained over centuries, is enough.

That is who lives there: no one who breathes, no one who bites, no one who flies or burrows. Only a crowded, luxuriantly plant green loop, cold and dark and damp, layered on itself, waiting out frost and drip and exile from the sun, and growing — leaf by leaf, knuckle by knuckle, family by circular family — into a place that was built to test whether anything would, and then was forgotten.
