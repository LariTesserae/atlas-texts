It starts underfoot, and it does not look like life at all.

Where the black plates are spotted — dull, amber-black, no thicker than a coat of paint — that is the first of them. It spreads across basalt and red shale and white salt alike, indifferent to what it covers, because it does not root. It has no leaf to turn to the sun. It eats the light itself, and iron leached from the stone beneath it, and it does it slowly enough that you could watch all day and swear nothing had changed. When it runs, the stone changes its temperament. A compass held low will hesitate and swing a few degrees off true, as if north has been pulled aside by something nearer and heavier than the earth. The ochre dust will not lie flat there. It gathers into a fine, clinkery grit, sharp to the touch, as if the pavement has already been smelted once and left to weather away.

That film is not a plant, and it is not a footprint, and it is not a stain. It is a metabolism laid flat. It takes in heat stored in the plates by day and releases it in its own slow schedule at night, so that even in shadow the stone stays too hot to linger on. It breathes inward. Salt crystals lengthen at its edges, gypsum crunches differently where it has passed, and the vapour that occasionally sighs from a fissure does not rise freely — it is caught, thinned, and worked over by the film before it can escape. It is the skin of the whole plateau, and it is hungry in the way a foundry is hungry: not for a meal, but for feedstock, continuously.

And it is not alone on the ground. It is fed, and carried, and stitched together by the second thing that lives here, which you will breathe before you see.

The air looks empty, and it is not. All day a drift moves low in sheets no more than a hand high, and in that drift there is a constant fall of heat-motes. They are too small to resolve as bodies — particles, spores, simpler propagules that are half fungus, half charged seed, half living spark. You taste them before you notice them: hot copper, ozone, a metallic tick against the skin like static before a storm that never rains. They settle on the film and on the bare stone, they carry charge from one plate to the next, they seed new growth of the amber-black spread where it has not yet reached, they stitch dust back into pavement grain by grain.

Their fields overlap everywhere. A single mote does almost nothing. Together they are the reason the emptiness feels full to the ear and the lung, even when the eye finds nothing for a hundred kilometres. Density in the eye, emptiness. Density in the ear and the lung, abundance beyond any meadow. They are the workers of the place, if a place so vast and solitary can be said to have workers. They do the small work of the foundry — transporting iron, venting excess heat, keeping the film charged enough to keep eating.

And then, far off, the roar you have been hearing without understanding it separates into individuals.

The mesas on the horizon are not all geology. Some of them breathe.

Once in a season, once in a decade, a range lifts its back a hand's breadth. It is not dramatic. There is no head to raise, no mouth to open in any animal sense. A flank that has been still for years — stepped, armoured with lag gravel too heavy for the wind, aproned with talus that has not shifted in centuries — exhales. Heat and iron-smelling vapour come out of fissures that are not cracks but intakes and exhausts, old and opened and closed by heat alone. The dust on its side slides in a slow, silent avalanche that takes hours to finish, as if the creature is shedding the very erosion that dressed it as a hill.

These are the Furnace-bodies. They are continental, solitary, spaced kilometres apart, and they feed on exactly what the film feeds on, only at a scale that turns feeding into industry. Ironstone, basalt, the sun's heat stored in the plateau, the deeper heat rising through it — they take stone in at one end, in the invisible sense of a process, and work it in internal chambers to temperatures that would shame a kiln. What comes out the other end is not waste, exactly. It is clinker, and fine magnetic grit, and more of the amber-black film, creeping outward over the pavement to begin again. Their metabolism is a foundry because the foundry is the only human thing that resembles them: intake, chamber, glow, slag, repeat, without locomotion, without urgency, without end.

You will not see them move, and that is why you hear them before you understand them. The low roar is a bellows sigh taking a minute to complete. The grinding far off is a mill the size of a mountain turning over once and settling. The crackle in clear air, popping like high voltage, is charge jumping between their fields and the drifting motes. Metallic ringing comes up through the soles of your feet into your teeth when you stand near a vent, long after the glow in the fissure has already gone out, long after the flank rose. Cause and effect here lag by minutes. You feel the heat of a metabolic pulse on your face after the pulse is over. Their shadows have a second shadow, faint and thermal, that lingers where the stone no longer is and moves on its own slow schedule. Nothing rushes, except for the brief chemistry of a living vent spitting a bead of molten sulphur-metal that hisses, sets in seconds, and then cools for years while the film reabsorbs it grain by grain.

Between the great bodies there are lesser ones, and they are life too, in the same industrial sense. A hot fissure that never fully cools is a throat. A salt pan that is a little larger each time you cross it — not because it grew between visits, but because near the vents the interval itself is not reliable, stretched by heat and charge and mote-density — is a lung that has learned to store vapour in crystal lattices instead of water. A patch of pavement that ticks as it expands in the day and ticks again as it contracts at night is a heart communicating through stone. A low mound that was not there in the last decade, film thickened and lifted and iron-hardened by motes, is a juvenile Furnace-body in the act of becoming a mesa. It will take decades to learn to breathe. It may take centuries to decide whether it will breathe at all.

That is how they die, if death is the word. A Furnace-body that stops — that no longer lifts, no longer exhales, no longer rings — does not fall. It settles. Its slopes find the angle they can live with and stay there. Its gullies choke with their own debris. Erosion, patient and ordinary, dresses it as a hill, then as a mesa, then as plain geology. The film continues to eat it slowly. The motes continue to carry its iron away. In a hundred years you could photograph it and see only a tiered plateau, paler with distance like all the others, repeating stratum for stratum to the horizon. Only the compass would still wander a little toward it.

They do not look at you. They do not need to. There are no eyes here, no ears tuned to a call, no legs to flee or pursue. The violence of the place is all in chemistry and noise and temperature, and it is carried in molten compounds sealed inside their own bodies instead of blood, in vapour sealed in salt, in heat sealed in stone. They sense each other the way fields sense each other — magnetic pull, shared charge in the air, the overlapping drift of motes between them — so that a breath taken by one on the far plateau will alter, minutely, the crackle over your head and the rate at which the film eats light beneath your boots. Kilometres apart, they are never alone.

Stand here long enough and the paradoxes stop announcing themselves. A hot place at altitude, under an ordinary white-yellow sun. A desert with no water cycle, whose life carries its fluid as liquid metal and sulphur. A mineral world with no green thing in it, whose food web is built not on leaves but on heat stored and released, on light eaten directly, on stone smelted to slag and slag weathered to soil that is not soil. An empty plain whose every cubic metre of air is busy. A wilderness with no road, no wall, no artefact, no bone that stays a bone — and yet working day and night at full production, because the production is the life.

You are not standing on the ground of their country. You are standing on the floor of the foundry, and the foundry is breathing around you, so slowly that the horizon moves only when it decides to, and so vast that the roar which is its breathing has become indistinguishable from silence.
