The living things here do not announce themselves either. You have to let your eyes go small, and stay at the edges, before anything registers as alive at all. In a place that has settled so completely, life is not a concentration or a movement. It is what has managed to persist, thinly, in the few places where the cold and the iron and the stillness do not quite forbid it.

The first sign of it is colour, and it is in the wrong place for a pond. On the north-facing wall, above the mineral stain, there is moss.

It is only a skin. In the places where the stone stays shaded and damp, a bright green has taken hold in irregular patches, no thicker than felt. Where the stone dries out, or where it is too bare and gets a little sun, it stops — not raggedly, but as if cut off by a rule. The boundary is abrupt. You can trace it with your eye: green here, pale dusty grey there, and no gradual thinning between them.

That is the whole of its world. It lives without soil, directly on wet stone, below the overhang of the rim where the cool air sits and never moves. The water below does not splash it. Rain does not wash it. It stays damp from the dampness of the wall itself, and cool from the cold that never leaves the pit. What feeds it is almost nothing: dust that has come over the rim, minerals leached from the stone, the faint chemistry of wet rock. It grows, but at the rate of stone. A patch that looks established to you may have taken years to get to the size of your hand, and it will not get much larger, because a few centimetres farther along the wall the conditions are already too dry, or too exposed, for it to continue. It does not flower. It makes spores, perhaps, in that still air, and they fall where they fall and almost all of them land on stone that will not keep them.

Lower down, where the shelf is shallow and the water meets the silt, are the sedges.

There are five or six of them, and they are the largest living things in the pit, though "large" here means a tussock of grey-green stems no higher than your knee. They stand in a broken ring where the margin is least steep, rooted in wet silt that is just awash or just above the waterline. They do not sway. The wind passes over the top of the pit and never enters it, so there is no movement in them at all, no shiver along the stems, nothing to carry pollen or to shake a seed loose.

Look at them for a while and their sameness becomes the point. They have the look of plants that arrived once, long ago, and have simply not died. The stems are thin and upright, a dull colour that matches the stone more than it contrasts with it. They do not thicken into a mat. They do not spread sideways to fill the narrow shelf, though there is open silt a hand's breadth away. They do not flower much — now and then a small, brownish head, unremarkable, that does not seem to lead to anything. A seed that fell into the water would sink into silt banded with rust and lie there in water that is cold all year and slightly acidic, and do nothing. A seed that fell on the dry bench above would dry out. So the ring stays broken, and the tussocks stay separate, each one confined to the exact dampness it can tolerate.

Their roots are in the same water that has defeated everything else: groundwater at a temperature that does not change with the season, loaded with dissolved iron from the spoil, offering almost no food. They take in what little there is and grow almost not at all. From one visit to the next, you could not measure a difference in height. That is not failure, exactly. It is equilibrium. The water level never rises to drown them or falls to dry them. The cold never lifts. Nothing disturbs them, and nothing encourages them. They are barely managing, and barely managing is enough to continue.

Under the surface, where your feet find the straight submerged bench and the flat floor, the third inhabitant is spread out in darkness that is not quite dark.

The algae are not in the water column. There is no bloom, no green scum across the top, no weed forest swaying below. They are a film, a dark crust, growing on whatever stays still long enough to be colonised: on the scattered stones and fallen boulders on the floor, on the damp stone below the waterline, and most clearly on the square slab of concrete lying under a metre of water. The slab's sharp edges and its four drilled holes are all softened and darkened by the same growth. The stones look older because of it, as if the algae were a kind of age.

It lives in the brief window of light. For most of the day the pit is in the shadow of its own rim, in that even, cool, room-like light. Only around midday does a pale patch of true sun move across the centre of the surface, and only then, through five or six metres of clear water, does anything reach the floor. The algae photosynthesise in that patch, for that hour or two, and then stop. That is its season, every day the same. It does not need flow, or warmth, or richness. It needs a stable surface, light that arrives predictably, and water that does not move enough to tear it away. The pit provides all three, and no more.

In the sheltered corner where the iron film forms and re-forms, the sedges do not reach and the algae do not spread across the surface. That rusty, faintly iridescent skin is chemistry, not life — iron oxidising where the groundwater meets the air. The plants keep to their margins. The moss keeps to its wall. The algae keep to their stones. They do not shade each other, or feed each other, or make a food for anything else. Nothing eats them. Nothing much dies to rot and enrich the silt. The rusty bands in the floor are not their work; they were laid down by the material itself, settling in thin lines. The living things are simply laid over the top of that finished geology, as a green skin here, a grey-green tuft there, a black crust below.

That is the extent of the population. No fish move over the silt to stir the algae. No insects land on the sedges. No frogs call from the margin. The moss does not thicken, the sedges do not multiply, the algae do not bloom. If you came back in November, or in June, you would find the same five or six tussocks in the same broken ring, the same abrupt edge where the moss stops on the north wall, the same dark crust on the same slab with its four holes. Their time is not the time of growth and decay that you see in the grass and bracken above the rim. It is the time of the pit itself: arrived, settled, and continuing at the limit of what the cold, still, mineral water will allow.
