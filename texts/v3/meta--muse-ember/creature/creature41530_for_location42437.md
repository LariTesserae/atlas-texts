If you look for who lives on the Vessik High, you will not find them in one place. You will find them in layers, in the order they learned to stay.

The first residents are the ones that do not move much, and do not need to. 

In the thin skin of the plateau, where the peat is spongy and the water lives just under it, the moss lives first. It is everywhere the stone is old enough to hold still - north sides of rocks in dark colonies of lichen, dull green sheets over the runnels, a wet fur on fence posts and warm stone and the pale south walls of the buildings. It is not decoration. It is food, and shelter, and ground itself, still being made. With it, in lines where water gathers, the sedge grows in ankle-high tufts, determined and sparse, and the dwarf birch hugs the ground and refuses to be a tree. They have made their bargain with the wind and the cold: stay low, stay wet, grow only where the land has stopped arguing for a while.

In the shallow sheets that fill the peat between runs of sedge, the water-life is thick. On the warmer days frogs and newts are, as you saw, thick as spilled seed, a bright, startled scatter under your boots. They are not rare and they are not shy, because they cannot afford to be. Everything here is eaten, in rotation. The air above them stands up in clouds whenever anything warm stops moving long enough - midges, little biting flies, a living weather that hangs around a head, around a collector, around a tram stop where a person waits too still. They lift like smoke from warm stone in the afternoon and settle again. They live by the condensation, by the damp that never quite falls.

Above that, sparsely and at a great distance, are the birds who use the whole plateau as one room.

The stoop-hawks work in loose spirals, small and dark, so high they seem small not because they are small. They dive, they vanish into the sedge with a soft shock of wings, and they rise again with something wriggling. They nest in the grass they have just disappeared into, indifferent to you walking a hundred meters away, because a hundred meters is nothing here. The ground-nesting plovers are lonelier still. One calls, once, miles off, and is answered once, miles off the other way. They do not flock. The vastness has taught them economy. A call is fuel, and an echo, as you noticed, does not come back.

On the ground they all depend on, the grazers move.

The moss-calves are not wild, and not quite tame. Shaggy, damp to the bone, they go in herds that cross the moor like a slow tide, heads down, eating not grass - there is barely grass for them - but the biofilm that grows everywhere in the wet: on collectors, on platforms, on the warm sides of buildings, on the trams when they cool. They need the thickness of their coat against the ordinary, everyday cold. They need the film. They have learned that the city is warm stone that never quite dries, and so they come to the platforms to graze the edges, and someone has to shoo them off, politely, without excitement. It is a daily negotiation. The river sheep move with them in the long season-long procession, following the shifting channels that abandon one gravel bar for another, swimming and wading where the land is still deciding its shape. Hawk stoops, plover flutters up, calf herd shifts, midge cloud lifts - that is the tempo. Predator and prey, grazer and film, at a pace you only notice if you sit still and stop being the interesting warm thing in the landscape.

And then, in the islands, the people.

Vessik is urban the way a tide pool is urban - completely, but spaced out. A district is a dozen structures around a shop, a school, a tram stop, wet streets between them, lit windows that hold the light rather than reflect it. Then kilometers of moor. Then another district. You can walk all afternoon and see no one, and that is not an accident of your visit. The Highlongers - the residents you hear complaining, quite reasonably, about damp in the stairwells - live like that by design or by long accommodation. They are not lonely in the way a city person is lonely in a crowd. They are solitary at distance, communal in the cluster.

They are quiet people, too. They comment on the cold while they wait for a tram, the way people anywhere comment on weather, and then they save their voices. Their boots are wet by the hundredth step. Their breath fogs. Their fingers go numb on a bag strap and they have learned to glove and unglove without thinking. They wear for the damp not as for rain, which passes, but as for a habit of the world. They expect to be fogged.

They do not remark on the impossible things, which is the most telling thing about them. When a delivery cart clips a corner and the mark knits shut by morning, they do not gather to look. When condensation collectors with no pipes, no fans, no moving parts stand in the fog and thicken it clear into cisterns, a few degrees warmer than the air for no reason anyone wrote down, they check the level and walk on. When a road quietly re-routes itself around a new gully rather than being repaired, they take the new way. When after a heavy dew a new floor is simply there on a tower where yesterday there was only a roof, a child might measure it, a parent might put a bed in it. Nobody raises an ordinance about it. The city is still rising, a few millimeters a year, and the people who live on it have learned to build with that rise rather than against it - to re-drive survey stakes, to jack and settle, to let a building heal its own skin. They live in a place that is coming into being, and they have decided, collectively and without a meeting, that becoming is the normal condition.

In each district they keep the small furniture of urban life, because they are urban: a market that opens, a school that lets out into the sedge, a regulation about noise after ten that everyone honors partly because the plateau already swallows sound and they do not want to be the ones to fill it. The tram arrives without wheels and without sound except the soft tick of cooling, and they board it wet-shod and stand, and the moss-calves are shooed from the platform before it goes.

There are also the ridge-builders, who may be the most Vessik of all.

Along the slow construction front where piled basalt plates are advancing, silently, to anchor the plateau against sliding, there is life that moves with the front itself. No engines. No hum. Plates settle, settle, settle, a new ridge printed onto the edge of the high country meter by meter. The people who tend it live on it, or just behind it, in the newest rooms, in buildings that have arrived in the night as a floor. They are the ones who say the front is going to be loud next season, though it so far is not. It is a builder's joke, anticipatory and dry. They know how to live on ground that is not finished. Their tram stops move. Their neighbors, in a sense, are the stones.

It is hard, finally, to say where the animals end and the city begins here, and the residents do not try. The biofilm grazes on the buildings. The buildings heal like skin. The collectors breathe fog in and run clear. The midges live on the warmth those collectors keep for no reason. The hawks use the roof-lines as perches. The calves use the platforms as pasture. The people use all of it, and complain about the damp in the stairwells, and shoo the calves, and wait for the tram, and expect - all of them, in their different ways - to be rained on and fogged and grazed and walked over, and to go on becoming after any one visitor has gone.

That is who lives on the Vessik High. Not a population. A procession, set at different speeds: lichen in decades, sedge in seasons, frogs in weeks, hawks in a spiral, calves in a tide, people in tram-times, and the plateau itself, rising, mm by mm, underneath them all.
