If you come at midday, in that plain, straight light, you will swear nothing lives here at all.

Nothing lives on the open stone. That much is true. The polished slabs, warm under your palm and damp though it has not rained, are too finished, too hot, too wind-scoured for anything to hold. A beetle could not cross fifty paces of it without being pinned and dried. A seed could not root. So the first living thing is not where you look for a plant. It is where the stone is first betrayed by itself.

Where a slab dips a finger's breadth it sweats. A thin film, a bead that never quite runs off, a darkening that appears even in full sun. And on the windward side of every stone, where mist is pulled up the cliff and combed out, moss has taken that betrayal and made it a mattress.

It does not look like much from above — black-green, swollen, a stain. Put your foot in it and it sinks to the ankle and comes up green and cold, and stays cold. That cold is the point. Moss here is not decoration. It is a reservoir, and a nursery, and a whole low autofocus world compressed into the thickness of a hand. In the water held between its leaves — water that arrived as a three-minute cloud, as condensation, as rain lifted up from below — there are springtails that leap when you disturb them, mites slow as dust, tardigrades uncurling and recurling, rotifers turning in the film. They live and die in a day, in a pocket. When the sun returns and the moss closes and hardens, they wait in a dried curl, or as eggs, for the next dark smudge of weather to cross the table. They are the most numerous inhabitants of the plateau, and the least seen.

On the black-polished stone, where no moss can hold, another skin grows: algae and lichen in thin, iron-smelling films, so flat they seem part of the polish. They bloom only when the stone sweats, a darkening that follows the old water-lines. Tap them and they do not flake. They are the food of everything else that bothers to come out.

Where the bowl is deeper, where water is kept a little longer, the moss gives way to things that have to drink more openly, and therefore to be more exposed.

Ferns unroll broad as hands from cracks, too soft for this height, too green. They do not unfurl slowly. When a rain cell tears open they drink and open, and when the cell passes and the sun comes back hard on warm stone they fold their leaflets in and go still, as if listening to the stone dry. Low shrubs grow in cushions, beaten flat by generations of wind into dense gnarled domes no higher than your knee, studded with small white and magenta flowers. The flowers do not open for the sun. They open only when the stone under them is wet — which is to say, they open for fifteen minutes at a time, in white noise, and close again. No pollinator is ever seen for them. Perhaps the wind does it, or the beetles, or nothing needs to: the plant holds its seed until the next wetting and trusts the wind curtain will do the rest.

Rosettes are the strangest of them, and the most faithful. Fleshy leaves lie flat against the rock in a perfect spiral, always clockwise, around the exact point where the stone sweats most steadily. No exception has been found. The spiral is not a choice the plant makes each generation; it is inherited, fixed, as if the drip itself turns that way as it emerges. The centre is always damp, always a little warmer, and in that centre the leaf litter rots faster than anywhere else, sweet and fermented, smelling of crushed fern and warm stone. Fungi work that litter within hours: pale threads, small mushrooms that appear and dissolve in the same rain cell, feeding the rosette that feeds them.

Everything that grows here leans, flattens, re-roots. No stem stands upright for long. A root threads into a hairline crack and holds by its own weight; if the wind tears a fern loose, the frond will root again where it lands in a damp bowl, as an animal might right itself. Growth is not steady. It is burst and closure, burst and closure, timed to the curtain of weather you can watch approaching for a mile off as a dark smudge, hear before it reaches you, and then lose in light and shadow and heat again. The plants are not rooted in soil so much as attached to a climate that visits.

The animals, if they can be called that, are the ones that have learned to use all that noise rather than be erased by it.

In a moss pocket loud with dripping, a beetle the size of a thumbnail, matte black, moves with slow deliberation through the mattress. It does not run. Running would be blown. It walks as if the stone were ringing under it — it is — and it feels the vibration through its feet and pauses for the gust to pass. It grazes the algae film and the moss spores and the dead springtails. It is solitary, or nearly. You may walk an hour and see one, and walk another hour and see none. When the sun hardens the moss it wedges itself under a slab and waits, legs folded, for the next drum of rain to hide it again.

Under certain slabs there is a hollow, a dark space between stone and stone where rain that fell somewhere else is still travelling, drip by drip, for metres. That is a different country, and it has one resident you are not meant to see.

It is flat, earless, like a piece of wet leather with legs — palm-sized, many short legs, no eyes worth naming, no ears at all. It breathes so slowly, a flank rising, that you would miss it except that it only comes out when the rain drums hard enough on the stone to hide its own breathing. Then it emerges onto the black polish and walks, and its legs find the water-sheet by touch and by the low organ note the stone makes under its weight. It has no ears because ears would be useless here, and then some: everything on this plateau announces itself by vibration through rock. It hears with its legs. It drinks condensation, it eats the film of algae washed into the hollow, it eats its own shed skin. It lives perhaps for years under that one slab, coming out twice in your lifetime, perhaps. No other of its kind is known to live near it. The hollow is its whole territory, its sky, its sea.

Higher, and briefest of all, is the hawk. It does not live here. Nothing lives in the air here. It is thrown over the edge by an updraft off the cloud sea, screams once — a thin sound immediately swallowed by wind and stone-boom — and is gone sideways, riding the same lift that brings the mist. It may take a beetle from a moss pocket, or it may only be testing the wind that never stops. It never lands. There is nothing to land on that is not already taken by weather.

Deeper still, following the drips that fall long after the rain has stopped, are the things that never come to the surface at all: eyeless millipedes, water-whitened isopods, pale worms no thicker than thread, moving in the dark under the slabs along the exact line of the sweat. They are fed by what the moss above filters down. They travel slowly under the whole table, in a world that is always damp and never wet, always ringing faintly. When you tap a slab with a knuckle and the low pot-like tone travels out and dies, you are, without knowing it, striking their roof.

And the people? People do not live here. They have touched it, rarely, with simple tools, because to climb this finished, roaring height and leave nothing would be against the rules of the place. A cairn of three round stones, hand-stacked, now sunk to its second course in moss. A blade of hard dark stone, flaked by a few blows, wedged in a crack as if left for someone later. A shallow bowl hollowed by patient pecking, with a loop of frayed cord through a drilled hole, meant to catch condensation drip. Whoever made them came for water, or for a point, or to listen until the noise made thought impossible, and then went down again. The plateau polished their marks almost back to nature. No building lasts, no path holds, no fire can be kept against the wind that sings through the old drip-holes. If a watcher returns, it is once in a generation, to empty the bowl and add a stone, and leave.

So this is the population: a mattress of moss full of invisible leapers, a spiral of fleshy leaves turning always the same way around a sweat, a cushion shrub older than anyone can count flowering for fifteen minutes in a storm, a thumbnail beetle walking slowly under cover of deafening, a leather thing breathing under a slab to the sound of rain, a hawk that never lands, and below it all a drip that has been falling since the table was a lowland, feeding a dark underworld that never sees the ordinary light at all.

None of them live together in any sociable sense. They live apart, in pockets, by weather. Between one and the next there is often nothing at all — ten paces, fifty, two hundred of naked warm stone humming under your boots. Then the curtain crosses, the stone booms, the moss swells black-green, the flowers open, the beetle steps out, the leather thing breathes, and for three minutes the table is riotously alive. Then the sun returns, plain and straight, and everything closes again, and waits, damp under the skin, for the next drink.
