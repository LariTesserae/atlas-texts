At first you would say nothing lives here.

That is how it presents itself. A floor of wet stone too new to be dirty, a wall, a cloud deck for a ceiling, water that does not run away, and a green so thin you have to search for it in the cracks. You walk in and your own breath fogs back at you. The place looks uninhabited in the way a building site looks uninhabited at dawn — work stopped, tools left, nothing yet moved in.

Then the sound starts, or rather, you realize the sound was already there and the stone was only handing it back to you.

A tick deep in the rock, as if the mountain is counting. A hiss on the membranes. A high, clear tone that sways up and down with the dim. A drumming, low and irregular, that passes over the floor and is gone, and then comes back from the other side.

It is not clear, at first, what is making any of it. The drumming could be rain, except there is no rain at that moment. The high tone could be wind in a crack, except the air is still and thick. The ticking could be water, except the pools are too shallow to tick like that. The lights that come on in the darker phases — faint blue-white, slow, deliberate, traveling up something vertical — could be a trick of the wet stone.

You wait, because here waiting is a kind of listening. The basin is small enough that everything will come to you if you stand still long enough, and loud enough that stillness is not silence.

A swarm comes first, because swarms are what this place does instead of having one animal in one place.

They cross the flat in a column too tight, too fast, clicking. Mouse-sized and then not mouse-sized at all, because there are too many legs moving under a body that seems to have too many joints. Blind to the permanent overcast, they do not look where they are going. They read the stone by heat, by touch, by the vibration they themselves are making. Their feet are dry and loud on the wet rock, a patter that the walls throw back at you like rain on tin, which makes them sound heavier and more numerous than they are, which makes them run faster.

And then they are gone, evacuated out of the basin in the same pulse, across the low wall and down into cloud, and the floor is bare again and ringing. You are left with their absence as a shape.

Other things hang.

High up in the condensing air, where your breath fogs and your eyelashes gather water, there are drifts of ash that are not ash. They hang and hang, winged things at rest in the lift, until something — the light sinking a fraction, the hum changing pitch — tips them. They drop. Their wings buzz at a frequency that does not so much enter your ears as settle in your teeth, and for two seconds the whole basin is full of them, silver-grey, wrong-proportioned, blurring into each other. Then they rise again and hang, and are ash again.

In the pools, which are only a finger deep and never still even when there is no wind, silver flashes.

Pool swimmers, if that is what they are, do not turn. They reverse. They flash to one end, reverse, flash to the other, and then vanish through a crack in the bottom that is too narrow for them, and a moment later a ripple appears in a different pool, three meters away, where no crack is visible from above. The water in some pools bubbles coldly while the stone underneath is warm to your hand. In others, water rises on its own, very slowly, up black filaments planted in the pool, against downhill, and falls from the tip as mist. The swimmers ride that rise, or flee it. It is impossible to tell, at this scale, which way the use goes.

On the outer wall, beyond the drop, larger shapes move.

You do not see them clearly at first because the dim drains them to moss-green and black, and because they are mostly on the other side of the low wall, clinging to stone that is falling away into cloud. You hear them: a low, hooting chorus, call and answer, call and answer, rising and falling in a pattern that is almost speech if you do not listen too closely, and completely other if you do. They go up and down the wall in groups, slow where the swarm was fast, ponderous, deliberate. Their calls make the filaments hum in sympathy, or the other way around.

These are the inhabitants you can point to. But most of the inhabitants are not pointable.

Some are only visible when wet.

They walk across the damp stone in their hundreds, translucent as glass when dry, taking on color and opacity only where condensation beads on them. As they dry in the warm air they become clear, and then invisible, though you can still hear their feet the way you could hear the swarm. Some cast no shadow — there are few shadows here anyway — but make a sound-shadow, a dent in the humming where they pass, as if the air is briefly thicker around a body you cannot see.

Some leave tracks a second before their feet touch down.

You learn to watch the stone ahead of a sound, because the damp will print a small, dark, many-toed mark, and a fraction later the foot will land in it. Whether they are sensing, or the stone is sensing for them, or there are two of them moving slightly out of phase with each other, you never learn. The place does not explain itself. It only repeats.

They eat, but almost never anything green. There is almost nothing green to eat. The mats in the sheltered cracks — dark, rubbery, olive films upholstering the stone where dust has newly gathered — are thin, fast, and vanishing. A week later the stone is bare again. They are grazed, or washed, or simply not re-planted, and the animals do not mourn them.

They filter the condensing air. They scrape the microbial film from the fresh faces of rock as fast as the rock weeps it into being. They eat each other in a fast, formal ballet that never seems to deplete because it never pauses. Born, swarming, eaten, born again, all within the same warm, wet, dim day. You will see a column boil across the floor, and behind it, later, the same column, thinner, darker, composed of the ones that were behind the ones that were eaten. Population high. Biomass low. Motion constant. The basin fills and empties like a lung.

And interspersed with them, far apart, often not at all if you walk the whole floor, are the other inhabitants — the ones that do not move in pulses but grow in place, and hum.

Black glass filaments rise from pools and cracks, alone, in thickets, in perfect rings. They are smooth beyond any natural stone, and too smooth, and warm to the touch, and faintly vibrating, and when you touch one your hand comes away dry, as if it sheds water rather than gathering it. Water climbs them. Dust gathers on them and then sheds as light. In the darker phases they pulse: a slow sequence of faint blue-white traveling up their length, like a message being sent up, or received down.

Rings half-buried in the rising stone, perfect circles of a dark material that is not metal, ring when the swarm-drum passes over them, as if answering. Needle towers, slender as reeds, sway even when the air is still, and make that clear high tone that joins the dripping and the drumming in a chord that never resolves. Lattice shelters, not built but grown, whose frames open and close like lungs, letting the warm air in and out, membranes expanding with the changing pressure.

You start by calling them plants. Then machines. Then you stop calling them either, because they will not hold the distinction. They assemble themselves from dissolved minerals in the weeping water. They repair themselves as fast as the young mountain cracks them. They take in condensation and give off heat and tone. They grow like crystals, behave like organs, work like machines built by something that never had to choose between the three. They are still extending, a millimeter at a time you can watch if you are patient. The rings are still sinking as the stone rises around them. The lattice has not finished its roof.

This is not a finished technology deployed and abandoned. It is a technology still being born, here, and the animals are born here, and the mountain is born here, and they are all growing into each other.

That is why the built things are only part of the story. The low wall one stone high, built in a rough spiral as if for a windbreak someone lost interest in. The dome of fired clay half sunk, its door filled with weeping water. The square cistern, empty, lined with faint handprints the stone has grown around but not yet healed. A leaning roof on four posts, underside blackened. Human-scaled, ordinary, interrupted. Not ruins — ruins would be older, more worn. Partial remains.

Something came here intending a hamlet. It built three or four shelters and stopped. Its inhabitants, if it ever had them, are gone now, or were never numerous, or left no bones that linger — because here nothing lingers. The swarm uses the spiral wall as a turn. The pool swimmers use the cistern when it fills. The filaments lean toward the dome as if listening. The lattice breathes in the space under the leaning roof. Stone swells around foundations. Water seeps up walls. The built things are not being destroyed. They are being taken back by growth.

And underneath all of it, if you stand near a pool long enough, you feel a bass hum in your chest, matching the slow pulse of the stone's own warmth. The filaments are humming it. The mountain is ticking it out. The animals are drumming it across the floor. The needles are singing it high. It is not one sound. It is the place breathing in layers, and every inhabitant is a layer.

You could walk the basin in twenty minutes. You could learn every pool, every filament, every crack that sings, every place a swarm likes to turn. And it would not be learned, because by the time you returned the water level would have changed, a new seam would have opened, a filament would be a hand's breadth taller, a lattice membrane would have swelled, the light would be paler or darker, and the whole grey palette would have shifted, moss to black to wet grey again.

What lives here, in the end, is not a list of creatures and devices. It is a speed. A wet, warm, dim speed of becoming, in which animals are born to be eaten moving, filaments grow to hum, stone cracks to heal, water rises to fall as mist, and nothing erodes — everything accretes. The mountain does not wear down. The machines do not decay. The fauna do not leave middens. They are recycled while still moving.

It is a small place, a high place, roofed with cloud and floored with pools, filled with sound. And it is not finished becoming, and neither is anything that lives here.
