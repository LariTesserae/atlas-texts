If you lift the edge of the mat — and it resists, gently, like skin — the first inhabitants are not the ones you can see. They are in the water that is always just under the roots, that dark pulse under a skin of roots. Small, translucent worms rise and fall with it. They do not travel across the plateau; they travel up and down an inch of it, over days, riding the slow circulation of the place. When mist condenses and runs down through the mat they rise with it. When the leaves drink it back and lift it again, they sink, slowly, in the dark, and wait. Their whole world is that vertical inch, warm and black and breathing. Beside them, in the deepest root-work, fungi make the teal light. It is not for show. It is warm, faintly, and the root-animals below use it the way others use a moon — a fixed glow to orient by while they feel their way upward toward the moss.

Above that water is the surface of the place, a living skin of moss and liverwort and seedling and root that closes behind your boot. It has its own grazers, almost too small to count as animals if you came looking for something to name. Springtails and mites in their thousands move across it as a sheet, a living stipple a few centimeters wide, turning the green to brown and back to green as they scrape, endlessly, at the film of algae and fungus on the moss. They do not have territories so much as weather. A colony is here this week, gone, imperceptibly, next month, having eaten a patch down to its roots and moved on. The click you sometimes hear in the leaf litter far off is not from them. It is from a beetle-like thing with no edges at all, soft as a seed, that lives buried in the felt of a fallen trunk. It cannot fly, it cannot run, it has no sharp mouth. It clicks through the mat itself, tapping its blunt body against the root-work, and the sound travels — through plant, not through air — for distances that would be absurd in open country. Its mate, perhaps a tree’s height away, taps back. They close the distance over years, following the vibration the way the plants follow the sound of dripping water, until the two soft bodies meet in the damp dark and the mat closes over them again.

The slugs are the great travelers of this skin, though you would never know it. The color of wet bark, they can cross a single stilt tree in a lifetime. They move a body’s length in an hour, then rest, then move again, leaving a clean trail behind them where they have grazed the moss from the bark. That trail heals slowly, and seedlings and other slugs follow it, so that a slug’s life becomes a road. A mother travels one branch, her young follow her mucus for a decade, and three generations may live and die on the ribs of one vaulted tree without ever touching another. Old slugs go mossy themselves. Moss takes their shells first, then their backs, until what looks like a raised ridge of moss on a root shifts, very slightly, and goes on shifting. To be still here is to be grown over.

In the cups of leaves, an entire world can fit and never need to leave. The frogs that live their whole lives in the cup of a single leaf are no bigger than your thumbnail, translucent where the leaf is veined, green where it is not. They are born there, from eggs laid on the inner rim above the water, and as tadpoles they never leave the inch of water the bromeliad holds for them. The adults hop only from leaf to leaf of the same plant — and the plants here do that nested thing, a bromeliad holding a smaller bromeliad on its outer leaves, so a frog family may be confined to a plant within a plant, a cup within a cup, for as long as the plant lives. They eat the larvae of insects that fall in, and the insects that fall in are kept from fouling the water by the frogs’ slow, blunt tongues. When a leaf finally rots and sinks — everything here eventually sinks and becomes floor — the frogs do not migrate across the plateau. They move, one by one, in the damp dark, to the daughter bromeliad that has been growing beside it all along. Their species, as far as they are concerned, has never moved more than a hand’s breadth.

Below them, through them, under them, are the mammals that tunnel the root mat and never surface. They are blind and soft-pawed, with no claws worth mentioning, and they push through the plant mass by pressure alone, feeling for the soft places where roots meet and have not yet bound. Their tunnels are not dug and fixed; the mat creeps into them behind them and closes, so a passage is a temporary thing, a wave moving through the floor. They surface never, but you can sometimes see their passage: a low moss ridge that was not there last month, or a patch of seedlings that has suddenly tilted, as the family below has passed under it. They live in small, tight lines, mother and young pressed together, and they wander for generations in one direction, following the same patient routes the water follows, toward a sound, toward a warmth, toward a place where the mat is looser. An individual may travel farther than a slug in its life, but the family’s trail, traced over centuries, is a slow river across the plateau, opening and closing behind them. When one dies, the tunnel does not collapse. The mat simply takes it back, and the body becomes part of the felt, food for the fungi that feed the roots that feed the trees that will one day fall and make a road.

They are not the only ones inside. In the hollow stems of the tree ferns lives a croaker, far away and low, whose voice comes up through the plant like water through a pipe. It lives its whole life inside that one stem, in the damp hollow, and its croak is how the mat knows it is there. Nothing else in its life ever touches open air. The stem rots slowly around it, obligingly, and the croaker grows with the rot, a tenant of decay.

Higher up, the hanging curtains are inhabited in a way that has no ground at all. Vines rooted in mid-air — rooted, that is, in the branch above them, drinking mist and wringing it out along their length — make roofs that hang for decades. In them nest the darting birds, small, brown-green, built like quick thoughts. They weave their nests into the curtain itself, and as the vine thickens the nest thickens with it, growing stronger with the tree, until the nest is no longer on the curtain but of it. Generations of the same birds return to the same hanging place, darting out and back so quickly you think you imagined them, feeding their young on the moths and the algae-slugs that live among the wet leaves of the curtain. They never come down. Their world is a swaying wall of wet green a few meters deep, and they know every inch of it by touch.

Above them, in the broken canopy, are the ones that never land. They glide only between the top layers, blunt-beaked, heavy-bodied, trailing their feet in the undersides of wet leaves as they pass. They do not perch on the floor — there is no floor to them — and they do not have sharp anything with which to tear or seize. They hang to sleep, feet locked to a leaf’s underside, swaying with it. They feed on nectar from the white, waxy flowers that stay open for months, and on the small mist-moths they catch in mid-glide with a slow, scooping mouth, and on the moisture that beads on the leaves they drag their feet through. Their droppings fall, patiently, for years into the same bromeliad cups below, and the cup-frogs below, three layers down, live their whole lives in water enriched by birds they will never see.

The moths are the connectors, and they are transparent as mist. By day they sit motionless under a leaf, wings folded like veins, invisible unless the leaf moves. By night they move — slowly, fluttering, never hurrying — from flower to flower, carrying pollen between blooms that may stay open for the entire season. A moth will be faithful to one flower, one plant, for its whole life, which is measured in months of nights. A flower that stays open that long is not waiting for a crowd. It is waiting for one partner, returning. In the cold hollows between the canopy layers, where thin white weather drifts a few meters and settles, there are even shorter-lived inhabitants: filterers that live in that drifting cloud itself. They hang in the mist, mouths open, catching water and the spores and pollen the mist carries. A storm is born here, travels inland for a week, and dies of its own dampness — and that week is a complete lifetime, with birth and breeding and death, for the animals in it. They are born in weather, and weather is all they will ever know.

Nothing sharp, nothing fast, means nothing here hunts the way a hawk hunts. There are no real predators, only overlaps. The frogs eat the moth larvae in their cups. The birds eat the moths. The slugs eat the moss the frogs’ leaves depend on. The tunnel-mammals eat the roots’ sweet tips, and the roots, in return, grow faster where the tunnels have loosened the mat. The fungi eat the fallen giants, and the slugs eat the fungi, and the seedlings eat the slugs’ trails. A thing that dies does not leave the food chain; it becomes architecture. A fallen giant sinks, first a raised road that slugs and birds use for years, then a trench of black water where tadpoles from a dozen cups gather and grow, then a mossy ridge, then the upper floor of a new forest growing out of its own rot. The inhabitants move with it, stage by stage, a whole community migrating across a tree’s body as it decays, never leaving the tree even as the tree ceases to be a tree.

The fern patches do the same thing, only slower and entire. A patch on a far slope is not the same patch it was a hundred years ago. It has grown at one edge and rotted at the other until its center has migrated, foot by foot, across the plateau. Its frogs and fungi and click-beetles migrate with it, passengers on a very slow ship, bound to a plant that is itself wandering toward the sound of water. When the patch arrives somewhere, the animals have lived whole lives in the traveling.

At the stone places — rare enough to stop you, a low spiral of unmortared stones half-sunk, a single roofless room whose doorway is now a tunnel of roots — the inhabitants are the ones that prefer what does not grow. Slugs come to the stone because it holds warmth differently, because the joints hold water differently. The frogs settle in the spiral’s center where rain collects longest. The tunnel-mammals pass through the root doorway as if it were a natural gap, because to them it is. A lichen-grazer, soft and grey-green, lives only on the worked stones, scraping at a film that grows nowhere else, and it is the only animal here that could be said to farm a ruin. The stone does not grow, so everything that lives on it is, in a sense, living on a thing that is only wearing down, very slowly, toward the shape of the hills.

And at the edge, where the mat thins and the roots hang over emptiness in thousands, in a living fringe, and water pours out through them in veils that break into mist long before they fall to the cloud sea below — there are the veil-hangers. They are attached to the hanging roots themselves, facing outward into the rising mist, suspended filter-cups that sway a millimeter at a time, catching what the air brings up from below. They will never descend to that sea. Their children, when they drop, are caught by a lower root, or another, or another, until the fringe filters them back into the mat. The waterfall is filtered by plants, and the plants are filtered by animals, and the animals are filtered by the edge, and the edge is only a rumor from inside.

The plants are inhabitants too, and some of them move like animals in all but speed. The stilt trees descend their roots like ribs, lift, step, descend again, walking root by root toward dripping water they can hear through the mat for years before they arrive. A fruit that drops does not thud; it sighs into the mat and begins immediately to become floor, and the animals that live in it do not notice the transition. Roots meet underfoot with a soft pressure, feeling for each other, and where they meet, a tunnel-mammal will follow, and a slug will follow that.

No one here sees a horizon. A slug’s horizon is a tree. A frog’s is a leaf. A veil-hanger’s is a veil. A moth’s is a flower that stays open for months. A mist-animal’s is a week of weather. A tunnel-family’s is the distance between one soft place in the mat and the next, traveled over a generation. And yet together — the worms rising and sinking in their inch of water, the teal fungi glowing in the dark, the sheet of mites turning moss brown, the soft beetles tapping through the roots, the mossy slugs paving roads, the thumbnail frogs in their cups, the blind families pushing their slow river through the floor, the croaker in his stem, the curtain birds in their growing nests, the gliders trailing their feet under leaves, the transparent moths keeping their nightly faith with a single flower, the grazer on the stone, the filterers in the waterfall — together they are the warmth you feel in your chest, the damp exhale of the air, the breathing of the canopy as mist moves through it. The place does not contain them. The place is what they do, over and over, at a pace that can only be measured in moss growing a fingernail’s width in a year, in a path growing shut behind you in a generation, in a lifetime spent crossing a single tree.
