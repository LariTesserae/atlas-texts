You do not meet the locals by seeing them. You hear one long before you understand what it is, and when you first understand it, the sound has already changed meaning.

It starts under the wind. The wind here never stops, it only changes key, but between its gusts there is a slower, lower work: a grinding in the stone, a hiss, a clank you feel as much as hear through the soles of your boots, a ticking that rises for an hour and then releases in a flat crack of thunder inside a trunk. For a while you take it for the plateau settling, the way stone cracks in the cold and expands in the glare. Then you are closer, and the pattern is regular. Up in the day, down at night. Building, building, releasing. Something is circulating. Something is being smelted.

It is a plant. That is not a small qualification here. It is almost everything.

If you weighed the visible world, nearly all of that weight would be one plant at a time. A tower-plant is a hundred meters of hollowed bole on a fluted, buttressed base, its bark breaking into copper-red plates that curl away from the wind. Its crown is a spray of needle-leaves, so fine and dry and pale silver they make a sound you hear through the light itself — thousands of flutes at once, screaming as the gusts rise and fall. Its roots, invisible under the crust, probably run for kilometers, mining. Its crown runs for hundreds of meters across. It is solitary because it cannot afford company. One per valley, one per ridge, one per thermal. The space between them is not emptiness. It is the distance a giant needs in order to live at all.

Its life is mostly inside itself, and that is the second unmaking of the desert. The trunk is a silo, a foundry chimney, a ribcage. Mineral teeth grind stone to powder in a gut-chamber. Catalytic pockets smelt iron and copper from the dust it has taken in. Valves of hardened bark open and shut with a clank. A spiral of living tissue — a slow rotary pump — moves a black, resinous oil in a sealed circuit, up in the day, down at night. Along the inside of the bark runs a conductive tissue that stores charge all day under the white light and discharges it in blue arcs when the storms come, the whole bole lighting faintly from within. It does not drink. There is no liquid for it to drink, ever. It takes its hydrogen from the air and from bound minerals it cracks open internally, its carbon from that same thin air, and it keeps its oil from ever touching the surface. Production, mechanization, electrification — all evolved, all internal, all unwalled. Its metabolism is industrial because industry was the only shape that would work at this size, in this cold, dry, wind-scoured light.

Its breathing is in its crown. The needles flex, shed, regrow. Some are caught and re-woven, some are mineralized before they fall, waxy and fire-proof, turning to that thin crumbling layer of ash-and-loam underfoot that is not soil so much as what vegetation leaves behind. At dawn the plant is dusted white for an hour by carbon-dioxide frost that sublimates straight back into the air; by mid-morning the frost is gone and the heat is on the grit and the mat-plants around its knees begin to pop and crack as they warm, sporadic as distant gunfire. The trunk groans as it cools at night. It ticks all day as charge builds. It is a loud organism because it is working all the time, and because a hollow fluted tower is an instrument whether it intends to be one or not.

Around the knees of the tower, the work gets tenants.

The chitin-plated grazers are low, blunt, armored things that move like slow tanks through the mats. You hear them rasping before you see them clearly, their plates grinding against crust and bark plates. They eat the oily flesh of the mat, and the mat needs to be eaten. A mat-plant is a ground-hugging continent of its own, a tough gray-green skin that spreads between the towers, cracking in the cold nights, healing in the day. After a frost-fall it can spread meters in a single day, violent and brief; then it dies back to a hard scar for a year and waits. The grazers push across that scar, break it, turn it, keep it from sealing over completely. Their stomachs are part of the plant's slow recycling, though they belong to no plant. They get what passes for moisture here from the oil in what they eat. Nothing here quenches a thirst. Everything here is eaten, breathed, or burned instead.

Under the mats the life goes darker and smaller. Burrowers live in the drier litter of mineralized plant residue, in the root zone where the tower is mining stone blindly for kilometers. At night the ground itself clicks and chitters with them. You cannot see half of what lives here by daylight because so much of it is either inside a hollow trunk, under a crust, or strung along the woven branches above. The fauna, abundant where the plants are and absent everywhere else, is a shadow population — using the giants, eating them, nesting in them, vocalizing not in song but in clicks, whistles, and low resonant hums that the hollow plants amplify and give back.

An umbrella-plant lives that shadow in a different architecture. It is lower and broader: a short swollen bole holding up a vaulted canopy that is truly woven, not merely leafy. Branches interlace, shed needles are caught, new growth laces over old, until the dome is a set of passages, perches, shaded chambers. Inside, after the glare, it is cool and surprisingly dark. The wind moving through it does not scream; it booms, a hollow note you feel in your sternum.

By the hundreds, wind-gliders nest in those vaults. Sail-winged, membranous, paper-dry. All day they cling in the chambers like tenants in a cathedral, restless, whistling low to each other, the woven canopy carrying their hums around the inside of the dome. At dusk they launch en masse, wings rustling like paper tearing, out into the thermals that rise off warm stone and the warm heart of the plant itself. They ride what the plant throws off — heat, dust, spore, the brief yellow blot of a dust storm — circle the ridge in the failing black-blue light, and come back to the vault by memory and sound. Their young cling to the laced branches in the passages; their traffic is part of what the canopy is. The umbrella-plant does not just shelter them. It grows around them, sheds into their perches, collapses under its own woven weight at times and rebuilds, needle by needle, from the inside, with the colony still in it, shifting to the uncollapsed chambers.

That is how everything here lives: inside, under, around, and briefly above a plant that is doing its enormous slow work. A grazer's whole life may pass while the tower it feeds around its knees has only come to one grinding, one building of charge, one release. A glider's generation rises and falls in the time between two frost-falls remembered in the tower's fluted trunk. The burrowers click through the stone at night like a second, subterranean nervous system. They are not a food web so much as a maintenance noise in a factory that grew.

And then, occasionally, the factory reproduces, loudly. A tower does not drop seed. It fires it. The crown bursts with a concussive crack as loud as any valve or discharge, and seeds like iron pellets are driven into the wind. That is the only migrant stage most lives here ever get: a hard, mineralized sliver of plant, launched across the continental emptiness, to crack open a new patch of crust far away, to begin a mat, to wait years for a tower to rise over it. Whole branches shed like falling factories. Whole canopies collapse and are rewoven. A plant can live for thousands of years in that settled, weathered balance — nothing young, nothing fragile-looking, everything as if it had survived a long argument with the sky and reached an agreement — and still spend its age in constant creation and destruction, pulse and scar, grind and crackle.

At night the frost comes, the plants contract and groan, ozone hangs after a discharge, the air smells of hot resin and roasted dust and never of wet earth, and the stars come out hard in the thin black. Inside an umbrella, the sounds close around you: wind above, work within the bole, gliders settling with their whistles, grazers humming low somewhere out in the mats, burrowers chittering under the crust. Your own voice is swallowed by the vault. It is not silence. It is a colony and a machine and a forest, all of it one plant at a time.

Walk away and the noise follows you longer than the sight. The flutes thin. The grinding fades. You have grit, and cool cutting air, and vastness that can make its own weather over the span of a single mat. It looks like nothing lives here.

Then the wind shifts, and from the next ridge you hear the next crown screaming, the next heart working, the next vault rustling at dusk — already shaping the distance, already making the empty place loud, industrial, alive, and utterly, strangely plant.
