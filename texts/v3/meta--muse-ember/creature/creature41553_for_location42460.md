Under your feet, first, before you see it properly.

The moss does not grow so much as it settles. It is a carpet of needles, deep green and yellow-green, cool felt over stone that should have no soil on it at all. Yellow where it is newest, a hand's breadth a season, deep green where it has been listening for much longer. It spreads the way the mountain itself is spreading — upward, in a thin embryonic skin, precipitating the fine mineral dust out of the air and laying it down as mineral again. Every needle is a trap and a root. It splits nothing and holds everything. Between the groves there is nothing like it for miles, only salt and pumice and black glassy riverbeds, and then, without transition, this felt. The edge is sharp. You step from blinding white flat onto green coolness and the sound changes in your teeth.

Above it the ferns raise their leaves broad as doors. They lean toward a light that never strengthens, a grey-blue twilight smeared with rock-dust, and they answer it by turning their undersides pale. At night, or in the permanent dim, they give off that faint green-gold phosphorescence you can pick out from a far ridge — a lamp lit in a desert, though no one is there to see it. A fern does not hurry. One leaf in a season, unfurling slowly enough that you could stand inside its curl for years. Some are green before they have emerged from the bud. Some are already brown and perfect while they are still closed. They are never torn. Nothing eats them. There is no beak, no jaw, no claw anywhere to scar that broad surface, and so the leaves remain whole, waterless, un-I mean un-chewed, un-nested, un-burrowed-under. The air is loud with the mountain and utterly empty of animal noise, and the fern just opens, and opens again.

The trees are the next thing to become itself, if you stay long enough. Bark like layered stone, stacked plate on plate, trunks hollow from the ground to wherever a branch ought to be. They are not trees in the ancient forest sense; they are resonant chambers wrapped in bark that has learned to look like rock because rock is what it is made of. Their sap is dark, iron-rich, alkaline, smelling of crushed stone and ozone, and it does not rise the way sap should. It is worked upward by vibration. The whole uplifted range groans at a frequency you feel in your sternum, a pulse so slow it could be mistaken for a heartbeat or for the mountain flexing under its own weight, and the hollow trunk takes that pulse and turns it, patiently, into pressure, into movement, into growth. Stone pops somewhere far away and always close — a sharp report, then another — and by the time you hear the crack, the plant has already begun to feed on it. The sound arrives before the crack that makes it. They grow in anticipation of a noise the world has not quite made yet.

That is what they are, though the name comes late: sonivores. Sound, dust, and dim light are their diet, in that order. The vines that drop from the branches in thick curtains are part of the same listening apparatus. They descend, and root again in mid-air, in the haze itself, precipitating enough mineral around the root to make a footing that wasn't there. They hang and re-anchor and hang again, a curtain that is also a ladder, also an ear trumpet. At the grove's edge the reeds and dry pods stand thinner and higher, every hollow stem an organ pipe tuned to the mountain's hum. When the constant wind crosses the salt flats they sing high enough to hurt, and the reeds answer at the grove's own pitch, not the wind's. You cannot shout over it here. You cannot think in complete sentences for long. The plants do not need a sentence; they need the tone to hold, for hours, for days, unchanging, and hold it does.

And so they flower. Once, over fifty years, a flower opens — pale, phosphorescent, with no fly to come to it, no bird to carry anything from it, no insect to buzz in the leaves it took half a century to make. It repeats itself anyway. It sets stone-like seeds, black and heavy as river glass, that fall and split and grow in a way that is hard to follow, because sometimes the fruit was already ripe on a branch that had not yet grown, and sometimes the seed splits before it touches the moss, and sometimes the sapling is already deep green before the seed has fallen. In a grove, all the ages are present at once: seed, felt, unfurling leaf, perfect brown leaf still in bud, ancient hollow trunk, vine still dropping. It is not a life cycle. It is a draft copied three times across the basin with slight variations, a grove pattern repeating out past sight and then, disconcertingly, beginning again — the same island in three places, each one alone, each one intact.

There is no food web because there is nothing to eat and nothing to eat them. Fallen leaves lie perfect on the moss. Fallen seeds lie among the moss like black stone, indistinguishable from the flakes the people left. No dung, no bones, no tracks except the faint parallel marks of things dragged or placed. The emptiness between groves is not lack of life; it is the condition of it. The plants live far apart — a day or more of bare white salt punctuated by islands — because the sound carries that far, and stone lifts that unevenly, and because a grove this dense is enclosure enough. Inside, the light is greener and dimmer, the drone is in your bones, and the glow of leaf and fern makes everything look newly made. The cliffs outside are sharp and unsoftened and still bending, like clay pushed up from below, and the plants inside look the same: emerging, pre-formation, unfinished but lush.

The people, when they come, come the same way the plants spread — sparsely, primitively, and without connecting anything.

You find a cairn on a ridge: single stones stacked by hand, no mortar, a small tower of intention. The same cairn, impossibly, on a second summit. A half-wall in a hollow, three courses high, curved from basalt slabs and lashed with dark plant fiber, never roofed. A knapping floor on a salt flat, flakes of black stone in a neat circle around a half-finished axe hafted clumsily with cordage and resin, abandoned mid-thought. A line of stakes whose cord is long gone, marking a boundary that was never completed. No paths link them. No settlement gathers them. The tools are stone and wood and woven stalk, made for a place that does not reward permanence. Occasionally a wall seems to be still building itself, adding one stone in a decade, in fits no one watches.

They do not live in the groves. They pass through them, rarely, the way the wind passes through everything, always in the same direction. They do not drink the grove's dark sap or eat its leaves. They sit in the circle where the flakes are, they stack and lash and leave, and their absence is so complete that their marks belong with the other unfinished things — the bending strata, the unsorted scree, the fruit ripening before the branch. A structure ruined from the north is newly built and newly abandoned from the south. Your footprints in the dust seem to form a moment before you step. The people live in that same folded time, which is to say they barely live there at all, in any continuous sense. They are encountered as a cairn, then as a wall, then as nothing for miles, then as flakes again — repetitions the way the grove repeats, the way some leaves are perfect before they exist.

What lives in this place, then, is not a community. It is a collection of solitudes tuned to the same drone. Moss making soil out of air. Ferns opening one leaf per season to a light that will never change. Hollow trees turning the mountain's groan into iron-rich sap. Vines rooting in air. Reeds answering a salt flat's whistle. Seeds like stones splitting in a soil that is also a skin. And, very rarely and very thinly, a person stacking stones one at a time, adding a course in a decade, leaving a boundary line with no cord, practicing the same patience as everything else that has made a home out of sound and dust and dimness — lush, waterless, and entirely alone, listening while the mountain, still under construction, listens to itself being made.
