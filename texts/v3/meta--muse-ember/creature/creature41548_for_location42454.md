If you stay long enough at the edge of the main pool, where the water has cooled just enough to be bearable to a fingertip, the first thing that stops being just a stain becomes a thing.

It is a film, grey-white, no brighter than wet tissue paper, wrinkled where it has shrunk and swollen against the rock. From a step away you would miss it entirely. Up close it has thickness. It gives, very slightly, when a drip lands near it. It does not move toward anything or away from anything. It grows.

This is the foundation, and it is not an animal and it is not a plant. It is a mat of bacteria, several kinds layered in the few millimetres of slime they make for themselves. The top layer lives in contact with the water and the air above it. The bottom layer lives against the stone, in water that has not seen air at all. Between them, chemistry does the work that elsewhere is done by sun.

Hot water wells up from the crack in the centre, carrying dissolved sulphur and iron in forms that want to become something else. That is their food, in a sense, and their fuel. One set takes sulphide and oxidizes it, step by step, to sulphate, releasing energy they can use to build more of themselves. Another takes dissolved iron and precipitates it as the rust-coloured crust you can see at the pool's rim. They do not photosynthesize. The column of light falling through the shaft is wasted on them — too hard, too hot, and, below the surface, not needed. They build structure out of a gradient: heat below, oxygen above, minerals coming up from underneath. Where the centre of the pool is over fifty degrees, even they cannot settle. Where the rim drops to the low forties, where seep water has cooled the hot well-water and diluted it, they can. There, in patches that together cover perhaps a square metre, they colonize.

They have no colour to speak of, no eyes, no mouths that open and close. They divide when conditions allow, which is when a pulse of slightly cooler, slightly richer water passes through. You can watch a whole mat change, very slowly, as that pulse arrives: the already pale film takes on a faint ochre, then fades again. It is not the mat moving. It is the water moving through a living sieve, and the bacteria answering in place, shedding the mineral they have oxidized and trapping what they can in their own jelly.

Their slime matters beyond them. It holds silica dust and iron particles that would otherwise wash away. It gives the new sinter skin something to grip. In that indirect way the smallest inhabitants are helping to lay down the little dams at the pool's edge, diverting the next rivulet. They are not trying to build anything. They are eating a chemical reaction, and the reaction builds.

They are grazed.

The amphipods appear as punctuation in that stillness — a flick sideways in the shallows, a translucent comma eight millimetres long, and then gone. If you watch for five minutes you may see two or three. If you watch for an hour you may learn their routes.

They are blind, and have been blind longer than this chamber has been open. They come in with the groundwater, through the seep that ticks in the corner, animals shaped by a dark, wet world that never knew about the shaft. There was no reason to keep pigment, no reason to keep eyes, so the body is clear as glass, segmented, the legs beating in a quick, lateral rhythm that carries them over the rock in short, startled bursts. They live at the surface film, because hot water holds very little dissolved oxygen and they must breathe across their gills where the water is thinnest and coolest. They scrape.

What they scrape is the mat. They move over the bacterial film with a slow, deliberate grazing, their mouthparts cutting through the gelatinous layer, taking bacteria, detritus, the odd trapped mineral flake. They do not clear a patch. They cannot afford to. Food is that sparse, and their own numbers are that low. One female carries her eggs in a brood pouch under her thorax, releasing a handful of fully formed young after weeks, not hundreds. She may live for a year or more in water that never cools, never freezes, never changes in any way except temperature. They tolerate what would kill a surface amphipod — mineral load, heat, low oxygen — because tolerance is the only entry ticket here. Anything less tolerant never arrives, or arrives and dies invisibly in the seep.

You will not see them often because, for most of the time, they are motionless, tucked under a flake of fresh calcite or in the damp skin between pools when the level is high. The flick is a reflex, not a journey. They are there in the shallows when you are not looking.

Above the waterline, on a dry ledge just out of the drip and nearer to the cooler draught from the shaft, is a different frontier and a different kind of animal. The springtails are at first just white specks on the pale rock, smaller than this typeface, and then, when the sun moves and the ledge warms, they jump.

They are not insects, though they have six legs and a talent for leaving. They jump by a forked appendage tucked under the abdomen, released like a latch, and they land and jump again, scattering. The jump is not for travel. It is for escape, triggered by vibration, by a falling drop, by the warmth rising through the stone. Their cuticle is hydrophobic; water beads and runs off them, so they can walk across the damp film at the ledge's edge without drowning. They have almost no pigment either. In a dark passage above they would read the rock by touch and by chemical traces in the air, the faint smell of bacteria and of wet stone.

What they eat up there is the dried edge of the world below: bacterial film that has been splashed and dried, fungal spores that may have fallen down the shaft on a mote of dust and never germinated into anything green, organic scraps washed in from the dry passages and then left stranded when the water receded. It is not much. They are not much. A dozen may be spread across an arm's length of ledge, each one jumping a few millimetres at a time in a nervous, collective shiver when the heat rises. In the cool of morning, near the draught, they are still. At noon, when the light column hits the floor and the chamber's heat pools at the far end, they are restless.

They, too, are colonists of a narrow band. Too wet below, they would lose their footing in the mats. Too dry in the upper passage, there would be nothing to eat. The ledge, warm and only damp, is the compromise they can live with.

The cricket is not a resident at all, and that is why it stands out.

Once an hour, perhaps less, a cave cricket comes down from the dry passage above — mottled brown, long-legged, antennae longer than its body, moving with a slow wave as it tests the air. It settles on a block at the chamber's edge, folds itself still, and does not feed. It cannot feed here. There is nothing green to chew, no fungi to graze, and the heat rising from the pools makes its own body lose water faster than it can afford. Its eyes, small and functional in a way the amphipod's are not, have no use in the white glare of the shaft. It sits, antennae moving, sensing the vibration of drips, the warmth of the rock, the chemical reek of sulphur, and after a few minutes of that stillness it turns and climbs back toward the dark, toward cooler stone and passages where it will actually live.

It belongs to the larger cave, not to this room. It strays into the wet pocket the way a visitor might step into a sauna and immediately step out — curious, tolerant for a moment, unable to stay. Its presence marks the boundary. Everything that truly lives here has given up something the cricket still has: colour, eyes, mobility over distance, the ability to choose a cooler place. The residents have traded those away for the ability to endure the heat and the chemistry minute by minute.

That is the whole community, and its most striking quality besides its smallness is how much emptiness it tolerates. The mats are patches, not carpets. The amphipods are individuals, not swarms. The springtails cluster on one ledge, not across the walls. Between them is bare, slick rock, fresh sinter, steam rising and condensing again. No one here hunts. No one here sings. The food chain is a line of dependencies so thin you can see through it: dissolved minerals, oxidized by bacteria, eaten by amphipods, and occasionally, in the drying film above, by springtails. Death, when it happens, is not dramatic. A dead amphipod becomes a trace of organic matter in the mat. A mat that gets too hot dies back and is recolonized from the cooler edge when the temperature shifts.

They live, then, not in spite of the light and heat that make the chamber so bright and so uncomfortable, but largely indifferent to the light and just barely on the edge of the heat. The sun pours in and is turned to warmth and to steam. The bacteria ignore the sun and use the earth's. The amphipods ignore the sun because they have no eyes to see it. The springtails jump when the rock warms, which is the sun's indirect message. The cricket sees it and leaves.

If you sit with them long enough, you stop looking for movement and start noticing change: a bubble rising from the silt and breaking without a sound, a ring expanding in a pool where a drip has fallen, a crystal ticking as it contracts. The inhabitants are part of that slow work. They are not resting in the room. They are working it, at the scale their bodies allow, building slime over stone, grazing it down, jumping at heat, enduring.
