If you go looking for whoever lives there in the ordinary sense — for eyes, for a face turned toward you, for anything that startles or flees — you will not find them. Nothing rises to meet you. The first living thing you touch is already under your hand, already under your foot, and it does not move away.

It is a felt. Algae lay themselves over every stone and brick and timber the builders left behind, black-green, a finger thick in the ditches, a forearm thick where water has been still in the lee of a wall. You do not pick it. You push through it. It muffles you. Cold mudbrick under it yields a little, and the algae yields more, and both go on. On the raised platforms, where the made earth is softest and the silt was trapped and trodden by hand, the felt thickens into something else: ribbons. Eelgrass-like leaves rise from the platform surface and hang from lashings and low edges. They are translucent and rubbery and very cold to the touch. Where a little more light finds them they are a dirty yellow-green; where the dark closes over them they go pale and stay pale, hanging in the still water at ankle height, brushing you and swaying only because you have imposed a sway on the water.

That is their full height, by and large. Their world is low. In this cold and dark there is no propulsion to grow tall or fast.

Along the sides of the dikes the moss climbs in layered cushions, cushion on cushion, softening the straight lines the hand made. In the canals the filamentous threads do the bridging work. Root to root, thread to thread, they span a pace-wide cut until the canal is roofed over in living matting. Water moves through it, if it moves at all, as through a sieve. In the wider canals, in the little square basins where two dikes cross, the same threads hang and trail and catch the fine suspended snow of silt as it falls and almost never arrives.

Most of them is not what you see. Most of them is underneath.

Under the mat creep rhizomes, millimetres in a year, and roots woven into the silt the dikes caught a thousand patient times and then caught again. A leaf unfurls and stays unfurled for a decade. A rhizome advances a handspan in a human lifetime and has had, undisturbed, an ancient time to advance. So the coverage that results is complete, not because any one plant is vigorous, but because nothing has ever interrupted it. No grazing snails crop it. No fish root through it. No crabs pick at it. No bird or insect above, because there is no above to fly in. Dead vegetation does not vanish. It sinks a little, softens, lies on top of living leaf, becomes part of the silt, and feeds the next layer up. Mat on stone, mat on mat, fallen leaf on living leaf, root woven into made soil. The population, if you want to call it that, is sheets and cushions and ribbons and threads, and below them, layer upon layer of their own dead selves.

They live exactly where the work is, which is everywhere. On a platform they are a garden bed. On a wall they are a mound of algae, the wall indistinguishable at the edge. On a bundle of timber stakes, black and soft as fruit in the anoxious cold, they are a green beard holding its shape. On a canal bank they tip the capstones and vine-entangle them a little further, year by year, until a wall two metres high is a ridge thirty centimetres high and the plants are part of why it stays a ridge. On forks and junctions once roofed with timber, they close the roof again in vegetation. In the reservoirs they rise from the submerged squares and hang in the lashings. You cannot put a foot down without displacing weed; you cannot trace a metre of floor without tracing a hand-laid edge under a mat that has outlasted the hand.

Their life is therefore not a drama but an accumulation. In the ten or fifteen steps of light that find their way down through ice and silted water, they grow, slowly, in the dim green-black. Beyond that, where it is colourless dark, they grow more slowly still, paler, thinner, translucent, but they do not stop being there — the pattern of platform, canal, ridge continues, and so does the covering, paler and slower the farther you walk into it. They trap the silt they need. The dikes that once held back water now hold back nothing but the silt the plants themselves have become, and for that reason they hold. Roots and rhizomes in made earth are the last mortar.

They make almost no sound. They do not breathe audibly, do not shift in herds, do not clear a throat. The only movement in them is borrowed: the minute sway your passing imposes, the slow drift of a silt-laden patch miles wide overhead that bends a ribbon a fraction and then lets it be. A torn mat will grow back over a human lifetime, perhaps. A footprint in soft brick will fill grain by grain, and the mat will resume over it. Nothing about them hurries, and nothing about them needs to.

That is who lives there, if you stand in the middle of a larger reservoir and feel the cold come up through the soles of your feet from the made earth and feel vegetation brush your ankles, unmoving except for you. Not a creature in the water. Not a shape in the dark. A thick, quiet, living upholstery over a vast, low, drowned plain of hand work — lush because nothing eats it, ancient because nothing disturbs it, one storey high from water to built earth to plant, holding its shape under its own weight, almost motionless, visible only as far as you can stir the silt.
