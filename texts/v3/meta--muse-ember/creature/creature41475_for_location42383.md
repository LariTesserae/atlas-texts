Stay still in it long enough and the bowl stops being a place and becomes a layering.

You see it first as wetness. Then as a skin on the wetness. Then as something moving under that skin, and under that, something else moving, and the eye learns - slowly, sideways - to separate them.

The first inhabitants are not the ones you can point to. They are the films.

A black yeast slips over the stagnant pools, never fully still, iridescent at the edges where the dim light catches it. In the pipe joints, where the water runs warmer from the pump, orange bacterial slime thickens and glistens and pulses faintly, not with a heart but with the cycle - when the generator coughs, when the pump engages, the slime tightens, as if listening. Over the damp concrete, undisturbed, a grey fungal felt spreads in soft mats. It lifts in pale ridges like dried foam. It breathes, very slowly. Its edge moves a millimetre a day, reclaiming a new section of rust, a dead insect, a speck of oil. Cream and mustard cords run beneath the stones like shallow roots, questing. They do not drink light. Nothing here does. They eat stone, metal, and decay, and in return they make a world soft enough for other things to live on.

They are ground and food and weather at once. The felt filters the air. The yeast feeds the water. The bacteria fix the iron that the steel has feathered out into stable, pocked rust. And because there is no green anywhere — no grass, no weed, nothing that has ever photosynthesised here — the whole place runs on this: on rot made mineral, on mineral made rot again.

Upon that, and in it, are the small armoured lives that belong entirely to the machines.

On the warmest pipes, where condensation never quite forms, colonies of tiny mites populate the iron. They are almost still, grazing the bacterial film in the joints. Nearby, beetles with glass-bead eyes, dulled by perpetual damp, walk the troughs and harvest flakes of rust one at a time. They do not hurry. The iron never warms fully, but here, relative to the pool-water, it is summer.

Below them, in the silt of the low bunkers and in the cracks between paving slabs, the ground itself works. Grey isopods in their hundreds, blind worms pale as paper, move through the mycelial cords, turning fungal growth into a fine humus that the mushrooms will use again. They are eaten, in turn, by things that cling above them, and they do not stop for it. Their work is continuous and silent and leaves no track except a slightly darker felt where they have passed. If you watched for an hour you would see the mat change, not grow exactly, but shift its allegiance from one rusted bolt to the next.

The water is crowded in its own way, because there is no true lake here, only a single water table joined into a mirror, ankle-deep in the lows, a film over black glassy stone in the highs. Under the black yeast film, pale blind things slide through the pipes. They are not fish in any ordinary sense. They have no eyes to speak of. They feel their way by the faint electric field leaking from the submerged cables, and by the taste of the water, and by the hum that comes up through the iron when the pump cycles. They have learned to ride the current that should flow downhill but has been trained to flow uphill, riding it like a road between pools.

Under the troughs, clinging to the undersides where the black water never quite reaches, dozens of translucent amphibians hang like salamanders that never grew eyes. Their gills flutter in the cold, rhythmic and slow. They graze. They do not feed on plants, for there are none. They take slow, rhythmic bites from the fungal felt and the orange bacterial mats that grow at the waterline, lifting their heads, tasting, settling again. They are hunted, occasionally, in the dark pipes by the pale eels. They are left alone by the dippers. The difference is not mercy; it is habitat. The eels live in the pipes. The dippers live on the surface and in the air of the machines. The amphibians live in the thin between.

In the air, there is a dust that is not dust.

Around the lamps — the orange one at the gate that no one comes to read by, the two bare bulbs in the shed, the one still burning under water in the lowest pool, casting a smudged glow up through the film — fungus gnats and pale moths hang in a dense, quiet cloud. They thicken when the generator coughs and the fans turn. A few fungi fruit beside the generator shed, blunt damp caps on thin stalks, and they do not fruit by day or night but by the electrical cycle. When the generator kicks, the caps exhale together, a visible puff of spores, and the gnats rise into it, feeding or being fed, it is hard to tell which. The fungi glow, almost nothing, a greenish phosphorescence in the darkest corners of the shed. Just enough for other things to gather by.

In the darkest bunker, a raft of translucent six-legged larvae hangs from the ceiling by threads, swaying together like a curtain. They filter spores from the wet air, patiently, in unison. Their swaying is the only regular movement in that bunker, besides the drip that lands every four seconds in the culvert.

On the walls of the flooded office, where there is a broken chair still inside and a kettle rusted to a brown shell, a colony of naked, soft-bodied slugs climbs in a continuous silent procession. They follow the condensation upward, laying down a faint glistening trail that other slugs follow exactly, excit and re-excit. They graze the mineral bloom and the fungal skin of the wall. They never hurry because the wall is always wet and the food is always there.

And, most visibly vertebrate, most sharply eyed, are the dippers.

Small, dark birds nest in the gearbox housings — the one still marked with a faded white 3 — and walk, bobbing, along the conveyor belts that sag and cross overhead. They pick insects from the trough edges with a dart of the beak. When they dive, they vanish into the black water and emerge shaking, shedding the spray, and the mushrooms beside the shed seem to listen a fraction closer. They are the only inhabitants who use their eyes as primary instruments, the only ones who truly leave the surface. Even they do not call. The quiet is the third rule here, after the wet and the absence of green. No one calls. They move.

It is in the touching that the place makes sense, if sense is the word. A fungal mat touches a pipe. A pipe touches a nest of larvae. The larvae touch a pool of tadpoles — no, of amphibian young, translucent and blind. The pool touches the leg of a dipper as it walks the belt. The beetle's foot touches the rust that the fungus is eating that the isopod is eating that the amphibian is eating. The density is not chaos. It is layering, a high saturation of presences in sixty paces, each using the one above and below it, each making the next possible.

The conveyor still moves, very slowly, carrying wet stones up to a chute where they fall with a soft plink back into a pool to be carried up again. It sorts nothing. The stones never accumulate. Occasionally a stone dropped in one pool is found an hour later in another, moved by an eel or a current. The condensation sometimes forms on the cold iron in brief clear patterns — fingerprints, rings — before running. These are not messages. They are just what the inhabitants live inside of, as cliffs and caves and feeding grounds. The machines, useless now, have become topography.

Several processes run at once and none stops. The water rises a fingertip with distant rain and falls a fingertip with the pump. The fungal mats shift. The isopods graze and are grazed upon. The spray falls on the screens and hisses faintly on hotter metal, evaporates a little, condenses again, beads on every cold surface and runs. The light slides grey across the pools and never warms them.

At the dimming that passes for dusk, the lamps become more important and the fungi brighten a fraction and the gnats thicken and the generator settles into its slower part of the cycle. The sound returns to its baseline: churn, plink, tick of cooling metal, drip, your own fogged breath.

Nothing here will become a meadow. Nothing will dry out or be rebuilt or be truly abandoned. The water has taken the yard into itself and the animals have taken the water and the machines. They wait not for an end but for the next cycle of their own loop - the pump cough, the spore puff, the eel riding uphill, the slug following the trail, the dipper shaking the black water from its feathers, the felt breathing a millimetre further over the rust.
