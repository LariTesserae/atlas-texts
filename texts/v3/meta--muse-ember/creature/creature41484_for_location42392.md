First it is not shapes. It is pressure.

In the water there is no empty, and so there is no first animal to name. Your hand meets a flank before it meets water. Your shoulder meets a flank. The light comes down and hits something, and something else, and something else again, long before it could reach the bottom. To live here is to be touched, constantly, on every side, by the neighbor in front of you and the neighbor behind you and the neighbor above who is resting his weight on you without meaning to.

Only slowly, staying under, does the pressure separate into kinds. It has been separating for a thousand slow years, but it separates for you only by staying.

At the very bottom, where the black glass is sharpest, is the floor that is not a floor.

The carpet pumpers live shell to shell. Each is the size of a fist, closed tight, locked by the shape of its shell into the row in front and the row behind, following the bowl's contour as if the bowl had been laid out for them. They have no eyes worth mentioning and no legs. What they have is a siphon, a gill, a body that can take in a mouthful of water and expel it again, click, click, in rhythm.

But no one pumps alone. A chemical change in the water, a vibration through the glass, a pressure wave traveling shell to shell, tells the next row to begin as the last row ends. One row pumps, then the next, then the next, all the way around the crater and back to where it started. A hundred thousand bodies turning like cylinders in a very slow rotary engine. That is the pounding you heard on the rim. You feel it in your sternum when you are under. The whole surface rises a thumb's width, then falls.

These are filterers, and what they filter is not plankton in any ordinary sense. Their food comes to them from above and from the sides, and what they take out of the water is passed on, refined, in the form of round pellets rolling in the hollows between glass columns. No stone rolls that round by itself. They make them, in their gut-factories, perfectly spherical, thousands upon thousands, and let them go.

They do not move. They do not need to. The world moves through them.

Threading that pump-wave are the drifters.

Larger, paler, translucent at the edges, they swim in lanes that never break. They do not dart. They do not chase. A drifter turns its whole body in a roll that takes a full minute, its mouth gaping wide the whole time, taking in the water the carpet has just pumped out. Inside, a sieve of fine plates strains out what the pumpers have rejected and what the scrapers have loosened. They turn, and turn, and the lane carries them on, and when one has gone around the bowl another has taken its place exactly, mouth gaping, roll beginning.

You can watch one animal for as long as you like and swear it has not moved. Look away. Look back. It is a body-length further along, and the lane is still full. The progress is not in the individual. The progress is in the lane.

What a drifter pumps out is cleaner water, and warmer with its own waste, and it sinks slowly, and the next lane takes it up. Everything here is downstream of something else.

On the surface, holding up the sky's reflection, are the raft-backs.

They are flat, broad, skin to skin, shoulder to shoulder, in a sheet that covers the water to the brim. They do not swim so much as cling. If one slips on the glass-smooth push of the pump-wave, two neighbors lean their weight and hold it up until it finds its grip again. Their backs are to the normal sun. The light catches there, and passes down through tissue that is translucent and alive, into the dimmer water below, in a way that is not a leaf and not a plant at all. It is light inside a body, being used by a body. They hold position for years. A generation of them is a held breath.

They link, too, by mucus cables, fine and strong, that drift down into the lanes. A drifter brushing a cable will orient by it. Food, waste, a signal, a tether — the cable is all of those at once.

Between the film and the carpet, in the cracks and on the naked glass, are the smallest workers, and the only primary makers in the place.

The black film is not algae. It is a chemosynthetic smear, sour, microbial, feeding on iron and sulfur that leach, almost imperceptibly, out of the young glass as it cools and settles. It grows nowhere else and needs no sun. Over it, and over the pale fungal threads that run thin and white through the cracks — fungi, not plants, exuding acid to soften the glass a fraction more — crawl the scrapers.

A scraper is no bigger than your thumbnail, armored, with a rasping mouth that works the glass in slow strokes. It leaves a clean groove behind it in the black. Within minutes, the film begins again. The scraper moves on, head down, following the groove of the one before, eating the place clean and letting it regrow. Their scrapings are the mineral food entering the animal world for the first time.

Down in the hollows where the pellets collect, in lines head to tail, are the passers.

I know no better name for them because passing is all they do. A passer takes a pellet in its mouth — a pellet made by a pumper, already coated with microbial slime — and holds it. It turns it. It sucks at it. It works its teeth, or its tooth-like plates, over one small arc. Then, after an hour if need be, it passes it to the next animal in the line, mouth to mouth, with a care that looks almost tender from above.

The line follows the bowl's deepest hollows, the highways the rollers have made. A single pellet may travel the whole circle in a month, gathering a lining as it goes, being polished by acid and by tooth and by the simple pressure of being held. It is an assembly line without a factory, production on a scale that would shame a factory, and nothing in it was built. The passers do not own the pellet. They do not keep it. They refine it and surrender it, and take the next one from behind, and all their lives are that exchange.

Among the carpet move the rollers.

Great ovoids, armored, eyeless, legless to any eye that could see them, they do nothing but roll. Very slowly they shift their internal weight, and the whole body follows, back and forth over the glass, over the shells, over the pellets. They compact waste and slime and dead fragments and push it all into the hollows, leaving a track of bare glass behind them as clean as a scraper's groove but far wider.

Within the hour the carpet leans in and closes over the track. Nothing stays empty. Nothing stays clean. Nothing stays untouched. A roller's track is a road for a morning and then only a memory of pressure in the shells.

And running — as quick as anything here runs, which is to say, slow — among all of them are the sparkers.

Small, elongated, translucent, with a nerve that runs clear along the outside of the body where you can see it. When a sparker discharges, a crackle runs its length and makes the water itself seem to ping against the glass. The hair stands on your arms even under water.

A discharge will stun a scraper mid-rasp. It will weld a dead shell to the living carpet, sealing a gap. It will split a shell the pumpers cannot open themselves, making its contents available to the lane. The power is faint. In this closed metallic bowl it is enough to be heard, and other animals turn toward it. They navigate by that ping, by the hydraulic thud of the pump-wave, by the grinding of shells on glass. Sound is their map. Touch is their country. Smell, where it exists at all, is the sour smell of iron and film.

They eat, then, not as animals elsewhere eat. There is nothing green to bite. The film is eaten by scrapers. The scrapings are filtered by pumpers. The pumpers' pellets are refined by passers. The passers' leavings are strained by drifters. The drifters' waste is caught in mucus and rained down on the carpet. The raft-backs take the light that reaches the surface and pass some of it down through their own flesh and excrete the rest as food for those below. The rollers gather all of it, again. The sparkers crack what will not open.

Death is not removal here. A dead drifter sinks, pale and whole, and does not rot into softness. The sparkers crack it. The rollers push it to a hollow. The carpet takes its shell into the floor, and the floor, which is alive, leans over it and incorporates it. Inside the shell, microbes feed. The pellet line will find it eventually. Death is recruitment.

And so they accumulate. Year after the slow year, shell on shell, pellet on pellet, mucus cable on mucus cable, mineral crust precipitating faintly on glass where the film has fed. It is industrial in every way that matters — mechanized, electrified, scaled, productive, relentless — except that no hand built it and no metal drives it. Engines without metal. Belts without belts. Power lines that are nerves. Waste that is feedstock. Product that piles up in a sealed crater with nowhere downhill to go.

The water holds them cool and even. Rain and snowmelt seep in from the high cold outside, almost imperceptibly, and leak out through the cooling glass, almost imperceptibly. No current. No tide. No season that changes much. A pump-wave takes minutes to go around. A floater holds for years. A roller crosses in a day and seems never to move at all.

That is how they live: loud and industrious and utterly slow, each barely moving, the whole never stopping, crowded to the point of touch in a small, high, plantless box, under a normal sun, making a world out of iron, sulfur, shell, and patience.
