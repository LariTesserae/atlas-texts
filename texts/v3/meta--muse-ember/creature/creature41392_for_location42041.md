It is hard at first to say what lives here, because almost nothing that lives here stands separate from where it lives.

The first resident is not a creature you meet. It is the skin you walk on.

The plateau is covered, spine to spine, in a single spongy mat — moss and liverwort and frost-fern and lichen, root-fur and broken stem, decaying ancestor pressed directly upon living descendant. You walk on plant, not soil. It squeaks under a boot, then sighs, then remembers the print for a minute while it bubbles back up. It beads. Even when it is not raining, it is near the point of becoming water again: it drips off its own edges, weeps off the low cliffs, breathes mist up out of its pores in slow columns in the morning that never quite become morning.

That mat is habitat and inhabitant at once. Under it, and inside it, the real work of the place goes on. There is no bare earth because the earth has been eaten and remade so many times it has become vegetation. Blind worms work it like slow bellows, inflating and deflating in the cold compost, pushing gas up through the skin so that a bog will rise a foot overnight, pop with a sound like a distant cannon, settle flatter, crack along a weeping seam and flood. Beetle-trains haul grit endlessly in patient single file, following last week's scent or last season's pulse, churning, aerating. In the deep districts, hollow fermentation chambers swell and contract on a slow factory shift. They do nothing but distill. The accumulated dead of ten thousand years goes in at the bottom black and old, and vapor exhales up, condenses on their roofs, and runs back in neat cold rivulets to be swallowed again. They bubble not from heat but from that fermentation, and when the mat over them finally gives, they throw spray half a mile across the wind.

Above the mat itself, for long stretches, there is nothing taller than your shin. Density is a trick of scale. The life is packed flat, and then suddenly, miles apart, it rears up.

On the iron-rich seeps, the smelter-roots drive down into the pale polished stone — stones polished to eggs and old teeth — and extrude amber resin that runs hot-sweet, smelling of hot oil and copper, then hardens into glossy lumps. They pool and crack in the cold. Small scrapers come to work them, birds peck at the soft stage, beetles haul the shards away, and the root grows them back. It is a smelter with no furnace, no hand, just a root tip doing chemistry at a pace set by ice melt.

The electricity is visible. After rain — which here means always, eventually — mycelium fires in waves across the mat, faint blue pulses traveling in straight lines, branching, meeting and sparking where the moss is thin. They are too regular to be weather. They look like production lines, and everything else seems to time itself by them. The beetle-trains pause when a pulse passes under them. The bogs give off their gas in time. Lying down in the mat, you can feel the piston rhythm come up through your bones before you hear it.

Those piston-plants are the other great machines. They stand on the weeping cliffs, thick as towers, ribbed and scarred, and they pump. They inflate with a groan, taking water up out of the stone slicks and fractures, hold it in their reservoirs, then deflate with a hiss and slam shut like a gate. Across distances so vast you cannot see the next one, they keep time. Not perfectly — never perfectly — but in a loose, ancient synchronization that makes the whole cliff-face breathe. The water they lift sheets back down in cold curtains, breaks, runs sideways when the wind finds it, and soaks into the mat again. The small grey birds that nest in the drip live off that leakage: they shriek at everything from the wet hollows, claim a drip-fall as a territory, and catch whatever flies up around the pumps — midges, spores, flakes of resin-softened bark.

Farther out on the windward ridges stand the turbine-stalks, sparse as smokestacks, alone. Their seed-heads turn slowly all day, clicking, flinging spores in white plumes that the wind carries off like steam. They have no leaves to speak of. They are mostly stem, a hollow architecture for catching wind and getting rid of themselves. Most of what they fling comes to nothing on the wet mat. Some of it takes, miles away, in a place that looks just like this place.

And between them, the sentinels. A trunk-tree rises a quarter-mile from the next living thing taller than a shin, its bark plated like a boiler, scarred in straight seams as if the stone underneath were being stamped through it. Its crown is a rosette far above you, turning slowly to drink the pale disc of sun behind the milky sky. It puts out seed, year after decade-long year, and the seed is mostly hollow. It is very old, and its oldness is in its bark — shapes of ancestors carried long after those ancestors should have composted to anonymity. Its root-arches weave terraces down the gentle slopes. Where one has died, the trunk becomes a tunnel, root-ribbed, smelling of loam and ozone, and you must crawl through it into a drip-lit chamber where water falls from the roof in a perfect rhythm until the echo becomes a drum. Nothing lives there in the way a bird lives in a tree. Everything lives there in the way moisture lives in a wall.

Other grown things are less monumental and more opportunistic. A boulder entirely swallowed by a fruiting vine is only a lump under green until the fruit ripens and the flocks come. A hollow stem arches over a black pool like a bridge no one built, and the pool-thing underneath — something that slaps and rolls and breaks the still surface so it seems solid until it opens — uses it for shade, for cover, for a place to come up and be a ripple. Living stems braid themselves into lattices over the bogs, shivering in the wind, humming a high note through their hollow middles. Islands assemble at dawn in the bogs, a raft of root and moss green and flowering, drift, lock against a root-arch, become ground for a season, then break apart and are eaten by the water they made.

Even the plants here walk. A root-foot at a time, toward the seeps, away from the trampled highway. A colony will die back all at once to black slime, and in that same long season be replaced by something that remembers how to be it but is not quite. Succession, without fidelity. Creation and destruction at the tempo of decay, which here is still very fast.

Life that moves on legs arrives the same way: in pulses, in crowds, in sudden departures.

For hours the plateau can seem empty except for wind combing the mat and your own boots. Then the ground trembles and the grazers come over a rise in a band a mile long, built like walking compost heaps, their backs plated with moss and small flowering plants that ride them into new country and drop off where the herd stops. Their legs are stilt-long. Their breath steams in great clouds that fog and bead on their own shaggy flanks. They do not rush. They advance like weather, shearing the mat, turning bog to churned black, bellowing a sound that starts in their chests and seems to come up from the ground itself — and leaving behind them a greener, trampled highway where the plant immediately begins, visibly, to reweave.

They are the place's great dispersers, and its great exhaustees. The young are fewer than the old. They carry the plateau with them in their fur and their dung and the garden on their backs, and they trample new channels in a day that will choke with fern and vanish in a month. When you see them pass at noon in perfect silence, you will sometimes hear their bellowing at dusk from the opposite slope, in empty air. They live, as everything here lives, slightly out of sync. The echo arrives before the sound that made it, crossing the mat out of order: the drip, then the pop, then the groan that caused both. The grazers react to a pulse they heard arrive yesterday.

The flocks are the opposite: nothing resident, everything departure. They come across the dim sky, blackening it for ten minutes with wings and screaming, then are gone, and the sky seems larger for their absence. They do not nest here, save for the grey cliff birds in the drip. They pass through the way weather passes through. What they leave — a seed in the fruiting vine, a spore disturbed, a scream that hangs in the hollow stems — is what stays.

Nothing here hunts the way a hawk hunts. Eating is slow digestion extended over landscape. The mat eats the stone by root-pressure and acid. The smelter-roots eat the stone by chemistry. The fermentation chambers eat the ancestors. The worms eat the compost. The grazers eat the mat that ate the ancestors. And the whole system eats itself, industriously, turbulently, unmaking itself into itself, at a rate that looks like stillness only because the tempo is so old.

That is why the reality breaks at the margins, and the inhabitants break with it. Shadows pool under things that cast none. A black pool will reflect not the grey-blue sky above it at this moment, but a warmer, sunnier day, and your reflection in it will lag a breath behind you, older or younger, in a coat of frost that is not yours. The horizon repeats, vegetated range upon vegetated range, the same weeping cliff pattern outlined again until distance seems less like distance than memory. Ice melts and refreezes while you watch it, the crack re-healing. Water runs uphill for a stretch on a strong wind day before remembering better. The dead plant-matter underfoot is so thick and so alive that it seems to remember being other plants, other animals, other seasons — and the living things carry that memory in their bark and shells and seed-heads, as a rhythm carried in a piston, as a pulse carried in a root.

They are, all of them, in a late stage. The sentinel trees put out hollow seed. The smelter-roots extrude less resin each decade, though the decades are long and hard to count. The grazers are old. The fermentation districts breathe shallower. Everything is deeply aged, near-flattened by its own weight, settled into a final soft form — and still moving, because the final form of this place *is* motion: wet, cold, breathing, fermenting, coming apart into green.

From any rise, the scale opens. The plateau does not end at a wall. It fades into other high, wet, plant-skinned reaches, other sparse giants, other black pools, other weeping stone, repeated until dimness swallows pattern. There is no path through it, no shelter, no mark of a hand. Only the mat under its inhabitants' feet, squeaking and sighing, the drip on whatever neck is under it, the slow blue pulse traveling somewhere below, and the sense, if you are only passing, that you are very small, walking lightly, very late, across a world that is still industriously, turbulently, and beautifully living by coming apart.
