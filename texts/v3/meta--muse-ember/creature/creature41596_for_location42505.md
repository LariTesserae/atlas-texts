At the edge, where the heat lets up by a few degrees, that is where anything has managed to hold on.

It does not start in the center. The center is too hot to stand in barefoot, too mineral, too full of vibration for anything to root. The pump throbs through the soles of your feet sixty times a minute, the fracture in the far wall hisses without pause, and the ground gurgles up every twenty seconds as if it is swallowing and letting go. The air hangs saturated with steam and sulfur and diesel. Nothing that needs to breathe easily has come to live in that.

What lives here lives back against the walls, in the narrow margin where the tray cools.

Seven or eight clumps in all, spaced far apart across thirty-five paces of wet clay, white silica crust, and black mud. They are as scattered as the artifacts: a toolbox on its side, a drill pipe weeping steam, a length of cable half-buried. No crowding. Long stretches of bare mineral between one presence and the next, because the bare mineral is most of the floor.

Stunted saltgrass at the edge of the spoil heap, gray-green and flattened, not rising so much as spreading. A low samphire rooting in the black mud where the mud sucks and holds, its stems thick and jointed and salt-pale, keeping its leaves to almost nothing. Sedges where the inflow pipe leaks and makes a cooler trickle, a little more upright, a little more green, but still thin, still marginal.

They do not thrive. They creep. A few centimeters a year is fast for them, and even that is not steady — they expand at the periphery and die back toward the center, keeping pace with the crust as it thickens by a fraction of a millimeter in a season, keeping pace with the water that wells up anywhere the skin is broken. One season the white crust creeps a little further over the black mud and takes another inch of ground from them. The next season a shard sheds from the sharp wall and buries a root. They persist in the places that stay just cool enough, just wet enough, just still enough, and that is all.

And in the very shallow water at that same edge, where the sheets are only two or three centimeters deep and the temperature falls below forty, there is another kind of living.

Patches no larger than your hand. A thin green-black film, algae or slime, lying flat on the water where the water is almost still between the channels. It does not rise. It does not break the surface. Where the pipe pours into the central pan there is a translucent mineral skin that heals instantly and reflects the sky like sheet glass; here, at the edge, the film lies under the water and dulls it. If you lean close you can see it in the shallows over the white crust, darker where it thickens, thinning to nothing where the hot sheet from the center runs over it. It lives only where the heat has dropped enough for it not to be scalded, and even there it lives in patches, with bare water between them.

That is the extent of it.

No birds over the water, though there is steam rising in columns perfect for circling in. No insects in the steam, though there is water everywhere. No frogs in the shallows, no tracks in the mud, no droppings on the concrete pad, no disturbance in the dust except your own boots. The register where a birdsong would be is taken up by the hiss and the diesel and the drip pinging off the steel tray with metronome precision. In a wet low place like this, on an ordinary day, you would expect all of that. Here the center is too hot and too mineral, the ground never stops shaking, and the air is too thick to breathe easily for anything animal.

It is not that life has been driven out so much as that it never arrived, or arrived and found only a narrow ledge of possibility at the cool edge and stayed there, sparsely. The plants and the film keep to that ledge, a few centimeters a year, while the steam cycles up and down as warm drizzle that never quite reaches the ground, while the pool level holds at exactly the same mark against the white crust, fed from underneath at exactly the rate it is lost.

What lives in the pocket is those clumps and those patches, and the emptiness between them.
