You will walk for days before you are willing to call anything here alive, and then you will realize you have been walking through the middle of it for days, and that is why nothing has moved to meet you.

No one here comes toward you. No one turns. The first inhabitants do not have a front. They live at your feet, in the layer you are stepping on, and they register you the way the peat registers you: by sighing.

The moss like wet felt over the stone is not, properly, moss. Up close, when you kneel until your face is in the damp sweet breath of the place, the felt resolves into thousands of small star-shaped rosettes, no bigger than a thumbnail, each with a center that opens and closes. Not quickly. A rosette opens for hours. It takes in the suspended green that fills the water — spores, pollen, fragments of leaf, seeds like dust, thousands of presences in the space of a hand — and passes it through itself so slowly that the water around it is a fraction clearer. Then it closes, and another beside it opens. Under the felt they are joined. A fine rhizome runs from rosette to rosette in the sponge-peat, carrying water, carrying warmth, carrying whatever the rosettes have filtered. It is hard to say where one ends. When your boot presses down, the whole sheet sighs and gives up a bubble, and the bubble sometimes rises, and sometimes does not, and hangs for a moment in the water as if reconsidering. That is not the peat exhaling. That is them exhaling for you, in the only speed they have.

They do not die in the way anything lowland would recognize. A leaf falls and is already half-decayed where it lands, already food for the layer beneath it. The rosette does the same in miniature. It unmakes its edge, softens, feeds its neighbor, and the rhizome underneath has already grown a replacement in the dark. A fresh frond here is never quite fresh; it unfurls with its edge already thinking about falling, because the tissue that made it remembers falling. Youth is not a phase. It is a brief openness between closings.

The second kind stands at your waist and chest, and you part it to walk, and it closes behind you without a sound. Broad ribbed leaves on hollow stalks, the stalks translucent with water. Inside each stalk the water sloshes faintly when you brush past, and you understand that you have not brushed a plant so much as a body with water in its gut. These are the upright filterers. Each leaf is a fan, ribbed to catch the dim green-gold light and to comb the current. In the morning — if there are mornings, only a dimming and un-dimming — the fans open. All the fans in a patch open at once, for reasons of pressure or warmth or something internal to the plateau, and the whole patch turns a deeper amber where the light has found a gap. Hours later they close, not because they are tired but because the current has reversed, and they have combed the algae straight one way and now must comb them straight the other, and the message, if it is a message, is the reversal.

Some of these rise from their own rhizome. Some rise from someone else's. A root pushes into a neighbor's stalk along a groove that was made for it eons before the neighbor existed, thickens, and drinks from inside. It is not predation. There are almost no predators here because there is nothing to gain by hurrying. The parasite becomes, in a season that is not a season, part of the host's fan; the host shades it, then dies in perfect unison with a dozen others, and the light falls through all the layers at once and the parasite — no longer a parasite — ignites into leaf. To be here is to be fed on and to feed, by turns, at the pace of a thought.

Overhead is the third layer, and this is where walking becomes parting. Uparching stems and hanging roots interlace so close that the water is full of their drip. The hanging tips are not roots in the lowland sense. They hang down long after the light they need has been taken out of it, reaching toward the muffled sounds that are always happening: the faint ticking as plants take in water, the creak when a stalk swollen with gas shifts its weight, the groan of a root under strain far away in the mist. Some of them, you come to understand, are growing not down or out but toward the echo of their own pressure. A tip will bend, over days, toward a sound that is not there, toward the answer their own pulse gives back minutes later from under the peat. They are ears that have mistaken themselves for roots. When two such tips find the same echo, they close around it and hold, and for a time the two colonies they belong to share water directly, tip to tip, without ever joining at the base.

And above that, where the water surface should be, is the ceiling. The drifting gardens are not gardens. They are the same inhabitants turned upside down, living with their rhizomes lashed into peat-like islands and their fans up toward the rain that dimples the surface far above your head. Their undersides are dark because they are faces turned away from whatever light there is. They turn, almost imperceptibly, in slow currents, like the hands of a clock no one is watching. For them, to travel is to rotate. A colony born on the north side of a mat will die on the south, having circled in a season that lasts the length of a human life and more. When a heavy rain fractures the light into moving nets, the ceiling fans catch it and pass it down in patches, so that bright places are not shallow and dark places are not deep. The ones below open because the ones above have turned.

It is only after you have learned to tell these layers apart — and then, after a while longer, to stop telling them apart, because a rhizome from the foot layer can thicken into a stalk, and a stalk can fall and become felt, and a felt can be lifted by a swelling of the plateau and become a hanging root — that you begin to see the rarer ones. The ones that move.

Once in a long walk you will see, suspended in a clear pocket below you, a shape long and unhurried with frilled translucent fins. It will not hurry because it cannot hurry and does not need to. Up close it is not one animal. It is a chain. Leaf-bodied filterers, tail to fin, joined by a living cord, each opening and closing its frill in sequence, so the undulation is not swimming but a wave of feeding passing down the body. It is carried by the current that combs the plateau straight and then straight the other way. It passes the green soup through itself and leaves the water a shade clearer behind it, and the water clouds again within hours, because the making and unmaking of leaves is always making more soup. It has no mouth that hunts. It has many mouths that wait.

The eyes you see in a cluster of moss are not for seeing you. Two small dark beads open in the green felt and close again. They are shutters. The colony needs to know when light has fallen through all the layers at once — that rare, brief event when the ceiling turns, the overhead layer opens, the waist-high fans lift, and the foot rosettes are lit to the stone. That light is the signal they have been waiting for, sometimes for years, to bloom in unison, or to die in unison, which here is the same gesture in different directions. The eyes open for the light and close for the shade. They do not watch the world. They watch for permission.

And in the black warm mud that holds the imprint of your foot and then, slowly, forgets it, are the ones who never make a fan at all. They live in the films over stone, in the hollows of leaves, inside the translucent stalks. They are what makes the stone tender, polished, grey-green. They exhale gas through the peat in invisible plumes. Warmth pulses up from them. The plateau breathes in and out because they breathe, collectively, in and out, swelling the stone a hair, lifting the lily-like leaves, showing a darker band on the wet rock and then losing it. Your own pulse is answered by theirs minutes later, not out of mimicry but because a pressure wave takes that long to travel through a body the size of a plateau. They think at that speed. A thought in one clearing is an event in another clearing an hour later, and by the time it arrives the clearing has already answered with a thought of its own.

Their beginning is not a birth. It is a release. Once in a long while you hear something that might have been a call. It is so muffled by layers of water and vegetation that it could be a current through a hollow stem far away. It is the groan of a stalk splitting. Inside, the water that filled it is suddenly full of dust no bigger than a breath. The suspended green thickens. For a space of a hand you are inside a cloud of them, each particle a baby filterer drifting, settling, opening a fan for hours in mid-water, drifting again. The water itself seems alive because, for a moment, the population is visible as weather. Most will settle on the ceiling gardens. Some will be carried uphill on the thin reverse streams that run on certain mornings along the old erosion grooves, carrying spores to places they could never have reached by falling. That is how they move across a plateau with no legs. They do not migrate. They recur. The same colony, by spore, appears in a clearing you left days ago, and the slabs there are a little more buried, or a little less, and the light has gone amber where it was green, and you cannot be sure you have looped. You have not. They have.

This is also how the built things came to be, if built is the word. You come to a place where the density thins, and seven slabs stand upright in a circle, half-buried in peat, smoothed by water exactly as all other stone is smoothed. A lintel lies across two of them. A little further on, a platform of woven roots, too regular to be only a mat. A doorway-shaped hollow in a boulder, water running through it. At first you think of hands long gone. Then you watch for a week, in the way watching is done here, by pressure in the ears and by light, and you see that the circle is a filter arrangement from when its members were young and upright and fanned. They stood in a ring to divide the current and take all of it, each taking its arc. They grew old standing there. A root thickened into a pillar. A pillar hardened. The fans unmade themselves, in unison, one last time, and left the husks. The stone does not crumble and does not sharpen because the husks are still, in the slowest sense, alive — dissolving at the edges into peat, while new sediment settles into the old hollows as fast as the old dissolves. The woven platform is a nursery mat, where dust-seeds stick together and are laced by tiny rosettes until they are big enough to make a stalk. The doorway is where one of the great ones sat for a millennium, and the current wore a passage through its ever-thickening base, body and stone becoming indistinguishable long before the body stopped.

Nothing here was made by anything that needed tools because the making is the living. A groove in the stone made by water that has not been at that height for an age is also a groove made by a root that followed it and thickened and became a pillar in the circle. A spiral worn to a rumor on a polished rock is also a fan that opened and closed against it for centuries. The plateau reached its final form in the deep past because its inhabitants reached theirs: spore, felt, fan, pillar, slab, peat, soup — a single life passing through forms, over a timescale that makes the stone look young by comparison, and the water look older than the stone because the water is the medium they dissolve back into.

They have no names, because a name would need a sharp edge, and nothing here sharpens. They have no predators because to move to eat would be to waste the soup you are already filtering. They have no prey that move because moving is the rarest phase, the chain-swimmer, the drifting baby, and even that moves only by water and by opening.

By evening — only a dimming of the dim, a thickening of the gold — the mist above the water has closed to within arm's reach. The leaves brush you with a touch that is almost, but not quite, a hand. Somewhere below, a bubble rises, or does not. Somewhere far off, a current turns the floating gardens another degree, and a patch of light falls through all the layers at once, and somewhere in a cluster of moss two dark beads open to see it, and somewhere else a thousand rosettes open to take it in. The inhabitants do not sleep, and do not wake. They open and close. They make and unmake leaves. They answer your pulse minutes later. They are waiting for nothing, having arrived at everything they will ever be, and going on, in silence, doing it all again.
