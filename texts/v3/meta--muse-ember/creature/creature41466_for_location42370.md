At first you do not see them at all. You feel them as a crowd.

The land is packed. That is how everything here lives — not singly, not territorially in the way open country is, but layered, one upon another, with no empty surface left between presences. A footstep finds water. A hand finds something alive. Even stone, where it shows, is not bare for long; algae soften it, roots vein it, and in a season or two the moss comes, and then something comes to live in the moss, and then something comes to live on that.

It begins low, because almost everything here begins low. The air is too heavy, too wet, too full of spores and seeds and suspended water to fly far if you can help it. So you walk, or you crawl, or you creep through the mat. Life is in the mat.

The mat itself is alive, and it is the first inhabitant you learn to recognize as an inhabitant rather than as scenery. Sphagnum in greens and in a deep, wet red climbs over sedge roots and over fallen leaves and over itself. It holds water the way a lung holds air, and it never lets go. Cotton-grass pushes up through it in white tufts that nod without sound. Sedges crowd, interweave, make a thatch so dense that you cannot see the peat beneath them, only the dark between stems. Under that, unseen but everywhere, are the fungal worlds that make the warmth you feel through thin boots.

That warmth is the center of the place. Beneath moss and leaf litter, fungi work in the dark, dense and industrious, fruiting and rotting and fruiting again. They are not a surface decoration. They are a network, a plumbing and a furnace and a gut. All the fallen trees, all the shed leaves, all the grazers that die belly-deep in the marsh, all the built things that the land has taken back — they pass through this layer. It processes them at a scale that dwarfs any single animal walking above it, and in processing them it releases a steady, faint heat, paradoxical in the cold. Invertebrates gather along those warm seams. Worms, pale and translucent, turn the saturated peat not because they need to aerate it in the usual way, but because it is food and shelter and warmth all together. Their tunnels are small, unhurried. They surface, they submerge. The ground they make is already wet enough to receive them.

On the open bogs the sphagnum is a city. You have to learn to look at different scales at once, because one thing is living in another thing which is living in another. A snail, bright green-yellow, polished as if varnished, climbs a stem that is itself climbing through moss, and trails a silver line that will be washed away by evening. It does not hurry. There is nowhere to hurry to; the stem will be there, the moss will be wet, the light will be the same grey-white hour after hour. Beside it a beetle works. The beetles here are metallic, green-black, polished like small tools left out in rain. They are the engineers of the fallen wood. They bore, they seal, they carry water in their bodies and in chambers they close with resin. You hear them more than you see them: a faint click in the damp air, sometimes a soft ticking that seems to come from the mist itself, though it is only a beetle moving in bark a few feet away. They build with thrifty precision, not for show, but because a sealed chamber in a wet place is a dry place, and a dry place is a nursery.

Because nothing is dry, dryness has to be made, and that is a kind of industry. The fungi make heat. The beetles make sealed rooms. The peat makes a filter, locking carbon for centuries and releasing it slowly, so that every drop that passes through it comes out clear and cold downstream. The rivers sort and grind. The plants on the terraced slopes stand in unnatural rows — you notice them only after a while, because everything else is so interwoven — and process light in shifts, one leaf after another, as if taking turns. None of this is a single creature's work. It is what happens when a thousand small presences each do the one thing they can do, wet and cold and continuously, without rest.

In the pools, life is so dense the surface moves. The peat pools are tea-colored and still, and then they are not still; they shiver from underneath. Amphibians fill them, body against body, translucent, with the feathery external gills they never lose. They do not call. They communicate by water; a pulse travels through the crowd when a crayfish or a water beetle noses in from below, and the whole pool ripples as one animal, then settles again. Around the warm spring pools, where water rises from deep compost and is a few degrees warmer than the near-ice springs elsewhere, algae grow in spirals, turning slowly in the upwelling. The spirals are not made by anything you could point to. They are made by the water, the light, and the microorganisms that live in the iron-colored seepage, coloring the thin streams rust-red as they process metal into food that fungi and roots will take apart molecule by molecule.

What walks above the mat is larger, but it does not make itself large by sound or display. The deer-sized grazers move through the high marsh in herds, parting the sedges with their bellies. Their coats are dense and dark and always wet to the underside, their legs long enough to push through peat that would bog a smaller animal, and they move at a pace set by the ground, not by hunger. They graze by pressing their muzzles into the mat and pulling, and what they eat is not one plant but the mat itself — sedge, sphagnum, the young shoots of shrubs, the fallen leaves that have not yet reached the fungi. They defecate into the pools and the channels, and the pools take it and turn it into algae and then into the amphibians and then into everything else. Their trails are braids through the marsh for an hour, then water fills them and the sedges close behind. A calf stays close, its head under its mother's wet flank, learning the corridors by touch. When they rest, they do not lie down on dry ground, because there is none. They stand, or they kneel in the warm seep where the fungi work hardest, and steam a little in the cold air, breath showing like yours, then settling.

Predators use the same corridors. They do not roam; they follow. A long, low animal, semi-aquatic, comes up through a channel and the water barely breaks. It is not chasing by sight. It is reading scent-currents, chemical trails smudged on wet bark and on stems, the vibration of a sedge as an amphibian shifts. It moves the way the water moves, unhurried, because everything here moves unhurried except the rain when it decides to fall on one valley and not the ridge beside it. The whole system is in motion in this slow, saturated way: herds migrating between high summer bogs and lower winter shelter, following not a calendar — willows leaf and drop as the rain tells them, not as the year tells them — but a gradient of wetness and warmth underfoot.

Birds are present in the way a held breath is present. They nest low, in the interlocked canopy or in hummocks of sphagnum, colored wet browns and greys that disappear against bark and peat. They do not sing. The canopy is too tight and the air too damp for song to carry, and perhaps they have simply not needed to. A parent returns, sits, and a chick inside the nest opens without a sound. You can walk within an arm's length and not see them until they slip away through sedges, silent as a shadow on water. At dusk they do not gather and call. They settle, and the mist thickens at ankle height, then at knee height, and the cold settles in a little more, and they continue to be there, packed into the green.

The trees are inhabitants too, and they are the oldest, slowest ones. Crooked from wind, their canopies interlocked so tightly that little light reaches the floor, they make, under themselves, a second canopy of ferns and broad-leaved shrubs that live in the shade of things that live in the shade of other things. Some have grown their roots above ground, stepping over bogs on stilts of wood, each root finding a new purchase while the old one rots and feeds the fungi below. A tree does not move in any way you can watch, but over years it has walked across a pool and left a causeway of its own body. Lichens grow on the bark. Moss grows on the lichen. A fern grows out of the moss, unfolding in a place where no soil should be, because the moss has made soil. That is how this place builds: not by clearing, but by covering.

Even the strangeness belongs to them. On certain slopes a fine rust-red dust covers everything to a depth that looks like earth, and it is not earth at all but spores, millions of them from a fungal fruiting that has gone to scale. Brush it and a cloud no wider than your arm rises, and hangs, and settles, and is taken up again by the wet air. In summer storms lightning does not always strike down; sometimes the wettest flats light from within, a brief corona that smells of ozone for an hour, and the inhabitants pause — not in fear, but in the same stillness with which they do everything, waiting for the air to clear. The static prickles on skin. The beetles tick a little faster in their sealed rooms.

They do not make noise because noise would be wasted. The spongy ground absorbs impact. The dense vegetation absorbs call. So they have learned other languages: touch, pressure, the slow vibration of a stem, a posture held in mist, a chemical smudge on bark that says, in a language older than ears, I was here, I am hungry, I am ready, I am passing. A sedge bent a certain way is a message. A pool that ripples in one direction is a message. Your own breathing, when you stop, is louder than anything they produce, and you become aware of how intrusive it is, and how quickly the place smooths over it.

They are abundant, and they are quiet, and they are always wet. They are born wet, they live wet, they die wet and are taken back, molecule by molecule, into the warm dark where the factories run. Nothing is allowed to be bare, and nothing is allowed to be finished. The bog creeps, centimeters a year, over stone that was once bare. The channels are settled and unfinished at once. Leafing, rotting, flooding, drying for a day, flooding again — that is not a cycle with a beginning. It is a saturation, a balance with no room between presences for anything new to insert itself, except the slow, continuous work of living on and in and through one another.

You leave, eventually, because the mist comes back down and your tracks have already softened. Behind you, green over green, they close. The grazers part the sedges again. The pool settles and then moves again from underneath. The fungi go on warming the peat. The beetles seal another chamber. The sphagnum climbs a little higher over a root, over a stone, over a beetle's discarded shell. It is a place that keeps no buildings because its inhabitants are the buildings, and the factories, and the waste, and the food, and they do not need a monument to show for all that industry. They are the industry, working and working in cold, vast quiet, and they will continue without you.
