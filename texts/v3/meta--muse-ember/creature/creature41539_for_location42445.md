If you want to know who lives in the hollow, you have to start with who can afford to. The noise takes away hearing first. Anything that needs to call or to listen for a call is already excluded — a bird’s warning, a mating song, a fox barking at a cub would all be swallowed whole by that white, tearing wind. The light takes away sight next, or at least it makes sight a poor instrument; even at midday the basin is blue-grey shade, and in winter the day never quite finishes arriving. And the cold, pooling damp at the bottom takes away speed. Nothing here can run for long, warm itself quickly, dry itself out, or burn through food in a hurry.

So the residents are not those who conquer the place. They are those who ask very little of it, and make very little noise in return.

The first and by far the most numerous of them are in the water film. There is no stream and no pool, but there is a skin of moisture over everything — condensation beading under stones, a damp sheen on peat that is never dry and never flooded. In that film, on and under the moss cushions in the sheltered seams, live the truly permanent population: springtails no longer than a grain of sand, mites translucent as tears, rotifers and tardigrades turning in the droplets that collect between moss leaves. They have no need for ears. They read the world by touch and by the slow vibration that travels through wet peat, and the wind’s hum, which to you is pressure in the teeth, is to them simply the background condition of the stone they cling to. They are eaten, occasionally, by something larger, but mostly they are left alone to graze on algae and fungal spores and the green dust that settles out of the fissure’s air, and to live out lives measured in days, under a moss leaf, in the dark they were born in.

Under them, in the peat itself, is a second, deeper city. The peat is black as wet leather and cold enough that your hand comes away wet from simply touching it, but for the worms it is a warm pantry. Their castings appear on the moss as small, gritty towers, already softening at the edges within hours. They work downward and sideways through the sponge, aerating it, digesting the half-rotted fern litter and the root hairs of herbs hugging the ground. Fungi thread through it all in white, invisible filaments, doing the slow chemistry that cold and damp make possible and fire does not interrupt. Rot is not dramatic here; it is patient. A dead frond may take a season to surrender. The worm has that season. The fungus has several.

It is only in the rich islands — where the peat deepens in the lee of a boulder, along the sheltered wall, in the seam out of the jet’s path — that the larger residents are allowed to show themselves. Moss is the foundation of every island. It grows deep enough to take the print of a hand and spring back over a minute or two, which is to say it is both a field and a forest at human scale. On the surface of a cushion a slug may be crossing, and it will take it the whole of an afternoon to cross a space you could step over without thinking. Its body is all damp tolerance; its silver trail dries behind it and is reused by the next slug and the next, a path worn in slime across the green. It grazes the moss selectively, as if pruning, never stripping a cushion bare because in this cold it cannot afford to waste that much time recovering from hunger. It has no hurry because the hollow has none to lend it.

Under a flat stone in the gritty middle ground, where a single tuft of grass rings a lone pebble at long intervals from the next, lives the beetle. Dark, industrious, righting itself with annoyed dignity if you lift its stone and unroll the light it was hiding from. It is not solitary by preference; it is solitary by arithmetic. There is not enough decaying matter dropping in the bare flats to feed a colony, so it patrols stone to stone, under and over, following the undersides where condensation gathers. When the wind pulses, the pebbles in the peat tremble almost imperceptibly and the beetle feels it through its feet and stops, antennae still, until the tremor passes. It will live for a few years, perhaps, moving less in a season than a fox moves in a night. Its great event is to find a dead fern stem and work it for weeks.

The plants are residents in the same slow sense, and some of them are older than any animal that will ever visit. The ferns stand waist-high in the sheltered hollows, fronds uncurling each year in the same green commas from the same buried crowns, shivering in unison when the jet unravels across the basin but never tearing. They do not grow everywhere — that is the point of the islands — and their lushness is not a sign of fertility so much as of neglect. Almost nothing eats them. A beetle may rasp a stem. A slug may take a leaf edge. Otherwise they can spend their energy low and wide, hugging the ground where the cold air pools and where the moss keeps their roots wet.

Lichens are older still. In grey festoons they hang from the edges of overhangs, swinging gently with the pulses, and crust the north wall in patches that must be many decades established, their edges polished and rounded by the same weather that rounds the cracks. A lichen is not one creature but two, a fungus and an alga living in a pact older than the hollow’s present shape. They ask for almost nothing: stone, damp, and light too dim for anything more ambitious to bother. They will outlive the ferns, the birch, the beetle, and the birds, growing a fraction of a millimetre and keeping it.

What woody things there are have learned the same lesson. Dwarf willow and birch exist here not as trees but as scrabbles and stools, their energy spent low and sideways, leaning away from the jet. A birch stool may be centuries old, a ring of short, contorted trunks rising from a single root system sunk into the peat, sending up suckers year after year in the same sheltered place. It will never make a canopy. The sky is only a grey strip between the ridges, and there is no height to spend on. Willow roots run shallow and wide under moss. Where moss has taken hold it has taken it completely, smoothing stone into upholstery, and the woody plants live inside that upholstery, not above it.

Only after all of these — the film-dwellers, the peat-dwellers, the moss grazers, the stone-patrollers, the ferns and lichens and stools — do you get to something with feathers, and it is telling how marginal even the birds feel.

A pair of small slate-grey birds, perhaps the only warm-blooded creatures that truly nest here. Their nest is tucked into a crevice out of the jet’s path, lined with moss and a few willow fibres, and they return to the same crevice year after year the way the ferns return to the same crown. They do not sing here, or if they do the wind takes it before you can hear. Their lives are a subtraction of their own: they flush from the nest at your approach and are gone over the ridge before you have properly registered them, and while they are gone the hollow is returned to its invertebrate majority. When they are present they feed on the larger insects — a beetle taken here, a larva there — a negligible harvest in an island of ferns. In the depths of winter, when the day thins to long dusk and frost feathers the sheltered sides of stones even at a degree above freezing, they leave altogether. The cold pool at the bottom is no place for a small warm body to overwinter on eggs. The residents who remain are cold-blooded and therefore honest about it.

The fox does not live here. Its track, pressed into soft peat and already softening at the edges, weeks old, is a record of a passage, not an address. It came over a low rise through the gap between the ridges, crossed the couple of hundred paces of bearable ground perhaps to get to the other side, and did not den in damp that would chill pups and rot a coat. It may have paused in the lee of the wall, out of the jet, and moved on. To live here would be to accept a territory too small and too quiet to feed a fox, in a basin where even its own footfalls are taken away by the hum. It visits the way weather visits: occasionally, and without staying.

And the human, whose traces are everywhere in small absorbed ways — the low arc of dry stone near the sheltered wall subsiding into moss, the causeway of flat stones sunk to ankle depth and vanishing, the blackened scrape where a fire was attempted and given up on, the flakes of knapped stone and the bone awl left beside the bench slab as if the owner would return in a season or a year — the human does not live here either. He, or they, built a windbreak never high enough to stand behind and lashed a wooden frame with root fibre that is stronger than it looks, and then the moss and peat began quietly mailing it all back to the ground. Nothing was built to last, and nothing has. The awl is still patiently sharp. The owner is not coming back in any season that matters to the moss.

That is why the hollow feels settled and not empty. It is inhabited by everything that can live at the speed of a finger’s width per year, at a hand’s breadth above the ground, without needing to be heard. The moss advances and keeps what it gains. The ferns uncurl where they uncurled. The fissure sings the same low note through every season, louder when the weather presses, and the lichens swing to it, and the fern fronds shiver, and the slug crosses the cushion, and the worm casts its tower, and the beetle rights itself under its stone, and all of it goes on under the deafening, ordinary noise, sparsely and carefully alive, in islands of green in a bare sea of wind.
