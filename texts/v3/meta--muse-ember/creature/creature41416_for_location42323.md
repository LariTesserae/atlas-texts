The first thing that lives there is the floor.

You do not find it. You stand on it, and it gives under you, warm, and springs back. Root and rhizome and stem woven through stem, fallen leaf pressed flat on leaf, gone no further than flat. That is not ground that plants cover. That is a population, wall to wall, a couple of kilometres across, laid over a sunken valley in layers too complete to walk through without pushing.

At your ankle it is a carpet of small fleshy shoots, each still carrying its two round cotyledons like tiny shields. They do not thin out. They do not open into anything larger. They stay at ankle height, spongy, and in the hollows between them water gathers black and warm and does not run. In the damp of those hollows the mat is tacky, sweet with sap. A few steps away on a rise it is fibrous as rope and papery, bleached by the white light that presses straight down and makes your shadow a small hard black patch under your feet, always in the same place.

A few steps away is a different weather, and the lives a few steps away never meet. There is no wind to mix them.

At knee height the stems rise near-touching, all leaning to a light they cannot reach differently. At head height broader leaves arch and meet and make tunnels and vaults and small rooms of green that close behind you without a sound when you push through. Above them other leaves, larger, translucent against the glare, make a second roof. Above them a third. Every layer is young in the same way: the trunks that rise like smooth green pillars have crowns still folded, fist-tight buds unopened; the ferns unroll to the length of your arm and stop mid-curl; the vines have climbed a newly split wall-face and stopped before flowering. This is not a forest that failed to mature. It is a forest held at germination, enlarged to valley scale, and every individual in it is living there, in that unfinished moment, indefinitely.

Nothing falls. The leaf litter underfoot is fresh and colour-bright and never becomes mold, because becoming mold would be time. So a leaf, once made, is a place for as long as there is a place. It does not drop and leave a tenant homeless. It does not rot and feed a different tenant. It stays.

That is why the smallest lives are the ones you are most likely to miss, and the ones that give the place away.

On the pale facets of stone above the green line — big as doors, sharp-angled, no patina, no rounded pebbles at their feet — thin filaments and algae are already tracing the cracks. Not a coat, not a moss. A first tracing. They live on split rock in the white glare, in the heat that sits in your throat like a mouth shut for a long time, and they do not soften anything. If you put your hand near, you feel no air move. They have no air to move in.

Lower down, on the leaves themselves, pale mites move almost invisibly across a leaf, bleached almost to the yellow-green of the glare. They are not in chorus. There is no insect noise here, no bird, no wind in a canopy because the canopy does not move. A mite crosses a dew-wet crease — a leaf at the stage just before it becomes a leaf, half-furled — and in that still, greenhouse damp it might be the only motion in a room of the vaults for a very long time.

The beetles are easier to see once you know them, and then you cannot unsee them. Shells like polished seeds. They walk one leaf and stop. Forever on that leaf. That is not a figure of speech here. The leaf will not fall, the sap will not carry them elsewhere, the light will not change to tell them to go. They sit in the pressed white light, their shells mirroring it, their shadow, if they make one, directly under them. They are the size of a seed and they have the patience of the whole valley. To watch one for as long as you can stand there is to watch it not doing anything you can identify as doing.

In the hollows, where the light is filtered, the same heat becomes damp, and the air does not stir at all. There, in cupped leaves and between roots, the black pockets mirror the white sky far above the rim and the green roof perfectly. Now and then a fine mist lifts off a pool and settles again, beading a nearby leaf. Now and then a single drop condenses on a cooler leaf high above and falls, and falls again from the same leaf, and again, with a small ticking you can set your listening by. It never rains from the sky. Moisture is made here and stays here.

Whatever lives at the edge of those pools lives in that ticking. It lives warm, and still, and upside down in the mirror, and it does not ripple the surface unless you touch it, and when you leave, the surface is glass again. You will not see it swimming. You might see, after a long time, a beading, a film, a paleness at the margin where the mist settled. It is not wet the way a stream is wet. It is damp the way a held breath is present.

This is the scale on which the one larger animal appears, if it appears at all.

Once, perhaps on the far terrace where the green is thinner and hotter, perhaps you imagine it: low-bodied, mottled green-brown, making its way along a root-bridge at a pace that makes it indistinguishable from the growth it crosses. It does not startle. It does not hurry. Its belly is in the damp hollow, its back in filtered light, its body the colour of stem and shadow and old leaf in a place where old leaf never darkens. The plants feed it, or it prunes them; the distinction is too fine here and too slow to matter.

If there is more than one, you would never know by census. A population that does not move perceptibly around a valley that does not change does not announce itself the way a flock does. It would be continuous with the mat you are standing on. Its footfall, if it made one, would be that muffled *thump* the root mat gives and swallows. A stem it brushes would creak once, the same creak, and hold.

On the drier rises — where you sweat at once and the sweat dries white on your arms — the lives are paler and papery and set farther apart. There the vegetation is thinner and the stone terraces bare. There, scattered faint and biological only, are the signs of building: low domes woven from living stalks, tied with vine, five or six across the whole basin, some intact, one collapsed into a ring of stems that has not decayed. No metal. No glass. No tool. Only stalk bound to stalk with vine, still green, still alive in the sense the whole valley is alive, which is to say, not growing and not dead.

Whoever made them lived on the rises, in the dry heat, in the white light that does not slant. They made a house out of the thing they lived among, and then the house and the maker became hard to separate. A dome is not a shelter they enter and leave. From the ridge it is part of the undulating sea of plant, an interruption only when you are close enough to see the tying. Inside the vaulted rooms at head height you push through, you may be standing beside a maker and not know it, because it does not move in any way that staging a return would require. It has not returned, or it never left, and here return is indistinguishable from staying.

The other marks belong to the same quiet population. A cairn of balanced stones on the raw mineral spine that runs across the basin, three high, unexplained, on stone not yet rounded or softened by weathering. A platform of bound reeds over the deepest pool, where the warm black water mirrors everything and the drip ticks. Faint parallel grooves on a smooth wall-face where something once dragged, or rooted, or was dragged by roots.

They are marks, not messages. They do not point to a migration, or a season, or a before and after. Nothing here has a before and after. The grooves do not fill. The cairn does not fall. The platform does not rot into the pool. A reed tied over deep water stays tied.

That is how everything that lives here lives: without a life cycle you could follow. Their leaves do not open, so they do not need to be pollinated in any urgency you can see. Their buds do not open, so they do not shade each other differently at noon than at any other hour, because there is no noon elsewhere. The light does not travel, so nothing here wakes or sleeps by shadow. The pools do not flow, so nothing here follows a current downstream. The stone does not erode, so nothing here is buried in silt. The fallen leaves do not fall and do not decay, so nothing here is fed by death.

They persist, densely, layer upon layer, below the surface and under the light. Mite on a creased leaf. Beetle like a polished seed stopped forever on one leaf. Filament tracing a crack on a new wall. A mottled body so slow on a root-bridge that you mistake it for root. A weaver woven into a dome of living stalks, a tibial pressure against a stem, a ticking drop somewhere above marking a passage that goes nowhere and does not end.

You could mark a leaf in your mind, walk away for what would be days elsewhere, and come back to find that leaf in exactly the same fold, catching exactly the same white light, with the same drop suspended at its tip, about to fall and never quite having fallen anywhere else. The lives on it would be in exactly the same place on it.

It feels, standing there, less like a place in nature and more like a place under construction left open to the sky. But the waiting is not of the kind that ends. The heat will be there when you return. The damp pockets will be there, warm and black. The green sea will stretch to the walls in the same unaged youth.

And in it, quiet, dense, and complete, whatever lives there will be there in the same way — not growing up, not growing old, not leaving, just living in the instant of its birth, held.
