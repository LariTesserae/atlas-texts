Nothing here lives apart, and that is why, if you come looking for inhabitants, you will look in the wrong way. There are no bodies set down in the basin like furniture in a room. Living is not something that happens on top of the ground. It happens *as* the ground, as the water on the ground, as the air just above the water. To be alive here is to be one of the layers, and to be never quite separate from the layer above or below you.

At the bottom is the Mat.

It is moss, and fungus, and root, and something else that has no good name in a drier climate — a luminous-brown felt, thick as your thumb, that goes everywhere because everywhere is the same level. It carries pulses. If you stay still, you can watch a brightening travel across it in a wave, unhurried and impossibly fast, crossing what your eyes judge to be a mile of sameness in seconds. In its wake the sedges lift, the duckweed opens, the new shoots extend. That wave is their spring, their dawn, their thought. It is not electrical as a wire is electrical, and it is not slow as plant growth is slow. It is chemical, phase-change, a coordination of wetness. Dewdrops along the path sort themselves for a moment into triangles, branching hexagons, lattices that hold and dissolve. A leaf you tore passing closes without a scar, its edge re-fusing, water beading off it too perfectly to be accidental. The Mat is skin, nerve, gut, and memory all at once. It does not heal a wound by growing over it. It re-negotiates the surface until the wound was never a separate thing. It has no brain, and it does not need one, because everything that needs to know the next condensation knows it at the same time, everywhere at once, through the fungal understory.

Above the Mat, in the water film no more than a hand deep, live the Film Folk. Duckweed in colonies, and tinier glassy plants that open and close in waves synchronized to the pulse. They are the basin's lungs, stippling the braided sheets that move toward a level they remember rather than a downhill they obey. A sheet will hover over a slope and hold, or thin at the edges and lift and drift a few feet and lay itself down again, leaving a dry patch that greens within minutes. The Film Folk ride that. They do not swim so much as they let the water decide their shape, opening to the signal, closing after it passes, photosynthesizing by the light that comes up from below, from algae giving a slow teal fluorescence in the still pools. They are eaten by nothing with a mouth. They are taken back, molecularly, by the fungus threaded through them. Death here is not an event. It is a thoroughness.

Above them, the sedges and rushes stand, translucent-stemmed, heads bowed with their own weight of water. Every reed is a hollow flute, and the damp air is always moving through them, so the colony hums. Not a song. A chord, many notes at once, shifting by degrees no instrument could hold. They filter the air as they breathe it, cleaning it for the Mat, and they catch the mist tide as it builds and as it lifts. They are not individuals; a clump is a throat, and the whole reedbed is a throat clearing itself endlessly. If you lean close, you hear the separate drip chorus start an instant before the drop falls — the reed announcing the water before the water happens.

Then the shrubs. Ancient, gnarled, no taller than you, wood silver-grey and smooth as bone, leaves thick and waxy and cold to the touch. Each one is very old by the basin's standards, which is to say it has endured a great many mist tides without ever needing to be anywhere else. They store the basin's level in their wood. They do not grow fast except in pulses, when the green tide crosses and they extend a fraction, then stop, saturated, holding. They are dwarfed not by poverty but by satiety. There is nothing to reach for. No sun to climb toward, no high ground to claim. Their shade, pale and cool and brighter than their casts, falls on the moss and is read by it.

The trees do the same work at larger scale, and more slowly. A grove is not a collection of trunks. It is one organism that has surfaced in several places, connected unseen through peat and fungal cord, unspooling rather than branching, trunks worn smooth by perpetual wet. You will pass a grove, an hour later pass what seems the same grove again, changed only enough that you cannot be certain. For the tree, that is not recurrence. That is simply its extent. It is both here and there because there is no there to be separated by.

The vines hang but do not climb. They hang in the damp and grow downward into whatever pool is below them, rooting in air and water at once, drinking mist directly. They are the basin's way of tasting the air for minerals, of lapping at the second ground that forms ten feet up when the tide lifts.

And the stones — the stones are the oldest inhabitants, which is why they seem least alive. Low, egg-smooth, pillow-smooth, half-buried in peat with a perfect symmetry that your mind keeps reading as intention. Some weep continuously from pores too small to see, a clean mineral sweat beading and vanishing into moss. Others tick, releasing pressure, grinding almost inaudibly under sheet-water. Stone here is not inert. It is a filter and a slow animal. Its pores are colonized by the Mat, its polished roundness is the record of water asking the same question for a geological duration. Things live on it, in it, through it. If you look very carefully at one, you will find a place where the water has polished it perfectly round, and that is the closest thing the basin makes to an artifact, a footprint, a signature. It is also a form of breathing.

In the air are the brief ones. Spores rise and do not simply drift. They assemble, disassemble, re-assemble in mid-air, building frost-like structures that catch the dim high light and throw it back down to the undersides of leaves. For seconds, a spore-architecture is a complete creature, a temporary city of ice and water. Then it unbuilds itself and is only spores again, waiting for the signal wave to call the pattern. Dew lattices do the same on the Mat — triangles, hexagons, crystalline grids that hold for seconds and dissolve. Somewhere in the interference nodes where drops hang and do not fall, tiny water-borne things live their whole lives without ever landing, riding surface tension, completing existence in suspension. A dropped leaf may hover for a long time, turning slowly, until the moss signal reaches it and it is permitted to settle. Gravity here is present but negotiable, and the small ones have learned to negotiate.

They are, like everything else, oriented horizontally. Nothing rises. Everything spreads, pulses, circulates.

The larger fauna are rumour because the basin does not need them. There is no predator, no carrion smell, no chase. The eating is done by water and fungus, cleanly and without spectacle. And yet, now and then, the rumour condenses.

There is the Grazer. You will most likely not see it. You will see the disturbance it makes at knee height in the mist, reeds parting in an orderly way, mist lifting in its wake, continuing uninterested at the pace of the basin. It does not run from you. It has no need to. It moves through the film and the shallow sheets, stirring the algae, letting the teal pools bloom clearer behind it, lifting the condensation off the hummocks as it passes. It is blind in the way we mean blind, because the light it navigates by comes from below and it has learned to feel its way by pressure and pulse. It leaves no print in mud — mud here does not hold prints — but sometimes, on a pool, a perfect circle of still water a few inches across, filling in and vanishing before you can test it. That may be its foot, distributed so thinly over the surface that the water holds still instead of breaking.

There is the Circle-Walker, larger if it is larger, rarer if it is rare. It steps only on water, on those impossible clear blue pools that are deeper than the ground beneath them allows, and only for an instant. You hear no step. You see the circle expand and close. It may be the same as the Grazer seen from a different level, or it may be something else entirely, a shape that exists only in the act of stepping on a surface that should not hold it. It never comes onto the peat. The peat is too solid for it, or too familiar.

And there is the Singer. Over the constant reed chord, occasionally, a single long low note rises and falls like a bowed string, source unlocatable because the source seems to be the sound itself, vibrating in the air above the reeds. When the mist tide lifts — when it peels off the hummocks and off your own clothes and climbs to make a second ground of air, and then rains out of itself in fine separate threads without disturbing the layer below — the drips sharpen, the chord rises, and for a moment the deep hum drops away, as if the whole basin has exhaled. That is when the Singer is most likely to be heard. It may not have a body to find. It may be a reed colony and a mist layer and a pressure change agreeing, for the length of a breath, to be a single voice.

During the lift there is a whole second population, the Mist-Dwellers, who exist only then, at ten feet, in the sheet of cloud that hangs and spreads. They are what is raining — not animals falling, but conditions made briefly animate, threads of water deciding to be separate for a while. Then it all settles again. Water that was pool becomes cloud becomes pool in a different place. The mist tide is the basin's heartbeat, and everything here lives by keeping time to it, not to days.

That is the whole of it, and it is not a whole of separate things. The Mat conducts, the Film Folk breathe, the reeds hum and filter, the shrubs and trees remember, the vines taste the air, the stones filter and weep, the spores build and unbuild, the Grazer stirs, the Singer sounds, the mist lifts and falls. No one is in charge. No one needs to be. The basin thinks in chemistry and phase changes, coordinating the next condensation across miles in a second, leaving no path, no wall, no footprint that lasts. A leaf heals. A footprint is taken. A stick you drove in is found lying flat, its shadow pooled beneath it in light that does not match, as if the basin has rearranged a small piece of itself for tidiness and moved on.

By the time you have crossed a small part of it, your breath has joined the air and hangs for a second longer than it should. Condensation forms on your eyelashes. The sound has got into your bones. You have become, briefly, a habitat — a warm, dry-ish surface in a wet place — and the Mat will take you back the way it takes everything, without waste and without ceremony.

What lives here does not live *in* the basin. It lives *as* the basin, breathing out and in, remembering its level, repeating its weather, conducting its signals from root to root, with no builder and no brain, and going on past your sight and past your hearing and past the next recurrence and the next.
