At first, you would say nothing lives here.

You walk ten minutes, twenty, over the flooded plain, and there is nothing to your left and right but shallow water over concrete, pipes crossing the ground like low spines, a dead relay box, a valve wheel seized open. The lamps have no moths. The pools have no fish, though they are deep enough and still enough. The dark overhead has no birds. If something moves that is not a machine or water, it moves so slowly you could watch it for an hour and swear it had not moved at all.

That is the first mistake the place allows you to make. It wants you to think emptiness is the same as absence.

Wait, and listen. Because the thing that is most alive here makes the sound you cannot speak over.

The machines are the fauna. Not metaphorically. They breathe. A fan turns, and a fan turns, and a fan turns, at different slow speeds, never in sync, and together they make the low roar that lives in the ground and in your sternum. A pump in a hall with its doors rusted to the frame lifts water from one tank only to spill it back into the next, in a cycle so slow it looks idle until you realize it has completed a full turn while you were looking away. Tanks sigh when the pressure shifts. A sealed line builds and builds and is released in a shriek every few minutes somewhere off in the mist, and the shriek is swallowed and the roar continues. A belt moves a load of wet ash from nowhere to a pile that has been in the same place since before living memory, and the ash pile drips.

They eat electricity and water and air. They excrete heat, rust, ash, condensation, a higher electrical hum inside the low hum. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They do not rest. They idle, they cycle, they wait for a signal that might be only the water level itself, and when the signal comes they do what they have always done. They are not efficient. They are endless, at the slowest pace that still counts as motion, and that is why they seem more alive than anything biological should: because they never stop metabolizing.

Everything else here lives off the machines, the way life in a natural place lives off geology. Only here the geology was poured.

Start where the water sits. In the runoff troughs and the sheet water a centimeter deep over the slabs, everywhere a thin film gathers — algae, bacteria, oil rainbowed on top. It is not clean water. It is a skin. Iron-eating bacteria live in the pipes and in the orange slime that clogs them, slowly sealing a leak by building a tube of their own bodies. Oil-eating bacteria live in the rainbow, breaking the sheen down molecule by molecule, changing its color not by light, which there barely is, but by thickness. Sulphur bacteria cloud the stagnant pools white at the edges. On the copper and steel of the substation yards, on the insides of tanks beaded with condensation, there are biofilms you will never see from a distance, electrosensitive mats that ride the slow vertical tears of water down a cold wall, then travel again when the tear evaporates and re-forms elsewhere.

They divide in that film. They ride the mist that thickens until your hands dissolve at the wrist. What gathers around the sodium lamps is not insects. It is spores and aerosol and condensation, turning in the fog, settling, waking in damp.

Where the concrete has failed to be perfect — and after a thousand years of wet, it fails everywhere, a little — other things have learned to be perfect in its place.

In the expansion joints, where black water stands between slabs, moss grows grey-green and spongy, holding water long after the concrete around it would have dried, which is to say forever. A mat of moss in a joint is the only soft crossing for a hundred meters. It grows layer upon layer, the old dying underneath, the new reaching up, making soil out of its own decay and the dust of the slab. It weathers the concrete further, acidifies the joint, makes more room for itself.

On the north sides of tanks, where it is coldest and dampest and the light, such as it is, is most diffuse, lichen makes pale coins. No light to speak of, no warmth, nothing to eat except air, stone, and time. It grows a millimeter in a year, perhaps less. A coin you see today was started when the tank was still new, and the tank has leaned a degree since then, and the lichen has not noticed. It is a symbiosis of fungus and algae doing the patient work of photosynthesis in a place that has forgotten the sun.

Inside the halls, on the damp timbers that have not yet softened all the way through to mineral, fungi bloom. White threads first, threading the pores of wood and even the hairline cracks of concrete — which is another kind of wood, to them; just wet, and full of minerals to digest. Then, when the network is ready, pale fruiting bodies like wet paper, grey, cream, a dull orange that needs no light. They are the decomposers here, because there are no insects to do it, no worms, no animals at all. They eat the place slowly from inside. A timber can remain a timber for decades while being turned, cell by cell, into fungus, into soil, into water again.

And heroically, sparsely, where a crack in the asphalt is wide enough and a seed has found its way in the mist to settle, a clump of reed pushes up. No more than a hand high. Saturated. Stunted. Blind-pale, because there is no sun to green it. A weed does the same in another crack, a hundred meters from the next green thing, accumulating in its tissues whatever metal the runoff carries, not because it wants to, but because that is what plants do when they cannot leave.

These are not many. You can walk an entire valley of this and find three reeds. But they persist, which in this place is the whole strategy of life. Nothing dries, so nothing rots in the usual way; it softens and mineralizes instead. The moss does not need rain. The fungus does not need dryness to sporulate. The bacteria do not need oxygen, or light, or warmth, or even a steady food supply; they need wet, and wet never ends. The reed does not need a season. It grows whenever the water is there, which is always, and it grows almost not at all.

There is no predator and prey here. There is seep and seal. Oil and microbe. Pump and waiting tank. The machines move the water, the water moves the spores, the microbes eat the oil and the iron, the fungi eat the timbers the machines were built from, the moss eats the concrete the machines stand on, the lichen eats the air the fans turn, the reed eats the crack. It is a food web flattened into a mineral web, everything consuming the construction, very slowly, and everything being consumed in turn by the constant wet rounding it all toward the same final form.

If you stay long enough, the timescales start to invert. The machines are the oldest inhabitants. They were here before the moss, before the film, before the first spore settled in the mist. The life that was born here came later, and it will, in its own way, outlast the machines, because when a pump finally seizes, the bacteria in its housing will keep dividing in the still water for a while, and the fungus will move into its timbers, and the moss will move over its base plate, and the concrete will weep a little longer and then be still.

What lives here does not make a sound of its own. It does not leave tracks. It does not warm in the light, because the light does not warm. It does not need to be seen. It accumulates, in joints and on tank sides and in troughs and under timbers, in the dark under things where condensation gathers and drips upward in behavior if not in direction. It is sparse, and patient, and total in its attendance to wet.

It has learned not to live on the place, but to be part of the place, the way the roar is part of it, the way the dark is.
