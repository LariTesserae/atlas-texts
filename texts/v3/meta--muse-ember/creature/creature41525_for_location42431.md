Nothing lives *on* the High Thatch. That is the first mistake the eye makes from the edge.

Everything lives *in* it, and *of* it, and under it. The mat is not ground that creatures stand upon. It is a skin, thick and breathing, and the living is mostly hidden in its folds — in the cool inch below the hot surface, in the hollows of its giants, in the water it holds inside other bodies. From a distance it looks empty because almost nothing here holds itself up against the sky for long. It keeps low, or it keeps enormous and alone.

Begin underfoot, because that is where most of it is.

Beneath the waxy red-green runners, where the sun burns your soles through a sandal, the thatch stays moist and chill. It is honeycombed. Generations of bellies and feet have worn tunnels through the fiber, not dug, not built, but pressed and re-pressed until the living mat has learned to keep them open, like the passages in a sponge. Things live there that rarely come out at all except at dawn and dusk and after rain: pale, soft-jointed crawlers with root-hook legs that grip the runners from below, blind or hooded against the light, feeding on rot and on the shed skins of the mat itself. They keep the tunnels damp by their own presence. They turn the dead fiber into soil, slowly, inside the living floor. At midday when the surface is too hot to sit upon, they are the plateau’s true population, thousands to a stride, moving in the dark fiber traffic while the surface above seems to have died of heat.

Their tunnels are why the mat heals behind you. Where a step tears a runner, something below knits it.

In the stone pockets at the foot of the escarpments, where water has collected in black hollows a hand across and stayed for years, another life waits so still it can be missed entirely. Frogs no bigger than thumbs. Their throats are pale, their eyes are bright in the dark of the stone, and they sing in the rainless hours in a thin, needling chorus that answers the drip from the leaves above. People do not drink from those pockets. Not because the water is poisoned, but because the frogs are the sign of it. Where the frogs are, the water will last. Where animals go when the mat dries, the frogs know. The frogs themselves never leave the stone. They eat what falls to them from the thatch — spores, midges, the occasional stunned insect — and they hold their moisture in their skin against the thin air. They are not a food. They are a memory of water made persistent.

Up from the tunnels, up into the air, the second population arrives as weather.

The insects of the High Thatch do not live as colonies you could point to. They live as events. For hours the yellow haze over the mat is only pollen and spores hanging in the heat. Then a sound gathers, not loud at first, a vibration in the air you feel in your teeth, and a sheet of fliers comes over the swell in a broad front, so thick it darkens the sun for twenty minutes and settles in a rustling carpet over the runners. It is not one kind. It is many kinds moving together, roaring, landing, tasting the mat and each other, lifting again when something big moves underneath them. When they settle you hear the mat crackle under them. When they lift it is like a sail being hauled.

At dusk they do something else. When the heat breaks and the giant leaves unfold with a sigh, the insects begin to sing — first one, a single thread of sound from somewhere in the thatch, then a hundred, then the whole plateau at once until your own voice becomes a rumor. They are the clock here. The frogs answer them. The pods popping in the cooling mat beat time like hand-drums underneath the choir. Nothing here can ignore that sound. Everything pauses to listen, because to listen is how you know where the water-band is moving and where the herds will go.

The herds come for that band, and they are the largest thing that moves on foot.

They are grazers, six-legged, built like slow houses. Their bodies are barrelled and low, their legs thick-jointed and deliberate, their heads down in the thatch for hours at a time plowing a swath a valley wide. Moss grows thick on their backs — not a few tufts, but a meadow, trampled by their own feet when they lie down, kept cropped by their own mouths when they turn their necks. In the logic of the place this is not decoration. A grazer’s back is a plateau in miniature, and it has its own tenants who never leave it: tiny six-legged grazers of their own, no bigger than beetles, browsing the back-moss; and hunters upon those hunters, quick and thorned, living out their whole lives on the warm, moving island, born there, dying there, their generations timed to the slow stride of the host. When a grazer is bedding down at night its sighs are audible for miles — a whole herd exhaling together, an avalanche of low grunts — and somewhere above, in its moss, a bird sometimes sleeps, turning in its sleep as the ground rises and falls beneath it.

They eat the mat to stubble and leave behind steaming dung that the thatch takes up within hours, visibly greening where it landed. They do not roam randomly. They follow the invisible wet band that crosses the plateau through the year, the slow diagonal of renewed growth where rain has actually landed and the runners have lifted overnight. Where the band has passed, the thatch is cropped and pale. Where it will pass next season, it is already thickening in anticipation, root by root, storing. The grazers know this by smell and by the dampness in the air at dawn. A herd is never more than an hour’s walk away from you, though you may walk a day without seeing one, because the whole plateau is crossed by them like slow tides.

Walking with them, through them, among them, are the walkers.

They look at first like plants that have stood up and learned patience. Lanky, thorned, green-skinned, with hooded eyes set in ridged sockets that give them a perpetual look of concentration. Their limbs end not in feet but in rootlets that curl and uncurl in the thatch, tasting it. They are photosynthetic — you can see it in the way they orient their flanks to the sun at midday and let their leaves drink — and they are predators. They do not chase. Chasing would be foolish here, where everything is so large and so slow. They wait. One will stand mid-step among a grazing front, one root-leg lifted for an hour, breathing, its thorns catching insects and the odd distracted young grazer that strays too close to its shadow. They take what the herd does not notice. In the old balance of the place they are not a menace to the herds; they are a tax upon carelessness, and the herds have learned to give them a wide, respectful berth without ever breaking stride. At night a walker will settle, its rootlets spreading wide, greening darker in the dew. At dawn it rises again, refreshed, terrible in its stillness.

There are other plants that walk, or nearly. A few have long, exploratory root-legs that step a meter in a season, creeping toward a seep or away from a crowd of grazers, leaving behind them a pale, withered trail that slowly greens again when the mat reclaims it. They are not hunters. They are refugees. Given the patience of the plateau, moving at all is an event.

And above the mat, half a mile apart, sometimes more, stand the giants. They are not trees. They are singular trunks as thick as huts, ribbed and succulent, flaring into leaves the size of doors, or hollow stalks big enough to walk inside. Each one is an architecture, and each one has its own tenants who belong to it alone. Birds nest in the forks by the hundreds, launching at dawn in a clatter that carries across a valley. Insects darken the interior hollow, where condensation sweats the walls in the dark and the air smells of sap and cool. At noon, predators will sleep in the shadow of a single leaf, because that shadow is a room, cool and still, while the open mat outside is humming heat. The giant leaves fold along their midrib by ten in the morning, slapping and booming in the wind like wet sails, and open again at night with a sigh you can hear if you are close enough. Water lives inside them, stored and sweet, and under their vast undersides at night the thin air condenses and drips in a slow tick-tock long after dawn. The giants do not move. They do not need to. The world comes to them.

People are the sparsest of the presences, and the most recent. A family of ten or twenty, following the herds rather than leading them. Their huts are lean-tos lashed from giant leaf-stalks, low because the wind will take anything high and the plants will take anything patient. Inside, a small fire struggles in the thin air and smokes blue. On a rack, stone adzes and bone needles and thorn work. No metal. A few gourds of stored water, precious and measured. A fence of sticks rotting back into the thatch by design, because on the High Thatch even a fence is expected to become ground again.

Their days are governed by water as behavior, not as climate. At dawn, while the ground mist is still ankle-high and the leaves are beaded, they come out to collect — cupped hands, woven scoops, hollow stems pressed to a seep, tongues out to lick. Children learn early which stone pockets are for drinking and which are for frogs, and that you do not drink from a frog-stone even when you are thirsty, because to do so is to take tomorrow’s water. They clip the mat for greens and fiber, setting their terraces in the shallow channels where they are already half-plant, knowing the mat will close over their work if they stop tending it. They wear the paths pale between the runners, the only places the thatch is actually bare. They know the hour when the insect choir will cover their talk, and they sit patient together until it passes, because shouting over that sound would be a waste of breath and water. They use the place the way the animals use it: shade when hot, hollow when it rains, leaf when they need a roof, a herd when they need to move. They do not tame it. A people who tried to tame the High Thatch would be outgrown in a season.

All of it is ordered by heat and by the brief, violent rain. Storms are born over the plateau’s own center and die over it, and watchers can see them coming from twenty miles away, turning in slow circles over the green, unsure if they will ever be reached. Most of the fall is virga, mist, steam off the hot thatch. What lands is drunk in an hour — channels darken, runners lift, pale shoots push up overnight and are cropped or hardened by morning. That small drink is the year’s hinge. When it comes, everything that can hold moisture does, and everything that needs it learns to visit: the tunnel-crawlers surface to feed, the frogs sing louder, the insects rise, the grazers turn their broad fronts toward the darkened swales, the walkers lift their root-legs and take one patient step, the giants open, the people come out with their scoops. For a day the plateau is deafening and green and frantic. Then the heat sits down again like a hand, the leaves fold, the runners curl, and the life goes back down into shade, into fiber, into the cool dark beneath the mat, to wait.

In the evening the light goes ordinary gold and the haze clears, and the true strangeness shows in plain sight without needing to be hunted. A grazer goes by with a forest on its back and a bird asleep in its moss. A walker pauses mid-step and will not set its foot down until night. A giant leaf, folded all day, opens in the dark. The mat, which all day seemed sun-cured and brittle, steams faintly where the day’s heat leaves it, and condensation begins again on every underside — a slow breathing in and out of water that never becomes a river, but becomes frogs and singers and herds and people, who carry it inside them from one brief fall to the next.

It goes on like this, at the pace of something old: rot and regrowth, migration and hunger, a vast living floor feeding everything that walks or flies or crawls upon it, and holding them all between its fibers until the next water comes to turn the whole green, audibly, all at once.
