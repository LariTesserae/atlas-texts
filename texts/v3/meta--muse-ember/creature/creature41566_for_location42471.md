At first, you could be forgiven for thinking nothing lives here at all.

The ground gives back only stone. Grey, polished at the edges, warm with a cold that has settled into it for longer than anything you know how to count. Under the thin skin of grit, the permafrost is always close. And under that grit, and over the stone in irregular plates, there is life — but it does not stand up to be counted. It lies down.

The lichen is the first of them, and it is not quite plant, not quite animal, not quite mineral in the way you would mean it. Black-green crustose plates, ochre, pale sage, spreading in patches no larger than a thumbnail and sometimes, where the damp holds, joining into a continuous skin. It is damp to the touch even when the air seems dry. It sponges the condensation that gathers on the north side of every stone at night, holds it for hours, and lets it go slowly, drop by drop, in the morning. It smells faintly sweet and ferric, like rain on a hot plate.

Up close, the skin is not flat. It is a slow crowd. Micro-colonies rise in tiny domes, breathe, exude a mild acid that eats stone a fraction at a time. In the spongiest parts, under the surface film, there are smaller grazers still — translucent mites and thread-thin worms with no eyes, moving through the wet mat by feel, scraping bacteria off the lichen cells and leaving the lichen cleaner for it. They are the first instance of the pattern you will see again at continental scale: everything here cleans, processes, and passes something on.

Between lichen plates, in cracks, there are cushion mosses, no larger than your hand. Each cushion is hundreds of tiny shoots holding water in the curve of their leaves, opening and closing with humidity like a mouth that only moves once a day. And every few meters, a tuft of stiff wiry grass, three or four blades, each blade ribbed, hollow at the base, carrying up a little of the damp from under the stone to where the dim light can, barely, touch it. That is the whole surface flora. No tree could root here. No shrub needs to. The lichen is the soil, the grass is an afterthought for decoration, the moss is a cup.

Life on the north faces of stones is more various. At night, when moisture gathers, things come out to lick it. Tiny, flat, sucker-footed clingers, the size of a grain of rice, press themselves to the cold side of a boulder and move up in a slow line, drinking condensation as it forms. They have no color to hide with; they are the color of wet stone, because that is what they eat. In the deep, smooth hollows where mist lies for days and then lifts an inch, something else hangs in the mist itself. You will not see it until the light catches it wrong. A drifter, no larger than a thumb, translucent, with a fringe of electric-blue filaments along its belly that pulse very slowly, filtering the damp air for spores and iron dust and the breath of the lichen below. It does not fly. It is carried, and it descends so slowly that from a distance it looks like a change in the mist.

In the few deep hollows where melt has collected into flat ice lenses — opaque, unmoving, like eyes set into the rock — there is life inside the ice. Not swimming. Not moving at all for long stretches. Pale, lens-shaped bodies pressed against the underside of the ice, their blue filaments dim, waiting for the season when the lens thaws a millimeter from below and a drop of water can run down a shallow swale and carry them, still dormant, to another hollow. You could put your palm to an ice lens and feel cold damp through the dry dust on the other side, and feel, if you waited long enough, a faint electrical tick in reply.

These small things are foundational, but they are not the dominant weight of the place. They are the substrate. The pressure, in every sense, comes from above them, and from a distance.

You will notice them first as a mistake. A boulder that was not there yesterday, a little higher along a ridge. Or, more often, as a rhythm in the ground under your feet. Not sound. Pressure. A slow, piston-like thud transmitted through permafrost and damp lichen, felt in the soles before it is heard anywhere, and in this damp, thin air it may never be heard at all. Sound is eaten here — by water in the air, by fat, by distance. The big animals have traded it away.

When they finally fill a dip in the land, they fill it the way a hillside fills — gradually, plate by plate, until the whole dip is plated backs. They are the size of a bus, longer if you count the slow tail of overlapping segments behind them, and they move on six legs that work like hydraulics. Each leg is a piston in a housing of fat and living tissue, ending in a broad, sucker-like pad that finds the damp lichen and lifts away clean. The legs cycle 1-2-3, 1-2-3, in a herd of dozens in perfect synchrony, at a walk that is barely a walk. A herd crossing a swale takes a season. The trail behind them is a polished groove worn into lichen and stone along a line they have followed for millennia, and if you live here long enough you can measure it deepening by a hair each year.

Every individual is built the same. It is not that they are all alike; it is that there is no other way to be built here. Hexagonal dorsal plates, overlapping like boiler plate, rust-colored, bolted in effect by tissue that can be regrown if cracked. The head is low, blunt, blind. The eyes are pits. There is no mouth for sound and they have never vocalized. The jaw is for scraping.

Watch the front rank and you see the industry. A head lowers, the jaw opens and closes in a strict rhythm, and it peels the lichen crust from stone with a soft, sumptuous shush. The scraped material is passed back — the animals behind do not scrape, they grind. A secondary chamber, muscular and warm, turns the lichen to a slurry no louder than a sluice. Further back, others ferment. The fermenting sacs are warm enough that you can feel heat rising off a flank in the cold air, and through a translucent segment of skin near the belly you can sometimes see a faint interior glow, blue-green, moving like a slow tide. That is not light for you. That is a byproduct. Further back still, the electric organ runs in a long strip along the flank, a tissue dense with stacked cells. When it discharges, it does so in a faint blue pulse that travels down the line of the herd, animal to animal, like a signal on a wire. It is navigation, it is language, it is a clock. The pulse is slow enough to count.

They do not make anything. There are no artifacts, no tools, no built structures, because nothing here is built. It is grown, worn, or processed. What the herd makes is heat, and electricity, and body mass, and nutrient slurry, pressed out at the rear in thin pads that break down into the lichen and feed it. Downstream, on the same ancient groove, another herd will process what this one has left, more slowly, and leave its own pads.

At scale this is a continental metabolism, scattered thin but total. One great body, then a hundred meters of emptiness where only the lichen tufts stand, then another. But the emptiness is balanced. The grazers’ feet polish the lichen along their line and thin it, so it regrows faster. Their breath and the moisture off their flanks at night create damp pockets ahead of the herd, so the lichen ahead is wetter than it would otherwise be. Their slurry feeds the crust behind them. Their warmth, spent over a season in a swale, thaws the permafrost a fraction deeper, and the water that gathers there feeds the next decade of tufts. They shape the land by grazing it at the pace of geology.

Maintenance is social because it has to be. If a plate is cracked, another animal will come alongside, butt it gently with its blunt head, extend a thick tongue, and lick the crack closed. The tissue knits under the tongue. If a leg’s piston stiffens, two others will lean their weight against it in rhythm until it frees. The parasites on them have a job, too. Small, flattened cleaners live in the joints between plates, rasping away necrotic tissue, and they skim a little of the electric discharge from the strip along the flank to run their own photophores. At night, in a mist hollow, you can see them — tiny blue lights winking in the dark seams of a sleeping herd, a constellation of points too small and too slow to be mistaken for stars.

Births happen standing, in the open. No nest, no shelter. A calf, if you can call it that, is already plated, already blind, already synchronized, and it appears as a swelling along the mother’s flank that splits, segment by segment, into a new modular body. It touches its mother’s leg with its own for a day, learning the 1-2-3 by pressure through the ground, and learning the pulse by the blue flash that runs through muscle and fat. Then it falls in behind her. There is no other instruction. The herd closes around a birth like a wall, not for protection from predators — there seem to be none worth naming — but because the line must not break.

Death is processed, not mourned. When an animal fails — a piston that will not cycle, a ferment sac that cools — it lowers itself to the ground in the groove and goes still. The herd moves on. The small fauna arrive. The sorters come first.

Sorters are low, worm-like, ridged along the belly, with heavy shovel jaws and no eyes worth mentioning. They crawl through bone and shell and broken plate and stone, lifting, testing by weight and density, and sorting. From a distance their piles look like industrial spoil heaps: neat cones of bone-grit here, a fan of plate shards there, a sorted line of stones by size. Up close you see the sorting is electrical as well as mechanical; their ridged bellies give a faint blue flicker when they test a piece for mineral content. They eat almost nothing that you would recognize as food. They eat the sorting itself, taking a little heat and charge from each piece they move, and they leave the piles cleaner and more legible than they found them. The lichen moves into the piles within years, feeding on the nutrient dust the sorters have concentrated.

The gliders take the piles elsewhere. Broad-winged, stiff, with wings so long they seem to be made of the same plated material as the grazers, they soar for hours without a wingbeat on the high air above the plateau — the one place where the air is clear enough to move fast. Every so often one drops, lands with a dull clink on its keel, picks up a sorted piece in its beak-foot, and carries it to a new hollow, often a hundred meters away, where it adds it to another pile. The gliders have a faint blue edge along the leading edge of the wing, visible only when the light is at its dimmest, and they use it the way the grazers use their flank strip: to signal position across distance without sound.

And then there are the ones you will mistake for boulders for your entire stay. They are torpid, plated, covered in the same black-green and ochre lichen as the stones around them, sitting in smooth bowls where mist lies longest. A rise of maybe a millimeter along the flank, once every few minutes, is the only sign they are breathing. They are not sleeping in the human sense. They are in a low-power state, conserving between the slow pulses of the herds, their electric organs dark, their parasites dim. When a herd passes on its ancient line, they wake in sequence, from nearest to farthest, like lamps turning on along a wire. The pulse reaches them by pressure through the ground and through the stone they are half-buried in. Stillness here is not death, and stone is not always stone. Some of them are old grazers who have gone torpid in place for years and will wake one last time to move a few meters and go still again. Some are a different kind entirely — sessile filterers who live their whole lives in one hollow, extending a fringe of blue filaments into the mist at night to catch what the drifters miss.

All of it moves slowly enough that you could live here and think nothing had moved. Lichen grows a fraction of a millimeter a year. An animal may graze the same square meter for a week, its jaw opening and closing as if it had all the time in the world, because it does. The weather changes elsewhere on the plateau while you stand in clear, dim light. The sun stays low and diffused through wet glass, the sky stays slate, the colors stay muted to grey, rust, black-green, and that faint electric blue under skin, which is the only saturated color the world allows itself.

What lives here, in sum, is not a collection of species so much as a single, scattered system for processing a cold, damp, empty place without waste and without sound. Lichen breaks stone. Small grazers clean lichen. Big grazers scrape and grind and ferment and discharge. Cleaners clean the grazers and borrow their electricity for light. Sorters sort what dies. Gliders move the sorted pieces to where they will feed something else. Torpid sitters wait in the hollows, breathing once every few minutes, holding the mist until the herd comes again. Ice lenses hold the dormant until a thaw carries them onward. And everywhere, under everything, the pressure of feet and the slow blue pulse keep time.

It is not wilderness in the human sense. It is industry that grew, not was imposed — standardized, recycled, maintained socially, synchronized by wire and ground, and carried out at a pace the stones would approve of.
