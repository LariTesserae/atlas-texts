It is not empty. It never has been, not in the way a sealed room is supposed to be empty. The living here does not sit in the room like tenants. It composes the room, layer over layer, until you cannot tell where the animals end and the damp steel begins.

The first thing you understand is the roof. It is not a roof. It is a ceiling made of birds.

Thousands of them, small and soot-coloured and starling-like, packed wing to wing and breast to breast across every beam, every horizontal pipe, the hoist track, the exposed rebar loops. They are so close that from below there is no sky between them, only a single breathing surface, dark grey-brown, wet-looking, heaving very slightly with the warmth of all those bodies together. Their feet have forgotten branches. They grip steel and cable and concrete with the same indifferent hold, claws curved for pipe rather than twig. Their plumage is filmed, matted in places by condensation and dust and their own waste, so that individual birds blur. Some are greyer, some almost black, one here and there with a pale feather that has never seen proper light.

They do not fly. Not as a thing they do. A ripple will travel through the mass — heads turning, bodies shifting a fraction of an inch — like wind moving through a field, slow and continuous, and then settle again. No one bird rises. No one bird leaves its place to feed or drink, because there is nothing elsewhere to feed or drink that is not already here, falling, dripping, running beneath them. They call through everything: a high, chittering, scolding, croaking chorus that never resolves, never answers, never stops to listen. They are, visibly, deafened by it. The valve hisses, the hoist clanks at the end of its travel, the pump shudders — and the mass only ripples. They do not startle. They have built their nerves into the machine rhythm until the machines are simply a second kind of weather.

They are fat. All of them, in a soft, low, unexercised way, because flight costs nothing and movement costs nothing and food arrives as a constant rain of waste and feathers and fallen eggs. Underneath them the pipes are whitewashed. On the deck below, the droppings feed what lives in the trenches. That is the design, if design is a word for something that simply persists.

Their breath, and the collective heat of the mass, makes small warm islands in the cold air. If you stand under the thickest part for long enough you can feel it on your face, a damp, animal warmth, and the moisture they exhale rises and condenses on the cold steel above them and rains back down in threads to keep the trenches full. They nest, if nesting is the word, in the insulation, in the tops of the ducts, among the batts that hang unfinished. You never see a nest cleanly. You see eggs, now and then, pale and speckled, that have slipped from the mass and cracked on a pipe and vanished. The rats take the rest.

In the damp air around the amber bulb hangs a second population, smaller and more ephemeral: moths and midges in a snow so thick the bulb has a halo. They bump and settle and rise again, dust on their wings, drawn for no reason they can use. The light is the only sun they have ever known, and it never moves, never sets. They fall into puddles. They stick to the fiberglass skin of the walls. They are the bodies you find, if you find any, curled in the corners of the deck plates, removed by the drip and the current before you ever see them. Others take their place in the halo without a gap.

If you look with a lamp at any vertical surface, there are eyes. That is the rats and the mice.

They run — that is their posture, even at rest — in the insulation and inside the cable ducts and between stacked crates, in channels made for them by the clutter. Mice quicker and smaller, rats larger and slower, both grey-brown, both damp-furred, tails pink and scaly and always in motion. Their squealing is a constant texture under the birdsong, rising and falling without pattern, sometimes a scream that cuts through everything for a second and then is swallowed again. They do not hide from you. They cross the deck plates over your boots, they run the pipes at knee height, they pause in a trench margin and look at you with copper eyeshine and go on. They take the fallen eggs. They eat the algae film at the edges of the water. They gnaw at insulation and at the damp skin of the wall, and in that gnawing they open new runs.

There is no night for them to slip out in. There is no night at all. The bulb burns the same dim amber at noon and at midnight, and they have no sleep in shifts. They rest where they are, whiskers trembling, ears turning independently toward sounds they no longer need to hear.

On the wet deck itself, among the puddles that tremble constantly from vibration and never give a clean reflection, sit the amphibians.

They are cold-adapted, mottled like wet concrete, and they sit in the film of water that is always ankle-deep and always the same depth, motionless for minutes at a time with their eyes open. They do not blink when your light hits them. They do not hop. Their skin takes in the moisture of the air and the puddles, the way lungs would, and their bodies are a precise colour for the deck — grey-green, algae-stained, speckled with rust. If you come close they will still not move, unless the vibration of your step goes through the steel into the water they sit in. Then, slowly, one will turn its head, or extend a leg a fraction, as if adjusting itself in a current. They are the only inhabitants that seem to watch you back. Everything else is busy being part of the mass or the flow. These sit apart, singular, patient, breathing through their skin while the water rushes past their feet.

Below them, in the trenches that cut the floor, is the third great layer.

Sumpfish, hundreds of them, pale and blind and eel-like, lie fin to fin in the black, oily current. They have no eyes to speak of — why would they? There is no glint down here except the occasional copper flash from the walls. Their bodies are soft, unpigmented, translucent at the belly, made for water that is always cold and always moving at the same slow, deliberate pace. They lie in the current, not swimming, letting it carry them, and then, all at once, with each cycle of the unseen pump, they surge upstream together, a single silver-grey body undulating, fins rippling, mouths opening into the flow. Then they settle again, fin to fin, and wait for the next surge.

They eat what the birds give the water. The droppings that fall from the rafters feed the algae in the trenches; the algae feed the tiny crustaceans that cloud the water so densely you mostly see them as a movement in the film; the crustaceans feed the fish. The fish, in turn, are eaten — not often, but just often enough that the number in the trench never climbs and never falls. Something in the water, something larger or hungrier, or the fish themselves when the current slackens, takes one. The water carries the rest of the evidence away to be pumped up and dripped back down again.

At the margins, where water meets metal, lives the smallest and most necessary world. There is a grey-green felt of algae and moss confined to the seams where calcareous water seeps from the concrete, soft under your boot through the made-mud sludge. A dozen pale, etiolated shoots push up through a floor grate toward a light they will never reach, white and stretched and still pushing. A film breaks and re-forms on the surface of the trench water — algae, bacteria, the skins of midges, the dust of feathers — and the fish break it from below when they surge. In the sludge are worms and larvae and all the minute things that turn waste into food, living in rust flakes and crushed insulation and crushed feathers, in a soil that was never outside. Nothing grows here in any real sense. It grows just enough to be eaten.

That is the loop, and it is closed. Birds drop waste; waste feeds algae; algae feed crustaceans; crustaceans feed fish; fish feed whatever in the trench is waiting; rats take eggs; breath and steam and drip keep the water at its level; water keeps the amphibians wet; the amphibians sit and do nothing and keep the deck alive with their stillness. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves.

So the animals do not age in any way you can see. A bird that dies — if a bird here can be said to die, and you will never find a body to prove it — is simply replaced in the mass by another, the ripple closing over the space. A fish taken in the trench is a space in the row that fills by the next surge. The moth in the halo falls and another is already there. Population here is not a count of individuals. It is a property of the room, like the water level, like the amber light, like the half-poured wall that will never finish pouring.

They are fat, and deafened, and they do not startle. They move constantly — the flock ripples, the fish surge, the rats run, the water drips and is pumped and drips again, the hoist travels back and forth carrying nothing — and nothing changes. They are not living in the Condenser Pocket the way animals live in a wood or a marsh. They are its marsh, its weather, its wet, dim, deafening, unfinished self, made animal.
