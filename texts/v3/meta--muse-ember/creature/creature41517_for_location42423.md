If you look for a resident, you will miss them for a long time. That is the first deception. The room presents as empty, and empty is mostly true — but the empty is not vacancy. It is habitat. Things live here in the intervals, in the warm damp between stems, in the substrate under your feet, in the sound before it has a cause. You do not meet them face to face. You feel them lean.

The floor is the first to announce itself, and it is not a floor. Press your palm and it gives back, warm and a little sticky, pulsing faintly under the skin. It is a weave — pale roots and damp hairy stems braided living and dead until the distinction is useless. This is the great inhabitant of the pocket, if inhabitant is even the right word for something that is also the ground and the walls and, in the far corner, the ceiling. It heaves, minutely, rib by rib, up and down under your weight like something breathing in its sleep. Sap moves in it. You can put your ear to it, and you will, because there is nothing else to do with all that stretched space between stems, and you will feel it thinking out loud in your teeth.

It is not one plant. It is many, agreeing to be one surface. Individual roots test and withdraw. Individual stems lengthen with a sharp *tok* you hear before you see the movement. Dead tissue is not left to rot here — there are no decomposers, no slow fungal siege — dead tissue simply becomes floor, becomes wall, becomes leaf again directly. Growth is the only recycling, so everything that dies here is immediately re-enlisted. The mat is therefore a crowd of ancestors and infants indistinguishable, all warm, all patient, all sensitive to pressure. Step and it settles. Stay and it settles around you, a fraction closer.

Above it, the ceiling lives too. Not one leaf, or not only one leaf, but many lapped like shingles, translucent, green-dark on the upper side and pale and hairy underneath. Light comes through them correctly — noon-colored, clean white-yellow, pooling where it should pool — not because there is a sky beyond, but because *they* let it. They are the source and the filter. When a shoot uncurls near the wall, turning its tip and turning, reaching in slow motion, it is not reaching for distant sun. It is reaching for the light that is already on it, the light these overhead leaves are making for it.

The undersides are busy in a way the tops never need to be. Hairs catch condensation. Leaf-cups open, not at random times but when the air is most still, like tiny mouths at a rain that has not yet fallen. The condensation itself is a resident of sorts: it sheets, swells, trembles on the leaf-ceiling, then falls all at once in a brief sudden shower — a dozen drops from nowhere in particular, booming hollowly when they strike leaf out of all proportion to their size — and then nothing for a long minute. You often hear the drip land before you see it fall. That lag is not an error. Sound arrives first here. It is how the plants coordinate.

Because the few stems are individuals with habits. One translucent stem near the light carries sap visibly, a clear pulse every few seconds, heart-regular, too slow to be human and too sure to be accidental. That pulse is a clock for everything smaller in the room. When it beats faster, spores rise. When it slows, they settle.

Spores are the weather. They drift in the void between stems in a fine, invisible weather of their own, rising when it warms, settling when it cools, cycling hourly as if this shallow pocket had seasons compressed into it. They are not dust. They are seeds of future rooms, and also messages. The cracking and humming and fluting are not byproducts of growth; they are pollination, warning, mating call, all at once. A hollow stem, moved by a wind that should not exist down here, makes a low note that never quite resolves. Overhead, unseen leaves rustle with a dry crash like surf even though nothing stirs. The plants are talking through the substrate and through the air between, and the talk is the pollination. To live here is to be tuned to a frequency you feel more than hear.

Water is a visitor, not a resident, and something lives in its visits. There is no river, no spring. A film shines on the root-mat and is gone. A puddle in a hollowed stem is there when you look away and not there when you look back, and vice versa. The plants drink first — always first, politely but absolutely — and what remains is held for minutes, maybe less, in leaf-cups and root hollows. In those minutes, micro-things bloom and vanish with it: motes that swim in a bead, skins of fungi that never quite rot, films that catch light like oil. When the water leaves, they do not die in the ordinary sense. They are reabsorbed, pulled back into the mat, into the pulse. To be alive here is to be ephemeral in layers: a spore season in an hour, a puddle fauna in a glance, a root in a season.

At the center of the room, where the emptiness is most complete, stands the one that has chosen to be seen. A single green shoot alone in a sunlit clearing, thriving in nothing, feeding on light and damp and silence. It does not climb, does not grope. It stands. Predator and prey are reversed here anyway, and this one is the reverse made still. Around it, the hunters do their work.

They are slow, vigorous, and polite. Vines grope across open air toward a stem over there. Rootlets test the crack above, fiber by fiber, hungrily trying to stitch the chamber shut again — those reaching fibers are the youngest and hungriest inhabitants, literally closing the door they were born under. Leaf-cups open at rain. Anything small that falls into the mat is slowly, politely taken. There is no violence that you can point to. There is only a long, patient attention. A seed let go from your hand does not always fall. Sometimes it hangs, deciding, then drifts sideways toward a stem as if tugged by attention. That tug is real. The plants notice motion in their void and lean, minutely, toward whatever moves. Distance itself stretches when they are attending: reach for the far stem and your hand must cross meters that a moment ago looked like centimeters; turn your back and it is close again. The void between leaves can go dark and leafy and far, a place you could lose yourself in, and yet it is also a clearing. The hunters live *in* that distance. They are made of it.

In the southeast corner light falls sideways and casts a shadow flat on the wall, and plants grow from the floor in both directions, toward you and away from you, which is also up and down. Inhabitants there pay no mind to your up. A root that is floor here is ceiling there, and the lives on it have oriented themselves accordingly, their "toward the light" meaning something sideways to you. Your reflection in a wet leaf blinks a half-second after you do. Something in the room is always a fraction behind you, listening for your timing.

Almost nothing animal lives here. Fauna is rare to the point of rumor, and rumor is accurate. If you are lucky, and still, and stay long enough that the plants stop treating you as a falling thing to be taken, something pale and blind and slow will uncurl from a curled leaf in the damp corner — a larva, a slug without a shell, something that breathes once every minute — and regard you with no eyes at all. Its regard is mostly absence. Then it goes back to being plant-matter again. Not hides. Not begins to hide. *Goes back*, as if animal was a brief phase it passed through, a mood of the tissue. Its presence is registered more by the plants leaning a fraction toward it, as if listening, than by its own movement: a faint scrape somewhere, a silk thread that is not there when you touch it, a pressure of attention from the mat. It may be the only animal, and it may not be one individual but a behavior the damp corner occasionally performs when the air is warm enough. It eats nothing you can see. It breathes once a minute, and the whole room seems to breathe with it, once, politely, and continue.

Smaller still, and more numerous, are the knot-eaters and the hair-feelers. The room keeps a trace of tool-use — no building, no structure, nothing fabricated larger than your hand — and that trace has become a habitat now that its maker has left. On the cleanest part of the root-floor, where light pools, lies a single flake of knapped stone, sharp, recently struck. Beside it a short length of vine fiber tied in a simple, competent knot. Beside that, a cup or two hollowed out of a thick stem, worn smooth inside as if from use. A score-mark, three lines cut into the living wall, still green at the edges.

Whoever made them did not live here. They visited, they worked, they left, and they did not build. But the inhabitants have moved in on the punctuation. Rootlets are feeling their way across the stone, hairs overgrowing its edges; in a season a root will lift it, gently, and carry it under. The knot is already being eaten as sugar by things too small to name, fibers loosening, sweetness drawn into the mat. The hollowed cups catch rain and hold the ephemeral puddle-fauna a moment longer; their smoothness is now a place for beads to swell. The three green lines in the wall are healing tissue, raised and tender, a wound that is also a scar that is also a message in the plant language. Nothing here is separate. The tool does not remain a tool. It becomes floor, becomes food, becomes landmark, becomes the reason spore weather settles there.

So the whole ecology is a temporary agreement, made yesterday when the rain tore the earth down through root and leaf-litter and left that fresh sharp-edged slit, white at the edges, sap still beading. These lives did not evolve here over ages. They assembled, overnight, out of canopy and soil and light, and they will dissolve the same way. The walls are already healing over the entrance crack, the stitching fibers meeting across the light. When it closes it will not be this room sealed, but a new room elsewhere, a pocket migrated a hand's-width under the earth, fresh and sharp again, warm and damp and lit. The inhabitants will migrate with it — not by walking, because almost nothing here walks, but by reaching, by sporing, by pulsing, by becoming floor and then leaf again a hand's-width to the side.

Stay long enough and you will learn you are not the only listener. The light follows the plants, not the sky; stand where no leaf reaches and you will be in ordinary darkness even though daylight is inches away through the ceiling. Water arrives when the air is most still and leaves when the plants are drinking hardest. Sound is the weather and the pollination and the warning all at once. And the most alive thing in the room, the thing that makes all the others cohere, is the long hollow hush between presences — the vast empty you could stand in with your arms out and almost touch the far walls, except that the far walls are not close. The residents live in that hush the way fish live in water. They are the hush, warming and dampening and talking in it, until the crack narrows and you must slip sideways back up into green canopy and sun, and the room behind you looks, from inside memory, perfectly normal: warm, sunlit, green, damp, alive, and mostly empty.

It is not empty. It never was.
