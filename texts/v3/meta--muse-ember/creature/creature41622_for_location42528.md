Under a slab, where the night has left its thread, something is already awake before the sun can finish the work.

The seam is damp and dark and almost airless. A bead gathers, swells, holds. The grey-rust pelt of loom that fills the crack quivers toward it, filament by filament, as rootlets might quiver toward water if it had roots. It never quite reaches. A pink-grey seam-worm lifts its blind front into the damp and drinks the thread before it can fall, then sinks back and resumes the slow turning of silt. Above it, matted tighter into the felt, are the loom's tenants — dust-mites no larger than a grain of sand, translucent, living their whole lives in the inch between one stone and the next, eating the spores that the mat sheds when it is wet and eating, when it is dry, each other in turn. None of them has a name the builders use. The builders have no word for a life too small to be hunted or eaten or tended. They know it is there because the loom thins where the mites have grazed it, and thickens again, and that is enough.

This is where the basin starts to live: not on the surface, where the light is hard, but in the pockets where the light cannot quite reach and the cold stone sweats.

The loom is the first inhabitant in order of size and the one on which all others depend. It is not a plant. It does not root, it does not leaf. It is a skin, a felt, a woven mat of filaments that lives by eating stone and is eaten in return by almost everything that has a mouth. In damp it is supple and it breathes — not visibly, but it softens, it swells, it gives off the faint smell of wet rock and old smoke, and the mites move faster in it. In dry it folds into papery sheets that crackle when a hog's foot lifts them or a child's hand strips them for the beds. It lives in days, not seasons. A mat that feasts on a fresh dunging will bloom thick in a week, outgrow its damp, die back to a crust, be eaten to dust in three more, and bloom again from the spores in the dust when the next dew finds it. The builders live inside its cycle the way tenants live inside a house that is always being rebuilt around them. They strip it in blankets while it is wet, twist it while it is still pliable into cord, sew it while it is still grey and rust and bone-white into cloaks that will later shed the reflected light. They spread its rot-fed leavings on crushed stone. They are its gardeners and its harvesters in the same gesture, and the loom, having no mind, accepts both.

Under the slabs and out of sight for most of the day, the seam-worms work the ground into a place that can be lived on. They are pink-grey, blind, stringy, patient beyond any builder's patience. A worm does not dig a tunnel so much as it persuades silt to be somewhere else — a mouthful at a time, a body-length at a time, for years. Where they pass, foundations loosen and walls lean by a hand's breadth. Where they surface at night, they eat the loose loom disturbed by their own digging, and their castings, rich and fine, go back into the beds. They are also food, and that is part of their tenancy. The builders do not hunt them the way hunters hunt in other places, with pursuit. They wait. A wall that has begun to settle is known to be wormed. Children crouch beside it in the cool of morning with bone hooks, motionless for hours, learning the particular quiet of a seam before a worm shows its front. The hooks are small and curved and the children handle them as if they were knitting needles. What they catch goes to the sunk pits where dung burns in a grey thread of smoke, and the slow heat, over hours, makes the meat tender and mineral-sweet. The castings of the worms that escape go to the loom beds. The worms that are caught would have made castings. Nothing in the basin is wasted, because nothing in the basin is plentiful except labor.

On the surface, asleep against walls, are the rasp-hogs.

You will know their breathing before you see them. A herd at rest is a street of low, domed shells mottled white and rust, shoulder to shoulder, six jointed legs tucked under them, heads lowered until the lip of the shell is almost a roof over their blind faces. Their throats work slowly and audibly, and the sound is wet and intimate, like someone sleeping with his mouth open. At dawn their shells sweat. The condensation beads on the upturned domes and runs down in tiny rivulets that the hogs lick up with file-tongues without lifting their heads fully. They are utterly silent when they move — padded feet on compacted silt, a herd shuffling in a loose body from colony to colony across the emptiness — and they are utterly unignorable when they settle to work.

For work is what they do, and the builders' city is shaped by it. A hog lowers its head to the foot of a wall, to a fallen slab, to the edge of a fungal bed, and rasps. The tongue is a file, the motion is smooth and unhurried, and over a season a wall left alone will be eaten at its base until it slumps. The builders do not fence the hogs out, and they do not tame them in. A herd is a force of weather with appetites. When a herd settles on a precinct, that precinct is besieged — not attacked, simply occupied, eaten at the edges, dunged upon, slept against, until the loom in its beds has bloomed thick and the stone has been worked thin and the herd, sensing the bloom elsewhere, lifts its heads all at once in the evening and shuffles away in silence toward the fresh beds. The builders follow, in a sense. Hog-herders walk behind the herds with scrapers, not sticks, and they scrape the dung when the herd moves on. That dung, rich in dissolved stone and fungal matter, is spread and worked into prepared beds, and for a week the loom grows as thick as a pelt. The hogs have fed the beds by eating the walls. The builders have fed the hogs by letting them eat the walls. The relation is older than any single wall, and both parties maintain it with the practicality of neighbors who know they cannot move away.

Above them, when the light is still kind, hang the veil-swarms.

They are not birds. Nothing with bone flies here. They are pale, translucent sheets that fold and unfold on membranes larger than their bodies, and a thousand of them can cross the bowl at dawn and dusk without a sound except the faintest cooling of the air as they pass. They live by drinking condensation where a surface sweats — off the upturned face of a dew-slab, off the warm side of a wall, off the domes of the sleeping hogs themselves, though the hogs do not seem to mind. When they settle, they settle in layers, ticking softly against stone, folding themselves into rectangles and fans and irregular veils of themselves, until a wall that was bare is patterned with living cloth. Small mites live inside the folds and hitchhike from wall to wall. The swarms have no young that anyone has found, no nest that anyone tends. They are born, perhaps, of damp and air and the meeting of cold stone and warm day, and they die in the same way, falling in dried sheets that the builders' children pick up and that the wind carries into the seams to feed the loom. The builders wear veils of woven loom against the reflected glare, but they also, without hurry or ceremony, mend their veils with a strip of dead swarm-membrane when the loom is short. The membrane takes the light differently, softer, more forgiving to the eyes. No one comments on the resemblance between the creatures overhead and the cloth over the builders' faces. It is simply practical.

And then, slowly, the builders come into focus, because they were there all along, low to the ground, in the shelter of the walls they are always rebuilding.

The first sign of them at dawn is not a face. It is a hand testing the cold of a cistern lid, pressing flat to the stone as if listening through the palm for the drip inside. Then another hand, another, at the mouths of cisterns across the bowl — deep, stone-lined pits covered with overlapping slabs, the city's heart. The builders' hands are broad and their nails short and worn, their skin pale under the veils they wear against the up-bouncing light. Their bodies are made by the work: stooped from hunching over low walls, knees bent from crouching beside wormed seams, shoulders thickened from carrying blankets of wet loom and sorted rubble. They do not stand upright to look at the world. They pass through it at half-height, between the far-spaced houses, in cloaks of woven loom that catch the last of the evening light and shed the noon glare, their faces covered to the eyes, their eyes themselves narrow and accustomed, squinting not at the sky — the sky is white-hot and high and cannot be looked at — but at the reflected brightness coming up off the floor, into chins and under lids, so that even in shadow they move veiled.

They speak in low voices, close to each other, because there is no echo to speak into and because a shout would be obscenely loud in this bowl. Their talk is therefore mostly hands. A tilt of the palm means *this slab has settled*. A finger laid along a hog's groove means *this wall is being eaten faster than we can rebuild it*. A cupped hand under a lintel means *drink*. Children learn this language before they learn to talk above a whisper, and the old ones, who sit for hours sorting rubble heaps stone by stone with softened hands, communicate with the briefest movements of all, having spent a lifetime reducing speech to what the work requires.

A builder's day is not divided into labor and rest the way it is elsewhere. It is divided into damp and dry, into the hours when stone sweats and the hours when it is drunk up by sun and thirsty mouths. At dawn, while the dew-slabs bead and the veil-swarms drink, the dew-tenders walk their fields of pale tiles tilted a degree toward a channel, righting those that have heaved in the night, tilting others back, listening for the faint thread of water running to the channels and down to the cisterns. The cistern-keepers, often the same people, lift slab covers a finger's width — no more, lest the damp be lost — and lower a hand into the cool, and listen for the single smothered echo of a drip. In a cistern's mouth, in the folds where air cannot move, a blind thread-creature hangs from the lining and eats the floating loom, and the keeper scoops it out with a bone spoon and flicks it onto the wall to live again, because it keeps the water clean and because it is there. No one thinks this is remarkable. It is a tenant like any other.

Mid-morning, when most of the sweat is gone, the children go worm-hunting and the hogs sleep with their shells beaded and cooling. The adults spread loom on beds, twist cord, scrape hog dung, sort the rubble heap that has shifted in the night as a wall was taken down and its stones re-stacked two paces over. In the open-sided workshops, hammerstones tap and scrape without ceasing — tick, tap, wet scrape — a sound so soft that you learn to hear it only when you are close enough to see the hands. The tools are hammerstones, flint scrapers, bone awls and needles, a little copper when a traveler brings it down from the rim. The work looks primitive until you see how much of it there is, how many walls thickening by one course each generation, how many courtyards filling and being roofed over, how many cisterns re-lined inside their own lining until the mortar of wet silt and dung has squeezed out and the stones' edges are soft as river pebbles from being handled by so many hands.

The builders eat what the basin provides in the order it provides it. They drink the cistern water, mineral and cold and faintly sour from the stone it has coaxed. They eat fermented loom cakes, flat and grey, kept in the squat windowless granaries where the cool holds. They eat seam-worms slow-smoked in dung pits, and occasionally, when a hog grows old and its rasping slows and its shell thickens beyond use, its meat, which is tough and must be worked for hours in the same slow pits. They eat nothing green, because there is nothing green. They lick the sweat from the undersides of lintels when the day is dry and the cisterns are low, all of them, without embarrassment, the way other peoples might drink from a spring. Their taste, if you could call it that, is for mineral and ferment and damp — the flavors of stone to fungus to hog to builder, taken in at every turn of the loop.

They live, most of them, in the same footprint as their parents. A house is a rectangular cell sunk halfway into the ground, roofed with two massive slabs, doorway facing the dawn. A child is born in that cool, under those slabs, and will spend a lifetime thickening its walls by one course at a time, rebuilding it two paces over when the ground heaves, using its own stones, until the cell is no longer clearly a house but a long low ridge in the plain, indistinguishable from the natural stone waves except for the hog's groove at its foot and the straight, purposeful mat of loom growing along its northern face. The old ones sit against these ridges and sort stones and remember where the walls were before the last heaving, and their memories are the only maps. When they die, they are not buried far. They are laid in the beds, in the crushed stone and dung where the loom will bloom thick, and in a week the mat covers them, and in a season the hogs rasp the ridge a little smoother, and the footprint remains, waiting for the next generation to re-stack it. It is not a grim practice. In a closed, moving place, it is the only practice that makes sense. Stone to fungus to hog to builder to dung to stone again. The builders are not above the loop. They are the part of it that knows it is a loop.

At evening, the glare withdraws a fraction and the basin begins its other work. The dew-slabs bead again. The veil-swarms rise, fold and unfold, and settle on the warm sides of walls in their living patterns. The hogs lift their heads from the walls they have been eating all day and shuffle, silent, toward the fresh beds, their throats still working. The tapping in the workshops stops one by one, like lamps going out. The builders walk the long worn roads home between the far-spaced precincts, cloaks catching the last reflected light, veils still on, hands loose at their sides, breathing audibly in the quiet because there is no reason not to and no one to startle. Somewhere a cistern lid is shifted, and a drip sounds, and echoes once, smothered.

Under the slabs, everywhere, the seam-worms resume their patient turning. In the damp seams, the loom softens and breathes and the mites move faster. On the plain, the herds settle shoulder to shoulder and their shells sweat in the cooling air. In the folds of the swarm-membranes, the hitchhikers sleep. In the cisterns, the thread-creatures hang motionless in the cold. In the sunk cells, the builders lie down under their massive slabs, in cloaks that shed the day's light, and listen — to the drip, to the hogs breathing against the wall, to the faint pop of stone cracking as it cools, to the sigh of dust as the ground settles one more fraction toward the middle.

The city does not sleep. It settles, and waits for its stones to be cool enough to make water out of air, and for morning to begin the work all over — the same work, the same eating and rebuilding, creation and destruction as a single act done at different hours, in progress for longer than anyone can count.
