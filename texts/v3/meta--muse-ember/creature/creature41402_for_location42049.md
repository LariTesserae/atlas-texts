At first there is only movement, and it does not sort itself out
easily into kinds.

Something white flickers, and flickers again, along the rim.
Something silver turns, all together, under a clear lid of ice at
your feet. Something dark and low pushes through the black slush
of the lanes, only its nose breaking the surface, and is gone.
Something hunched in smoked hide and bone moves head-down between
roofs, carrying a block of ice bigger than its torso, and its
shadow is blue and its breath is white and it does not look up.

Everything is touching something else. Everything is eating or
being eaten or rebuilding what the night has lifted and tilted.
You walk a little way and the ground under you is already
different, and the lives in it have already rearranged themselves
to meet it.

The people emerge slowly from that general press, because they
are the ones who mean to be still and cannot.

They are short from the cold, or they have learned to be. They
wear slitted visors of bone and smoked hide lashed tight against
their faces, and even behind that the light finds them, so they go
with heads down, eyes watering, brows beaded with condensation
that never quite dries. Their hands are red and chapped and
perpetually wet, their fingers stiff in the morning until they
work them in fat, until the bone needle clicks and the gut cord
is chewed soft between teeth and tied and tied again.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them packed into seventy
houses, which is to say there is no house that is only one house.
A wall of flat slate and sod and woven skin leans on the next
wall. A roof of ice slabs weighted with stone overlaps the roof
behind it. Lanes run no wider than two bodies, and those lanes
are really ditches with planks laid over them, because the
water must run somewhere and it will run through a doorway if
you let it.

Their day is the place moving under them. Frost heave lifts a
corner a finger's width in the night; by first light someone is
under it with a lever of bone, someone else is packing slush
and stone beneath, someone else is re-hanging a door that will
not close plumb. An ice lens grows under a retaining wall and
shoves it outward into a channel, and before the midday melt
someone has bailed the flooded threshold, dug the channel
clear with a stone adze, tied the wall back with sinew while
another watches the water climb. A chief dwelling discovered at
sun-up to be sitting two paces closer to the market hollow than
it was yesterday — the slab it stands on has drifted in the
slush — is not argued over. It is merely, in a season, a fish
store, and its former doorway becomes a hatch.

They make everything by hand, constantly, because anything
made once will be unmade by noon. Ice for building is sawn
with stone-toothed saws and dragged on sleds, because wheels
would sink. Lamps burn fat and the dung of the burrowers, not
wood — there is almost no wood to burn. What sod they use is
scraped thin from the cracks and hoarded. The few knives of
true metal exist, small and prized, wrapped in fat and passed
hand to hand, never left to freeze damp in an open palm.

They speak low. The cold takes a shout and makes it a puff.
So the market hollow, ringed with drying racks and shared ice-
storehouses and the tall chimney of the hearth-house — the only
chimney that can clear the roofs — murmurs rather than cries.
Fish for fat. Fat for cord. A stone blade for a morning's
mending of a neighbor's wall. It is an anthill's trade, quiet,
shoulder to shoulder, visors tilted toward the sound of a voice
close to your own ear.

Above them, and quite indifferent to their quiet, the birds hold
the rim.

They are small and white and tern-like, and they pack the
polished basalt in collars so dense the stone disappears. They
arrive without song, in a rush of air, and settle shoulder to
shoulder, and depart again the same way, a silent white rain
coming and going all day. Their droppings streak the sunny
faces of the walls in pale bands, and that waste is not waste
here. It feeds the algae that blooms in the melt channels.
The algae feeds the mites and springtails and beetles that live
under every loose stone, grazing lichen in dull orange and pale
green. The beetles are eaten. The mites are eaten. The chain
does not leave the bowl.

Below the people, and closer than the birds, the fish hold the
pools.

The pools never dry because they cannot drain. They sit over a
lens of ice a hand's breadth down, clear as window glass in this
hard light, and the fish swim under their gray lids in schools
so thick the water flashes silver when the sun hits at its
white angle. You walk over them without noticing, except for
the muffled thump when one hits the underside and turns with
the rest. At night the lids thicken, and in the morning they
crack in the sun, and new pools open and old ones seal shut and
the schools divide and rejoin in the pottery-like hollows worn
in the floor. They are visible the whole time, preserved in
their own element, like something you dropped a decade ago and
can still see through the ice.

In the slush between the channels, where a boot sinks and
meltwater rises around the sole, the worm-eels work.

Blind, translucent, pale as tallow, they wriggle through the
saturated soil eating frost and eating each other. They have
no need of eyes in ground that is always half-frozen and
always lit from below by refracted sun. Under the same stones,
in the lichen crusts, the springtails flick in clouds too small
to see until you lift the rock and they are suddenly everywhere,
and the mites follow, and the beetles bite at the mineral
film left by the weeping walls.

And in the melt-ditches between houses, paddling with only
its nose above water, goes the burrower.

Half mole, half otter, no bigger than a forearm, furred thick
against water that is colder than the air. Its tunnels through
the slush collapse and refill daily, and it digs them again
daily, erasing its own path. It leaves dung in pellets that
the people gather for lamps, as they gather the birds' waste
for the channels, as they gather everything. Its fur is too
valuable and too wet to be taken lightly; the animal is a
neighbor, a competitor for the same ditches, a pest in a
culvert and a resource in a lamp, all at once. Children learn
to listen for its paddling by the sound of the water closing
behind it.

There is green, but only as punctuation, and it lives tucked
in as tightly as everything else. Moss stains the cracks where
water lingers longest, green-brown and faint. A few willow-thin
shrubs, no taller than a knee, cling to the channel banks,
their roots feeling for warmth in the stone, their leaves
pressed flat against the wind. They do not make a meadow.
They make a suggestion of one, and the animals graze and brush
past them and keep moving.

The dead are not separate from this, either.

They lay them in shallow niches cut in the rim wall, where the
weeping water finds them and seals them in transparent ice.
They remain visible through the stone, smiling faintly, part
of the architecture the living walk past every morning to
bail out a lane. Nothing here rots. A tool dropped in the
slush comes back months later intact, faintly glowing. A body
in a niche is the same. The bowl keeps what it is given and
lays it down again under your feet, doubled and distorted,
so that walking the town you are always walking over its
history: last year's lane, a drowned storehouse, the fish
turning below, the ancestors smiling up through the weep.

By evening the light does not soften, it just drops, and the
birds come home in that silent white rain, and the fish turn
under the lids like slow silver weather, and the people go
back into their tilted, re-tied, re-leveled houses with their
visors loosened, their mittens damp again from the air of
the room itself, and lay down shoulder to shoulder in the
warmth of the communal hearth-house's overflow, or in their
own small fat-lamp glow.

It looks, from the rim, established. In balance. Long-existed.
Up close it is wet, crowded, bright, freezing, and never for
a minute still — and every living thing in it, from the mite
under the stone to the bird on the rim to the man carrying ice
head-down through the lane, is awake to the same churn, eating,
mending, turning together under a sky that gives them light
and takes away their sound.
