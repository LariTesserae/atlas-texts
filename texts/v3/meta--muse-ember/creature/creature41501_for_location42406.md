The first lives are the ones you could kneel beside and still miss.

They are not green, or not green in any way that promises growth. Crusts of black and rust-orange lichen follow the seams in the stone, a coin, a dollar, a hand, a few meters apart and then not again for a long stretch. Up close they are not on the stone so much as in the seam, occupying the crack where the stone gave up being sharp. They do not spread. You can come back to the same patch after what feels like an hour or a day and it is exactly the size it was, its edge exactly as crisp, its color exactly as leached. It is not growing over the rock. It is holding a place in the rock where damp condenses and stays.

If you look longer, the crust has a second crust, very faint, just under it, offset by a thought. When you blink it aligns. When you turn your head to get the light right — the flat light that never moves — it slips. The lichen seems to have learned to live in that slip. It feeds not on sun, for there is no deciding sun here, but on the film that forms on anything that sticks up and then neither evaporates nor increases. It drinks the promise of damp. It breathes so slowly that to call it breathing is generous. It was perhaps that way once and has been holding the pose ever since.

In the cracks where that damp collects most reliably there are the cushions of moss, dark olive, frozen mid-growth. Their tips are beaded with ice that does not melt, and each bead is large enough to see your own face in, distorted. The moss does not root in soil because there is no soil to root in, only gravel that has sorted itself and stopped. Its roots go down into the settled scree, down to the place where the sound comes up through the soles of your feet. The mist pockets lie in shallow basins with a hard, almost liquid edge, and the moss lives at that edge, drinking a mist that never lifts. It grows downward more than up. By midsummer here — and there is no midsummer here — it would look the same.

Very rarely a single wiry tuft of grass has taken hold in grit, one to a square meter, bowing outward in the same posture all the time. That posture is not wind. There are no gusts. It bowed once, toward the weak sun it saw in a tarnlet, the bright sky in the water that is not the sky above you, and it has kept bowing toward that absent sun ever since. Its stems are hollow. If you cup your ears near it, it gets louder, emptier somehow, as if the tuft is listening through you.

The bird is the first animal you are sure of, and even then you are sure of it only by the inconstancy of its eye.

A pale, ptarmigan-like thing sits on a stone so perfectly colored that you notice it because its eye blinks, and does not blink quite in time with the stone. It does not move while you watch. It may not move at all in any sense you can measure. Its metabolic bargain with the cold is to have thinned itself to almost an absence, not because the plateau cannot support it, but because the plateau does not invite it to be active. Once in a long while you swear its chest rises. The breath, if it is breath, fogs nothing. It sits, and its feathers do not ruffle, and droppings white on black rock below it are weeks old and still crisp, and a feather pressed into ice a hundred paces away belongs to another of its kind that sat there and has not yet decided to leave that impression.

You may in a whole day of traversing hear one call — a short, stone-like whistle that seems to come from below the ground and above the sky at once — and not hear another. It is not a call for a mate, or for warning. It arrives a fraction of a second before you look in its direction. It is a way of saying: I am still settled here.

The tracks are the same. Crisp as if made this morning, and yet weeks old, and crisp tomorrow, because nothing here works to erase them and nothing here works to work at all. The thing that made them walks so slowly that the impression settles before the weight is fully lifted. It walks mostly in the duplicate. That is why the stone twenty paces away, when you turn back, seems for an instant to be also at your boot, small and near and impossibly detailed. The walker steps there, in the second ridge running parallel to the real one, and its feet find the gravel here. It leaves a track that belongs to both ridges at once. It does not need to be seen to go on walking, and so it is rarely seen. You infer it, as you infer most of this fauna, by signs that have not faded.

In the tarnlets, no larger than your hand, frozen at the surface but liquid a millimeter below, other lives have made a different bargain. They live under the surface, in that millimeter, and never break it. They mirror the grey sky and keep a different reflection — a brighter sky, with the sun in it that is not there above — and they navigate by that sun. They are translucent, patient, each one complete in a space smaller than your palm. They do not drink the water so much as they live in the film that condenses on everything, on a stone, on a tuft of grass, on your sleeve, and then remains. When you stoop into a mist pocket and find it cold and beaded and still, you are stooping into their pasture. They stay where the moisture stays, which is everywhere in promise and almost nowhere in fact.

The built things are in keeping with this thinning, and they are not, strictly, built. A low ring of unmortared rocks, ten stones in a circle no head-height, with nothing inside it. A single slab leaning against another, forming a shelter that shelters nothing. A line of cairns, each no higher than your knee, running across a flat for fifty meters and then stopping, aligned to nothing you can see — not to the dim sun, not to a water pocket, not to each other in any honest geometry.

Those are places where something sat. Not rested. Sat, in the sense the bird sits, in the sense the lichen holds a seam. It placed a stone, or a stone settled by its weight, and then it placed another, at intervals that have nothing to do with distance and everything to do with listening. The cairns are pauses. The walker walks, stops to let the roar roll through its boots into its chest, stacks a knee-high pile to mark that it has listened here, walks again. The ring is where one stayed long enough that the circle became a habit of the ground. The leaning slab is a roof for a creature that does not need shelter from weather that can no longer alter anything, only a second surface to lean against so it can feel the resonating in two directions. The parallel scratches on a vertical face, too regular to be frost, too worn to read, are made by something tuning itself against the stone, or tuning the stone against its duplicate, until the two align for a blink.

That roar is not background. It is the medium they live in. Loud, continuous, low, like surf from very far below, like ice under pressure, mineral singing that makes your teeth ache. It does not come from a single place because they are not in a single place. Each inhabitant is hollow in the way a settled slope is hollow, a way of resonating without moving air. The sound travels for miles because there is so little to absorb it, so little that lives here except in thinning. They communicate by adding to it, a fraction before it arrives. When you cup your ears it gets louder, and emptier somehow, because they have noticed you cupping, and have adjusted their resonance to yours. The emptiness amplifies them, and they amplify the emptiness.

They reproduce, if that is the word, by duplication. Not by multiplying. By offsetting. A lichen makes a second crust under the first. A bird’s eye blinks in the duplicate a moment late. A shadow falls two ways — one faint, proper, and another, slightly paler, that points in a direction the light cannot account for and does not move with you. That second shadow is not a mistake of the light. It is a companion. Offspring here are not smaller versions elsewhere. They are the same thing, a thought away, running parallel, overlapping like a double exposure. The horizon folds into a ring too perfect and too close because they are looking out from the bowl of it, and also far, because the duplicate is far.

They die by settling. No carcass, no rot, because decay would be work, and work would break the equilibrium. A walker that sits too long becomes a ring. A bird that sits too long becomes a stone with an inconstant eye. A crust that holds its seam long enough becomes part of the seam, and the seam becomes part of the crust, and neither can be told apart by any effort of looking that does not slip. A footprint stays. A scratched line stays. A pearl of water under an overhang lengthens, trembles, and retreats back into the rock before it can fall, and if you leave and somehow return it will be lengthened in exactly the same way.

If you stay long enough — and staying is the only verb the plateau fully honors — you would thin with them. Your breath would come short without effort. Your shadow would learn to fall a second way. You would begin to drink condensation instead of eating, to listen through your boots, to place a stone where you paused, to bow toward the sun in a tarnlet. You would not be invited to be active. You would be settled.

And that is how they live here, sparsely, a hundred paces of nothing between one living thing and the next: crusts holding seams, moss drinking mist that never lifts, grass bowing to an absent sun, a bird sitting stone-colored with one eye blinking out of time, a walker in the duplicate ridge leaving crisp tracks for weeks, small translucent lives under ice navigating by a brighter sky, and all of them humming through gravel and settled slopes, arriving a fraction of a second before you look, keeping the plateau in a state where weather can no longer alter it. Not stillness as pause before movement. Equilibrium as a way of life.
