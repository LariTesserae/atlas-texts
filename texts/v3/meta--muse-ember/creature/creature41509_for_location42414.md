Up close, the green stops being a colour and becomes a surface.

You kneel — if you kneel at all, because the mat does not invite kneeling — and find leaf upon leaf, shoulder to shoulder, a hundred percent cover with a sharp edge, as if it had been cut. It is lush in the way a forest floor is lush, continuous and saturated, but it has no height to speak of. The rosettes that make most of it rise no more than a hand’s breadth. Their leaves are thick as fingers, rubbery, blue-green and deep green above, dusty purple underneath, overlapping in tight circles like shingles. They look wet. They shine in the normal white-yellow sun. Touch one and there is no wetness in it at all, no sap, no give of water. The shine is wax and oil, a dry polish. The leaf is cool-warm, firm, a little springy, and faintly salty at the edges where mineral has bloomed.

It is not moss. It is not a meadow that has survived. It is something that has taken the look of lushness without taking any of its usual means. The first thing to unlearn is the habit of counting individuals. There are no separate plants here in the lowland sense — no stem to trace to a root, no trunk, no single life you can lift out and name. What meets the eye is a mat: a continuous living skin, and the forms inside it are less like neighbours than like parts of one working surface.

Between the rosettes sprawl the ribbons. They are long, flat, succulent straps, metres long, lying along the stone and rooting wherever they touch a crack. They do not branch in the air; they run, they bend at right angles to follow a fissure, they darken at the rooting point and flush orange at the free tip — iron, perhaps, laid down where growth is newest. Where a ribbon crosses a vent-line it straightens out, as if the geology had laid it. A mat often has the geometry of a factory floor: straight runs, regular spacing, colonies following the cracks and vent mouths in lines that go on out of the green and into the white plain, where they simply stop.

Set into the mat, like tanks set into a floor, are the amber bulbs. Only their domes break the surface, translucent, honey-coloured, amber in the sun. Inside them something moves very slowly — a warm, salty melt carrying wax, oil, and dissolved metal in faint bands. They are storage, and heat-buffer, and perhaps a reserve for when a vent surges and the chemistry of the place briefly runs too hot and too rich to use. They never open. They never fall. They swell and settle by fractions, over years, and stay where they were made.

Standing rarer and taller — knee-high, never higher — are the chimney reeds. Each is a hollow hexagonal column of fused leaf, like a small stack, six-sided because the leaves have packed together under pressure. At the top there is a pore, and from it comes a faint warm vapour that is not water. It carries a smell of hot stone, of sulfur and hydrogen sulfide, of ozone, of metal. Now and then a chimney pops, softly, and releases its spores. The reeds look like they were built to vent something, and in a sense they were: excess gas from the mat’s own photochemistry, excess heat from below, the waste of a process that runs all the time and has nowhere else to go.

Painted over the black basalt around and under the mat are the crusts — emerald, rust-red, sulfur-yellow lichens, leathery and tough, in discs and rings and exact radiating lines. They follow the vent mouths as if outlining them. They are the most two-dimensional thing here, a colour laid directly on rock, and they mark where mineral vapour is strongest. Where they have lived the basalt is not cleaner, it is worked: pitted, darkened, chemically altered in the exact pattern of their growth.

And everywhere, flat on the surface, are the small star-shaped flowers. They are open all the time. They never close at night, because there is no need to protect them from anything; they never close in a dry season, because there is no season. No insect comes to them. No bird carries anything from them. Their points lie flat, each tipped with a bead that catches the light, and they are less like blossoms calling for visitors than like terminals, focused points on the surface of the mat.

That is the outside. Inside, the life is stranger, and more industrial.

Cut — or rather, press hard enough to feel the resistance — a rosette leaf, and there is no cell sap to find. In place of water the leaf holds a molten-salt solvent, an ionic liquid, warm and salty, that conducts and carries and does not evaporate. The photosynthetic machinery is suspended in that melt. Sunlight falls on the waxy upper skin, carbon dioxide is taken in from the warm, still air, and the chemistry proceeds at something like factory scale: oxygen vented out, energy stored not as starch but as wax and long-chain oil, metals pulled in and concentrated until they vein the tissue — copper blues in the blue-green, iron rusts at the ribbon tips, purple dusts the undersides where the light never reaches. The only water in the plant is water that was never water in any usable sense: the tiny amount bound in crystal lattices in the rock, mined out grain by grain and locked away in mineral inside the leaf, never released.

The roots do not drink. They mine. Their dark tips exude acid that dissolves basalt, obsidian, pumice, salt plate, ash — grain by grain, in place. Phosphorus, iron, copper, potassium, trace elements: they are taken up into the salt melt and carried, by slow convection in the ribbons and chimneys, to the rosettes to be used, to the bulbs to be stored, to the soil around the mat to be laid down again as richer ash and as ore. A colony that has lived in one place for a long time leaves the ground under it darker and heavier with metal, the rock softened and pitted by acid, the ash bound into a crust that will not blow away. The landscape as a whole does not erode — there is no water to carry anything off and no wind to winnow it — but locally, under the green, the rock is being refined. The plant is a smelter that grew itself. Root for smelting, leaf for photochemical capture, ribbon and chimney for pumping and venting, bulb for storage. Nothing here was built; everything here works.

Below the plateau a magma chamber runs like a furnace that never cools, feeding vents in regular spacing through the residually new rock. Heat and mineral vapour come up along those lines, and the mats follow them, because the mats are fed from below as well as from above. Sun from the sky, gas and heat from the earth, carbon dioxide from the trapped warm air: that is the whole supply chain. The still air over a dense mat is faintly electrified. The dry tips build static charge and discharge it in tiny silent arcs — blue-white ticks you feel in your raised hair more than you hear. The spores ride those discharges. That is their only journey, and it is a short one. A spore is thrown a hand’s breadth, perhaps a little more, and falls almost straight down into a crack where static and mineral vapour meet. There it roots.

That is a life, as far as this place allows one. A spore lands. It etches a pit in glassy rock with acid. It takes up mineral into salt. It puts out a ribbon to the next crack, roots there, feeds the new rosette from the old through the melt in the ribbon. A bulb forms where the traffic is heavy. A chimney rises where the pressure of gas is greatest. A flower opens and stays open, maturing spores all the time, seasonless. The rosette expands in its circle, overlapping its neighbours, until the mat has the hundred-percent cover and the sharp edge. Nothing moves except growth at the margin, and the slow internal circulation of salt, and the occasional pop of a chimney, and the tick of static.

There is no autumn in it. Leaves do not wither and drop, because there is no autumn to drop in, and because the leaf is already half stone. If a leaf fails, it does not rot. It petrifies where it lies, preserving its shape in mineral, becoming part of the ground it came from. The centre of a long-lived mat is therefore not dead in the ordinary sense; it is the colony’s own past, mineralised, with the living skin creeping outward over it. The mat ages by turning itself to rock from within while remaining green at the surface. A footprint in the ash stays. A fallen leaf, in the rare case one falls, stays. Nothing is turned over, nothing is eaten, nothing is buried by anything else. The history of the colony is visible in its own body.

And no one ever eats it. That absence is complete, and it has shaped everything. No insects in the flowers, no birds over the plain, no lizards in the sun, no mollusks on the rock, no tracks in the ash, no webs, no burrows, no dung, no bones. The lushness never had to move, hide, mimic, sting, or run. Its wax, its salt, its metal load, its hardness are not defences evolved against herbivores; they are byproducts of a chemistry that has no use for water and every use for mineral. Above the mat the volume is empty — no canopy, no flying things, no second layer of life — because this life has no use for volume. It occupies surface, and crack, and the thin gradient between hot rock below and hot air above.

The sound it makes follows from that. The leaves are rigid and do not rustle. There is no water to drip or splash, no wind to move through grass, because there is no grass. What sound there is belongs to the place rather than to the green: your step crunching a salt plate, the tick of cooling mineral, the high hiss of a distant vent, the soft pop of a chimney reed, the crackle of static when you walk through a dense patch and feel the hairs lift on your arms. The mat itself is silent, not because it is dead, but because stillness is its medium. Air too still to carry spores far, too still to rustle, too still to bring weather. Lushness here is not a season. It is a permanent condition for whatever can claim a vent.

So what lives on the high warm tableland is not a population of green things, but a set of chemical surfaces — lithic, gas-fed, salt-bodied — each one a mat that began from a spore, expanded along the lines the geology gave it, and will go on expanding at its edge for as long as the heat below and the sun above agree to keep it warm. Each green island, kilometres from the next across white mineral plain and black glass, is a separate life that will never touch another, its spores falling close to home, its static arcs carrying them no farther than the crack they were born in. Saturated green, shining as if wet, in a place where water will never fall — and inside it, no water at all, only warm salt, wax, oil, acid, metal, and light, working without pause and without ever having built a thing.
