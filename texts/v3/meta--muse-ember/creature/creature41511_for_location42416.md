It starts underfoot, before anything you can see distinctly.

The first inhabitants are the ones holding the ground together, and they were not there long before you stepped on them. Sphagnum, head upon head in a mat so dense it can never be wrung out, each tiny capitulum cupping water, sheltering mites and rotifers and bacteria in their pores. Through it, and through the black peat beneath it, runs the mesh — not a root, not a wire, something in between, teaching water which way to run and when to thin and freeze, keeping the skin over water from collapsing under a boot. Where a channel crosses it you can see a faint shimmer, as if the water were passing over an eye. The sphagnum treats it as it treats everything: as a support to grow around. The mesh treats the sphagnum the same way. Neither is the background of the other.

In the peat pools, in water that was not a pool yesterday, the amphibians are already there. They are everywhere despite the cold, because they have arranged to be. Gelatinous strings of eggs are looped around sedge stems in water the color of tea, in the hollows between sphagnum heads, in wheel-ruts of cold that will be gone by noon. The adults are small and dark and almost silent when they move, and then, in a lull between gusts, the chorus starts — rising and falling under the wind like a second, smaller storm. They do not wait for spring. A warm surge moving through the soil mesh is spring to them. A burst ice dam upstream is a cue to spawn in the newly dammed pool, which warms, which attracts them, which the next gust may scatter. Their lives are timed not to a season but to an event.

Above them, the sedges and grasses and flowering herbs are not a community in the established sense. They are a crowd in the act of arriving. Thick, dark-green leaves, waxy, angled to steal light from a lid of cloud, tufted so tight that leaves touch leaves and roots touch water. A bank can rise a hand-span in a night when warm water runs through the root mesh, and be flattened the next morning by a storm front colliding over the basin, and spring back upright in the lull, as if it had only been ducking. Ferns uncurl in the wet air and tear in the dry gusts. Low willow and dwarf birch interlace until you have to push through, their branches furred with lichen. On the drier ridges the lichen and fungi take over — saturated yellows and rusts, climbing, hanging, furring every fresh stone. In the hollows, fungal mats bridge a channel by morning and glow faintly orange, and are torn open by a water pulse by noon. No one of them is finished. Each is a temporary agreement between growth and churn, remade daily.

The channels themselves are inhabited in transit. Bacteria and algae film the tea-colored silt; iron-eaters stain the fresh cuts rust-orange. Pale fish appear in water that was not there an hour before, fast and blind-looking in the clear rush, riding a dam burst downstream, finding a side pool, vanishing when an ice plate shifts. Their eggs stay in the peat. The self-healing ice sheets that flex rather than break in the channels carry insect larvae on their undersides and, for a few minutes, carry the frost lattices too — perfect hexagons within hexagons forming on the still sides of stones, on the undersides of leaves, on the warm nodes your boots step over — until the turbulent air tears them away.

The birds are the first thing that makes a sound you can separate from the roar, and even they give up on separation. Wading birds stand knee-deep in the channel braids, in congregations that shift as the braids shift, calling over everything in short, hoarse bursts. A long call would be torn apart. A short one survives. Every call is answered by three others — not in conversation, but in location. In fog and dark and a wind that carries sound farther than it should, a bird is kept in the flock by being heard. Something cries from the ridge, something flashes in the water, something moves in the sedges: the density is the point. Solitary life would be lost here. So they live layered: breeding bank this year on a cut that did not exist last spring, abandoning it when a cliff sheds a slab and damns the pool below.

The small mammals move under the birch canopy in dense, nervous lines, following woven trails between burrows in the peat skin. They swim as readily as they run; their fur holds water the way sphagnum holds it. They are mostly nocturnal by necessity — eyeshine lifting and dropping as they move through the dark, which is not night as an absence but night thickened by cloud and wet air. They eat sedge roots and berries and fungi when the mats pulse bright, and they are eaten, presumably, by something that also lives in the churn and is rarely seen. Their nests are not dug so much as occupied, in the springy hollows where the mesh holds firm, and given up when the water table surges sideways across a whole flat in a glassy sheet.

Around the nodes, the insects persist, stubborn and loud. The nodes are warm through frost — heat exchangers, chemical exchangers, signal repeaters — and in the cold they are hearths. Moths and flies and beetles cluster there in the few minutes the frost lattice holds, pollinating the dark flowering herbs, drawn at night to the orange glow of the fungi mats. They do not care that the frost is a byproduct. They care that it is warm, and that everything else in the basin is cold.

And scattered at intervals that never quite become a pattern, the built inhabitants, who have been adopted in exactly the same way. The weather pod on stilts near the third channel is half-furred with moss, its mast leaning after the last rockfall, because birds nest on the lean. The maintenance shed is sunk to its windowsill in peat, its light blinking when the wind hits it from the north, its door opening onto sphagnum that has grown up to meet it; inside, a technician checks a reading with a wrench and a case of replacement modules, the way you would prune willow. The relay, fallen and cracked open like a shell, still ticks faintly from inside, an injured thing still calling for a flock that will not come in the usual sense. The bridge is not a bridge in any old sense — a pale strip that unrolls itself across a new cut overnight and retracts in the morning, leaving only two anchor pits in the mud. At night it is awake and you can cross. By day it sleeps in the mud, filmed with the same bacteria as the channel walls. A child who grew up here would think bridges were supposed to move.

The people who live here, when they are here, are migratory in the same way. They do not own the basin; they lodge in it, in rotations, checking the mesh and the lattice and the nodes the way you would check a fence line in a storm. They have learned to hear individual calls through the roar — a bird, a relay, a node warming, a dam about to go — because the air lattice carries sound farther than it should and keeps the condensate from turning to lethal ice-fog, and because everything else in the place has learned to be heard that way. They replace a module, unroll a bridge by walking over it, and leave, and the moss keeps growing over the place they left.

None of them hold territory. They hold timing. Growth, melt, freeze, collapse, regrowth is not a cycle here; it is what living is. The sedge arrives. The fungus arrives. The amphibian arrives on a surge. The fish arrives on a burst. The bird arrives on a bank that arrived that morning. The technician arrives with a case. The bridge arrives overnight. The lattice arrives in the air, unfelt, and holds it all from flying apart.

By the time you have walked far enough to see ridge and marsh and channel network overlapping into the next valley, you understand that the inhabitants are not separate from the churn that makes the sound. They are the churn's way of persisting — layer upon layer, wet and dark and cold and loud, arriving in a storm and arriving ever since.
