The first residents are not the ones you can see. They are not the ones you can hold.

They live in the steam, in the yellow-white rings where it condenses. A mat of bacteria and archaea, thin as felt, spreads itself across the shallow pools where the water gives up at body heat. It is orange at the center where the heat is worst, yellow at the edge, brown where it has begun to bury itself. All day it does one thing, patiently and without pause: it takes the dissolved minerals out of the water and lays them down. Grain by grain, day after day, it builds travertine. It builds the very terraces it lives on. Factory floors, banded and stepped, built by the workers who stand on them. When a vent hisses and sighs and hisses again on its cycle of minutes, the mat does not move. It simply works faster in the pulse.

Around it, in the cooler film of water, other films answer. In the bogs the water is green-brown and still, and there a different production is organized. Diatoms and desmids and the root-threads of fungi spread under the surface of the peat, fixing, storing, turning mist into mud. They have been doing it layer on layer for longer than the rim has had its softened shape. The red peat is not soil in the usual sense. It is a stored history of them, compacted and kept wet. They live in it, and they make more of it, and the cushion plants that sit on it like mattresses are only the visible roof of their work.

Above the peat, the plant inhabitants take over in layers so close that they touch. Thigh-high moss first — not a single moss but dozens, clubmosses creeping in rings, creeping ferns holding their spores underneath. They do not root deeply. They root sideways, drink sideways, and make a sponge of the ground. When you step, you do not step on ground. You step on them, and they close behind you.

On a single limb there may be five kinds of plant in its length. A bromeliad sits in the crook and fills itself with rain, and inside that small, clear tank a whole second population lives: midges, mosquito larvae, a glass-frog tadpole or two, mites walking the meniscus. The bromeliad does not eat them. It drinks their waste. Higher up, orchids root directly to bark in white clusters, their roots silver and flat, drinking not from soil but from air and the drip that never stops. Their flowers are waxy and brief, built for one kind of pollinator only, and they open in the thin, hot light as if unaware of the steam below.

Tree ferns unfold above them all, trunks furred with adventitious roots, fronds making their own small weather underneath. When one falls — and they fall regularly, toppled by their own weight in the saturated soil — it does not rot and vanish. In weeks it is overtaken. Seedlings push up from its trunk. Ants colonize its soft interior. Frogs tuck themselves in the damp hollows of its fronds. It becomes a nursery before it becomes soil, a bridge of life laid horizontal.

And the highways run through all of it.

The leafcutter ants do not eat the green shards they carry. An ant column a thousand long will pass a fern without breaking pace, each worker holding a piece of leaf overhead like a sail, moving in a line so steady it looks poured. They carry it to the mound, to the fungal farm inside, where other workers tend the gray, spongy gardens in dark chambers kept at the humidity of a greenhouse. The fungus does the digesting. The ants do the farming. The leaf is only feedstock. The system hums without sound — their communication is chemical, a laid trail of pheromone on the moss, refreshed with every pass. At night the highway goes quiet, and the trail fades. At dawn it is laid again.

A few meters away, the termites have built differently. Their towers are clay-colored, rough, like miniature cooling towers with ventilation shafts that run from the hot base to the cool top. Inside, chambers are kept at a precise temperature by the movement of air through those shafts — warm at night when the plateau cools in vapor, cool at midday when the sun is hard and the ground itself is warm under your palm. They do not forage in columns. They stay, mostly, inside their own climate, tending fungus of their own and the damp walls that regulate it. The tower breathes. You can feel it if you press your ear to the clay: a faint, regular exhale.

These are the builders. Around and between them, quieter, move the others who do not build but harvest what the builders make possible.

Frogs are in every pool that is not too hot. Glass frogs cling under leaves in the morning, translucent, their hearts visible, drinking through their skin the condensation that beads and falls *upward* off the warm stone. Bark-colored toads sit motionless in the moss until your footstep sends a ripple through it, then hop and vanish. They do not call all day. At dusk, for ten minutes, a chorus erupts from the gullies — a sudden, dense, plipping wall of sound — and then stops as if switched off. The rest of the night they are silent again, communicating in the subsonic tremors that travel through wet earth and root.

Hummingbirds work the bromeliad and orchid flowers in a constant, irritable whir. They are territorial to the point of absurdity at altitude where the air is thin and the insects are small. One will chase another from a white cluster, hover, return, hover again, never resting. They are the only ones who move fast here; everything else moves at the pace of growth.

Giant spiders string webs between ferns across damp gullies, webs so fine in the mist they are only visible when a midge blunders into them. They do not build a new web every day. They repair, thread by thread, in the same damp air that never quite lets the silk dry. Below them, insects of every size move silently — springtails in the moss, beetles the color of wet basalt, flies that live their whole lives in a square meter of bog.

At night the small mammals come out. No larger than your hand, they keep to neat runways pressed into the moss under the shrubs, from burrow to fern to water. You will not see them unless you wait, and then you will see only the flick of movement and the moss closing behind them. They eat the insects, the fallen spores, the soft shoots. Snakes take the frogs in the pools. The runways are also escape routes.

And above it all, using the same engine that drives the steam, the hawk rides. The geothermal updrafts give it free lift. It circles patiently over the second plateau and the cloud-filled gap, patient because at this height, in this thin air, patience is cheaper than flapping. It is watching for the snake. The system is complete in that small, silent circuit: mat builds stone, peat builds soil, fern builds shade, ant builds farm, flower builds visit, frog builds chorus, snake builds meal, hawk builds shadow.

None of them announce themselves. That is the adaptation to the quiet. Moss and wet leaves absorb sound, heat thickens the midday, and most of them have no need to be loud. They speak in chemicals laid on a leaf, in vibrations through a root, in a single plip that does not carry past the next fern. What you hear is mostly water — a drip from a frond, a trickle finding its course, the soft hiss that is the mountain's own breathing — and far below the rim, thunder from weather happening under the shelf, under them.

In the late afternoon the clouds rise to meet the rim and the whole population disappears into white for a while. You stand in cool vapor, water beading on everything, and the inhabitants drink it directly from the air, as your metal cup would fill itself if you left it on the moss. Then the sun breaks again, the ground warms under your feet as if the engine below had turned up just slightly for the evening shift, and the work resumes: the mat lays down another band of mineral, the ants lay down another trail, the termite tower opens its upper vents, the frogs take in the condensation on their backs and prepare, for ten minutes at dusk, to be loud, and then to be silent again.

They have run this plateau long enough to perfect their processes. They are doing so without us, at full production, under ordinary light.
