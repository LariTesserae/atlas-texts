At first, there is nothing.

You look for it the way you would look anywhere else — for a grass-blade, a wing, a track — and the plateau refuses. No soil to root in, no birds crossing the white glare, no insects rising from the damp. Your breath fogs and is gone. Your own bootprints fill with water. It would be easy to decide that this is a place that has simply subtracted life, and left the stone for you to walk on alone.

It takes a while to notice that the subtraction was not complete. It was only made in one direction.

Crouch at a seep-line where the water lingers longest, where a sheet a centimeter deep lies in a hollow and never drains. In the darkest wet, there is a grey film threading across the stone. Not green. Not a leaf. It has no root and no stem, it does not climb toward light — there is light from everywhere, so there is nowhere to climb toward — it runs sideways, following the water. If you touched it, it would taste of metal. It lives on vapor and on dissolved stone, on what condenses out of the thin air and what the stone gives up, a little at a time.

It does not hurry. It waits for the mist to roll in from the cloud sea below and deposit itself instantly on every cold surface, then it waits for that mist to burn off in minutes. In the wet minutes it darkens and thickens. In the dry, white minutes it pales and holds. Its day is not sunrise and night. Its day is wet and dry, wet and dry, over and over, sometimes on both sides of the same slab at once — frost blooming on the sunny side while it melts on the shaded side.

Where the threads thicken, something else answers them.

In the sheltered cracks, where slabs lean shoulder to shoulder and leave only hand-wide passages, rosettes open. They look like growth, and the plateau is careful not to let them be mistaken for it. Some are quartz, clear and pale, opening in the damp and closing in the glare. Some are salt, flowering where the water has left its minerals behind. Some are dendrites of clear ice and black volcanic glass branching like frost on a window, delicate and hard at the same time.

They do the work of flowers without being flowers. When condensation passes over the ridge like a sigh, they open to catch it. When the light bleaches everything, they close to keep it. Water beads on them from air that is not raining. They crystallize and dissolve, deposit and release. A quartz rosette may take a season to form and a minute to unform. A salt flower may live only as long as one of those passing showers of wet air, and be born again in the next hollow when the next sigh comes through the stone crowd. You cannot count their generations. There are too many, and they are too brief, and the humming of the slabs — a low note when the wind finds the right gap, organ pipes in the narrow places — sets them shivering so that frost flowers bloom and decay in seconds, a whole life lived in a vibration.

And the threads are moving, after their fashion, along the melt films.

Water here is not a river. It is a skin. Thin films crawl across polished slabs following old channels carved and recarved until they are smooth as veins. The erosion is mature, settled-in; the channels have been there long enough to be roads. The grey film follows those roads. It enters the basin carved into a boulder, polished inside by a stone bowl left beside it, and lines the inside of the bowl. It enters the small round shelter in the wind hollow, whose roof has collapsed inward, and creeps across the blackened floor of the fire-pit, where no ash survives the wet. It finds the hammer stone with the groove worn through its middle for a haft, and the flakes of worked black glass still sharp in a water film, and branches over their edges in black dendrites.

Nothing hunts here, in the animal sense. But stone and water trade places constantly, and that is a kind of hunting. The water dissolves a little stone and carries it into a crack; the salt and quartz deposit it again in a rosette. The rosette closes and holds the water a moment longer; the water takes a little more stone. Predator and prey have been replaced by exchange. The threads live in the middle of that exchange. They are not eating the stone, exactly, and they are not drinking the water, exactly. They are tasting both, and staying.

Slowly, as you walk deeper, the scale changes. The threads in one seep-line are not isolated. Follow the water — up, sometimes, toward the highest rim of a hollow, trembling and stopping before it rejoins itself — and the film follows. Follow a drip that falls for a half-second upward below an overhang before it remembers to fall down, and something microscopic rides it, or is left by it. Follow the channels under the slab field, through the dense upright thicket of weathered towers no larger than a town, and the film is there, lining the hand-wide passages, waiting in every shadow that pools the wrong way, toward the brightest source, as if the sun were beneath the cloud sea.

It is one thing, distributed. Not a colony of many things, but a wet mineral skin stretched over the whole plateau — grey threads for its nerves, rosettes for its mouths, the water sheets for its sea and its blood. The stone crowd is its scaffold. The double light, from above and from below, is its weather. It has no eyes and no mouths. It knows wet and dry, cold and glare, vibration through stone. It hears the ice crack like a distant gunshot and sing through your boots, and it answers by blooming.

The builders were tenants before you.

They were few, and primitive, and old enough that their work has become part of the erosion. The line of cairns, knee-high, walking straight toward a summit that is already the summit, then looping back past itself at a slightly different height where north points two ways at once. The low wind-wall. The drilled holes and pecked lines. They tried, as you thought for a moment, to make a place habitable, and settled for making it bearable. They stacked stone against wind. They carved a basin to hold the water that would not drain. They left tools.

They are not here now. There are no bones, no footprints but yours filling instantly with water, no smoke. What they built has been taken over, not rudely, but completely, by the skin. The cairns are wet at their bases and threaded at their joints. The shelter is a hollow the threads use to stay damp longest. The bowl is a small, perfect sea. Whatever the builders meant the basin for — washing, drinking, ritual — the plateau has repurposed it as habitat. Their flakes of black glass, sharp still, are now perches for ice dendrites. Their fire-pit, blackened, is a dark seep-line that never quite dries.

That is the only kind of death the place acknowledges, perhaps. Burning off is death. Evaporation is death. A rosette closing in the glare and not reopening is death. Then mist rolls in again, the stone weeps again, and life — if you want to call it that — condenses out of air that seemed empty. It has existed long enough to learn how to be impossible and keep doing it in balance: water climbing toward a rim with no drain, echoes arriving out of order so you hear the drip four times before your boot strikes, distance folding at the far end of the slab field so you see the far crest through the shoulder of a pillar. The inhabitants do not mind. They do not orient by north, or by up. They orient by wet.

By the end of your walk, the brightness has not lessened and the cold has not gone, and you understand that you are not looking at a landscape with inhabitants in it. You are standing on the inhabitants. The plateau is crowded not with stone, but with the stone-and-water skin that lives on stone, hundreds of towers thick, breathing in mist and out in white light, humming when the wind finds the right angle.

You fog the air with your labored breath. For a second, that fog condenses on a cold slab in front of you, beads, and is taken. A thread darkens by an almost invisible amount. A salt flower you had not noticed opens its white petals to a damp that did not exist a minute ago because you made it.

You are the only animal here. Briefly. And for that brief time, you are not a witness to the place. You are its weather.
