If you put your hand flat on the warmest stone in the entrance chamber and leave it there, the first thing you will feel is not the stone. It is a small movement underneath it, a shifting, a hesitation, a committee deciding all at once to curl and wait.

Under almost every stone there are them — isopods, pale and segmented, already half-curled into their own shells; many-legged worms working the black humus grain by grain; beetles with shells so clear you can see the dark gut moving inside them like a slow tide. They do not run. In this place nothing that lives has learned to hurry away from a disturbance, because there is nowhere else to go and no sound to warn them anyway. They fold, they press tighter into the damp, they go still and let your heat pass over them. Then, when your hand lifts, they uncurl inch by inch and return to the same work: eating the mineral crust and the flattened leaf and the fine grit, eating each other's leavings, turning dead matter into the soft dark layer the next layer will eat.

They are the bottom of everything, and they are everywhere the bottom exists. Where the floor is dry enough to hold a footprint, they still live in the pockets of damp the eye cannot find — in the cups of old stalagmites, in the folded hearts of moss, in the thin film on the underside of a root. They breathe through their skins. The warm air that never evaporates quite keeps them wet, and wetness is the whole of their world. They are blind or near-blind, and they navigate by touch and by the faint chemical trails of those who went before them. You could watch a single isopod for an hour and see its antennae tap, tap, tap at the stone ahead of it, tasting a route that has been tasted a thousand times.

Above them, in every sense, live the moss-dwellers. The moss is not ground cover here. In places it is waist-high, a forest cushioned in velvet, and to live in it is to live in a landscape where a beetle is as large as a deer. Clear-shelled snails trace the undersides of leaves and the green stems of ferns, leaving a faint, shining line they will follow back. The many-legged worms tunnel the moss from within, loosening it, making it breathe. The beetles climb it to eat the mushrooms that fruit from it. The mushrooms are not decoration. White bells, orange cups, soft puffballs — they open and close in slow pulses, feeding not on light but on fallen matter and animal waste, and every so often they release their spores in an invisible sigh. The gnats that drift through the dim air catch them on their bodies and carry them from leaf to leaf, blundering, never quite flying, landing hard and then lifting again on a current of expanding warm air from the deep chamber.

The ferns near the one grey-green crack of light are the greenest things in the place, broad and patient, and they are tenements. A translucent frog sits on a frond so still you might mistake it for a bead of condensed water until you see the heart beating visibly under its skin, quick and red in the green darkness. There are dozens of them, and hundreds more if you count the eggs laid in neat clusters on the undersides of leaves above the pools. A frog here inflates its throat and makes no sound at all. Courtship, warning, calling — all of it has been pressed down into touch, into the slow swell of a body against a leaf, into chemistry in the damp air. They watch heat, not movement. When you enter, they do not leap. They sink. A whole frond sighs downward a fraction, frog and leaf together, as your warmth passes, and stays sunk long after you have gone.

The pools are their own country. They do not flow. They sit, warm and mirror-still, in basins scooped in the floor, and the algae in them gives off a faint glow like cold stars under water, brightening for a moment when something stirs. In them live the blind amphibians, their bodies pale and soft, their feathery external gills fanning in the water without pause. They never need to surface. They turn in the small, complete volume of a pool as if it were an ocean, gills opening and closing, finding food in the suspended rot and in the tiny lives the algae feeds. A single pool can hold a colony of them, family upon family, all of them feeling the vibration of each other's passing through the water itself.

And in the pool chamber, half-buried in the silt at the edge where the water is warmest, waits the eel.

It is long, blind, eel-like, and it does almost nothing. Hours of nothing. Its tongue flicks out to taste the air and the water — taste heat, taste the frogs that come too close to drink, taste the amphibians whose gills stir the glow. When it moves, it moves all at once and then is still again, swallowing what it has taken in the dark. It is the largest hunter down here, and it is also, in its way, a boundary. The frogs and the gilled ones know the scent and the faint disturbance in the water and they keep a careful, silent distance, crowding the far leaves, the far stones, the cooler edges of the same pool. Predation here does not chase. It waits, and the world arranges itself around the waiting.

On the walls live the lizards. Glass lizards, eyeless and lidless, press themselves flat to the warm rock in the shaft of dim light and in the warmer hall beyond it, breathing in slow, visible pulses that travel along their flanks. They have no eyelids to close, no eyes to close them for. They navigate, hunt, and find each other by heat on their skin — the heat of a beetle under a stone, the heat of a millipede crossing the floor, the heat of a frog cooling on a fern. They eat millipedes and beetles and whatever small thing crosses their patch of wall. Their feet are padded. They cross the floor without a sound, flattening further when you approach, becoming part of the stone's warmth until you have passed.

Deeper in, where the air grows hotter and thinner, the rules change. There a single species of pale millipede rules by itself, marking the boundary of the livable world. They are slow, and solitary in presence if not in number, and at night they move in migrating columns across the floor of the central hall — a living river a few animals wide, eating the moss line by line. If you knew how to see it, you would come back in the morning and find a chamber reshaped: a cushion thinned, a trail cleanly shorn, a new smoothness where the column passed. Their waste feeds the mushrooms. The mushrooms feed the beetles and the gnats. The beetles feed the lizards. The millipedes feed, in turn, on the moss the whole system has grown, and they are fed upon, and the cycle closes tight because nothing leaves.

In the mud, where the floor turns soft and dark, lives the animal you will rarely see directly. Mole-like, it swims through the mud as if it were water, its whole body undulating, leaving tunnels that bulge and collapse behind it. It aerates the mud, opens channels for the worms, brings the deep rot up to the surface where the mushrooms can take it. You will know it by a slow ridge moving under the surface, by a softness where there was firmness, by the way the damp rearranges itself overnight. It is one animal, perhaps, or a few, and its work is the work of a river in a place that has no river.

And threaded through all of it are the plants, who are not background but participants with their own slow ambitions. Ferns grow toward the light-crack and also — just as plainly — toward heat. Roots hang from the ceiling like ropes, the living ones questing downward for a pool, the dead ones mineralized into stone. The strange succulents, pale and waxy, lean into the warmest wall and flatten themselves against it, storing damp in their leaves and flowering downward, their blossoms hanging under the plant to be found by crawling insects moving leaf to leaf in the dark. Moss grows toward both. Everything that can grow does so in reach of everything else, fern touching ceiling, moss touching stone touching root touching a lizard, because in a place this small, abundance means nearness. Nothing stands alone, and so nothing mistakes itself for separate.

That is how they speak to each other, and how they will speak to you if you stay: not by sound — the porous stone and the soft bodies drink sound away — but by contact and heat and the faint tick of millipede legs spaced far apart, by a fern rubbing a rock when warm air pushes through the tunnel, by a single drip falling after an hour of silence. They notice you long before you notice them, not by sight but by the warmth you bring down the fissure on your back, the damp beading unswept on your skin, the slow collective ripple as frogs sink, lizards flatten, millipedes pause mid-step, isopods curl, and the eel in the pool stops flicking its tongue until your heat has passed and the place can seal itself quiet again.

They do not need you to be quiet. They are already quieter than you can be. In the four chambers, by the one light, in the few warm pools, they go on eating the damp and the dead, growing toward warmth as much as toward light, migrating in columns, drifting, fanning their gills, beating their visible hearts, opening and closing their mushrooms in slow pulses — a complete world, measured in centimeters and hours, living shoulder to shoulder in the heat before dark, and continuing long after the fissure has closed behind you.
