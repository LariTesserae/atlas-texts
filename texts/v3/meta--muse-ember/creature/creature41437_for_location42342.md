At first there does not seem to be a population to count. There is a density, a near-touching, so many presences per cubic foot that the eye tries to separate them and fails. The leaf is green, then it is also sheltering. The beetle is dark, then it is also carrying. The water is clear, then it is also held, transpired, condensed, and weeping again. To live here is not to occupy a niche. It is to be a layer in the same wet mass, and to be touched constantly by the layers above and below you.

Start with the light-makers, because everything else works in their remainder.

On the low vault are patches of phosphorescent lichen, faint as deep-water moonlight. They are not bright. They are enough. They have grown into the seep and the sweat of the ceiling, and they pulse, very slowly, not in flashes but in a tide over minutes. When they brighten, the tendrils above them brighten too. When they dim, the whole room dims with it, and the roots below send their pale electrical travel from one wall to the other, carrying the news downward in a wave that takes minutes to cross twenty steps. That wave is conversation, and irrigation, and rationing. It tells a shaded rosette on the floor that a photon has been caught up top and can be re-split and shared. It tells a beetle under a leaf that something clicked on the far wall. Sound made in the air becomes vibration in the felt, and vibration in the felt becomes a decision in a root to grow, over a season, toward the warmth and noise that made it.

The lichen are the eldest. They do not move. They do not need to.

Below them hang the unfinished. From the vault come strands that pale children of the felt are still lowering: root tendrils and hollow stems, searching, tasting damp air. Some have already reached the floor and joined it, thickening where they touch, beginning to be wall. Some are still a hand's breadth short, and in that gap they hesitate. A tendril this length may have been growing for years. It advances a hand's breadth before the next winter of the world above. It may shrink back when the water table falls, then grow again, unsure. When it finally feels the felt below — the knock of a worm, the drum of a drip on a leaf carrying down its message — it hardens. In three years there will be no way to tell where rock ended and plant began. That is how the room is still being born: not by erosion from outside, but by these slow descents joining, and by root acids and frost picking at the one raw corner where a new seep has only just opened, grey and sharp and still weeping sediment mist. There, white root hairs are already tapping the stone. They are the pioneers of this corner, transparent infants drinking mineral, tasting their way into being rock that can be lived in.

The walls are the climbers. Vines overlap leaf on leaf like shingles, but they are not decoration. Each leaf is at once roof, mouth, and house. Their undersides are cities.

Under the rosettes — those pale succulent rosettes with translucent windows in their leaves — crowd the frost-feather beetles. They are blind, beetle-like, their shells ridged like frost feathers, mandibles clicking in a near-constant, polite chatter that you feel in your feet as much as hear. A beetle under a rosette may live its entire life in the shade of a single leaf. It has never seen the lichen light directly. Its world is the pressure of the leaf above it, the humidity, the vibration of the felt. Many carry a small leaf growing from a shell, or a tuft of moss from a flank. That leaf is not a burden. It shades the beetle while it feeds on fungal film and licks sugars from root hairs. The beetle's droppings feed the leaf. When the beetle dies under its rosette, it does not leave. It is composted in days, dissolved back into the mat by fungi and worms, and the leaf on its shell roots into the felt where it fell. To count the beetle as separate from the plant that grew on it would be a human habit, not a local one.

The rosettes themselves are the mouths and the elders of the floor. They open and close not only to light but to the rising and falling of the water table, which comes and goes with seasons far above they will never know. Their windows let in a thin, needled light and also let them look down — if looking is the word — into the felt where their roots are packed knuckle-tight with other roots. They photosynthesize in this half-dark with an efficiency that should not be possible, conducting along their stems at a temperature where everything else is inert. They fabricate antifreeze on demand, keep ice out of their cells in patterns like venation made of glass, and repair a torn edge in an afternoon. If a slug tears itself on a stem, they seal it with living gel. If a slug climbs onto a stem and stays put, the rosette does not expel it. For a while the slug becomes architecture, a slow-moving part of the plant's support, leaving a silvery track that fades. The plant remembers the salt of it.

The floor is the main body. For inches, maybe a foot, it is felt: interwoven roots bound tight as rope, rhizomes, cushions of moss and liverwort, clubmosses, all of it living substrate. Its breathing is done by worms. They rise and fall in the felt in a slow peristalsis, lifting the surface an inch and letting it sink, making the whole living room subtly rise and fall like the flank of a great animal. Their passage loosens the felt, lets water sink, lets air — what little air there is — move. They are not seen often, only the arch of their backs under a film of water, and the faint crackle of ice-laced stems expanding as they pass and take up water.

Across the leaves move the pale slugs, translucent as ice. They are the gardeners and the messengers. They graze fungal film, they carry moss on their flanks that can sport and spread from their backs to new leaves, they climb tendrils to become part of a wall, they leave tracks that other slugs follow days later, reading a path in silver. When they are wounded, the plant heals them; when they feed, they secrete what the plant needs to keep its windows clear. A slug crossing the twenty steps of the chamber is not making a short walk. It may take a season. Its whole world is leaf after leaf after leaf, each one a continent of its own in miniature, each one drumming under drips.

In the shallow pools — no deeper than a fingernail over the root felt — sit the weep-frogs. Frog-shaped, eyes beaded black, sitting very still, throats pulsing. They do not leave their pool. Their pulse is breathing and song and pump at once. Up through their throats, water is moved to the root hairs waiting at the pool's edge. Down through the felt, their croak travels, not much as sound in air but as pressure through the mat to the beetles on the far wall. On the pool surface, bubbles of plant exhalation form and pop in sequence, a slow alphabet no human ear quite catches. The frogs sit in it, listening. They are the lungs of places that have no lungs.

The air belongs to the mist-gnats and midges, a thin but persistent cloud thick enough that you breathe them at the edges of your mouth. They are not a swarm with a center. They are damp plankton, living on the exhalations of leaves, on the faint warmth of passing animals, on the sweet rot that is instantly composted because nothing here is wasted. They rise and fall with the ankle-high mist that never lifts. They are prey, thought, movement for the things that dart.

Those darting things are the felt-shrews. Small, quick, shrew-like, they vanish and reappear through the root mat, chasing midges, eating seeds of light, disappearing under a rosette and out three leaves away. They are the fastest thing here, and fast is relative — a quick thought across the felt. But even they are slowed by the density. To move at all they must push through curtains of leaves and feel living things part around them. The parting is not reluctant. A vine will lean, minutely, away from a warm body. A leaf will turn, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmth of a hand that stays still long enough. That is how they forage: growth follows sound and moisture and faint warmth, creeping toward it over seasons. The shrews leave warm, noisy trails in their wake, and in a year a fern in the corner will have unrolled a thumb's length toward where they have been passing.

And underneath and through all of it, invisible except as faint threads between roots, are the fungi. They are the instant recyclers. A dead leaf falls into the felt and is gone in days — fungi first, then worms, then dissolved back into sugars and salts the roots drink. There is no litter here, no humus in the human sense, because death is not left to accumulate. The fungi live in the roots, in the beetles' guts, in the slugs' mucus, in the frogs' skin. They are the reason the plant can seal a wound, fabricate antifreeze, share light downward. They are the reason nothing is separate.

They do not reproduce in solitude. A spore rides a slug. A moss tuft rides a beetle. A hollow stem is a house with a tenant curled inside, using the plant's stored water and, in return, keeping the stem clear of clogging rot. A fern spore waits in the felt for a shrew to carry it, or for a tendril to brush the flag.

If you stay still long enough, they will begin to treat you as they treat each other: as warmth, as vibration, as slow architecture to be grown around. A slug will climb. A leaf will turn. Your footprints in the felt will fill in, not quickly — nothing here is quick except the drips, the clicks, the croaks — but inexorably, because the felt is alive and rising and falling and root hairs are already tasting the disturbed silt.

That is who lives here. Not a list. A set of agreements, made over evolutionary time at the molecular level, patiently, between light and water and leaf and animal and fungus. The chamber is not a cave that has plants in it. It is a plant that has hollowed itself a room to live in, and the animals are how the plant moves, hears, cleans its windows, and keeps its walls from sealing too soon. It is small enough to hold in your arms. It is dense enough to contain a continent. And it is still being born, tendril by tendril, click by click, drip by drip, around every warm thing that passes through.
