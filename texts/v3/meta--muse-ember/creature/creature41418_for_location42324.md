Nothing here lives in a particular place, for long.

That is the first thing to unlearn. If you look for a resident — a body with a home, a nest, a territory — you find white on white, and the white moves. Life, in this pocket, is low and diffuse and mostly in transit. It does not build a burrow. It happens along a film, a seam, a brief tick of cooling. At your scale the floor is empty for paces at a time. At theirs, it is a highway.

It starts as specks.

Thousands of them, mites and springtails and things smaller than the resolution of your eye, crawling the bare mineral in no hurry at all. They are the same pale as the stone, translucent at the edges, and they catch the ceiling light only when they turn. Their trails are faint and shiny behind them, a script that fades in seconds. Between bodies there is still mostly emptiness — this is a dense crowd by mite standards and a wilderness by yours. They move in lanes that are not worn, but preferred: along the shallow contours of the dome, around crystal teeth where the rock is still budding, toward the spongy places near the walls where damp holds longest.

With them, re-writing themselves as they go, are the nematode ribbons. No thicker than a hair, white-gray, they writhe and re-writhe across a hand's breadth and then stop, as if considering the shape they intend to make next. A ribbon can be a straight thread, a knot, a loop that holds its own tail for a moment. They leave no wake except a brightening of the condensation under them, and other specks will follow that brightening, tasting it with legs and mouths you cannot see. Everything here feeds in that opportunistic way: on the film itself, on mineral exudates sweating from the spongy wall, on each other's discarded skins. Predator and prey use the same lanes and apparently do not mind.

The rain — the brief, indecisive drizzle when beads grow too large and choose down — changes everything for a few minutes. The condensation cycle waxes and wanes every little while, and with each wax the crawlers surge. What was scattered becomes a migration. Specks climb the central dome toward moisture, ribbons uncoil uphill, and for a short, feverish interval the bare floor is almost completely covered in slow movement, and then, as the film thins and evaporates halfway, empty again except for the faint mineral freckles left in mid-air where drops vanished.

Not all residents crawl. Some hang.

From the glassy ceiling descend a few thread-like things, a hand's breadth long, root or fungal filament or something between, stopping in mid-air. They are nearly invisible until they block the light, when they become a thin shadow-line against the glare. They do not need to root. They seem to drink: condensation, cold light, the mineral breath of the ceiling. In the cracks where the wall stays spongy there are velvety patches of moss, not green but a sickly pale lime, and they have the irritating habit of seeming stronger in your peripheral vision than when you look straight at them. When something passes over them — something that darkens the light — the lime flickers out, bleached whiter for a breath, then returns.

Beside the moss, a cluster of tiny transparent fingers, fungus perhaps, no taller than a thumbnail, inflates and deflates with a rhythm that is not quite the room's and not quite their own. They are residents in the strictest sense: they stay. But staying here does not mean rooted. It means they have agreed, temporarily, to be in that crack.

The larger life — larger meaning visible without squinting, still small enough to hold in both palms — keeps to the damp.

Shrimp-shaped things, translucent and blind, swim through the film on the floor. Their gills pulse visibly, opening and closing in the damp, and occasionally one launches itself off the surface in a short darting burst through the air itself, curving, falling short, and re-entering the film with a tick that sends rings through the puddle-mirrors. They are not swimming in water. There is no water. They are swimming in the decision of water to exist, and for the half-second of a jump they are swimming through nothing at all and do not seem to mind.

Along the junction where wall meets ceiling, where the mineral skins over and moisture runs longest, glide the millipede-things. White, segmented, dozens of legs moving in perfect soundless sequence, they follow the seam round and round the pocket, testing it. They are blind in the complete sense — no eye-ghosts, no milky buds — and navigate by gradient, by the coolness of the film, by the ticking of crystals setting near them. One, for a long watch, will make the same circuit without deviation, pausing at the corner where the plump stalactite pulses downward, tasting the air, then on again. They molt in pulses, the whole population in a loose sync with the room. A white skin splits along the back, the soft animal steps out paler and smaller and more translucent than before, and the husk, still warm-seeming, is immediately colonized: specks swarm it, ribbons thread through the old legs, and within another condensation cycle it is a patterned stain and then nothing.

Above you — and “above” is a habit you keep losing — are the inverted ones.

Slugs without shells plaster themselves to the glassy ceiling, bodies spread thin, moving at a pace that makes geological sense. They leave bright mucus routes behind them that catch the light and hold it, so the ceiling slowly acquires a faint, luminous cartography of everywhere a slug has been. As they move, you feel it before you hear it: a pressure, a high cold tone ringing faintly in your ears, as if the light itself were being plucked. They graze on what grows on the ceiling: the threads, the condensation, the thin mineral skin that only exists in the glare. They are the gardeners, if anyone is, quietly mapping the one surface that never softens.

In the corner where a crystal tooth and another have not yet grown together, the spider waits.

It is too many-jointed, with legs that bend in an extra place and a body that is mostly leg or mostly mouth depending on the light. Its eyes are many and milky and useless for seeing shape — which is probably the adaptation. In a place where shadows fall upward and sometimes arrive seconds before the thing that owns them, an eye for shape is a liability. The spider hangs in a web that is not a web: a few strands of condensation stretched between crystal teeth, trembling with each tick of the room. It does not rush. It waits for the shrimp-things to swim too high, for a millipede to stray from the seam, for a rising drop to pass through its strands and be retained. It eats by absence as much as by touch, letting the room bring things to it.

For the room contains a hunter that is mostly absence.

You notice it first as a patch of darkness, roughly animal-shaped, paler than dark and darker than white, moving on the floor a few seconds before the creature that casts it. The blind things flee it even though they cannot see it — they feel the cold deepen as it approaches, the film under them ceasing to brighten. When it passes over the moss, the lime goes out. When it passes over you, your breath fogs again, briefly, as if you had stepped back out through the crack. It is hunting the millipedes and the shrimp-things, and it hunts them sideways in time: the shadow arrives first, the body follows, and by the time the body is here the shadow has already pooled upward onto the ceiling like a stain, soft-edged and patient.

And it, in turn, is hunted — or accompanied — by something that only exists in the puddle-reflections. In the shallow mirrors that appear with condensation, you can sometimes see a shape that has no body on the floor at all, lifting its head when you look down, or looking up when you look down. The reflections of the residents are older by a breath. The reflection-hunter watches the real hunter from the other side of the film, and the real hunter, blind, seems to know it is watched: it will pause a lane early, turn, though nothing is behind it on the floor.

That, and the builder, are the only ones who seem to intend anything.

Near the far wall, three raw stones have been arranged in a low cairn, half-sunk into the spongy floor as if the floor claimed them mid-construction. A spiral has been scored into the soft wall with a claw or a dragged crystal, each groove still wet and beaded. And standing upright is a single broken arc, like a doorway or a rib or a handle, made of hardened mineral, ending in mid-air where the rest of it was never formed.

No one sees it done. You turn your back, the condensation changes stage, and there is one more stone, or the spiral is a quarter-turn longer. Whoever made them had only bodies and patience, and did not finish. Perhaps it is the spider, grown ambitious; perhaps a millipede that has grown extra legs for the purpose; perhaps something that only comes into being while being watched and therefore never quite arrives. The small things shelter under the broken arc — specks, a young shrimp-thing folded in the damp, a ribbon noosed around its base — as if it were already a roof, a ribcage, a promise of a cave that the pocket may make one day or may seal away forever.

The builder’s work is the only sign of a future tense here. Everything else is present and unfinished and content to be so. The animals may grow eyes in some later pulse, or lose the ghosts of eyes they never had. The moss may root, or evaporate. The slugs’ luminous routes may become permanent veins in a stone that hardens; or the ceiling may soften again and the routes dissolve. The residents set like crystals and then, impossibly, unset. They are born by budding from the spongy wall in some cases, by splitting from a husk in others, by riding a rising drop up to the ceiling and being absorbed with a tick, to begin again elsewhere in the pocket.

They are quiet not because they are few — at their scale they are a teeming, feverish crowd — but because the room swallows sound. The spongy walls take the scrabble the way they take water. What you hear is mostly the room itself, and them filtered through it: a granular ticking as a crystal pops, a wet sigh as a pore opens, a leg far away, tick-tick, as if through cloth.

For now they go on in the cold glare that comes from everywhere and nowhere, drinking condensation, following lanes, leaving bright trails that fade, gathering under an arc that was never finished, fleeing a shadow that arrives before its owner, and being watched, in the mirrors, by something that has no body to cast one.

And somewhere in the wall, between one pulse and the next, a new tooth of crystal ticks, pops, and — with all its residents still crawling, swimming, hanging, waiting — begins, very slowly, very quietly, to become a place where they will live, and to un-become it again.
