The first life you notice is not separate from the place at all. It is the place, breathing.

The plant lives here the way a heart lives in a chest — not as a guest, but as the organ that makes the rooms possible. Its body is everything the others stand on, hang from, drink from, and are slowly returned to. Sap rises in the thick stalks in a slow piston-thump you can feel through the sponge under your boots, is lifted into the vast leaves, and is let out again in warm sighs along the valve-lines under the veins. The tissue that carries that charge is alive with a low tingle in the air; sometimes a spore pod deep in a stem splits with a soft snap and a crackle of discharge, and a cloud of dust-thin spores drifts down through the dripping chambers.

Those spores are its children, and they do not wander. They settle on the black water, on the polished leaf-wax, on root arches worn smooth, and become, instantly, more plant — a seedling skin on a puddle, a fringe on a vein, a new fiber in the carpet. It produces endlessly and grows not at all. A leaf that was wide as your body last month is wide as your body now, its margin scalloped smooth, its lip curled into the same room. Fruit hangs heavy in the storerooms where folded leaves make natural hollows, sweet and ripe and never quite picked clean, because the plant has learned to offer exactly what its residents will take and no more.

Life under the leaves, where the light comes through translucent and stays at its moderate, fixed afternoon forever, belongs first to those who do not need to move much.

The tree frogs are glued in clusters to the gray-green undersides, belly to wax, eyes half-closed against the constant drip. They are not asleep. They sing. Their croaks are metallic clicks and resonant booms that swell and recede in waves from room to room, company answering company across a long empty hall of damp air. To them a single leaf is a cliff face and a roof and a pond's edge all at once. They stay where the condensation beads and runs, they lay their eggs in the film of water that gathers where a vein meets a stalk, and their young slip down the polished surface to the cup below. Because the light never changes, their singing never changes either — no dusk chorus, no dawn — only the steady industrial rhythm of the place, frog-throat timed to piston and drip.

Along the veins themselves move the beetles. Thousands of them, small and armored, in old, unhurried files from stalk to leaf tip and back again. Their shells strike the wax with a faint clatter that your ear wants to call metal. They are grazers, living on the fine film the drip leaves behind, on fallen spore dust, on the sweet exudate of the valves. They do not build and they do not stray; the vein is their road, wide to them as a highway, and they walk it in both directions forever, a mechanized procession of rivets. 

Where they walk, the termites walk too, faster and more numerous, carrying leaf fragments many times their size along the same raised pipes. Their bodies clack against each other in the airless grooves beside the vein. They are the plant's own recycling crew: what the beetles scrape, the termites haul; what falls, they break down and carry down again into the sponge, where the plant will eat it. It is an endless, clattering conveyor, and the line never breaks because there is nowhere else for it to go.

The snails go the other way — slow, solitary, inching across the polished upper surfaces where water has run for so long the wax shines like stone. Each leaves a silver trail that the next drip erases and the snail remakes, hour by hour. They eat the algae film that greens the wet places, tasting the leaf the way you might taste a wall. You meet them one at a time, in a dripping chamber, then walk far through echoing emptiness before you meet another, exactly as still, exactly as intent. Their shells are their rooms; the leaf is their world; the distance between two snails is, to them, vast and sufficient.

In the cupped leaves, where water stands perfectly level in a pond that overflows by one measured drip every few seconds, there is a whole other scale of life. Dragonflies stitch above it, bright wings catching the fixed light, hovering over water they take for a lake. They lay eggs on the leaf lip; their nymphs live in the cup's skin of water, hunting among duckweed and seedling leaves that cover the surface even here. The cup overflows into the cup below, and the dragonflies follow the overflow down, generation after generation finding the same pond at the same depth, fed by the same drip. A frog will park itself at the edge, half in and half out, and sing into the water so the surface trembles. Below, in the stem tunnels that hum faintly with moving water, the larvae ride the slow current from one cup to the next, never reaching a river, never needing one.

Between the rooms, in the air itself, are the fast lives.

The birds are small and bright and too quick for the stillness — they stitch the air between leaves with sharp calls, flashing from one dripping chamber to the next along the long, empty halls where nothing else grows because the plant already occupies it. They nest not in branches but in the natural curve of a folded leaf, in a hollow stem's mouth, in the cradle where a stalk joins a leaf. They eat insects and fruit and spore-dust, but their appetite is part of the equilibrium: they take enough to live and leave enough to seed. Because there is no evening, they do not roost to sleep in any true night; they rest in the same moderate light, alert, then dart again, their calls as regular as the piston — *thunk... thunk...* below, *tick... tick...* above.

The small mammals pad softly between the rooms, feet muffled by the springy carpet that closes over their prints. They are nocturnal animals living in a place with no night, so they have become animals of all hours — moving, pausing, curling under a leaf to sleep in the warm air for a while, then moving on. They follow the root channels where the drip has cut a miniature river into the plant's own body, drink from the puddles without breaking the green skin too much, eat fallen fruit, and leave their droppings in the sponge to be eaten by the plant. You rarely see more than one at a time. The spaces between presences are enormous, even for them; a company of frogs here, a lone mammal there, a termite river flowing dark and silent through a root arch, and then a long stretch of nothing but drip and sigh.

Down in the carpet — which is not carpet but pulped root and fallen leaf-stem, flattened, wet, fibrous, recovering underfoot — the millipedes flow. They move like dark rivers through the carved channels, around smooth bends, over root arches worn to a shine by a million passes of water and foot and fur. They eat the dead parts of the plant as the plant eats them in turn, churning the sponge in a continuous, almost imperceptible circulation that the roots themselves seem to direct. Worms and smaller burrowers work the same fibrous mat, turning it over and over, so that what was leaf becomes floor becomes food becomes leaf again. Nothing rests on another material; nothing is wasted outside the one body. A millipede that dies in the channel becomes, within days, part of the channel.

And on the water — on every puddle, every cup, every slow black mirror — lives the skin. Duckweed in tiny green plates, algae in a film, the plant's own seedlings in their first leaves, packed edge to edge in a total cover. They photosynthesize under the unmoving light, divide, are grazed by snails at the edges and by beetles where the water laps the stalk, are rained on by the constant mineral-free drip from the ceiling leaves, and divide again. Your reflection is broken by them. The mirror is green too. For all their abundance they never thicken; they live at exactly the density that covers the surface and no more, a living lid that keeps the water warm, used, and perfect.

All of them breathe the same warm, already-used air, beading with condensation on hairs and shells and wings. All of them have fitted themselves to a world without weather, without season, without morning. They do not age in any way you can track, because as fast as one drops into the sponge, another is born in a cup, another hatches from a spore settling on water, another files in along a vein. The chorus repeats, the piston thumps, the valve sighs, the drip falls at the same interval, and the lives it carries go on inside it — not growing, not declining, not new, not collapsed.

They are not visitors to a factory. They are its workforce, its tenants, its returning parts, grown quiet and finished, living in rooms that are lungs and veins and cups, in a place that will be identical when you leave and return.
