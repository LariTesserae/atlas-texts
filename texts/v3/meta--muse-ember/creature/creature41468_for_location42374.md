If you look for a resident here the way you would anywhere else, you will not find one in any form you know how to look for.

You look for something to move against the water, to root against the stone, to make a sound that is not a drip, to come and go. You look for a dark shape in the brightness, a green edge between slabs, a wing crossing the bleached light, an insect at your ear. There is none. The light takes everything and gives it back flat and white. The pans answer it as mirrors. The wet stone answers it as shine. The mist hangs and does not go anywhere. The only motion that is not yours is the patient, multiplex motion of water itself — a tick in a joint, a sheet over a lip, a drop that forms and hangs and falls from an overhang inside a vault, in full brightness, while you stand dry outside.

You could mistake that for living. The place breathes damp, it weeps from joints made by hand, it sweats cloud inside its covered channels and rains there, slow, onto the water below. But it is not breathing. It is only water finding the seams it was made to find, and the seams are everywhere, because the whole height is built solid of itself — basin to wall to channel to stair with no natural ground left between, no soil, no place unmade where anything could hold.

From a little way off there are things that look, startlingly, like life again. In pools that have been left standing, where the water has evaporated a little in the cool brightness and left its lime behind, constellations of white crust branch out rosette-like across the tile. They look like small flowers. They look like frost. They look like snow that has forgotten to melt. If you lean closer they do not open, they do not close, they do not have a center that lives. They are the water's record of itself, extended point by point as the pan slowly goes down. The dead thing is the bright one.

The living thing, if you can call it that, is the dim one. You have to crouch to it, and to get out of the light to see it at all.

Under the lip of a basin, where the measured edge turns back into shadow, there is a patch that is not shade. It does not move when the mist moves. It is pale grey-white, matte against the wet shine around it, a few finger-widths wide, and it follows the joint rather than the face of the stone. In the deepest, dampest seam between two basalt blocks, the lime mortar has darkened, and along that dark line the film has whitened it again, thinly, as if dust had settled and then stayed wet. Inside a vault where the inner rain falls, it spreads in the same way — not in the channel where the water runs, but on the underside and the sheltered return, where condensation collects and does not scour. A copper staple hammered to hold two stones together blackens where it crosses one, a small dark halo on the pin, the only place the pale film grows dark instead of light.

It is fungal, in the loosest sense. It is not a plant. It does not green the place, it does not make it look inhabited from any distance, and up close it still looks like damp shadow. It has no leaf, no stem, no face, no step, no call. It does not move across the stone the way an animal moves, or reach up the way a vine does. It extends. By a fraction, in a season. Millimetres in a season, the description of its life is almost a refusal of life: slower than spreading, slower than the widening of a damp stain by the width of a hair in a day, slower than the creep of shadow across a basin.

It lives only where the place is not itself. The exposed faces are too bright, too clean, too constantly washed by sheeting water to feed anything. The channels must never stop moving and must never pool dirty, so a hand clearing them would clear them clean. Nothing organic is allowed to enter the system, and in the main flow nothing does. But the joints are not the flow. Under the slabs, in the fill that drinks and holds and weeps again, in the hairline between a slab's edge and another slab's wall, there is a sheltered damp that the water finds but does not rush through. There, in the absence of light, on mineral alone — wet lime, cold clay, granite, slate, a little blackened copper — the film can stay. It is fed by the wetness and starved by everything else, and that is enough for the timescale it keeps.

Over years it would map the plumbing that it lives in. One finger-width under one lip, another along one vaulted channel where the rain falls inside, a pale line whitening a lime course here, a dark bloom on a copper joint there. No single colony is an individual you could point to and follow. It is a thin geography, spread across the darkest seams of a bright height, each patch too slow to notice, too quiet to smell — the smell here is still only cold stone and wet clay and lime, clean to the point of sterility.

That is the inversion of the place. What looks like a flower is mineral. What looks like a shadow is alive, after a fashion, and it lives precisely because it does not look alive. It asks nothing of the water except that the water not quite reach it. It does not break the rules; it slips under them by being beneath the lip, inside the seam, on the underside, where the inner rain falls on it drop by drop and does not wash it away.

The only animal that has ever stood here is you, and you are not a resident. Your soles are wet but not dirty. No footprint crosses a slab except the faint drag of a tool, and yours will be taken by the next sheet of water as soon as you lift your foot. You cannot root here. You cannot eat here. You have no season here in which to grow a millimetre. You are the thing the place has been designed, not by cruelty but by function, to wash away.

So you walk on, and the terraces step down and step up again, basin and wall and channel and stair, under the same bright, wet, quiet air. Behind you, under the lips and in the seams, the films keep their season-long work of extending, whitening and blackening by fractions no eye can catch in passing.
