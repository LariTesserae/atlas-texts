First, what lives here does not stand where you can see it. It lives in the floor, and underneath the floor, and only rarely and briefly above it.

The floor itself is the first of them. The mat is not dead ground that plants have taken hold of. It is plant, all the way down to stone. Inches to a foot thick it lies in one unbroken felt — roots and rhizomes pressed flat, leaf-bases compressed, half-dead stems woven and re-woven — creeping even in the cold. In frost-heave it tightens and loosens. Under your palm at dusk, when the air has gone to water and the grey smear of sun is gone, you can feel it moving without wind: a faint, slow tremor traveling through the rhizomes, sap and trapped air moving like a tide in a shallow sea. It is olive and grey-green and straw-pale where it dies, beaded always with condensation, each waxy leaf holding its drop until the drop is heavy enough to fall. Sword-leaves stick up ankle-high. Cushions of rosette keep their heads down. Creeping shoots run and run and never lift. This is the greatest living mass on the plateau, and it is also a roof.

Under that roof is a city by any measure except open air.

Peel back a square — it resists like hide — and the light is lost at once. Just below the surface, where plant respiration holds the temperature a few degrees above the air, it is warm, damp, close, and loud with small work. Pale grubs the length of a thumb, blind, soft, segmented, chewing endlessly through old plant mass and making it new again. Blind beetles with no eyes at all, only antennae longer than their bodies, clicking and rasping as they test the dark with them, shredding leaf-base and stem. Worms that pulse and burrow, pushing through and sealing the tunnels behind them. Things with too many legs that fold themselves into tight, patient shapes when the mat is torn, waiting for dark to unfold again.

They are the farmers. Everything else on the plateau depends on them in the end, because in this cold nothing rots quickly. Decay is arrested. The only way the old mat becomes new is to be chewed. The grub drops are tunnel walls. The beetle's rasping opens the felt to water. In the cold, relatively warm pocket between the living surface and the black fresh stone, they work through the whole vast extent without pause, a continent of mouths under a continent of root.

Above the mat, life moves in waves because it cannot afford to move continuously. Heat is expensive here, and movement is heat.

The drumming you hear before you see anything is the herds. They come from beyond the fog in lines that do not seem to end — not a line of animals but a line of worn path, a foot deep in the root felt, kept clear by generations of feet. Low and shaggy, heavy-shouldered, off-white and smoke-grey to the point of soot on their flanks, their horns rake forward as they feed, tearing up the ankle-high shoots and turning the mat over with their heads. You feel them through the plant ground long before your ears sort the sound from the wind: a low thunder transmitted in the root felt itself. Their breath freezes in their scarves as it would in yours. They follow the fog, because the fog leaves beading water on every leaf, and the mat is most easily torn where water has softened it.

Behind and between them go the smaller runners. Quicker, lighter, harder to watch for — they live on what the herds and the mat expose: grubs turned up by horns, beetles flushed from tunnels, root-tips sweet enough to dig for. They are eaten in their turn.

Higher than the runners, but only when the light is at its grey peak, come the birds. Not singing. Shrieking. Ten thousand at a time filling the hollow air between the pillars for a minute, a sudden weather of wings and cries in a sky that is otherwise empty and close, and then gone, leaving the emptiness louder by contrast. They ride the thin cold above the plateau, wheel over the fresh tear of the cliff where cloud rarely lifts, and drop — when they drop — onto the runners below.

The predators almost never run. They follow the herds singly, each one alone, patient and pale. You do not see the animal so much as the effect: the grass-like shoots of the mat bend in sequence, a ripple traveling across the felt as something low and close to the ground passes through it, never breaking surface if it can help it. They eat grazers and runners and, if the chance comes in the dark hours, anything not large enough to rake them away with a horn. Everything eats everything else here, in its turn, in slow cold motion.

And then, far apart, there are the pillars, and they are lives too, on a different timescale. A pillar plant is a single hollow column, wider than a house at the base, ridged, scarred with old leaf-bases, furred with frozen condensation, rising twenty, forty, sixty meters straight and blind into slate-dark air. It grows in pulses so slow you would miss it if you watched, and ends at the top in a stiff crown of fronds that seem less to feed than to wait — to catch the brief grey light, to catch fog, to catch sleet. Inside the hollow, fog condenses and collects in dark pockets, and only occasionally overflows in a secret cold thread that trickles down the inner bark. You can hear it if you press your ear to the trunk. Nothing smaller lives comfortably inside the dark of the trunk except what comes down that thread, laps, and retreats — and the scratch-marks at shoulder height on the bark outside, where claws or hands have tested for water inside and found only ice and resin-smell.

Between pillars there may be days of walking with nothing rising beside you. Each pillar is itself a condensation point, a weather-maker on a smaller scale, a landmark in a world measured not in miles but in pillars. When one finally dies — rarely, and spectacularly — the frozen trunk splits with a crack that echoes for miles and topples in one piece. It will lie where it fell, preserving its shape, for decades. Nothing rots quickly in this cold. The mat incorporates it foot by foot, and it feeds the felt long after it has ceased to stand.

The people, if they can be called that, are as sparse as the pillars and adapted to the same rationing. There are few of them. No metal, no wheel, no fire that lasts — only stone, bone, antler, fiber, and knowledge carried in the head.

They do not build villages. A settlement is a single hut pressed against the base of a pillar where the wind is less: a ring of stones half-sunk into the mat, blackened inside by a fire long cold, a frame of bone and woven plant fiber stretched overhead, collapsed under ice, its entrance still tied shut with sinew. One hearth, re-used for a generation, then abandoned to the mat, which swallows it without rotting it. The stone remains. The fiber skeleton remains. The place is marked long after the maker is gone.

They live by digging for edible root-tips with stone picks, by weaving the mat itself into blankets because it insulates better than anything they can make to last, by mending nets with bone needles, by traveling the herd-paths and the fog-paths. They listen to the pillars in wind to predict the weather cells that form over the plateau itself — the place is large enough to make its own sleet for an hour, needling the fronds and vanishing instantly on tissue just below freezing. They build a cairn no higher than a knee not for burial or warning but to mark a condensation pocket that only drips in certain weather. They leave a hand-axe of chipped stone hafted with antler frozen in the mat, not lost so much as left for the next hand on a path no one walks alone.

Their fur and feather are off-white and soot-grey like everything else that must survive the dark without wasting color. They move, when they move, in the same pulses as the animals: a season of following herds, a season of staying with a dripping pillar, nights when frost splits the young black rock so loud they wake with ears ringing, days when fog so thickens that the pillar they were walking toward ceases to exist until they are almost against it.

There is nothing here that glows, nothing that walks as a plant, nothing that violates physics. In the darkest hours you may press your palms flat into the mat and swear you feel a faint warmth spreading, a light of another kind — but it is only the living heat of the city underneath, and the slow circulation of the whole plant floor breathing under your feet, very slowly, while a herd drums toward you through the root felt, a flock crosses somewhere unseen, a crack opens a fraction wider in fresh stone, and condensation beads, leaf by leaf, across a surface too large to count.
