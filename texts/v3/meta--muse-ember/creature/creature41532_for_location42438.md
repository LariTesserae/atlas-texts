At the very smallest scale, the Weave is furred.

The dust that settles on your sleeve is not dust. It is leaf-moth, shed from the canopy so far above you cannot see where it is coming from, and what falls is not dead — not all of it. In the warm damp between the bands of root and stem, in the polishing of a path and the hollow of a handhold, there are mites too small to count and too slow to notice, living in the knots where lashings were tied and never untied. They eat the faint sweat of the fiber, the damp left by palms, the pale film of lichen-plant that grows where the erosion has polished the weave smooth. They lift when you step, a tiny leaf of their own rising and settling, and the floor you thought was floor exhales under you.

That is where everything else begins. Nothing here is separate from the plant that holds it, and so nothing here is large enough to be separate for long.

In the pools — not basins, but the cup of a folded leaf, the concave dip worn by generations of feet at a threshold — lives the first animal you will actually see if you crouch. Translucent, the length of a thumb, almost invisible except for the slow pulse of its heart showing through its belly in time with the sap below. It does not swim. It waits. Its mouth hangs open like a trap, and it hangs open for a day if it must, for a glider to stoop, for a drip to cross the surface, for the thing it eats to decide it is safe. When it snaps, it is neat and final, and the pool is warm from its body and from the water that climbed there against its own nature. They live their whole lives in water that may, if the leaf shifts by a hand's breadth, simply be reabsorbed into the weave and gone.

The walls weep, and the walls have their grazers.

Along the dark, moist bark of a dome you will find them if you listen instead of looking: the scrape, patient as filing, of root-grazers in the cork layer. Blind, eyeless, thick-bodied, with jaws made for living wood, they do not cut so much as worry at the growth, keeping the cork from sealing too thick, from closing a vent, from strangling a corridor the makers once meant to stay open. Inside the hollow towers there are more of them, colonies of them, whole middens of chewed fiber in the warmest seams. Once in a while you will hear, from inside a wall you are leaning against, the soft chewing of something unseen. That is not a flaw. That is the building being kept from growing shut.

Lower than the grazers, and slower, are the wall-slugs. They travel the veins of glowing sap that run secretly through root and stem, drinking the condensation that gathers there, leaving behind a trail that catches the amber light like a snail's silver, polished and faintly blue. They are the cleaners of the veins. Where they have passed, the sap does not stagnate and sour the way the ripe fruit does in a sealed hollow stem — and there, too, there is a population, a small, fermenting world inside the rot, making the sweet, heavy smell of wet bark that follows you through a gatehouse. The slugs move upward along the same slow negotiation the water moves, hand over hand, and they appear to follow memory as the plants do: the same groove, the same curve, even long after the making hand is gone.

In the vaults where the light never reaches, the blind birds nest.

You will not see them. You will hear the papery flutter and resettling, a colony stirring in its sleep, and feel the warm air they have made for themselves in the dark under a dome. They live in the dusk inside the leaf, where time is thickest. In a thick-walled dome a shoot can put on a year of growth in the span of a single visit, and the birds have learned — or their bodies have learned for them — to live and die inside that single visit. A leaf unfurls, and midges are already born in it, mating and laying and dying before it has finished opening. An insect swarm moves in when the condensation comes, thickening the air, and the birds take them neatly before the chamber dries again. The dome is a nursery and a stomach at once, and the birds are its quiet, blind attendants, navigating by the shape of the hollow and the warmth of their own feathers.

Out on the bridges, the scale changes, and so does the pace.

The herds come seasonally, following the pulses of new growth after a wet period — after the great deepening of condensation that marks spring here, when the whole weave breathes and a deck-leaf sighs open across a gap. Small, hoofed root-eaters, built low to the weave, travel the long, intentional arcs of the polished paths in procession. They crop the young leaf-decks down to a springy turf, and behind them the turf thickens again. The paths under your feet were widened by hand, but they are kept wide by hooves. The herds remember routes the bridges have forgotten; when the sway of a bridge settles and the plant thickens where the stress is greatest, they still cross in the same order, at the same slow, thoughtful interval, as if crossing were itself a kind of thought. Between herds, a bridge may stand empty for months, growing its railing-hedge a little thicker, waiting.

And the only creatures who treat the emptiness as a road are the gliders.

Between tower and tower there is often a hundred meters of warm, dim air, and a fall of mist, and the underside of a leaf the size of a house, weeping. The herds will not cross that without a bridge. The grazers will not leave the wall. The gliders cross it for no reason you can see, on membranes so thin the amber light passes through them, never in a hurry, rarely calling. They launch, they fall a little, they rise a little on the warm air that never cools, and they land somewhere else on the weave as if the distance had been short. Carry something living, and the path between towers seems to lengthen — the gliders, who are always carrying themselves, perhaps feel it longest of all, and have made their whole life out of an interval that is longer inside them than it is from outside.

At night — that dimming of the already dim, that pause — the whole population changes its light.

The sap-veins beneath the floor-weave pulse, blue-green, like a heartbeat seen through skin, and their light falls upward, lighting the undersides of the great leaves from below. The slugs glow along them. The pool-hunters, translucent, show their hearts. The birds stir, because the dark has become a different kind of dark. Shadows fall upward here, heavier than the light that makes them, and no one who lives here seems to notice, because they have never lived under any other light.

None of them call this place home. Home is a word for something you can leave and return to, and an animal that is part of the wall, the pool, the bridge, the dome, does not think in that way. A root-grazer does not know it is maintaining a corridor. A herd does not know it is mowing a lawn that someone's hands once laid out. A glider does not know it is the only citizen of the empty spaces that hold everything up.

They are not visitors to the plant. They are residents of its architecture, and like the architecture itself, they grow toward memory. The herds keep to the old processions. The slugs keep to the old grooves. The birds keep to the domes that were once cradles. Long after a vine rots and another grafts itself across its place, the chewing continues in the wood that replaced it. Decay and growth keep pace here, and so do predator and prey, at a pace that feels less like hunger and more like digestion — muffled, internal, a body going about its slow work in the warm dim, being built knot by knot and leaf by leaf, by hands long gone and roots that remember their hands, and by the small abundant lives who have taken over the job of keeping it worn-in, inhabited, and listening to itself grow.
