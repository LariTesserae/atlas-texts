Most of it is empty.

That is the first thing to understand about who lives there. The bowl is two hundred meters across and cathedral-high in the middle, and the volume of it is almost entirely uninhabited water — cold, green-grey, dim, moving twice a day in a slow pour from one wall to the other. Nothing resident swims out in the middle of that. The fish that cross the flooded street do so as a transient body, a school, and then they are gone. Everything that *stays* lives in a thin skin, pressed up against the made surfaces. Life here does not live in the water. It lives on the built, in the literal sense, centimeter by centimeter.

Start at the walls, because that is where the density, such as it is, is highest.

The retaining walls are vertical concrete, barnacled to the waterline that never ends. Below that, every vertical surface has been taken over. Mussels have incrusted the steel tanks and the legs of the crane and the sides of the conveyor gantry until the metal looks furred. They are blue-black, wedge-shaped, packed so tightly that the oldest ones are buried behind the younger ones, their shells bored and then smoothed by other animals, layering like roof tiles. Barnacles sit between them, volcano-shaped, opening and closing with a patience that makes the pumps seem frantic. When the sluices flush every twenty minutes or so — a deep, soft roar that you hear in your sternum before you hear it in your ears — the whole wall population opens at once. Feathery cirri beat. Gill filaments unfurl. Anemones that have colonized valve handles and rivet heads and the rims of pipes open on those same handles, pale tentacles waving in the current, catching what the current brings.

They do not move. They will live and die in that one spot, filtering the same cold water their whole lives, growing slowly because the water is cold and the food is dilute. Their shells, when they die, are not removed. They become substrate for the next larva, which settles on shell rather than steel. Over years the crust thickens. The industrial stops being a surface and becomes a reef it grew itself.

The algae are sparser, and that sparseness tells you how little light there is. On the south-facing concrete there are thin green films, no thicker than felt, that green the slabs in patches. Where a little light leaks through the grated roof of the turbine hall, kelp-like fronds unspool from pipe joints and from cracks in the asphalt. They have no roots worth mentioning. They grip metal with a holdfast the size of a coin and hang in the current, swaying with the same slow rhythm as everything else. They grow, they tear, a frond breaks off and drifts across the empty street, and something — a shrimp, an amphipod, a small wrasse — picks at it downstream. It is the only vegetation, and it barely counts as vegetation. It exists because the water is clear enough at twenty meters for a shaft of green to reach it, and because there is nowhere else for anything green to be.

On the floor, between the slabs, is a different community. The cracks are filled not with sand but with crushed brick, concrete dust, shell grit, and droppings. That is the silt here, though it was never silt. Shrimp pulse in it, brown and translucent, flicking backwards to stir it up and then settling to graze the biofilm off the grains. Amphipods and small isopods work the same layer in a constant, low shuffle. You hear the crabs before you see them — a clicking and ticking from inside the pipework — but down here you hear the smaller work: the faint sift of detritus being turned over.

The crabs are the true tenants of the buildings. By the thousands, in the turbine scroll, in the hollow legs of the crane, in the pump house with its doors open, in every pipe joint and hollow tank leg and sump. A single crab on a pipe is what you see when you cross the street with a light, because the rest are at home. Each hollow is an apartment. A brown crab will sit in a pipe elbow for hours, claws tucked, eyes on stalks swiveling in the dim, waiting for the flush to bring food past its door. At night, or when the current changes, they emerge in a slow procession to feed on the mussel beds, shuffling sideways over concrete that has been pitted and then smoothed by their own predecessors. They molt in secret places. The cast shells accumulate in corners and are picked clean by amphipods within days. Not much is wasted, because there is not much to waste. The decomposers in the old oil sumps — bacteria first, a faint iridescence on the water trapped in the concrete, then the amphipods that eat the bacteria — are working the longest-lived food source in the bowl. Oil, in cold water, takes decades.

The fish are the opposite of the wall-dwellers. They do not stay. They use the place.

Herring and pollock and small mackerel come through in schools, hundreds at a time, turning as one body in the long empty street between the turbine hall and the tanks. They follow the current, they follow the sound of the pumps cycling — thump, thump, thump, mindless and tidal — and they scatter when they hit the shadow of the gantry or the dark mouth of the pump house. Then they regroup. Seen from below, they are a silver weather system: they darken the water when they turn, they lighten it when they turn back. They are not feeding much in the street. They are passing through it, using the buildings as a break from the harbor outside, a place where the flow is predictable and the walls are close.

Under the catwalks, where the light does not reach and the hull of the turbine hall throws a permanent shade, the bass hang. One, or two, or six, motionless for an hour at a time, fins barely moving, gills opening slowly in the cold. They do not school. They hold a station under steel, facing the current, waiting for a crab or a shrimp or a straggler from the herring school to be carried past. They are older than they look, fat and slow, grown at the pace of cold water. A crab will know its exact shadow the way a tenant knows a neighbor's step. Most life will happen a meter away from it, carefully.

And through everything, twice a day, comes the tide of new life. When the sluices open, the bowl breathes in. Plankton that were not born here are carried in on that pour — copepods, larval crabs, larval fish, algal spores — drifting over the asphalt street where nothing lives, over the open water between tanks where nothing lives, until they hit a wall. Then they settle, if they can. A barnacle larva feels for steel with its antennae. A mussel larva finds the crust. A kelp spore finds a pipe joint where light leaks. The residents of the bowl are mostly immigrants who decided, once, not to leave.

There is no central predator, no dominant species, no drama. The system is layered like the shells on the wall: sessile feeders at the bottom, grazers and scavengers in the middle, ambush fish at the top, and the schools passing through it all like a rumor. Everything moves a little slower to match the temperature. Everything is ordinary in the way reef life is ordinary when you watch it long enough. A mussel opens. A crab crosses your light. A bass turns its head. The pumps change key as a second one kicks in. The sodium lamp in its glass housing blinks red, amber, red, amber, patiently, and the eyes of a shrimp reflect it for an instant and then it is gone.

They will outlive the labels. DO NOT SMOKE, AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL — the letters are already just suggestions, mustard stains on a tank. The lives do not need them. They were not built for this place, and this place was not built for them, and in the long time the water has had to work them over, the corners have rounded enough that neither one notices the mismatch anymore.
