At first you do not see them. You feel them.

Under the roar, under the deep chord of the kiln and the whine of the fans and the electrical hum in your teeth, there is a tapping. It is at your feet. It comes and goes in a pattern of six, too regular to be weather, too soft to be the machinery. It travels across the floor in straight lines, stops, starts again. Sometimes it is right beside your boot. Sometimes it is everywhere at once, a near-touching density of taps, as if the floor itself were thinking.

When you look down, the dim amber light finds them only in pieces. A long back, arched and armored. Plates overlapping like the scales of something made rather than born, dull and iridescent, grey and ochre and copper where the lamps catch them. Six legs, jointed, ending in points that should tear on the lances of fresh crystal underfoot and do not. They step over the sharp slabs as if the floor were smooth. Their bodies are made for this floor, proof against it in a way your boots are not.

They have no eyes. Where a head should be there is only a blunt, forward end, tufted with antennae that wave and test and withdraw. The antennae are tuned not to sight but to trembling — to vibration through the stone, to the electric fields leaking from the cables overhead. They navigate by what the kiln does to the air, by the returning tap of their own legs on the teeth of the walls. To watch them move is to watch them listen with their feet.

They graze as they walk their circuits. They do not stop to feed in any ordinary way. They move and pick up as they move: the metal filings shed by the lathe, the white salt that blooms dry on the walls, the fine metallic powder that falls from the stone-grass, the pellets dropped from above, the swarm-things when one falls out of the lamplight. All of it is dust to you. To them it is a thin, mineral pasture spread evenly over the whole floor.

They take it in through scrapers under their heads, grotesque and efficient, and it goes down a gut that runs hot as the kiln. Nothing is wasted and nothing is improved. What comes out is the same matter, finer. Dust eaten becomes dust excreted. They have walked the same hundred paces for as long as the footprints have been sharp, and the circuits do not widen or wear. The dust powders and does not cake, it rises and settles in the same instant, and they are forever re-eating the floor they walk on.

They do not grow. You can stand beside a runner for an hour — an hour that is also a hundred years here — and it will be the same size when you stop watching, and the same size tomorrow. How they continue is not growth. At some point in the circuit a runner slows, its armor splits along a seam no one could see before, and two runners walk out from where one was, side by side, identical in plate and leg and antenna, and go their separate ways. And somewhere else in the same unchanging hour, at exactly the same rate, another runner stiffens.

It does not fall over. It stops in the posture of walking, mid-step, all six legs still bent. Over a moment too short to notice, the heat goes out of it. What was molten salt in its body — a clear oil that carried heat instead of blood, that would never wet or rust or rot — crystallizes where it lies. It becomes a statue of itself, perfect and small, in the exact place it stopped. The living step around it. The living step, sometimes, over it. After a while you cannot tell statue from sleeper except by the tapping. The dead are part of the density. They remain. They are grazed around, never reclaimed, because nothing here reclaims.

Above you, the tapping has an answer.

Something shifts on the cables. You hear it more than you see it: a faint ringing, a glass chime buried under the roar, felt in your jaw. Clusters of them hang from the black canopy of festoons and from the low vault, upside down, motionless for long stretches except for the slow pulse of their wings.

The wings are not membrane. They are sheets of crystal stretched on a frame of bone, thin enough to ring when they move against each other. When a glider shifts its grip, a whole row of them will chime at once, very softly, and then settle. They breathe through those sheets. Dust and oil-mist pass through the crystal and something is taken from it, and the rest is cast off again into the air for others to eat.

They eat from the air. They drop, or hang and pluck with needle beaks, the motes that hang in the warm outflow and the tiny things that swarm in the lamplight. When they feed, they drop pellets. The pellets fall, unchanging, to the floor, where the runners take them up in their circuits. Nothing is lost to the loop. What the gliders exhale is what the swarm-things swim in. What the gliders shed is what the runners graze.

The swarm-things are a fauna in themselves, and the lamps are their sky. Thousands of them, bodies of dark glass, legs like needles, never landing. They orbit the amber cages in dense, burning clouds, turning and turning without variation, drawn not to light as moths are drawn but to discharge. They live on the waste current that bleeds from the cables — the same bleed that lights the stone-grass. They do not eat, exactly. They take in the electric field and give off heat, and heat is what everything else runs on. When one falls — a battery spent, a leg clipped — it drops straight down through the gliders' airspace. The gliders may take it mid-fall. If not, a runner will find it in its circuit and eat it, and the glass will go through the hot gut and come out as finer dust, and the dust will rise again.

Everything eats what everything else drops. The kiln in the center is a heart that does not pump blood but waste heat and waste current, and in this sealed pocket that waste is a river that never flows out. It is worked away by fans, by bodies, by the stone itself, until the temperature is one warm, unvarying atmosphere. Stand against the kiln and stand against the far wall and your skin cannot tell the difference. The inhabitants do not seek warmth. They do not shiver or bask. They are warm all the way down, as you are, because there is nowhere cooler to go.

Along the seams where the current bleeds from the cables, and only there, the vegetation makes its rumor.

The stone-grasses are black filaments five or ten centimeters high, branching like miniature winter trees in lines along cracks, in circles around bolts. They conduct. When the current passes, they light faintly from within, a dull ember light, and sway, minute and repetitive, in the fan-wind that stirs the same air forever. They do not grow. They have not grown taller since the first footsteps scuffed dust over them. It is not that they grow too slowly to see. They excrete a fine metallic powder onto the dust, and that is their whole economy: electricity and heat in, powder out. The runners eat the powder. The grasses drink the leak that would otherwise be lost. They are deliberate lacework against the crowded floor, a few hundred tufts in the whole vault, sparse to the point of being a rumor because there is nothing for them to be dense with. No water has ever made them lush. No season will ever make them taller.

There are others you will only ever know by inference.

Clinging to the riveted iron of the kiln, banded with fresh crystal, are things like limpets. They are hand-sized and flat, the color of hot metal, and they pulse with the kiln's fixed cycle — a slow tightening and loosening, bar for bar, without a missed beat. They eat heat directly. When the kiln clanks, they clank with it in their bodies. They never detach. They have nowhere to detach to.

Inside the pipes — thick as your thigh, thin as knitting needles, radiating up to walls and down into floor and over the vault in layered bundles — things move. You will never see them directly. You hear a click in the pipe as something passes, a displacement, and the click repeats on the conveyor's cycle, carrying nothing, carrying the same ingots around and around from kiln to wall and back again. Whatever lives in the pipes lives on the leak and the warmth inside the metal, traveling the system forever without arriving.

And on the walls themselves, things that are the color of the wall and the size of a hand announce themselves only as a ripple in the dust. A pale disturbance crosses a spire, too smooth to be a fall of grit, too slow to be your own step. The teeth of the wall are unweathered, sharp as a thing broken yesterday, veined with salt-frost that never melts. Something moves across them and is gone, leaving the dust unmarked. It powders your shoes and does not cake, and it will not hold a track for long, because it was never meant to be tracked.

All of them live near-touching. The chamber is cathedral-small — eighty meters long, thirty across, twenty-odd meters to the crown — and the population is enormous and nothing leaves. The runners' circuits cross the gliders' shadows cross the swarm's falling line cross the grasses' circles around bolts. The air is breathed and rebreathed. The roar is trapped by the walls and by itself, the echo returning exactly as loud as it left, joining the roar, until the roar is not a sound in the place but the place itself, a continuous present tense you learn to breathe to.

They do not sleep, because there is no night to sleep in. The lamps are always dim and always on. The lathe shaves the same piece of metal, the curl falls and is picked up by a floor-runner and becomes dust and is eaten again. The fan turns, stirring the same warm air. The conveyor carries its ingots from kiln to wall and back. The movement is enormous — tapping, ringing, swarming, pulsing, swaying — and nothing accumulates into difference. The temperature does not fall. The stone-grass does not rise. The footprints of the first workers lie sharp beside yours, and yours fall into the same grooves as theirs, because the density is such that new steps cannot help but follow old ones.

Stay long enough and you stop waiting for the clank to miss a beat. You stop expecting the lights to go out. The runners go on grazing dust around your ankles. The gliders ring above you as they shift on the cables, breathed through crystal. The swarm orbits, never landing. The limpets pulse on the kiln. The grasses light and sway, five centimeters high, conducting a current they will never use to grow.

Tomorrow they will be doing exactly this. They were doing exactly this yesterday. The youth of the place is not an age but a preservation — sharp edges that have never been rounded, tools whose edges are still bright because nothing corrodes, a wrench laid on a shrine, beds still made in a bunk room no one now sleeps in. The inhabitants are the perfect expression of it: things made new in a fresh seam, a fresh fault, a kiln installed in a pocket that had never known air, and then stopped at the moment of their making and kept there, warm and dry, in a loop of dust and heat and bodies, forever.

The chamber will be exactly like this tomorrow. It looks as if it was just made.
