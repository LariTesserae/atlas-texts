You meet them first not as people but as a current.

Streams of them, in identical felt coats, moving between machines along lanes that may not be lanes tomorrow. They come off a casting hall in a pack shoulder to shoulder, because there is no shoulder-width to spare, and they peel off one by one into doorways and down cellar stairs and up ladders lashed to the outside of a wall. Up close the sameness breaks. A coat is soot-black at the cuff, moss-green on the north shoulder, rust-orange where a foundry door has brushed it for years. Pockets are turned inside out from use. A warm stone lives in one, a spare bolt, a twist of vine fibre, a moss tile for trading. Their hands are broad, cracked at the knuckles from cold metal, quick and practiced at wiping. Their voices, when you get close enough to hear one, are not voices so much as a handling of air — low, close to the ear, as if any sound raised would travel too far through the packed town and get lost or answered by machinery that moves too slowly to care.

They do not live in houses. They live above the works, beside them, in them, in the layers the works have left spare.

A family is not a household in a building. It is a cluster around a heat source. A warm pipe in a wall, a corner of a loom-shop where the air never quite drops below a certain damp, the underside of a condenser where the drip keeps a fern alive. When a vine takes root on a roof and insulates it, a family moves up to tend it. Not because they own the roof, but because a tended roof is a warm roof and a warm roof will not freeze and crack and take the rooms below with it. So they move up a storey, built across two older roofs by a walkway, and the children learn the new route across trusses and tram wires the way other children learn a street. In summer they thin out and live in the works themselves, in cooled sheds and sorting yards between shifts. In winter they migrate — not away from the town, but deeper into it, from the cold yards to the warm loom quarters, from the outer scaffolds toward the condenser farms, following heat the way something follows a river that isn't there.

No one is simply a worker or simply a resident. The districts bleed. A foundry crew will sort and mend in the afternoon, a sorting-shed crew will tend a boiler in the night watch. Everyone knows the basic trades because the town will not stay built if you don't: how to spot a slab heaving under frost before it lifts a cart off its mark, how to lay a temporary trestle and take it up again, how to reset a felt curtain against a doorway so the cold does not eat the room behind it. The specialists are those who have settled into a niche the way a plant settles into a crack.

There are the Dewhands, who work the morning with brushes on slow rails and by hand, wiping condensation off the backs of machines, off window insides, off leaves thick with it, off their own sleeves. The water they gather goes into tanks, needful and thin. There are the Vine-Layers, mostly children and old women, who sit by pipes in perfect geometry, twisting young vine around metal to insulate it against frost, singing — not singing, breathing in rhythm — so the vine knows the circuit. There are the Slab-Watchers, who stand at a corner for an hour doing nothing but watching a concrete pour breathe up a hand's breadth, ready to reroute a conveyor or a tram loop or a stream of coats around it. There are the Tank-Keepers in the condenser farms, who know their tanks by their blind fish. The fish rise when the drip comes, pale and eyeless, turning in the green algae water, and the keeper reads them like a dial: too fast, too slow, a condenser cycling wrong.

There are the Eel-Handlers, who work in the dark warm tanks where boiler eels stir, clearing silt with their forearms, talking to them in the town's low register because the eels are part of the machinery and the machinery is part of the household. There are the Fern-Tenders, who water — if you can call it watering — furnace ferns whose fronds go translucent where heat hits them, who transplant a shoot from a motor to a wall where it will hold heat, who know that a plant short-circuiting a machine is not a failure but a redesign waiting to happen. There are the Bazaar people, who open in a doorway with warm stones and spare parts and moss tiles and algae cakes, and are somewhere else by afternoon because the doorway is now a conveyor or the slab has heaved or a crew needs the space to recast. They are not nomads by philosophy but by necessity; trade follows the temporary gaps.

Children are the fastest inhabitants, and they live highest. They run messages across rooftops, under bridges that may be gardens next week, over creaking walkways at the fourth story where a pipe runs diagonally through three buildings for good measure. They are smaller, their coats cut at the arms so they can climb, their faces pink from the air. They know every heave and every vent by heart. On cold mornings when a hundred stacks vent at once and the whole sky fogs for an hour, they stand on the slag heaps and watch everything glisten and bet — quietly, in the way of the town — on how long condensation will run uphill on the underside of a condenser when it cycles. They lose. They win. The bet is not about the water. It is about paying attention.

The old ones do not retire so much as they settle. They sit. An old tender by a generator housing, an old woman sorting moss tiles by feel, a man whose whole job now is to listen for the drip inside a wall, never quite making a puddle, and to know when it changes pitch. Their bodies have learned the town's pace: slow because heavy things move slowly, economical because warmth is shared or it is wasted. They sleep stacked, in warm tunnels and cellar layers, under felt curtains hung to keep cold from the doorway, breathing each other's damp air. They eat from what the town makes: fungal shelves harvested from under warm conduits, algae from the tanks, blind fish when the tank is cleaned, moss tiles softened in warm water. It is not farming. It is gathering from infrastructure, left and right.

And they are not the only inhabitants, and not even the most numerous.

The plants are residents with full tenancy. Moss is carpet over every north-facing stone, over roofs, over the backs of sleeping machines — not decoration, but insulation, a roof that does not freeze. Ferns occupy furnaces. Vines occupy pipes. Fungal shelves bloom under every warm conduit and are cut and re-bloom. Lichen paints the stacks rust and sulphur yellow because the stacks are there to be painted. Algae fills the condensation tanks green and multiplies. Nothing is cut back. The town does not own the plants; the plants occupy the town as fully as any factory, and the people have arranged their lives to be useful to each other. A wall that is ferned holds its heat. A family that tends it holds its place.

The animals follow the same rule: wherever there is a warm, wet, protected pocket in a cold desert of air, something lives in it, small and numerous and everywhere under every plate. Boiler cats sleep on generator housings, fat, unbothered, deafened pleasantly by the hum they chose; they migrate from housing to housing as the vibration changes with the season. Pigeons nest inside the bellies of cooling towers and fertilize the moss with their droppings. Rats gnaw through felt and make prettier nests with what they steal, and are tolerated because a pretty nest is a warm nest. Lizards bask on sunlit iron in the grey-green noon. Worms work the new soil — the spoil, the ash, the ground-down walls — as fast as it forms, turning waste into ground to stand on. Bats hang in the attic layers where the tram wires hum, navigating by a sound no human bothers to listen to. Dragonflies patrol steam and pollinate the furnace ferns. Starlings and midges and spores pack the air along with breath and grinding dust, because even the air is not empty.

Strangest, and most ordinary to them, is how constantly they swap places. A machine gives birth to a plant in its own crack, and the plant shorts the machine, and the machine is redesigned to welcome the short. A building grows a new floor when someone moves up to tend a vine. A wall collapses and is not left a ruin but ground down by its own inhabitants into fill for the next wall, which rises a foot to the left. A man dies — quietly, in a warm tunnel, in winter — and his coat becomes a curtain, his warm stone passes to a child, his body is taken not to a graveyard, because there is no ground here that does not need building, but into the soil-forming work, into worms and moss and fill, remade while still, in a sense, in use. Nothing here erodes into the past. Everything is emerging, and everything is being taken apart and remade.

That is why they speak so low, why they move in streams, why you cannot quite get a face to hold still. They are a temporary arrangement of land and plant and machine and animal and person, packed, quiet under pressure, building themselves in the cold, in the damp, on ground that heaves under their boots. You watch a child lick dew from a rail and run it across a roof to a tender who will pour it into a tank. You watch a cat shift on a generator as the cranes turn all night like patient animals above it. You watch a fern uncurl out of a motor and a motor hum, uncomplaining, under a fern.

The light is still normal. Your breath is still showing. The drip, drip of condensation is still falling somewhere inside a wall, never quite making a puddle, and they are still listening for it, still wiping it up, still living in it.
