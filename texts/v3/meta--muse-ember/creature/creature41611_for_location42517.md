In Neth Hollow, no one lives alone, and no one lives loudly. The town is not the main population of the bowl. It is a small, patient clearing made inside a much larger, much quieter one — thousands of slow bodies moving through mist, hundreds more moving across moss, and, between them, a few hundred people who have learned to make their lives fit the routes already there.

They are born into that choreography and never quite leave it. A child of the Hollow has never seen a sun disc, never stood in rain that fell from above, never heard an echo. She knows the world first by touch and by wetness: the beading on her forearms at dawn, the damp of her boots that never dries, the cold pearl light that never brightens into morning. Her house may be rebuilt three times in a season when the young rock shifts a finger's width and leans a terrace wall, and she will help carry the stones back one by one without complaint, because complaint, like shouting, would be wasted sound here. Moss takes it.

The people live by districts, and the districts are trades. Below the raised granary — stone pillars lifting the seed and dried fern-shoots clear of the damp carpet — are the weavers' terraces, where looms of wood and bone work all day in the thin air and the workers speak low, mostly to themselves, mostly with their hands. Near the sharpest quarry are the stone-cutters, with obsidian knives and cold copper awls, fitting black basalt without mortar, puzzle-piece to puzzle-piece, for houses that are one room, sometimes two, roofed with sewn mats of fern and hide. At the east gate live the herders, though they herd in no way a lowland would understand: they open the gate, they stand to one side, they touch a passing flank to say *this way*, they count by tally-stick what passed through.

Everyone, in some hour of the day, is a net-tender. Water does not run here, so people collect it where it clings. Before the mist thickens in late afternoon, they stretch woven nets on poles in the empty fields and lower cupped rocks mouth-up on roofs, and they run their palms along stone troughs cut into the terraces to coax the round, clear drops — gathering on undersides, spreading slowly uphill toward the coldest rock — inward to a hollow lapilli no wider than a hand. It is primitive, organized patience, and it fills the whole day in small ways. A household can go weeks without rain and never be thirsty. The air itself tastes faintly metallic and green, and nothing is ever truly dry.

They talk little because sound does not travel in the cloud bowl. Moss absorbs it, soft bodies absorb it, the flat grey light seems to absorb it. So the Hollow has replaced voice with other things: tapped stones, pressed palms, flags lifted from the watch cairn at the bowl's highest point. The flag is for the big events, when a migration is coming up through the white sea below the rim and sound would never carry the warning. One flag for a grazer herd, two for drifters rising thick, a third, held long, for hunters descending. Children learn to read those signals, and the lights on living bodies, long before they learn many words.

And the living bodies are everywhere, in a motion that makes almost no noise at all.

Highest are the drifters. Translucent ribbons one to three metres long, they hang in the ceiling-mist like slow kites, like deep-sea eels that have learned to swim in air. They have no wings and no eyes that matter. Their skins drink the condensation directly from the cloud, and when they are full they exhale it back as a fine, deliberate mist that settles on the ferns and moss below. On a still hour there may be thousands, parting silently around a person walking the pumice street, closing behind them, filling the whole bowl with glassy weather until you are inside it and cannot see the far wall. The people depend on them the way they depend on the nets: drifters are the lungs and the rain-makers of the place, a living weather system that never falls, only gives back.

Below them, the ground itself moves. The carpet-grazers are goat-sized, blind, velvet-grey, with sponge feet that make no sound on moss. In herds of hundreds they flow from one fern island to the next in broad, straight passes, eating the moss meadows as they go and leaving behind a clean, mown trail. They do not hurry, they do not call, and they do not respect the town's walls or gates or streets — they pass straight through them, under the one-room houses, between the net-poles, as if the streets were laid out for them and the town simply borrowed the plan. When they feed, pale patches of light bloom along their flanks, a slow silent language of touch and glow by which a herd knows its neighbours' mood, its hunger, its readiness to move on. Herders learn to read those lights the way lowlanders read faces.

In spring the grazers slough their fat outer skin, soft and grey, and some in the Hollow say that skin does not die. It lifts, it thins, it rises on the cloud-breath and becomes a drifter. No one can prove it in the fog. No one needs to. In a place where nothing stays in one shape for long, it is the right kind of story to be true.

Above and among them, patient and thin, are the hunters. Long-legged, long-necked things that come down from the rim rocks on tendril-thin limbs, eyeless as the others, they hang and wait for a drifter to rise within reach. Then a neck extends, a hollow trumpet-like tongue snaps, and a ribbon vanishes — no cry, no splash, no disturbance in the mist. It is a slow ballet in the pearl-grey light, repeated all day at the edges of the bowl. The town watches it the way it watches the settling of a wall: not with fear, but with the knowledge that everything here is balanced by an appetite somewhere else.

Between those three great motions, smaller lives fill the cracks. Moss-crabs no bigger than a thumb scuttle sideways through fern thatch and roof-mats, eating mould and fallen spores. Blind moths erupt in a soft cloud from the sponge-moss at dusk, dust the air for a minute, and settle again without a sound. Worms live inside the rock fractures themselves, moving when a crack hisses faintly and settles a millimetre deeper, carrying moss-roots into fresh stone. And the plants are lives too, in the Hollow's isolated way: a fern ten metres across, single and enormous and silver-green; a pale-barked tree alone on bare rock, its canopy one vast umbrella, its roots thrusting upward through cracks to drink condensation straight from the air; a mat of moss inches deep and springy, which is soil and carpet and roof-thatch and silence-maker all at once. Between those fullnesses there is often nothing for thirty paces — just sharp black teeth of young basalt — and that emptiness is part of the design. Lushness here does not crowd. It gathers, intensely, in islands.

So a day in Neth Hollow is a day of yielding. At dawn everyone is beaded. The grazers move out across a moss meadow in their clean straight lines. The drifters drink and exhale and the ferns drink in turn. People mend a net, re-sew a roof, lean a wall back upright, tally the water, lift a flag. At noon the streets can be entirely empty — not because the town is abandoned, but because everyone has stepped aside for a herd, or slipped under a tree's umbrella to feel the slower, heavier drops fall from its leaves, or is inside listening to a loom with the close attention the place demands. In late afternoon the mist thickens and passes through like something exhaling, and even the hunters come down lower, necks outstretched among the drifting ribbons.

At night it gets only a fraction darker. The drifters glow very faintly where they sleep in the mist. The grazers' flank-lights pulse slowly as the herd settles, still flowing even in sleep. The small hearths smoke against the cool, and the cloud ceiling stays, low and brightening and dimming as if something large turns behind it. The people settle into their damp houses — houses that may lean by morning — and wait for the condensation to come again.

That is the rule they all live by, animals and people alike, whether they know it as a rule or not: water does not flow here; it clings and is given back. Sound does not travel here; it is taken and replaced by light and touch. And nothing — not rock, not roof, not grazer, not drifter, not fern, not child — stays in one shape or one place for long. Everything is young, and everything is moving, and the town at the top of the world is a brief, carefully built clearing in the middle of that motion, inhabited mostly by things that never speak at all.
