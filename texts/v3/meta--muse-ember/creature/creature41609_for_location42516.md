### Who Lives in the Coiled Grove

The first lives are not the ones you can see. They are under the floor.

Beneath the uneven slabs — packed rubble, sun-baked brick, clay beaten flat — the made soil is busy and blind. Clay-worms move through it in slow coils, dry as hair, eating plaster-dust and dead leaf, eating the scrapings of the walls themselves. They have no eyes because there is nothing under a slab to see, only the patient dark where crushed brick, leaf-dust, ash, and dung were sifted by hand and fed back into the terraces that make the floor of the Grove. When they turn, the earth above them settles a fraction. When they eat, the Grove eats its own shed skin and makes it ground again.

Above them, in the beams, the kiln-beetles bore. They go through wood and plaster the way a vine goes through air, patiently, one mandible at a time. Their shells are domed and stamped with ridges, and if you bend close — the walls are low, everything is close — the ridges look like miniature roofs. A beetle knocks once inside a beam and is still again. That knock is not quite a sound the keepers answer to. It is a sound they listen for, to know the beam is still being turned over from inside, still seasonable, still alive enough to be worked.

Between the bricks, in cavities built for them or obligingly enlarged, live the small furred burrowers. They hoard pods in the wall-hollows, curl in the warmth that pools there all day, and come out only a little to sniff the mineral dust. The keepers built for them once, deliberately, and have been building for them ever since without quite deciding whether it is kindness or construction. The burrowers do not seem to mind the distinction. They live in the wall the way a thought lives in the head.

On the surfaces, where the light falls and the plaster is pale, are the plaster-slugs. They are dry things, no slime at all. They glide across a wall and leave a clear film that hardens like resin in the sun and peels away in flakes. They are cleaners, though no one taught them the word. Behind them the wall is fresher. Clay-lizards bask on the warm tiles beside them, skin granular as unglazed pottery, flicking at the air, licking mineral dust from it. Their feet make no sound. Their tails are thick with stored pulp.

In the hangings, the moths. They flutter in the woven cloth and eat fiber, lay their eggs in the seams of baskets, in the loose place where a reed was woven too tight and the next reed too loosely. They are the quiet undoing of woven things, and also, perhaps, a kind of weaving themselves. In the eaves, in every thatch and vine-tangle, the thatched wrens nest. A few dozen may be visible from one spot, puffing out their chests, saying nothing, watching with bright eyes. They do not sing. In this place song would travel too far and die too quickly in the thick air. Watching is enough. Their plumage, if you look long enough, repeats the weave of the nest it was fledged from.

And then the green, which is not background and not decoration, and is certainly not wild. The wall-figs root directly in mortar, dissolving brick for minerals with their roots, becoming beams with their branches, shading the bricks they are eating. One fig may take a decade to cross a courtyard. A coiled vine may advance a handspan in a year, climbing out of a pot that is built into the wall, reaching for the next pot built into the next wall. Plaster-ferns unfurl from cracks between tiles and shed powdery spores instead of seed, so that the air itself stays faintly dusty with them. Roof-beans hang in long fragrant strings from the thatch. Basket-mint and kiln-grass grow in every container that has been left for more than a season, and many containers are not left at all — they are stacked, nested, hung, planted through, pot holding plant holding pot holding plant.

Nothing here grows in earth that was not made, and nothing grows quickly. Leaves are waxy and thick-veined and slow to wilt, their fine pores opening at dawn to gather the faintest suspended dampness bound in the air, and even that never gathers into drops. Sap is thick as warm honey and hardens in the sun. Roots are dry as hair. When a leaf falls, it is not allowed to rot in a heap. It is gathered, ground, and mixed into new plaster or new potting earth. The plants are inhabitants in the full sense: they hold the place up, they eat the place, they are eaten by the place in turn. A vine that thickens across a gap becomes structure. A fig that crosses a courtyard becomes a wall's elder.

Among all this, dark against the pale plaster, move the keepers.

There are only a few dozen of them, and they are easy to miss at first because they do not hurry and they do not stand out. They are patient people, low-voiced, absorbed by leaves and cloth and earthen walls. Their skin is dark, resinous, sun-darkened, dusted always with lime and clay. Their hair is like dry root, tied back with vine-rope. Their clothes are continuations of everything else — woven reed, bundled leaf, leather stitched to a wooden frame, cloth patched where a moth has been at it, no two garments standard, nothing machined, everything thumbprinted. A keeper's house is low and plastered and roofed with thatch and vines, and if you could see it from above, through the leaves, you would notice that it resembles the floor plan of the whole Grove: its own tiny courtyard, its own tiny wall, its own ring of pots.

They tend. That is most of what can be said of them, and it is a great deal. They coil clay. They plaster. They re-thatch. They prune, thin, re-tie the green poles of the half-built room at the east end, break and rebuild. Their tools are few and kept carefully: stone flakes, bone needles, wooden paddles, reed knives, a few bronze awls passed down. No wheels except the slow turn of a potter's hand. One keeper may hum a single note for a long while as she coils, not a song, more a way of keeping the air steady in the room. When they speak, they speak little, and low, and unhurried, and the words are absorbed by leaves and cloth and earthen walls before they can travel far. A sentence may take a whole morning to arrive, and by then everyone listening has already understood it from the hands.

They do not drink water, because there is none. What they drink they take from pulpy pods and resinous fruits, sucking the thick pulp, chewing the dry aromatic pods, drying the pulp further and storing it in clay jars against the long blurred seasons. They eat roof-beans and the mineral-sweet pulp of ripe pods, and the little stores the burrowers hoard are not raided; the keepers have their own jars, sealed with resin. They do not wash in water. They dust themselves, rubbing sun-warmed brick-dust and plaster-dust into the pores of their skin, letting the fine film peel away like the slugs' film, hardening, flaking. When they are cut — a reed knife, a sharp fresh edge of stone — what seals them is not blood that runs but sap that thickens, like the plants', honey-warm and quickly sun-hard.

They live long. In a place where seasons are long and blurred and a year may pass with only a change in the color of the light, a life is not measured in days. Animals live long and breed rarely. Plants unfurl one leaf in a morning and rest for months. Keepers notice growth the way one notices a child growing — not day by day, but suddenly, between visits, the doorway is narrower, the vine has crossed the window, a nest has appeared where there was only thatch. A wall leans a fraction more toward the sun. A roof-tile slips a finger's width. A beam sighs in the heat. They notice, and they mend, and the mending is never finished because the place is young and because the place does not want to stay finished.

Their children — few, and long in coming — grow the same slow way, learning to coil by watching a potter's hand for seasons at a time, learning to listen for the beetle's knock before learning to speak much. A child will spend its first years in one courtyard, then another, then the corridor that measured eight paces yesterday and measures nine today, not because anyone extended it but because a plant has thickened against one wall and a pot has appeared against the other and the space between has politely adjusted. The keepers know every such adjustment. They are the ones who cull.

For the Grove copies itself, and so do its inhabitants. That is its law. A tile left on a windowsill will over a season be mirrored by a leaf whose veins follow the tile's pattern. A pot overlooked in a corner will sprout a smaller pot from a crack in its side, and the smaller pot will sprout another. A bird's nest woven in the roof will be echoed months later in the weave of a nearby basket. A beetle born in a warm upper room will carry a shell a fraction more domed than its mother, like a kiln built inside a kiln. A keeper's face, over years, comes to repeat its mother's face in smaller and slightly wrong detail, as a house repeats the Grove in smaller and slightly wrong detail. The smaller the copy, the more clearly the pattern survives and the more the details go wrong — and the wrongness is not violent. It is gentle, and terribly patient. It accumulates the way dust accumulates, the way a vine accumulates across a roof.

So the keepers break the extra pots. They thin the vines. They re-plaster over the too-eager wall. Not because the copies are harmful, but because if they are left alone, the Grove begins to think in copies. It forgets which was the first.

Deeper in, the forgetting has gone further, and the inhabitants there are harder to name. There are rooms that were never built, or that were built once and dismantled, which nevertheless have a coolness, a smell of lime, a chair-shaped hollow in the plaster where something sat for years. Someone lives in that coolness, in the way a memory lives in a room after the sitter has gone — not a body, but a place held for a body, a habit of sitting baked into the wall. A staircase in the north wing has gained a step that no one remembers laying. The step is fresh, sharp-edged, plaster still pale. Doors sometimes open onto the room you have just left, but rearranged — the same pots, the same vine, the same light, in a slightly different order, as if the room were remembering itself incorrectly. In one small chamber near the center, the light from the window falls across the floor and stays there a little after the sun has moved on, slowly absorbed by the leaves on the wall, as if the plants were drinking it and were in no hurry to be done.

Nothing here is an original any longer. All faces are copies of copies. A leaf in the innermost room looks like a roof seen from outside the plain. A bird's plumage repeats the weave of its nest. A keeper's house resembles the Grove, and the Grove, small enough to hold in your view from the central courtyard, resembles a single house, and that house resembles a pot, and the pot resembles a shell, and the shell resembles a roof. It is difficult, after a while, to say where making stops and living begins, because the keepers make pots that sprout, and vines that become beams, and houses that become Grove, and children who become copies, and all of them — worm and beetle and slug and lizard and moth and wren and fig and fern and keeper — eat dust and light and mineral film and resinous pulp, and are eaten in turn by dust and plaster and ground-up leaf, and go on, slowly, in the warm quiet.

Stand in the central courtyard at midday and you can see them all at once in layers, three and four deep: the wrens watching from the eaves, the lizards basking on the tiles, the slugs filming the walls, the vines turning their faces up, the pots turning their faces down, the keepers dark against the pale plaster, pruning, coiling, humming a single note, listening for a beetle. The leaves turn. The clay ticks as it settles. Nothing is hurrying. Nothing needs to.

They live by making themselves again — leaf by leaf, brick by brick, shell by shell, face by face — slightly smaller, slightly wrong, in the warm quiet of their own interior, and by gently forgetting, together, how any of it was first made.
