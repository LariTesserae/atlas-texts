The people are the least numerous living thing in Steamhollow, and the most intermittent.

There are maybe seven hundred of them, but you rarely see more than a dozen at once. They exist in the pools of amber light, eight meters up, and they vanish between them. A turbine crew appears in Turbine Row when the load shifts, thin shirts plastered damp to their backs, shorts, sandals on sticky feet, moving slowly because there is nowhere cool to hurry to. A laundry shift sorts in bleach and hot oil steam, hands pruned white, stepping daily around the young stalagmite growing from a leak under a pipe. A market seller under the hydroponic tubes cuts watery lettuce that is white-green and has never felt wind. Then a lamp in an empty district is switched off to save power, and within minutes they are gone from it, and the dark is not empty.

They live by the lamps, not by any sun. The sun is a rumor brought down by lift drivers — surface weather, seasons, a blue — and the children who are born in the bowl ask about it the way surface children might ask about a vent. For them the day begins with a shift change and a flicker in the turbine hum. Sleep is taken in whatever darkness a house can make for itself, on raised stone platforms above the damp floor, in rooms that never truly dry. Bodies have adjusted to the 38 to 42 degrees by doing less. Skin is pale and flushed at once, macerated at the feet, always sweating because nothing dries here. No one wears a coat, no one carries a jacket for later. Movement is short, practiced, between a house, a shed, a pump, a canal crossing. Distances across the 240 meters of the bowl are measured not in minutes but in how much heat you are willing to accumulate doing them.

It is a working town, layered on top of a drilling camp. The first hands are still here in some houses, the ones whose thresholds have been quietly lifted two stone steps because the canal water crept up. Others are transients who came down on the lift with a trade — a welder, a pump fitter, a clinic orderly — and who work a rotation and go back up with their ears popping. Some families have stayed long enough to have children who have never seen sky. The clinic has its pale, stunted ferns in planters outside, watered by those children with buckets of condensate scraped from pipes. That is the most gardening anyone attempts.

What they eat tells you who else lives here. Most food is not grown in soil. It is mushrooms in the warm dark rooms under the turbine hall, fed on sawdust and waste heat, turning steadily in conditions that would kill most surface crops. It is fish, and fish again, because the hatchery built for protein leaked years ago and now every connected pool is stocked. The hydroponic lettuce and tomatoes are a supplement, grown under tubes that compete with the street lamps. People keep lightly, eat lightly, depend almost entirely on things that already like heat, dark, and constant wet.

Because the world they depend on is not theirs.

In the canals the water looks milky and slow, and the milk moves. Translucent, blind cave fish and shrimp are so dense that they travel as a single current along the south canal, along Low Canal, through the shallow warm sheets that fill the vacant lots where a building collapsed. When you wade, they brush your waders in a soft, continuous touch and part and rejoin behind you. They have no eyes to speak of, no color worth naming, only gills and mouths and a patience tuned to sediment and algae and whatever falls in. The hatchery fish, escaped and feral, are now part of that current. The town farms them and the town is fished by them in a way — the water level in the pools rises and falls with their generations.

Under floor tiles and wall panels and drain covers is another town entirely. Lift a drain cover anywhere in the inhabited bowl and hundreds of white isopod backs will shift and ripple away from the light, a living mat, pale and damp and eyeless. With them are pale cave crickets, long-antennaed, clicking in the quiet corners where the industrial drone drops between pump cycles. Their sound is an interval, like the drips from the ceiling, clean and fainter: *tick*, click, rustle. The children know to put covers back gently because the isopods clean the drains of detritus the pumps miss. The workers know to wave, not to swat.

The lamps bring the third layer. Troglobite moths live in the dark between the pools and erupt around any light left on, a blizzard of dusty wings that settles on skin, on food, on the line-dried laundry the town barely has. It is not enough light to keep them off the food as the surface does, so the moths are enough to feed the next layer. Up on the ceiling, fifteen to twenty meters up, is the blanket: perhaps ten thousand bats, packed in the irregular rock between props and cables, hanging motionless in the heat, chittering in a rising and falling chorus whenever a lamp flickers or a turbine load shifts. Their guano falls in quiet places and smells of ammonia, and on it grow fungus gardens — pale, spreading, slow — that in turn feed the isopods and crickets and the drip-water fungi that stipple the walls. At night, or when the pumps are throttled and a street is used for transit because it is marginally less hot, the bats drop through the lamp pools and take the moths on the wing. A working town would call this pest control. Here it is just the ceiling eating the light's bycatch.

In the shallow pools at the edge of town, where the water is ankle-deep and warm and slick with biofilm, blind salamanders hang motionless for hours. Their gills are feathery and white, their bodies translucent enough that the warm stone shows through. They are waiting, in the specific, unhurried way of cave animals, for drip-borne insects to fall from the ceiling sheets on the west wall, for a moth that missed the bats, for a cricket that slipped. A salamander pool grows a little wider each year, not by flood but by patient erosion, and the salamanders follow it, and the town, if it notices, builds its next shed a little farther back.

And under all of this, quieter still, the majority. Thin green slicks of algae move in the canals under the lights and stop in the dark. Moss and liverwort make tufts only where drip water falls regularly and a lamp happens to stay on long enough to matter — a few centimeters of green on wet stone, stunted by heat. Biofilm coats the warm water at ankle depth in the low lots, and the white mineral crusts are colonized at their edges by bacteria that like sulfur near the southeast vent, where the rock glows dark red under dust and the air is close to 50. In the hot brine wells themselves, 45-degree water steaming where it meets the cooler walls, things live that have no business on the surface and have never seen a lamp at all. The town steps over, around, and through all of it. A new stalagmite is not removed; it is stepped around until it is large enough to be a landmark. Sediment in thin dunes is washed away and returns.

None of them are monsters. That is the point that keeps surfacing as you watch. The fish are ordinary troglobites. The isopods are ordinary detritivores. The bats are ordinary insectivores made extraordinary only by number. The salamanders are ordinary ambush predators in a place where ambush takes a year. What makes Steamhollow is scale and the absence of drying, of cold, of night. Life here does not dry out, does not freeze, does not get a season to die back. It accumulates. It clicks and squeaks and splashes underneath the one unvarying industrial drone, a forest noise in a stone room, louder — thousands to one — than the people who built the lamps.

The people live in shifts and pools. The animals live continuously. The heat belongs to no one but governs both, dictating which fish pool is too hot for waders, which street is only traversable when the pumps throttle, which mushroom room can be left unlit for a day, which ceiling roost will slump a little farther this year. If you stay a week, you will learn to tell the bat chorus from the pump hum, to put a drain cover back before the isopods scatter too far, to see a fish current as a current and not a trick of milky water. If you stay a year, you will notice the white lines have climbed higher, the salamander pool has widened by a hand's breadth, a house has been lifted.

Nothing here is finished. The rock is still being made, the crust still climbing, the generations still turning over in the dark between the lights — the people brief and bright in their pools, the rest patient and everywhere, making the place more itself the longer it goes unwatched.
