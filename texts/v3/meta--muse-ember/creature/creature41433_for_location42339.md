At first step, nothing lives here.

The bowl is too bright for that, and too small. You have climbed through cloud and thin air that makes your lungs work like bellows, and now the top is a ringed plateau you could cross on foot in twenty minutes. Ash, pumice, frost-shattered rock, snow that never quite melts. The sky at noon is hard and dark blue, almost black at the zenith, and the sun is vertical and white. Everything bleaches to greys and whites. Shadows are the only color left.

There is nothing green. Nothing that burrows, flies, or crawls. The wind sometimes carries something up from the cloud sea below — a dead insect, a seed — and drops it, and it lies there unrotted, frozen dry, until the sun turns it white. It does not live. It is kept.

Look closer, in the coldest hour before dawn, and you will find the first inhabitants. They are not here at midday. They have already vanished.

On every windward edge, every wall, every glass shard and the tops of stones, hoarfrost has furred the mineral surface overnight. Needle-fine, white as magnified snowflakes, feathered and branching. In sheltered cracks the growth is larger: ice in blades and rosettes, crystal gardens pushing a finger's length into the air. They grow in the dark, in the bitter cold when your breath stings your nose. They compete for shadow and for the little moisture there is. Salt migrates up through damp stone and blooms beside them, a pale crust, another kind of efflorescence. These are the closest thing to vegetation in the bowl — structures that grow, collapse, break off with a faint chime like a tiny bell, and are replaced before the next night.

When the sun hits them they do not melt. They sublimate. The thin, dry air takes them straight back to vapor. By the time you are squinting through a shaded hand, squinting against the glare off snow and ice lenses set into rock like eyes, they are gone, and the stones they grew on are, on the sunward face, bone dry.

What persists through the day lives lower and more hidden.

In the hollows where the ground dips and the wind cannot reach, water hides in pockets no bigger than a saucer. Melt pans, frozen over with a clear lid even at noon. A lens of ice trapped under a stone. Water that never runs. It freezes, it evaporates, it stays put. On the shaded north face of a block a bead of condensation will sit all day, cold and damp to the touch, while a step away in sun a black shard of volcanic glass is warm under your palm. A cloud will sometimes pass over the rim and for five minutes droplets form on everything, darkening the ash, then the sun burns it off within the hour. Twice a day the bowl changes state, and these small waters are the ones that endure, half-living under their lids.

Underneath all of it, densely, are the stones themselves, and they are not still.

The ground is crowded. You cannot take a step without stepping over, around, or between things: angular blocks the size of fists, shards of black volcanic glass that catch the sun and look wet, plates of pale rhyolite worn smooth, loose cinder crunching underfoot with a dry, granular squeak, sudden patches of fine sifting grit, a slab of clear ice frozen into a hollow. They lie in layers. In the lee of a boulder a drift of snow a hand's breadth deep, under it a crust, under the crust a tongue of ice, under that more stones. The rims are rounded. The slopes are at their resting angle. Everything is weathered into place.

And yet it is always moving. Frost heave lifts a stone a fraction at a time in the night and lets it settle at a new tilt. Daily freeze and thaw — brutal here, where the sun can warm a dark shard above freezing while the air around it stays bitter — works the cracks like a slow wedge. Ice creeps in the pockets, expanding the hollows it fills. At dusk, when the light goes oblique and everything is long and blue and the ice lenses glow from inside, the ticking starts as the whole surface cools at once: a sharp *ping* as a stone contracts and splits a hairline wider. Wind balances wind: what is stripped from the crest by the hiss over the rim, driving grains of snow and ice dust in a dense, low saltation a few centimeters off the ground, is laid down in the bowl on the lee side, scouring one face and depositing on the other. The surface is always arriving and departing, never quite the same, never changing its shape.

Scattered through this mineral cover are the other remains, the ones that did live here, briefly.

Low, semi-circular windbreaks made of uncut slabs fitted without mortar, crouching into the ground, their tops slumped and settled like old backs. A hut, or the idea of a hut, barely shoulder-high, open to the sky, its inner face blackened once by a fire lit only a few times. A line of small cairns, three or four stones high, spaced close together across the white, so you could follow them even in whiteout if you knew which way was forward. Tools left where they were dropped: a smooth maul for pecking, flakes struck from the black glass, a few shards of crude ceramic cracked by frost, pecked lines on a flat boulder too weathered to read. No metal to speak of — a single rusted nail, perhaps, in a wall.

Someone came up here with stone hammers and patience and tried to make a shelter from the mineral world. The mineral world has been slowly taking it back ever since, heaving the walls outward, filling the hollow with grit, frosting the hearth. They left, or they died, or the cold took the brief warmth of their fire. What they built is now being rearranged by the same frost and wind as everything else. Things here do not rot. They bleach, they crack, they freeze, they are ground down and lifted again. A footprint taken at dawn is filled with rime by the time you turn around.

So the bowl is inhabited, densely and quietly, by growth without living, by water that hides rather than flows, by stone that creeps and tilts and pings in the cooling, by light that bleaches anything exposed to paper white in weeks and throws back at itself off snow, by shadow that lies so hard and blue-black it looks like a hole. At midday it can feel almost warm on glass and unnaturally cold in the one step of shadow beside it. At dusk the cloud sea below turns gold, then grey, and the rim keeps its shadow circle complete long after the center has gone dark.

It is not a dead place. It is a place where the living has been subtracted, and what is left — ice growing like a garden, water in pockets, stone endlessly rearranged by cold — carries on, busy and silent, at the top of the world, in a room no bigger than a village, ringed by sky.
