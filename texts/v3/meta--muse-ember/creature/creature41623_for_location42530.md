First there are the lights moving.

Not people. Lights. A pair of them, shoulder to shoulder at the end of a cable, travelling down a canal that is not wide enough for two submersibles to pass. They dip when the carrier dips, rise when he rises, swing a little at the end of a step because the water will not let anything stop all at once. Behind the beam the dark closes immediately, as if it had never been opened. Ahead of it, for a second, you can see a wall, a hand on a rail, the knuckles white, and then only the light again.

They move the way everything moves down here. Slowly. Not from tiredness. From economy. A kick makes a weather that hangs after you. A clang travels out past the berm and far beyond it. A raised voice would be a loss of heat and a risk all at once. So they do not hurry, and they do not speak, except very close, breath to ear through the hiss of a regulator, or mouth to the inside of a helmet, and even then the words are few and damped.

The suited figures at the dock are the same figures, only at work. Heavy suits, patched and re-patched until the patching is the suit. Glass fogged at the edges. They unload a load that has come in across the berm on a cycle no one measures in days, and they do it by hand because hands are cheaper than machines and because touching the thing tells you its weight and its temperature and whether it is leaking. They pass things to each other without looking, by feel, and tie everything down with cord that has been tied down before. Nothing is dropped here if it can be helped. A dropped thing does not fall. It drifts, very slowly, onto a roof or out onto the silt, and is lost to the town's careful inventory of heat and metal and light.

Inside, when the suits come off, they are quieter still.

The flooded ground floors are living rooms as much as engine rooms. Ten degrees near the engines, five by the portholes where they bother to heat them. Families — if that is the word they use — live in layers against the warm walls, in the lee of a smelter, above a battery cradle, sleeping in shifts around the pipes that carry waste heat through the floor. Closeness is not affection here, though affection grows out of it. Closeness is warmth. Two bodies in one suit, two bodies in one bunk, a child tucked between the adults against a main conduit so that the thump-thump that passes through the whole hamlet in a second becomes, for the child, proof that the world is still running. They hold rails and conduits the way surface people hold a banister, not for support but for information. A pump starting at the north end registers at the south. You can feel it in the sternum, in the palm, in the teeth. They navigate by it more than by sight.

Their bodies have learned the place. Skin kept soft and wrinkled by water that never gets warm enough to be kind and never gets cold enough, inside, to kill. Hair cut short, not for fashion but because long hair trails and tangles and cools. Eyes large and slow to blink in the amber, children carrying lamps bigger than they are, the brightest thing in any room, the light fading behind them to absolute black in a few metres. The old ones go partly blind from it and stop carrying lights at all. They become Listeners. They sit with a palm flat against the main pipe for hours, sometimes for years in the practical sense, feeling the pump's cycle change with temperature, with load, with somebody new arriving. They are guided from room to room by children who tug lightly on a sleeve and then let go, trusting the hand to find the rail.

Communication is mostly touch. A tap on metal, a pressure put through a pipe that no one outside the berm would hear as anything but a thud. A hand traced slowly across a back, map-like, to tell a story. On the rarest occasions, when the pumps are at their lowest sigh, a voice, low and hoarse from disuse, from close enough that the damp of the breath is felt on the skin. Words are expensive. They are spent like light.

Work is done in the basements and the backs, where it is warmest. Smelters, battery cradles, vats where replacement parts are grown chemically and slowly, where food is grown more slowly still. They refine minerals from seawater and stack them in containers in the west quarter, floor to ceiling, and the containers are never shipped out in any quantity that matters. An outsider would call it pointless. Inside it makes sense in the way a heartbeat makes sense. The work gives the heat somewhere to go. The rhythm gives the hands something to do while the pumps cycle. A person who has a task does not waste heat worrying. A person who tends a vat for a lifetime knows that vat's particular bubble, its particular smell of warm oil and ozone, the way it changes when the current worries the berm.

Food is honest and sparse. Mats of algae thicken in the warm outflow channels behind the factories, green to rusty brown, and are scraped and eaten and fed to the vats in turn. Bacteria bloom on rust and turn it back into something else that can be eaten by other things, and the town encourages them the way farmers encourage any crop, by keeping the water slow and warm enough. In the cones of the work lights the small translucent shrimp gather, invisible until they move, picking at what falls. The people sweep them gently aside, or sweep them in. They are not quite livestock and not quite wild. They clean the light and the light keeps them.

The larger life is kept at a distance and allowed its margins. Outside the berm, in the black, blind fish hover, silvery and waiting, and do not enter. The town's lights and its warmer water are not for them, and the people, without quite knowing why, are glad of the boundary. Once a year, or once in some longer cycle, a larger shadow passes at the very limit of vision, too far to name, too indifferent to threaten. When it passes the pumps seem to sigh lower and the lamp-carriers walk slower, and no one speaks of it afterward except by placing a hand a little longer on a pipe. In the east canal a solitary crab keeps a culvert, moving with terrible slowness, and is left alone by a kind of consensus that is older than any of the current children. Children leave flakes of rust near its doorway. No one says why.

Children are born in the warm flooded rooms, and learn to swim before they learn to walk, and think water is air and air — that thin, imagined thing beyond the portholes in the container stencils — is a story for when you are being taught taps. They do not know the sun. They have never seen a sky that is not black, and they do not miss it because there is nothing in their vocabulary for missing. Their first play is to carry a lamp too large for them down a canal and watch the dark close behind them. Their first work is to tie things down so they will not drift. Their first prayer, if it can be called that, is to lay a palm on a warm pipe before sleep and feel the town's pulse travel the whole hamlet and come back to them.

Arrival is rare. A submersible comes in across the berm, a pair of small moons that you see leave and do not see return, and unloads a few bundled figures, or a few crates, or nothing much that can be named. The newcomers learn the rule without being told it in so many words, because the rule is in the bodies around them: do not waste heat, do not waste light, do not make a noise that will travel, do not leave the berm without a reason, do not drop anything you cannot retrieve. They learn to breathe shallowly, to move slowly, to share a bunk and a suit and a breath loop. In time their movements match the others' and their hands find the rails without looking.

Departure is rarer. Sometimes a figure who has been there long enough that the suit is mostly patchwork will go out with a crew and not come back, either because the work took them beyond the berm or because they chose, in the way things are chosen here, to become part of the outside dark. The town does not mourn loudly. It cannot afford the noise or the heat. When someone dies inside, the body is kept warm against a wall for one more full cycle of the pumps, so that its heat is not wasted, and then is carried out, slowly, to the berm, and settled onto the silt where the blind fish will find it in their own time. It becomes geology. It becomes food for bacteria. It becomes part of the slow accounting.

And so they go on. Not fallen, exactly. Settled. A machine left running that has grown hands to tend it, and hands that have grown quiet enough to tend each other. The warehouse workers who grow replacement parts for parts that grow replacement parts, the lamp tenders who trim the amber portholes so they blink in patterns that are not quite signals and not quite not-signals, the Listeners who know by touch whether the north pump is tired, the children who think the black beyond the light is simply the rest of the room, the shrimp in the cones, the crab in the culvert, the algae that only lives where the plumbing gives it permission, the bacteria turning rust into life — all of them held together by pipes that pulse, by walls that share warmth, by a silence that is not emptiness but activity muffled and made careful.

They call the place Hushport when they call it anything at all, and they call it that rarely, because naming a thing makes it a little larger than it needs to be. Inside the berm, the lights continue in their amber and blue patterns. The crane turns its small arc and turns back, on a timer set before anyone now living there was born, and no one stops it. The marine snow falls through the cones of light and out the other side into black. The people move shoulder to shoulder down the canals, lamps swinging, hands on rails, feeling the town think through its metal, keeping each other warm in the cold that occupies everything and holds it all, finally, together.
