If you stand still for long enough, the place stops looking empty.

It does not fill with a rush, or a call, or a shape you can point to. It fills the way damp fills — from the edges of things, in the seams. First there is only the warm dark and the drip of condensation and the soft giving of the litter under your feet. Then, slowly, the litter begins to move.

Not much. A springtail the color of ash flicks itself a millimeter over a blackened leaf and is still again. A mite, oribatid and armored, walks with absurd slowness across a fungal mat, grazing as it goes, its whole life measured in the width of a fingerprint. Under the same leaf, in the thin film of water that never quite evaporates, nematodes thread and unthread themselves, and rotifers in a moss cushion contract and expand, pumping. None of this is visible unless you look for it, and even then it is mostly outline: something small, deciding to be elsewhere.

That is how everything here lives — by deciding, at the lowest possible cost.

Lift a fallen trunk, the punky one lying where it fell years ago, and the scale changes without changing its quiet. The wood is not wood anymore but a soaked architecture. Termites have papered its interior with pale galleries, ventilated, repaired, kept at a humidity and temperature they themselves maintain, a nursery built out of chewed fiber and kept warm by their own bodies. Along a seam where the bark has sloughed, an ant column runs — not fast, never fast, but unbroken. Workers carry leaf-fragments and grains and larvae and each other, moving in one direction on one side of the path, the other on the other, a slow conveyor of mandibles and legs that does not pause for you. In the damp grooves millipedes curl and uncurl in twos and threes, isopods graze the fungal skin in a line, a slug leaves a single bright trail of slime over a shelf fungus and follows it back. Beetle larvae as big as grains of rice work the softened interior, chewing and voiding, turning solid into frass, frass into soil. A centipede, flattened and unhurried, patrols the same log for anything smaller than itself. Nothing here hunts in a dramatic way. Everything eats, and is eaten in its turn, at a pace set by heat.

The inhabitants are not organized around a center. They are organized around surfaces.

The bark of every standing trunk is a pasture. Algae and liverworts and thin lichens green the wet side; barklice graze them in little herds, pausing, grazing, moving an antenna's length. Caterpillars flattened against the bark do the same, camouflaged to the color of damp wood. Small tree frogs arrive at dusk of the mind — there is no true dusk here, only a thinning — and sit with their toe-pads spread flat, absorbing water through their skin, listening. Their call, when it comes, is muffled as if through cloth, one note from a root hollow, answered distantly from another pool, and then nothing for a long while. Above them, in the lianas lashing branch to branch, other frogs, other ants, other caterpillars occupy the same vertical intervals, each in its own layer, each touching what it climbs. Epiphytes crowd the forks: bromeliads cupping rainwater in their centers, orchids rooting in a mat of moss, ferns tucked into bark crevices. Inside a single bromeliad tank is another world entire — mosquito larvae wriggling, tadpoles of a frog that breeds nowhere else, protists, bacteria, the slow fall of leaf-dust to feed them all. The plant does not know it is a pond. The animals do not know they are in a plant.

Higher still, the production floor runs. The canopy leaves are broad and glossy and dark, built to an economy of leaked light. They are eaten where they hang, quietly, by caterpillars that are green on green, by beetles that skeletonize a leaf edge, by birds whose presence you register as a soft tick filtered down through three layers of foliage. The birds are here, in number, but they are not loud. Heat and density have taxed sound out of them. They move short distances, glean, preen, sit. Nothing calls just to be heard. Below them the middle layer is a tangle made habitable: saplings holding out one leaf at a time, palms throwing out fronds that will fall and make a shelter on the floor for a season, torn ferns unfurling at geological patience. Each leaf that is made will fall, and each fall is a delivery.

What falls is received.

On the floor, aroids spread flat and wide, gingers push up a single shoot, seedlings make a leaf, wait, make another. They are not competing dramatically for a patch of sun because there is no patch of sun to compete for. They are waiting in the rationed light, growing at the metabolic rate of shade. When their leaves drop — green, then brown, then black, then indistinguishable — they join the litter, which is less a layer than a population. A fresh leaf on top is whole and faintly green; underneath it one is brown and still whole; underneath that, black and perforated; underneath that, lace. Springtails, mites, fungus-grazing fly larvae, beetle larvae, termites, ants, snails, earthworms from below pushing up casts — all work the same leaf at the same time, from different sides, for different parts. The fungi do the chemistry no animal gut can do alone, secreting enzymes that dissolve lignin, turning the indigestible into the edible, then being eaten themselves by the grazers who cannot eat the leaf directly. Inside the guts of termites and millipedes and beetle larvae, fermentation vats bubble with bacteria, extracting energy from material locked away for seasons. What one animal cannot use, the next can. What none can use, the fungi take down to humus.

And under the humus, the soil itself is alive in the way a slow animal is alive. Earthworms turn it a grain at a time. Enchytraeids thread through it white and blind. Nematodes crowd the water films between particles. Roots from trunks and saplings and palms ramify through it all, wrapped in mycorrhizal fungi, trading sugars made far above for minerals and water, the plumbing and cable of the system running invisibly at ankle depth. Fallen trunks, shelf fungi, moss, liverworts — nothing lies bare, and nothing lies uninhabited. A trunk that fell a century ago is still a trunk in outline, but its interior is a city of galleries, a pasture of fungi, a highway for ants, a sponge that holds rain and releases it as humidity to the air that never dries.

In the tannin-dark pools cupped by roots, filmed with spores, the inhabitants are mostly microbial and amphibian. Bacteria and protists cloud the warm still water. Fungal spores ride the surface. A frog's tadpoles graze the film; a frog's feet ring the mud. When rain comes — brief, heavy, soaking the litter — the pools rise a fraction, the films break, the whole system drinks and then exhales again into the saturated air. Midges and gnats hang in the leaked light not so much flying as suspended, waiting for anything warm to pass. Sweat on your skin does not evaporate; to a mosquito that is an invitation, to a midge a weather pattern. The air itself is a habitat, dim and thick, and everything breathing it pays the same heat tax: move less, move in lines, move at night if you can manage it, conserve.

There are larger lives, in the sense that you can see them without kneeling. A slender tree snake, moving at the pace of a thought along a liana. A small rodent or marsupial, mostly nocturnal in habit even in a place without night, rustling the litter for seeds and insects and then going still. A bird in the canopy far above, ticking softly. But nothing here is large enough to be a spectacle, and nothing behaves as though it were. Predation is distributed, small mouths on small bodies, a spider in a curled leaf, a predatory beetle in the leaf mold, a frog eating a midge that ate a fungus that ate a leaf. The food web is not a pyramid. It is a mat.

That is the character of whoever lives here: they are packed, not ranked. For every leaf overhead there are a dozen mouths somewhere in the system eating it, or eating what ate it, or eating the fungi that were eating it. They touch everything because everything is touching everything — leaf touches leaf, vine touches trunk, root touches fungus, ant touches leaf fragment touches ant. To step off the litter, to press your back to a trunk, to set your hand down, is to enter a neighborhood at its own scale, to be noticed immediately and then, just as immediately, ignored, if you do not move.

And they are quiet because quiet is cheaper. The vegetation eats sound the way it eats light. A footfall is swallowed. A drip lands on a large leaf, runs off, lands again. Mandibles grind almost inaudibly in the litter. A beetle ticks inside wood. An ant column rustles over dry leaf for a second and is gone. Silence here is not absence. It is muffling — the accumulated effect of millions of small, slow, warm bodies working in a wet, soft, enclosed space.

They have lived this way for so long that the place has taken their shape. There is no season to reset them, no cool spell to thin them, no dry interval to expose them. Only a constant heat, a constant dimness, and the small pulses of rain and leaf-fall cycling through. A tree lives for centuries and rots for centuries after, inhabited the whole time. A leaf takes seasons to become soil, and the soil becomes the deep, settled profile under your feet, alive all the way down. Change happens the way erosion happens: gradually, in balance.

If you stay, you begin to time yourself to them — your breathing slower in the thick air, your movements smaller. The air closes warm around you. The dark holds. Underfoot, the litter gives and gives. Above, the ceiling of leaves goes on past valley after valley, and the work goes on under it: feeding, grinding, carrying, rotting, growing, in lines and layers, with no beginning in sight and no end worth naming.
