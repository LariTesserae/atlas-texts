Life in Vesskar does not present itself. If you wait on the Sill long enough, watching for movement, you begin to understand that what moves down there is not meant to be seen from above. The basin is too open, too wet, too loud for anything to hide, and yet almost everything that lives in it has learned to be incidental — to live in the tolerances of the machines, in the warm damp the city makes for itself, at the edges of the places people have built for themselves.

The people are the first thing you will find, and even they are sparse. The Terraces hold them in six-storey blocks of basalt-faced concrete, widely spaced, so that between one building and the next there is mostly empty avenue, hardstand, gravel, wet pavement that never dries. A block that looks as if it should hold a hundred households holds far fewer, and some floors are shuttered, their basalt darkened by years of runoff, their balconies empty of laundry. Those who live here live here for a long time. They are not passing through. The stairwells are wet underfoot, the handrails warm from the pipes that run behind the walls, and the air on the landings is close and mineral, smelling of hot water and damp concrete. In the mornings the steam lies at knee height and does not lift, and people step through it in shirtsleeves, their breath fogging faintly in the humidity despite the warmth. They hang laundry on the small balconies and it steams next to the washing steam, never quite crisp, always a little soft at the edges. In window boxes there are the meh plantings — moss, small ferns, a hardy herb or two that can stand the drip from above and the heat from below — tolerated rather than tended, the way everything green is tolerated here.

They are born with ear-plugs. Not metaphorically. A child in Vesskar is given a set early, on a string, and wears them around the neck like beadwork, like a piece of jewelry that is also a tool. They put them in when they go down to the Spine, or even only to cross the centre where the roar is deafening, and take them out at home, and put them back in again, all their lives. The plugs are different — some foam, some wax, some old hard rubber worn smooth — and a person may keep the same pair for decades, string replaced, colour faded. Because they are half-deafened even on the Terraces, where the hum is still loud enough to shiver puddles, they speak quietly outdoors, in low indoor voices, as if not to waste sound that will be lost anyway. In the working districts shouting is a formality; conversation is done with signs and written notes and shrugs and a hand on a shoulder. A child crossing the empty avenue between blocks with a paper note tucked in a pocket is an ordinary sight, and the note is not an emergency. It may say dinner is at six, or the pump below the stair is leaking again, or come down when the lull comes. Children learn to read and write early, and to carry messages the way other children carry balls. No one hurries. There is nowhere in the basin that is far, in walking terms, and yet distances feel large because of the emptiness between them and the slow way the air hangs.

Down in the Spine and the Yards the same people live a second life, by shifts. They tend the turbines in the gorge, the pumps hammering water through pipes under the streets, the fans and conveyors and incline trains. They know the machines less by sound — sound is useless there, a wall — than by vibration through the soles of their boots and into their teeth, by the heat on their hands when they lay a palm on a casing, by smell: wet stone, hot metal, ozone, sulfur. They work in the warmth that rises off everything, shirtsleeved even when the flat grey-white light makes the high pass look cold, their skin damp, their clothes never quite dry. A lull, when a pump rests or a conveyor stops for minutes, thins the roar but does not end it, and the puddles keep shivering. Some of them will go deaf by degrees, not all at once, a ringing settling in when the hum finally stops for the night. The old ones sit on balconies and on the warm handrails at the top of the stairs, watching the avenues stay empty, and they are the ones who remember when there were more people and less water standing in the flats. They have settled, a few careful centimetres, like the foundations.

They keep no pets in the central districts. Steam and sound are unkind to them, and the narrow balconies are already full of laundry and plantings. Pigeons, who might be expected in any city, are absent altogether, as if the constant drip from above and the heat from below and the noise from all around have voted them out. In the absence of pets and pigeons, the animals that do live here belong to the city in a different way.

In the warm ducting of a boiler house, tucked into a bend where the metal stays warm through the night, there is a nest of grey finches. They are functionally deaf, or they have made themselves so by living a whole life inside the roar. The noise that drives everything else out does not drive them out; they build with copper wire and string, stolen in fine strands from the looms of the plant, woven into the insulation. They breed without regard to season because there is no season to regard, only warmth and wet and light at midday, flat and grey-white. You will not see them from the Sill. You will see them if you are on shift, flitting in a small grey arc between one warm vent and another, alighting on a beam beading with condensation, shaking water from their feathers. They eat nothing much that you can name — midges in the steam, seeds carried in on boots from outside the basin — and they show no fear of people, because people are also just another warm, moving surface.

Under the Spine, in a culvert where the sluggish flow runs pale and mineral-bright, there is a blind newt. It has never seen light. It lives its whole life in a few metres of dark water, pale as the silt it rests on, breathing slowly, feeding on the larvae that gather where the warm outflow cools enough to be survivable. No one owns it, no one feeds it. It was there before the culvert was cleaned last, and it will be there after, because the culvert is always wet and always warm and always running, in the patient increments of the place. Its eyes closed over generations ago, or it was born without them needing to open; down there, seeing is not a sense the place rewards.

Where that cooler outflow meets the marsh flats at the basin’s edge, there are small fish. You will see them only as flecks, if you watch long enough, moving in the sheet of water that stands at the surface and reflects the towers in ripples that never settle. They stay where the water is still and not too chemical, where the reeds have taken hold, and they dart and pause and dart again, silver for an instant. They are not kept. They are not fished. They belong to the margin, to the place where the city gives up on being a city and lets water stand where it wants to.

At night the Yards belong to moths. They gather thickly around the sodium lights, in clouds so dense the lights look furred, and they are eaten by nothing. No finch comes for them, no bat finds them in the steam, no spider has built far enough out into the hardstand to reach. They live their short, confused lives bumping against warm glass beading with moisture, wings shedding into the gravel, and by morning there is a pale dusting under each lamp that the rain — the city’s own rain, falling again from its own plumes — washes into the gutters. People walking home from shift pass under them without looking up, ear-plugs in, shirtsleeves damp, going to blocks where the stairs are wet and the handrails warm.

The rest of life is smaller and less animal, and perhaps more at home. On north walls, where the spray hits and the sun comes flat, black-green lichen spreads in maps no one reads. In warm puddles that never dry, orange algae blooms in slick mineral-bright sheets. In gutters where runoff slows, tufts of salt grass and sedge push up, glossy and stunted, their roots holding the silt. In the marsh flats, reeds stand in the shallow water, unmoving except when a pump somewhere below sends a shiver through the whole basin and they tremble in sympathy with the puddles. A few plane trees have been planted in square concrete pits in a terrace plaza. They survive, leaves shining with condensation, never really thriving, their trunks wrapped against the heat of the pipes below. Bacteria live in the warm water, in the scale inside boilers, in the orange silt that runs down the basalt road-cuts. Moss lives in the joints of the paving. Fungi freckle the underside of the cooling towers where the plumes hang and cool and condense. None of this is encouraged. All of it is tolerated, the way the people tolerate the damp in their basements and the hum in their teeth, the way the city tolerates itself wearing into shallow bowls that collect water exactly where the water always wanted to go.

That is the arrangement. The people did not drive the other lives out, and the other lives did not ask for room. The finches took the warmth, the newt took the dark, the fish took the margin, the moths took the lights, the lichen took the north wall, and the people took the Terraces and the Spine and learned to carry notes across empty avenues and to wear their silence around their necks. Everything lives slowly here, in long patient increments — a tank filling over weeks, rust penetrating by millimetres a year, a child growing up and taking the same stair, a newt growing old in a culvert, a finch building again with wire in the same warm duct. It does not grow fast. It does not die fast either. It weeps, it hums, it steams, it drips, and in the wet and the warm and the endless, ordinary roar, it lives.
