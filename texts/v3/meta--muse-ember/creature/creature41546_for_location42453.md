Almost nothing lives here, and what does will not satisfy the shape of the question. There is no resident in the way a reef has residents, no animal turning in the midwater, nothing darting, grazing, hunting, hiding, migrating, or calling. The loudness is all water and rock. The flashes at night are not eyes or glow. If you are looking for a *who*, you will keep looking past the only things that are there.

What is there is plant, and only just.

On the spires — the few places where the ground succeeds, for a while, in being ground — there are strands of algae.

They are not green. In this clear, high, cold light they are dark almost to black, black-red where the sun touches them directly, a light-drinking color that seems to take the blue out of the water beside them and make it brighter by contrast. Each strand is unbranched and ribbon-thin, kelp-like without being kelp, a few meters long at most, anchored not in soil, for there is none, but in the small cracks of newborn stone. Basalt still glassy, chalkier stone still crumbly: the algae take whatever purchase a fresh seam will give them before the seam sighs, cracks wider, and edits itself away.

They live entirely at the mercy of the current, which is also the only weather they will ever know. When a gyre leans against a spire, the colony streams out like a flag, every ribbon aligned, pressed flat, trembling at the edges in the turbulent flow. When the current pauses — and it pauses only locally, only briefly, while the great fronts grind on elsewhere — they go limp. They hang. They do not curl toward light or away from it. Then a different current arrives from a different quarter, perhaps hours later, and the same ribbons stream again in a new direction, as if the colony had changed its mind about the world. It has not. It cannot. Its only movement is growth, and otherwise this imposed streaming and hanging, again and again.

That is, for a spire colony, a life. Growth along the crack while the stone holds. Tearing, when it doesn't. A spire that has risen thirty meters can slump sideways without storm or earthquake to blame, dissolving back into silt that never quite settles. The algae anchored to it do not swim from the ruin. They tear loose with it, or are left for a season on a hill that swells, brightens along a seam, and flattens. They have no defense against this because nothing here has ever needed a defense against them. Nothing eats them. Nothing has evolved to want them. They are not camouflaged, not thorned, not toxic, not hidden. There is nothing to hide *from*, and nothing to hide *in* them.

Away from the spires, on the luminous patches of the plain itself, there is the other form, if it can be called a form.

Low mats, rootless. They do not grow on the bottom — the bottom is an unfinished highland, still being put together and un-put — they hover above it, photosynthesizing in the filtered sun that reaches all the way down here because the plateau is high and the water is clear to a degree that makes emptiness a substance. They lift and sink in slow, independent clouds, one mat rising while its neighbor, a hundred meters away, is sinking, neither quite landing, neither quite rising out of the light. Over days a mat may drift with a gyre, stretch thin, thicken again. It never roots. It never becomes a place other things could live on. It is a cloud doing the minimum work of being alive: taking light, making the chemistry of life, and making it for no one. The oxygen, the long chemical signature of photosynthesis, accumulates in a water where nothing will ever breathe it.

Between the spire colonies and the plain mats there is, to all purposes, no relationship. Colonies are separated by hundreds of kilometers of nothing. A strand on one spire will never know a mat has lifted and faded on the plain, and the mat will never cast a shadow on a strand. They do not meet, compete, feed, or shelter one another. They are not a community. They are two sparse habits of the same plant, repeated wherever light and a pause in the current briefly agree.

And they end, as they live, without an event.

Nothing hunts them, so they are not killed. They grow until the current is stronger than their hold, or the stone beneath them gives way, or they simply tear — a ribbon parting, a mat thinning at the edges — and then they drift. Far into the empty blue, far beyond the spire that made them, far beyond the patch of plain they hovered over, they go on streaming and hanging, growing at the slowing pace of cold water, getting thinner against the light. Eventually they fade. Not sink, not rot into a visible carcass, not fall as snow to feed something below. Fade is the exact word the place insists on: they become less and less, until the clear water around them is clear again and there is no mark that they were ever there except that the blue is, for a moment, very slightly darker where they were.

That is the entire food web. Grow. Stream. Hang limp. Tear loose. Drift. Fade.

It is a life lived in a loud, computing, collapsing, reforming ocean, in perfect solitude, by organisms that do not move toward anything and are not moved toward by anything else — dark ribbons on stones that will not last, and dark clouds over a plain that will not hold them, with only distance between presences, and distance, again, between one of them and the next.
