Before you see a person, you hear the lives that were already awake.

Frogs first. Glassy and green, they live in the north drain where the water never really stops moving, and in every gutter that becomes a stream by four. At dusk they start — one, then a dozen, then a chorus so complete it erases conversation, sound bouncing off the three rock walls and back into the bowl. By day they are pressed flat against damp stone and moss, translucent enough to see a dark heart working. That is the background noise of Tinta, the sound the town measures time by. People do not silence them. They speak louder, shop louder, loom louder, and wait for the frogs to take over again in the evening.

Under them, in the felt itself, are the smaller tenants. The inches of moss, liverwort, root mat and rotting leaf that you step on are not ground, they are housing. Snails travel it in slow silver lines up wet walls, leaving a map of where they have been to eat a begonia or an orchid root. Beetles work the rotting logs laid under houses, clicking in the dark, turning old platform wood into soil for new platforms. Shrews run the thatch and the felt at night — too fast to see properly, quick and blind and hungry — and mice nest in the roof mats with shredded moss, keeping the thatch from ever being quite dead. Dragonflies patrol the aqueducts, hovering at the dripping bamboo joints, picking off the mosquitoes the ducks miss. Nothing here lives alone, and nothing lives far from water for long.

Then, in the warm dark under the living floors, the domestic animals who make a house a house.

A Tinta house is built up around them. The lowest storey is not storage, it is a pen in the felt, open to the rain only through a crack in the woven wall. Pigs live there, black and wet and enormous in the small space, snorting against the living poles of the house and keeping the floor above them warm. Guinea pigs popcorn and squeak in their own smaller hollows, hiding under husks, fed greens and tubers by children who crouch to reach them. Chickens scratch and cluck in piles of husk and leaf, laying brown eggs on moss. In the morning you do not wake to a rooster on a roof; you wake to the pigs shifting under your bed-platform, the guinea pigs complaining, the chickens already working the felt outside the door.

The people live in the middle, in the woven, breathing part. They are a minority in their own town, by weight and by number, and they move like carriers rather than owners.

They wake with dew beaded on everything — on their wool ponchos, on their hair, on the looms set in the doorways. The ponchos never fully dry. They pull them tighter against the cold that drops fast after sunset, smell of smoke and wet wool and green wood hanging low around their shoulders. Their legs are strong from the terrace-steps, their hands rough from bamboo knives and iron hoes and split willow. They are organized not by street but by flow, and they talk about themselves that way: the upper drain people are first up, because the communal bathhouse and washhouse and the stone-and-wood shed with the waterwheel are theirs to open. The miller — usually a woman who knows the wooden screw by its jam, who feeds maize and tubers into it in handfuls and listens for the grind under the roar of water — lives there in practice if not in body, because she is there before most have had tea of boiled tubers. The sluice keepers walk the bamboo aqueducts all morning, lashing a dripping joint, clearing a silted settling pit of gravel and moss.

The lower drain people live with the ducks. In the morning the ducks are herded — quacking, in a shuffling line that children learn to steer with a stick and a call — along the lanes that will be canals by afternoon, down to the ponds that form in the lower terraces after rain. There they eat mosquito larvae and stray greens and do the town’s quiet pest work. They come back at dusk the same way, damp and cross. The weeping rock people live closest to where the springs bead out of the cliff, where their woven walls are always dark with damp and their planter troughs are always full. Their orchids do best. Their children learn the names of mosses first.

Everyone farms in the same spiral. Terraces that are also lanes, lanes that are also drains. Old men sit for hours on a dry platform in the market clearing remembering which terrace slipped in which night’s rain, which hollow the frogs took over after the retaining weave gave way with a soft sigh. Their memory is a ledger of soil. Young families will clear an abandoned hummock of moss and fern because the soil remembers tubers, plant oca and potatoes and squash and beans in the same step that a frog will later use as a nursery. They carry baskets down the steps all day, up and down, shouting over the waterwheel and the looms and the bells from the pack mules — animals who live in Tinta between trips over the pass, stamping in their pen, bells quiet when they rest, loud when they are loaded and start down the spirals again.

Children are the most constant wild presence. Barefoot on the felt because sandals fill with water, they move between worlds without noticing a boundary. They net the small, speckled cold-water fish from the settlement drains with woven scoops before the afternoon rain makes the current too fast and washes them away. They chase dragonflies they will never catch. They uncurl a fern from where a roof drip falls because it is a satisfying thing to do and because the house needs that root to hold. They feed the guinea pigs, herd the ducks, carry water in small bamboo cups from a sluice to a chicken, and get sent to hang and re-hang the market partitions when the wind changes. They learn by carrying.

The women at the looms in the doorways work through most of the noise. Wooden looms clacking, weaving ponchos and blankets that will be damp by evening and smelled of smoke by morning. Moths crowd the oil lamps in the market at night, drawn to the light in the clearing, and a few will get into the wool before it is folded away. The women know which moths eat and which just burn.

And all around them, the visitors who are also residents. Hummingbirds are constant at the planter troughs built under the eaves, iridescent, furiously territorial, chasing each other from rain-wet fern to rain-wet fern, their thumbs hanging in the air where the roof drips. Tanagers and other mountain birds call from the forest wall that closes the fourth side of the bowl and will sometimes land, bold as thieves, on a drying rack of potatoes or greens, taking a taste before the household shouts them off. Bats come out of the granary eaves at dusk — the granary raised on stone feet in the driest corner of each terrace cluster — and sweep low over the market lamps, taking moths and mosquitoes in the same pass. At dawn the mist comes up to eye level and everything is beaded again, and the whole population, human and animal and plant, shakes itself off and starts the work of diverting, re-weaving, replanting, carrying.

They live this way because the materials enforce it. A wall that is not growing is already a garden. Dead thatch goes onto the beds, dead wood into the felt, bones into the settling pit where moss and microbes break them down into next year’s fill. Rot is not failure; it is the rate at which Tinta makes soil. A house leans a degree more each year as its living poles grow unevenly, and is propped and re-woven until it leans back. A lane is a pond in the wettest month and a stall in the drier weeks. A terrace is a crop and a drain and a path and a frog habitat in the same step.

In the evening the rain stops as if cut, the frogs take over, ducks settle in their pens, pigs sigh under the floor, bats go out, lamps are lit in small warm circles, and a loom may still be going somewhere in the mist. From the ridge above, it does not look like a town at all. It looks like a patch of particularly busy, particularly alive forest that learned to build itself into houses — and the people inside it, damp ponchos pulled tight, listening to the water dripping off the aqueducts onto moss, are just one of its more talkative species.
