Nothing lives here the way a town is supposed to be lived in. No one breathes in the pods. No one walks the catwalks. The portholes that still glow do it for no eyes, and the lamps burn in their spaced rows for no one to read by.

What lives here has had to learn to live in sound first.

The roar is not background to them, it is weather, it is ground, it is the way a bird feels wind. The low continuous bellow of the turbines and pumps, the higher shriek elsewhere, the irregular hammering of that press cycling every forty-seven seconds on nothing — all of it saturates the water so completely that an animal cannot separate a signal from a wall. So they do not try. Their skin is a speaker. Their lateral lines, their gills, the soft bellies of the shrimp, the packed silt in a fish's gut — everything vibrates. They navigate, if they navigate at all, by the pressure pulse that rolls through the whole chamber every ninety seconds or so, water contracting and expanding a fraction around everything, making chains tick against pipes and lamps sway on their piers. To them that is not an interruption. That is a heartbeat. A clock. They pause for it, then go on.

They also live in heat and chemistry before they live in light, because there is essentially no light to live in.

Start at the smallest scale, on the things themselves. On a copper wire fisted thick, running through the water like a slow root, there is already a fur. On a pipe as thick as a thigh, on the scoured side of a pier foot where the current is fastest, on the inside of a valve rim, a film is growing — bacteria eating what the machine sheds. Iron-oxidizers where rust breaks down, sulfur-oxidizers where the brine is richest, copper-tolerant things in the lee of the wiring. They are not green. Nothing here can be green. In the amber cones of the lamps they are grey-white, slimy, almost alive, and only in the quietest corners behind baffles, where the turbulence drops enough that a mat can hold together, do they gather in any thickness. There they smell faintly of a cellar and a hot spring at once. They precipitate. That is most of what they do.

Because there are no plants, the place grows stone the way a forest grows leaves, and those mineral gardens are their architecture and their food and their graveyard all at once. Silica in needles and fans catching the lamp light. Sulfur in pale yellow rosettes. Iron oxide in dark, shrubby tufts that snap if you brush them — colonies, really, that have hardened their own waste into a thicket. A shrimp can pick at the tuft. A fish can hide the line of its body behind a fan. Bacteria live inside the rosette and make more of it, slowly, year after year, in the shelter of pipes and pier feet. The city slumped a little into the flow; the living crust slumps with it and finds its balance.

Zonation is everything, because the chamber is not one water but many weathers stacked inside each other. Near the basin in the middle, where the heat exchangers boil locally and clouds of fine bubbles spiral and shear instead of rising straight, it scalds. Only the thermophiles can be there, the archaea in the plume itself, riding the upwelling in the centre of the permanent convection cell, invisible even when they are thickest, making the heat shimmer look alive. A little further out, in the fast lanes that follow the same canals between the factory blocks year after year, the water is cleansed by turbulence and almost nothing can settle. Surfaces there are scoured bright. Life is scoured off.

Then, at the outer wall, where the hot water has spread at the top, cooled against rock and concrete, and returns along the floor in a comfortable, steaming bath, it becomes habitable in the animal sense. That is where the edges are. That is where the sound drops, very slightly, enough to think, and something can cross a lamp cone.

You may swim for an hour in roar and pressure pulses without seeing it. Then it is there for a second: a blind fish, long and unpigmented, riding the return current along the floor. It has no eyes to speak of — the sockets healed over with skin — and no pigment in its flesh because pigment would be a waste in a place with no sun to hide from. Translucent, eel-long, it uses the rhythm of the turbines the way a bird uses wind, letting the fast lane in a sunken canal carry it between blocks, turning where the lane turns, never fighting it. It tastes the water as it goes: copper, hot stone, faint sulfur at the back of its throat. It is not hunting so much as tasting and vibrating its way along the terraces, keeping to the cooler return, avoiding the basin. It spawns, perhaps, into that same return. The eggs sort themselves like heavy things into the channels and stay there, in the silt with everything else that has sunk.

The shrimp are more resident, and more numerous, though numerous here still means almost nothing per volume. In the intake screens, where the warm outflow is steady and the fungal film gathers, translucent shrimp the size of a thumb pick at the mat with their forelegs, gills working steadily in the heat. Their shells are clear enough to see the dark line of the gut through them. They cling when the pressure pulse comes, and shed their exoand lipus skins into the eddies behind the piers. In those permanent eddies — where a dropped glove, a length of insulation, a plastic crate will circle for years slowly coming apart — amphipods and other small crusts live on the debris itself, grazing it, housing themselves in it as it rots. Light things do not sink; they orbit. The town's junk has become littoral.

There are bivalves, small and sparse, clustered on the cooler side of a pier foot, filtering the silt that the current drops. There are copepods and ostracods drifting in the empty water between lamps, too small to see except as glitter in a cone of light, like the suspended particles themselves. All of them are pale. All of them are slow except when the current decides for them. None of them needs to come up for air. None of them has ever seen a surface. They have evolved in a pocket no larger than a great warehouse, drowned its entire life, and they have become as much a part of the machine's weathering as the softened concrete and the rusted steel: they live in the dips worn into stair edges, in the sheltered white stencils — LEVEL 3, INTAKE, KEEP CLEAR — under their grey film, in the sagged permanent arcs of rubber hoses.

Once, very rarely, something larger passes through the high, empty water between the central vortex and the wall. Too fast and too far to identify. It bends the heat shimmer as it goes. It might be a fish from far deeper in the geothermal system, come in through an intake pipe and not liking the sound, or a blind catfish grown huge in a dark aquifer beyond the concrete, curious for a second and then gone. It does not live here. Nothing large can. There is not enough food, not enough quiet, not enough cool water to sustain a predator, and the water is too chemical and too loud for anything to want to try. They do not live here so much as they pass through the edges of here, in the pockets where the hammering drops enough to think.

So the population, if you can call it that, is marginal and mineral and almost invisible: a film on copper, a mat behind a baffle, a rosette of sulfur, a tuft of iron that snaps, a thumb-sized shrimp picking at a cellar smell, a long pale fish tasting copper on the floor current, a cloud of single cells turning over in the boiling basin, shedding and growing stone.

The press cycles. The conveyor turns carrying no load. The pump surges and settles into its roar and never stops. The crane hangs mid-swing, swaying a fraction in the current, never completing its arc. And in the lee of all of it, in the quietest sheltered places, the living crust grows a little thicker on the pipes, the shrimp molt, the fish ride the return lane again, and the whole turbulent shape settles, again and again, into the same old form — chaotic on the scale of seconds, completely predictable on the scale of years, and inhabited, just barely, by things that have learned to be made of the place itself.
