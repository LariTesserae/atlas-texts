If you count what lives here by weight, by volume, by sheer persistence, there is only one kind of resident that matters. The rest are guests, and rare ones at that.

The plants are not *in* the world. They are the world, and they go on being it whether anything else is present or not. The mat under everything — that white, spongy peat of fiber, pulp, shed leaf and root-hair, frozen and resealing itself — is not soil that plants grow from. It is what plants have become after they died, pressed together until there was nowhere else for more plants to be. Everything else is built out of that.

A single individual is a continent. The nearest might be an hour's walk away across blank mat, and when you reach it, it is still rising. A low dome fifteen kilometers across, ridged like a frozen lily pad, its surface one overlapping leaf on top of another, pale green-white, hoarfrosted at the edges. From its rim hang curtains of roots as thick as bridges, arching down and fusing with the mat until no one can say where the dome ends and the floor begins. Inside, under that arch, is a hollow as dry and quiet as a cathedral nave. The light only gets in through a crack far above, where the leaves have not quite sealed. Condensation beads on the cold, curved walls of living tissue and runs slowly down in dark, sweet streaks.

Inside that hollow, and across the whole dome, the plant lives very slowly and very thriftily. There is no river to drink from, no lake. It lives on damp — on mist, on the melt of its own frost, on moisture stored in its thick pulp. It is cactus-cold. When the ceiling light dims a fraction, as it does when the sun outside shifts, the whole dome inflates in the damp cold and lifts the ground by a meter. When the light returns to full, merciless brightness, it deflates. Roots pulse under the mat. Tendrils lash out and retract. To stand on it for long is to learn not to trust any slope.

And it moves, in its way. Because the mat shifts and the ice roof a kilometer up creaks and splits and heals, because the plants themselves are forever cracking the floor, nothing stays where it was placed. A dome will unfurl — a hundred sail-sized leaves opening in a ripple that travels across its surface like a wave over water, pushing a small weather of displaced air ahead of it. Another will bud off a daughter, and the daughter will slide out across the empty floor, scraping a fresh furrow of white fiber behind it, edges still upturned. Spores gather until a fissure gives way in a white plume. The plume catches the shafts of light coming down through the ice and hangs there, glittering, expanding into a slow snowstorm that will in an hour whiteout the kilometers between two domes and lay down a centimeter of future plant on emptiness.

The weather here is not moved by wind. It is moved by growth.

Death is the same manner — not violent, but exhausted. A dome folds inward leaf over leaf, over hours, until it is only a low steaming mound. Then, from the center of the old tissue, a green spear stabs up and begins again. It is impossible to say where one individual ends and the next begins, or how old any of them are. Everything is sharp because everything is young. A fissure opens and its walls are a brilliant unclouded blue, barely covered in green. Shards of frozen sap litter the paths, clear and hard as pottery. Nothing has had time to be worn smooth.

This is the scale on which life happens. Between domes there can be a day's walk of empty mat, flat and bright, where only fallen translucent leaves have drifted and frozen into lenses, and frost-crystals grow overnight in lace and do not quite melt even under the light, only sink, glisten, and reform. The emptiness is not a lack of plants. It is the distance plants require.

Other life fits into the margins of that distance.

In the root arches, in the hollows under the domes, in the damp mat where it is thickest, there are small things that live entirely inside the fibers and rarely surface. Blind, pale crawlers no bigger than your thumb, with too many legs, that thread their way through the peat eating fermentation and cold sap. They are pale because light never reaches them. They are blind because it would not help them if it did. They are there as long as the mat stays undisturbed, and when a dome collapses or a fissure splits, they are exposed all at once, briefly, before burrowing again.

On the leaves themselves there is a broad, flat grazer that moves like a slug and is exactly the color of the leaf it eats. You only notice it when it moves — a slow, rippling shift of tone across a house-sized leaf that might otherwise be taken for wind. It does not hide. It *is* the hiding, pressed thin as the plant it feeds on.

And something with wings that makes a sound like a sigh and never lands where you can see it. In air so cold and thick and swallowed by the sponge of the floor that sound dies quickly, a sigh is close. It crosses the kilometers between domes on the air displaced by unfurling leaves, perhaps, or on spore-storms. It is not an ecosystem. These are guests in one.

The builders — if they still live here — are guests of a different sort.

Their evidence is scattered as sparsely as the fauna, and as far apart. A few domes bear a small structure: a hut lashed together from living tendrils, woven and tied with fiber cord, its roof a single large leaf stitched to the frame. Or a windbreak of upright root-stumps, lashed upright and left to grow. A fire-pit, cold, rimmed with ash the color of bone. Halfway across one of the great emptinesses, a line of stacked stones, or a signpost of hardened stem pointing nowhere you can tell — perhaps to the next dome, perhaps to water that has not yet rained, perhaps to warn of a collapse that has already happened.

They make nothing that lasts, because there is nothing else to make it from. No metal. No stone that is not borrowed. Everything is plant, or the rare animal that died in the plant: a hand-axe of flaked bone, a knife of sharpened chitin, a needle of fish-bone. The structures do not age. They are either fresh, woven living, or they are gone back into the substrate. The plant grows up through the floorboards. The leaf-roof seals shut. The tool left behind freezes into the mat and becomes indistinguishable from a shard of sap.

So if they live here, they do not stay. They would have to live as the plants do — in the hollows under the domes where condensation runs, in the lee of a root-arch, moving between continents of leaf days apart, following the fine rain a plant exhales when it warms under the light, following the spore-snow. They would build with living tendrils so the world could keep growing through them. They would make fire seldom, in a place that gives no warmth from its light and keeps its own warmth deep inside tissue. They would leave and be reabsorbed.

That is the turbulence of it: not that it is hostile, but that it is indifferent in the way a plant is indifferent — busy with a growth so vast and so quiet you can walk for hours before you realize the landscape behind you has entirely changed shape. Each dome its own horizon, each growth-spurt its own weather, each silence its own event.

Nothing is crowded. Nothing is old. Nothing stays.
