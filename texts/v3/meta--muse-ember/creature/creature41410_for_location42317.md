At first, you think no one lives here at all.

You can walk the Low Ring for an hour at midday and meet no one, and the emptiness does not feel like absence. It feels like the plan. A house, thick-walled and deep-set, its small dark mouth closed against the glare. A hundred paces of naked ledge, packed shell-pulp polished by feet. A kiln, beehive-shaped, warm to the touch though its fire is banked. A row of storage hollows in the rib, their reed doors woven shut. A loom under a shaving of shell, the warp already threaded, the shuttle lying across it as if a hand had just set it down. A mat rolled and left in the sun. Jars in a niche, still beaded with yesterday's harvest.

The signs of life are everywhere, and the living are nowhere, because the living are either inside, behind the two doors and the meter of mud and fiber, where the drone is softened to a pressure in the chest — or they are out on the ledges, far apart, working by touch with their ears plugged and their eyes mostly closed against the amber light.

And then, very far ahead on the curve of the ring, you see a figure move.

It is not fast. Nothing in Iskara moves fast, except the dust the wind lifts outside and the moths at dusk. The figure bends, straightens, bends again over a clay pipe set into the wall. Its hands are the first thing you understand. They are stained yellow-brown to the second joint, the nails black at the rim with old resin, the knuckles thick. It does not look at the pipe. It lays its palm flat against the wall beside it and waits, feeling. The hum runs up its arm. Its fingers make a small adjustment to a lashing, test, adjust again, satisfied by a vibration in the bone more than by any sound.

Only as you come closer — and on these ledges closer can take a long time, your own shout answering you a minute later thinner and higher — do you make out the plugs. Wax and fiber, worked into the ears, protruding a little like pale buds. The Resin-people wear them from childhood, and you see them everywhere once you have seen one: in the child sitting cross-legged on a reed mat, hands moving in that quick, precise grammar that is midday speech; in the old man walking the steep slope between rings with a jar of cut moss balanced on his hip, his step unhurried and sure on resin-slick stone; in the woman on the Reed Terraces, her forearm wrapped in woven fiber, coaxing a bell-vine trumpet open with her thumb.

They are weathered, like the tools in the throat-chamber. Rounded by handling. You do not get a face all at once in amber light that has no true shadow, only softer and brighter ambers. You get hands first, then posture, then the way they move from task to task as if tending not a garden but a sleeping animal.

For that is what they farm, and they farm it in two parts that cannot be separated. The pod must sing for the jungle to live, and the jungle, by catching and rerouting the air, helps the pod sing. The Resin-people, with their primitive, patient, layered building, farm both.

Each household has its stretch of wall. It is not a yard. Their yard is vertical, and it is an instrument. Curtains of hook-fern hang from the fissures above and below their house, each frond a hollow tube open at both ends, quivering in the rising air. In the stillness of a normal garden they would starve; here, when the shell expands and sings, the cilia along the fronds catch the fine silicate mist the warm flesh exhales and the vibration strips the nutrients from it. A fern beside a tuned flute will double in a season. A fern in an unpiped niche, with all the light in the world, will languish and sigh its tubes shut. So the Resin-people pipe their gardens. Clay flutes set into walls, hollow ribs left open, ceramic bells and beaten gourds hung from rope bridges — these are not decoration. They are tuned to the pod's note, and the gardens are tuned through them.

Below the ferns, cushion moss carpets every ledge not built on, a hand thick, velvet green, crunching dry underfoot when the heat takes it and sweating when the shell expands. Morning and evening the people go out with jars and knives to harvest it — to cut it and chew it and store it — and what they get is not water. There is no water in Iskara. What they get is sweet amber sap that tastes of warm resin and green stems, and it is their drink and their oil and their glue. On the Reed Terraces the bell-vines sprawl, their trumpet flowers opening at noon to the light and the noise, slapping gently against each other in the updraft, gathering clear beads on their lips, bead by bead, for a thirsty hand to gather. Around the low kilns the drum-leaves grow broad and taut as skins, folding and unfolding with the temperature, beating a slow, green time against the kilns' bellows-rhythm.

So a Resin-person's day is divided not by hunger but by sound. At dawn there is contraction, a ticking and creaking as the shell settles, and for an hour you can hear your own footsteps. That is when they walk out onto the Great Drum in the center — that shallow pit, thirty paces across, stretched with dried pod-skin taut as a drumhead — not to worship it, not to cross it at midday when dust jumps on it in patterns, but to listen with their feet. To test its tension. To tighten its lashings with hand tools. The soles of their feet tell them what the drum needs, and then they go back to their houses, to their pipes, to their green.

By midday the shell is glowing from outside and expanding, and the note is in their teeth. Work does not stop, but it changes. They work by touch. Kilns breathe heat in pulses and the bellows are worked by feel. Looms are threaded by feel. A child learns the hand-speech at its mother's knee while the drone roars overhead, hands flashing, face intent, because a spoken word midday would be swallowed and spat back distorted from the far wall. Even the thickest house cannot keep it out entirely; it arrives as pressure, as a hum in the sternum. So they plug their ears and they talk with their hands, and they learn to read a room by the set of shoulders, by a tapped reed.

Distance, here, is not loneliness. A few hundred people keep a hundred paces between houses, sometimes more, and for every inhabited cube there are ten empty ones, broad ledges where a shout will travel a minute and thin. They know each other by rhythm more than by face: the particular pulse of a neighbor's kiln, the particular pitch of a neighbor's fern curtain, the creak of a neighbor's pulley-rope three rings above. To announce yourself at midday you do not shout. You tap the pipe that runs through both your gardens and feel the answer come back through the wall, or you send a bell on its rope swinging, a purely visual bell, and wait for the hand that waves it still. A new face on a ring is an event precisely because it is so rare, and the face that is new will be examined at length, in that amber light, by hands that have not stopped their work.

Up on the Bell Rim, where the shell is thinnest and the crown pales to milky white, the life is sparer still. Almost too much to bear up there. Only the tenders of the great resonators go, and they go with their plugs deep and their work done quickly, fitting a flute, checking a lashing, then coming down again to the Low Ring where the drone, for all its pressure, is at least bearable. Some go only to look through the pinholes worn by erosion at the white plain outside, at the empty sky, at a world they will never walk. They come back down and do not speak of it, except at night.

Night is the interval in which the Resin-people become fully present to each other.

When the sun drops the amber walls go from bright gold to deep dusky brown, the shell contracts, the great note thins and thins and, for a brief astonishing interval, ceases. In that silence you can hear a loom three rings away. You can hear resin bead by bead. And the thick houses open.

They take out their plugs. The wax-and-fiber buds come free with a small, practiced twist, and the ears, unplugged, seem to open like flowers. They emerge onto the ledges, take out their jars, and do the work that needs language: mending, harvesting the last beads from the closing bells, arguing — a low, normal-voiced argument that carries cleanly across the empty ledges and is not distorted on return — teaching. An elder will sit with a child on the Great Drum at dusk, both of them barefoot on the taut skin, and place the child's palm flat so the child can feel the low creak of expansion beginning again, like a held breath being released. Hands still do much of the teaching, but now there is speech underneath them, and the child hears for the first time that day what its own voice sounds like inside the seed.

It is in these intervals, and only in these intervals, that you understand how they live so far apart. They are not scattered by accident. The city was planned, or grew in fits, for a population that thinned, or that learned to live far apart because the world they live in is mostly empty air and mostly deafening song. To live far apart is to have room for your garden to sing, room for your pipes to resonate, room for the spore-dust — that fine green snow always falling from the central updraft, never melting, coating their mats and their hair and the rims of their jars — to settle undisturbed. Their sparseness is an acoustic necessity.

And they are not the only lives keeping the music.

In the wall cracks, where the resin has split and the mud plaster has fallen away, the geckos live. Deaf and pale — deaf because sound is irrelevant to them, pale because the amber light asks no pigment — they move with a slow, deliberate languor that matches the shell's own pace, insensitive to the midday roar that fills your teeth. At dusk, in the brief silence, they hunt. Their prey rises then, in small clouds: the moths, glass-winged and blind, who navigate not by sight but by the feel of vibration in the air. They ride the harmonics the way the ferns catch them, their wings catching the fading light, climbing the lazy central column of warm air as it rises through the ribs, spiraling, falling, spiraling again. Eaten, or not. The geckos' tongues flick against the wall. The moths' blind navigation is perfect until it is not.

And once in a while — you will hear it before you see it, a soft, leathery snap in the upper air — the glider slips from the eaves of the Bell Rim. It is the only animal in Iskara that ever leaves. A small thing, built for the updraft, it rides the warm column up past the crown, tucks through the low resinous keyhole on the northern side, and wheels out over the white plain. Out there there is nothing: no water, no green thread, no other life to see. It wheels for a time that seems to stretch, a speck against the glare, and then it turns — because the world, for it, is not out there — and slips back through the keyhole into the amber light, to its eave, to shake the dust of the plain from its wings and settle. The Resin-people on the Bell Rim watch it go and watch it return, and they know it by its particular wingbeat. It is the one life that can test whether the outside is still empty, and it always comes back.

The green, too, comes back to itself at night. The bell-vines fold their trumpets. The cushion moss stops sweating. The drum-leaves close against the cooling kilns. The hook-ferns hang still, their tubes silent for an hour, breathing only the slow tide of heat that moves through the seed as its weather. And then, imperceptibly, the pod begins to warm for the next day, the first low creak of expansion runs through the ribs, the fundamental note returns so low you feel it in your ribs and in the soles of your feet, and the whole layered, patient economy — shell, jungle, city — wakes together.

That is how they live there: not quickly, not loudly, not close together. A few hundred people and uncounted green mouths and deaf pale hunters and blind glass wings, all of them spread thin across a mostly empty hollow, all of them listening — or learning not to listen — to the same low hum in their teeth, and keeping, interval by interval, the music going.
