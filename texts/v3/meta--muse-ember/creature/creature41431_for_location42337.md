First, there is nothing living here.

Not hidden nothing, not the kind of nothing that scatters when you reach for it. A bright, deafening, normal-lit emptiness, so complete that for a long while you begin to think the pocket was made to keep life out.

You hear it first, because everything here arrives by sound before it arrives by sight. In sky-water the roar carries close and fast, and through it, if you stay still enough, you learn to make out the small noises that are not the pocket itself: a faint change in pressure, a low thickening in the hiss where a current turns, a crack far below that is not rock cracking but something else trying to become rock. Life, when it is here, makes that kind of noise. Not a call. A change in the medium where a presence has thickened.

Then, usually along the light side, you see the first thread of it.

The vegetation — if you want to call it that — does not grow up from anything. It hangs.

Green-black filaments, algae and sky-moss and something between the two, strung from droplet membrane to droplet membrane on lines you cannot quite see until the filament is there to show them. They are sparse to a degree that feels deliberate, as if the pocket can only afford a few at a time. You can cross the whole of it and find none. You can drift in a slow spiral for an hour and find only one, swaying not in wind but in current, turning its length a fraction toward the light, tasting iron and rain.

Where one is, the water is faintly, cleanly green. Where one is not — most places — there is no smell at all, only the mineral coolness on the skin. The filament is not rooted, because to be rooted would require a permanence the pocket does not allow. A membrane holds it for a while. The membrane merges, or pops, or is sheared away, and the filament must let go and be caught again a pace farther on, re-strung. It photosynthesizes in almost nothing, and needs almost nothing, because the light here is perfectly ordinary and falls from everywhere. It grows an inch, perhaps. It splits at the tip and the split drifts and dies. It is eaten, not by an animal, but by light and water and reabsorption. It un-strings.

It lives longer than almost anything else here, and still you would not say it lives in colonies. Each strand is alone by a distance of empty bright water. It is a community of one, repeated rarely.

The glider is rarer still, and it does not begin the way a living thing is supposed to begin.

You will not see it youth or egg or brood. You will hear the pressure change first, a moment before the shape, and then a thickening in the glitter — translucent at the edges, opaque in the center, crossing the pocket on slow, shallow strokes as if it were swimming and the water had forgotten to be only water. A ray that has learned to move through the sky by not quite flying. Its wings are not wings exactly, more like broad fins pressed out of the medium itself, beating the sky-water aside in long, unhurried lamplight. It trails a wake of disturbed droplets that hang and fall up and hang again behind it.

It is large enough to matter. It crosses the whole pocket in minutes, indifferent to the currents that tumble a raft or a hand, indifferent to you. It does not avoid you, exactly. You are too finished, too dense and warm, to register as part of its world. It perceives shear and swell and the breathing of the walls, and it moves through them by a patience you do not have.

There may be a handful of them in existence across long time. There may be only one at any one time. The pocket seems to admit that rule: when one thickens enough to be a glider, another cannot thicken at the same moment. Two fully formed would resonate in the same small water and un-make each other. So life here is not scarce for lack of food — there is little to eat, and less that admits eating — but singular by law. Each glider is the only one. When it reaches the far rain wall it thins at the edges first, then at the center, and dissolves back into the emergence that made it, droplet by droplet, and the pocket is empty again.

It sheds nothing that lasts. No bones, no young. Perhaps the skin it loses in the crossing knits elsewhere into a filament, and that filament, weeks later, is reabsorbed, and that reabsorption is part of how another glider begins. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is kept.

Around and under these two presences there are the attempts.

On slower currents, mineral dust and ice crystal begin to knit a crust thin as breath — a community, if a community is the word for something that has not yet decided to be stone. It glitters. You hear it cracking long before you see it. It holds for a moment, a shelf, a floor that was not a floor, and if a filament happens to be near it may touch and hold for an hour. Then the pocket breathes differently and the crust loses its claim and is water again. They are not alive, not quite. They are pre-alive, rehearsing. The same with a filament that grows and is eaten by light. The pocket is full of gestures toward life that never complete themselves before they un-happen.

That is why the made things startle.

They are not alive, but they were lived with. A half-woven raft of reeds lashed with sinew, tumbling slowly, half-rotted back to fibre. A baked clay cup tied to a bladder float, still holding a palmful of sky-water that is not quite water. A tiny windbreak of sticks stuck into a crust that is now only a memory of crust. A cairn of smoothed pebbles stacked on a shelf that has already dissolved. A knapped stone tool. A patched hide stitched with bone needle, smelling impossibly, faintly of smoke, this high, inside weather.

No one lives with them now. No one seems to have ever *lived* here, in the way a people lives in a place — in numbers, in houses, in seasons. These were visitors' marks, perhaps. People from the ridge far below who left things to the cloud. Things that fell up through the rain wall and were caught. The builders' ancestors might have been the thing that became the glider, or they might have merely passed through, once, with early tools and manual craft, and left a handful of primitive, tender constructions to drift.

And yet while they last, the artifacts are inhabitants of a sort. They tumble in the currents, loosening. Their lashings soften. A cup keeps its water long after its bladder has softened, and for a while that water is a different water — held, remembered by clay. A hide keeps its smoke. Things made by hands carry a warmth and a smell that nothing native to the pocket ever makes. They are the only warm things here. They are also unfinished like everything else. Edges soften, weaving becomes a suggestion of weaving, and in time they un-happen as surely as a crust or a filament, only slower, because they were made to resist un-happening before they came here.

So the population of the pocket, if you can call it that, is not a population. It is a succession of instances, rarely coinciding: a filament strung between membranes on the light side; far away, a crust knitting and un-knitting on a slow current; farther still, perhaps, a glider thickening out of the roar to cross and dissolve before the filament notices; and in the empty middle, a raft and a cup tumbling, remembering hands that will never come back for them.

Nothing raises young here. Nothing gathers. Nothing stays to be seen twice in the same place. If you remain long enough, watching the wall breathe in and out, you understand that you have joined them in the only way the pocket allows — temporarily, out of number, by thickening in the medium for a while and then, inevitably, by letting the water take your shape back.
