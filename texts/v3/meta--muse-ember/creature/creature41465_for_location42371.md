The first living thing you meet down here is not standing up.

It is a skin. In the shallow hollows where the drip gathers and holds, a filmed-over green, no thicker than breath on glass, too still to be water alone. Under the amber lamps it looks almost luminous; away from them it disappears altogether. It is algae, and cyanobacteria, and the first soft rot of leaf, living in the top millimeter of the pools the way surface pond life lives in a summer ditch — except here the water is near freezing and the light is weak and what little warmth there is comes from a lamp or a man's boot, and so it grows not in hours but in weeks. It thickens, it breaks, it reforms after the ten-minute patter from the roof. It has no root and no stem to catch on. It simply has to hold on in the cold wet and not dry.

That is the level at which most life here exists: holding on, flat.

Because beneath the film, and everywhere the film isn't, is the mat itself, and the mat is alive all the way down. What looked from the adit like a single green floor resolves, when you kneel in it, into thousands of small separate lives pressed together so tightly they have become one body. Moss first — cushion moss and sheet moss, star moss, those pale grey-green tufts that have climbed the locomotive coupling and the shed foundations and the lower trunks of the poplars to a man's height. Liverwort, flat as coins, dark as wet leather, spreading where the light fails. Sedge, the true workhorse of the plain, each plant a loose fan of thin leaves and a knot of rhizome just under the surface, sending out runners a few centimeters a year. The rhizomes are the real estate here. They braid and knot and hold water between them, they lift the thresholds of the huts, they find the joints in the pipes from the generator and seal them. If you pull a handful up — the crews do it with the harvester, but gently is not their pace — it comes away as a sod, a felt, roots below live and white and tender, roots above brown and dead and feeding the next layer. A finger's width of compression under your boot is a season of growth.

Nothing in this layer hurries to be seen. No flower worth noticing: a sedge spike, pale and late, dwarf willow creeping almost invisible along the ground with buds like beads, waiting for a summer that down here is a lamp. It is tundra logic transplanted underground — survive the cold, survive the wet, survive the dim, spread sideways, don't waste energy on height.

Height, when it comes, is an exception, and it comes in islands.

A birch grove, twenty meters across, maybe a dozen trees no thicker than your wrist, skin white-grey, leaves small and already yellowing though no season tells them to. They were not planted; they seeded themselves from the surface years ago, or were carried down the shaft on a boot, and they have grown at the speed of cold shade: a ring a decade. Between them the mat is bare of trees, just sedge and moss, for two hundred meters.

Then a stand of poplars, once in rows. You can still find the spacing if you look for it — six meters, eight meters, the geometry of someone's plan. Now maybe one in three remain, trunks leaning slightly toward the nearest lamp, bark fissured and furred with moss to shoulder height. Their roots have gone sideways under the mat looking for water that is everywhere but never really available in the way roots want. When the roof drips and the mat darkens, a poplar here does not drink; it waits. It has learned patience from the place, or the place has worn it into it.

Under a drip line where water falls steady enough to be a resource, ferns unfurl. Not large — knee-high at most, fronds stiff and dark green, fiddleheads tight for a long time before they open, and sometimes not opening at all. Lichen, meanwhile, lives on the boulders and the tops of limestone blocks and the rusted boom of the half-sunk crane, in pale maps and crusts that may be older than the mine that uncovered them. A lichen does not age as an animal ages. It simply accretes, a millimeter in a good year, indifferent to the roar above it.

And inside the felt, where you cannot see unless you turn it over, is the second population, the one that makes the first possible. Fungi thread the dead root and leaf in white mycelium, breaking the sod down one cell at a time so the moss can feed. Bacteria bloom in the gutters and in the thumb-width trickle by the rail, in the warm seep around the pump house. Springtails — white, rice-grain things that jump if you breathe on them — graze the algae film and the mold on fallen birch leaves, thousands to a square meter. Fungus gnat larvae live in the damp mat itself, pale worms in the rot, and when the adults emerge they do the only foolish thing any animal does down here: they rise to the lamp. Around one amber strip, on a still night, there is always a cloud of them, dancing, rising and settling, living out a whole adult life in a few days in a circle of light a kilometer from any other.

A mouse lives, or has lived, on that scale too. You will not see it, but you will see its road: a flattened line through the sedge from the maintenance shed, where there is seed and warmth, to the birch grove, where there is cover, and back again. The line is smooth, worn by feet and fur, not by a vehicle. It runs at night, presumably, when the crews are up and the headlamps are off and the basin belongs to the noise and the mat. It eats something in the shed, something in the grove, and it winters, if it winters, in the wall of the shed where the frost is kept out by a generator's waste heat. That is the entire mammalian ecology of a kilometers-long chamber: one mouse's commute.

Other animals are only visitors, and brief. A moth fell down the shaft once, or was carried down, and drowned in the pump house gutter — wings spread, grey, a single specimen. A raven from the plateau crossed the shaft light high up, once, and turned back, as any surface animal would: no food down here, no perch, no way to climb two hundred meters of wet concrete on a cold downdraft. No birds nest. No insects live in the open. The cold and the light and the noise have agreed, in their different ways, to keep the air empty.

The warm-blooded regulars are human, and they are not residents either, exactly. They live up top and descend for a shift, in parkas and ear defenders, shuffling across the mat in a way that does not so much walk as test the ground. Their lives down here are measured in small, repeated gestures: a kettle left on in the rest cabin, a chair pushed back, a calendar open to a month three years past, boots outside the shed door stiff with frost because no one wants to carry them back through the wet. They do not farm so much as tend: they check the generator, they oil the conveyor, they walk the harvester at walking pace and slower, watching the felt tear and knit. A man who worked here in his twenties might come back at sixty and not recognize the groves; a man who works here now will leave, at the end of a shift, a warmth of his own body in a room that will be cold again within the hour, and a smell of diesel and damp wool.

And so the inhabitants sort themselves by how long they can afford to wait. The algae film waits weeks. The moss waits years. The sedge waits seven years for a crop cycle. The birch waits a generation. The lichen waits centuries. The mouse waits a night. The gnats wait days. The crews wait out a shift and go up to where the air moves and the light is a winter afternoon and there are birds.

What they share, plant and fungus and animal and man, is lowness. Nothing stands up here except by accident and at cost. Everything that lives, lives flat against the cold stone, in the mat, under the lampshade, in the flattened path through the sedge. The roar goes on above them, slow and deafening and indifferent to all of them — and below it, in the green felt that covers a basin, life goes on at a pace set by stone, holding water in its teeth, growing sideways into the dark, and never, quite, being finished.
