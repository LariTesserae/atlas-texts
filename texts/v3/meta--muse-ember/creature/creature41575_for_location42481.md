At first, no one.

You stand for a moment on the mat — four minutes to cross the whole of it, if you cross it at all — and the Hollow seems uninhabited in spite of being unmistakably urban. Houses stand far apart, towers far apart, ponds empty except for the slow skin of mist over them. Then something shifts, not in the distance but underfoot, and the emptiness resolves into presences: a breathing, a dripping becoming a rhythm, a leaf-tip tightening somewhere to replay a sound it stored yesterday. What lives here does not arrive. It surfaces.

It surfaces in layers, because that is how it lives.

The first to make itself known does not move to meet you. It is already sitting in your doorway.

The loaf-frogs are the size of a bread loaf, pale and translucent at the belly where the water-core shows through, and each house seems to have chosen one, or been chosen by one. They sit square in the threshold of a trained plant, unblinking, damp, taking the damp air in through their skin. They do not guard in the human sense. They hold the door open in the chemical sense — their bellies pulse at the frequency that keeps a doorway's sap-flow unclotted, their skin filters the spore-dust that would blind a leaf-roof. If a house needs a new room, the frog is moved, gently, by a tendril of its own house, to the place where the wall will thicken. If a house is abandoned, the frog softens, flattens into the mat, and is recycled into substrate within an afternoon, and another frog elsewhere, far across the wet page, swells a little larger to take its place in the pattern. They are residents, furniture, and plumbing at once, and everyone steps around them as if around a sleeping cat, though they are never quite asleep.

Deeper in the low central pond — the one that deepens and shallows with the hour, where the root-bridges cross and re-cross — lie the long ones.

Crocodile-long amphibians, half-sunk, eyes at the waterline, skin like wet leather over bone. They are the oldest continuity here, older than any pruning, and you step around them politely the way you would step around a monument that might decide to move. They do not hunt. Fish arch over them in the condensing air, frogs sing over them, vendors' thickets unfurl within a few meters of their snouts, and they do nothing but breathe, slowly, and now and then burp up a great warm bubble from the pond-bottom that pops against the mat and sets your teeth aching. That pop is a message. Root-networks carry it farther than sound should travel in wet air. The long ones are listening posts, living capacitors in the pond that remember earlier layers of the city — a street that slumped here before the pond was a pond, a house that melted into a mound two growth-surges ago. When a root-street sags and must be eaten and regrown a meter to the left, it is usually after consultation, of a kind, with a long one resurfacing to let the water rearrange itself around its flank. They eat nothing you can see. Everything eats something here, but they mostly eat time, and the slow traffic of spores and rot that the pond cannot otherwise hold.

Above and through, not on, comes the second layer: those who do not use the mat as ground but as medium.

The birds that nest in the hollow stems and spore-spires never fly out. That is only half true. They burrow. A small grey-white bird will slip from a roof-leaf into the spongy mat at your feet, disappear with a soft sigh of moss, and emerge twenty meters away in the Weeping Quarter, shaking water from its wings, carrying in its crop a thread of mist-fish eggs or a packet of nanospores tuned to repair a particular tower. They migrate internally, through the root-mesh that is also a sky for them, and they are the postmen and pollinators and midwives of the place. A building is encouraged to grow a new room in an afternoon not by force but by tuning the sap-flow, the light, and the song of the birds nested in its eaves — and the birds, arriving from below, bring the song with them, note by note, from another house where the same song was needed and stored.

With them move the air-fish, which jump not in ponds but in the low air, arching on threads of mist that hang, for a second, between nothing and nothing. They are thin, silver, insubstantial until they land in a bead of condensation and stitch it into a drop large enough to fall. Some do not land. Some live their whole arcing lives in the weather-system of beads that hang mid-air on invisible spore-silk, drinking the moment before it rains. And with them, deafening in pulses, are the paper-winged insects, wings wet as tissue, moving in choruses so loud the plant surfaces themselves drum in answer. They are the market criers. They do not sell. Their coming and going is the signal that the Bazaar, below, is about to open or fold.

Because the Bazaar is not a place its inhabitants go to. It is a state they enter.

When it opens — like a flower, in an hour, across the central low ground — the thickets at the root-bridges unfurl. Vendors are not behind stalls. Vendors *are* the thickets. A vendor's thicket is a carefully tended stand of broad leaves, each leaf a different ware: this one stores a song in its tension, tightened by wind so it hums with yesterday's market; this one sweats sap-sweet clear as resin-glass; this one is woven root-cloth, still half-living, which will stiffen only when worn and warmed by a body. The vendor is the tendril at the center that leans out, samples your damp hand, decides what you might trade. And the crowd that swarms to it is not a crowd of people but a transient confluence: loaf-frogs hopping from doorway to doorway carrying water-light in their bellies, air-fish dropping in to leave a thread of mist, burrow-birds surfacing with messages from the Nursery Quarter, insects deafening the transaction into applause, and — moving among them, surprisingly familiar and not — the Pruned Folk.

They are the only inhabitants shaped like a person would recognize as a person, and even they are not quite. They live in the Nursery Quarter where tower-thickets are pruned into lofts, and in the Weeping Quarter where houses are hollowed stems that drip all day, though they move unhurriedly between both, and up, rarely, in the spore-spires. Their skin is damp all the time, coppery-green at the wrists and ankles where grafts took. Their feet are broad and root-toed, soles callused with moss, so the mat does not learn them as strangers; it already knows their weight and raises them a fraction before they sink. Their lungs are branched like small leaf-systems. They drink through their feet when standing in warm ankle-water, and breathe through their skin when the frog-chorus thickens the air. They are born, if born is the word, as spores on the warm substrate and raised by a house-plant that feeds them sap-sweet until their limbs differentiate, like a room differentiating in a trunk.

They do not build. They tune. A Pruned person will press a palm to a living wall and listen for the chemical pulse in the root-network, the same streets the fauna use, and will adjust the light by encouraging a leaf-roof to lean one way, and will sing, or let the eave-birds sing, until the sap-flow chooses a new direction and a doorway half-buried in root opens a stair. They prune, they nudge, they repair with nanospore clouds they summon from the spire-crowns, clouds you can only see against the light when they bloom and stitch a torn leaf, feed a frog, condense a future rain. They trade the way everything trades: offering a song stored in a leaf, a length of root-cloth, a spore that will become a frog, accepting in return a burp-bubble's warning that the pond is about to rise, or a bird's arrival with gossip from the other side of the mat, fifty meters away and acoustically beside you.

They are sparse, as everything monumental here is sparse. You could walk the whole town in four minutes and meet, in Bazaar-time, all of them — and in folded-time, almost none. When the Bazaar folds shut, the crowd does not go home. It goes back into the substrate as if sucked down: frogs flatten, thickets refold, insects thin, fish evaporate into the beads, birds burrow, Pruned Folk step off the mat onto a firm woven road and let the mat sigh over their footprints, closing them. Death here is not allowed to linger, and neither, for long, is a person. When a Pruned person dies, the house that raised them will thicken its walls with them within hours, sealing them in resin-glass amber that is not dead but stored, to be eroded, in time, into new growth. There are no graves. There are layers.

Highest, and least seen, are the Spore-Spire Keepers. Six, eight towers, cathedral-tall, scattered like punctuation, release fine clouds from their crowns. The Keepers live in those crowns, among the cloud, in hollowed pollen chambers where the air is always stitching. They are rarely distinguishable from the spore-cloud itself, a damp figure with light behind it, tending the infrastructure that is also the engineering. They tune the dew-harvesting — dew harvested at the scale of molecules and released on schedule, so the place waters itself under a merely grey-white ordinary sky — and they let it down in soft irregular rain from the beads. If you look up at evening, when light cools through the towering leaves and the ponds reflect the spires imperfectly, you might see a Keeper move, slow, through its own cloud, and understand that you have been breathing what it exhales.

All of them together are the maintenance crew and the messengers and the plumbing and the choir. Everything eats something, pollinates something, repairs something. The insects eat the mist that the spires make. The fish eat the insects' falling. The frogs eat the fish's threads and give back water. The birds eat the mat's warmth and give back song. The long ones eat the sediment of old streets and give back a pop in the teeth that tells a bridge where to sag. The Pruned Folk eat sap-sweet and give back pruning. The mat eats them all, gently, constantly, and gives back ground.

And so, if you stand still long enough, the ground beneath you adjusts to the shape of your feet, learning your weight, raising a soft callus of moss to keep you from sinking, and you realize you have become, briefly, an inhabitant by the same rule as the frogs in the doors. The Hollow does not have residents the way a city has residents — separate bodies in separate houses. It has a single churn, ancient and finished and never finished, in which a frog, a vendor-thicket, a burrow-bird, a long one in a pond, a damp-skinned person with root-toes pressing a leaf-wall to listen for a pulse, are all local strategies for the same act: erosion and growth, plant wearing down stone, plant wearing down plant, city wearing down into plant, plant wearing up into city again.

By evening they settle, but they do not sleep. The mat sighs under scattered footsteps, some yours, most not. Water weeps from the rocks. Somewhere a leaf tightens to replay a market sound for no one, and somewhere else a bird surfaces in the dark, bringing a song from the other side of the world, four minutes away.
