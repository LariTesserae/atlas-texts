To list who lives here you have to stop thinking in individuals.

Nothing here walks to where it lives, or arrives by wing, or settles and then leaves again. The residents arrived as seed, as spore, as a root tip finding a mortar joint, and then they stayed, widening. If you try to count them you end up counting surface. This much moss, that much fern, a hand of liverwort under the third damp streak on the east wall. A square hand is a population. A lane no wider than a doorway is a street of them, shoulder to shoulder.

The first and oldest town is the floor.

It does not sit on the quarry bench and the poured concrete and the tilted brick paving so much as replace it. In the sump margin it is ankle-deep and springy, a continuous mat built up one leaf-layer at a time. Moss makes the close, bright green carpet that takes the imprint of a boot and springs back. Sedge stitches it at the water's edge, tougher, upright, with a fine, patient fringe. Watercress runs in the shallow channels in dark, succulent ropes. Over it all creep the aroids with leaves like wet spades, overlapping, heart-shaped, beaded with condensation, rooting again at every node so that a single plant becomes a colony in a season.

Dig with a heel — though no one does — and you find black hummocks under the living top, peat made of its own dead leaves that never quite rotted fast enough to disappear. That dark layer is the ground, really. The stone below it is a memory. Seepage wells up through it wherever a mat is thin, so the mat is always wet from below as well as from the drips above. On the highest hummocks, which never quite dry, ferns unfurl in the green-grey light, frond by frond, slow as a hand opening. They are darker where the light finds a seam in the canopy, and where the tiers shade them they go lank and bright, stems long, leaflets spaced out as if feeling for something.

That is their whole economy: water is never scarce, light is always scarce, and space is never empty. So they do not race. They fill.

The walls are the second town, older, slower.

From the limestone itself, algae and liverwort keep the established routes — the dark runnels where water finds its way down year-round — softened at the edges in green. That is the wild wall. The built wall is different. Roots have come out of mortar joints in the mill house and the boiler house and the two warehouses and the small workshops, and they have not burst anything. They have found a crack, let water in, widened it a fraction of a time, and then stopped because there is nowhere more to go. A brick bulges slightly where a root arm passes behind it. A lintel sags, not because it is failing, but because a tangle of stems has grown into it and become load-bearing in the way a tendon is load-bearing. Lean against the wall and it yields a little. It smells of wet earth and iron.

Every drain and gutter and handrail has been claimed on the same terms. Creepers follow pipes like veins, leaf after leaf along the copper until the copper is verdigris underneath them and the pipe itself is just a shape under the vine. In a crate left open in a warehouse, seedlings push up through the slats, pale at first, then green. If a door is left open, the mat flows in. If a machine must move, it keeps a small bald circle clear — a fan as wide as a cart turning in its own warm, vibrating air, a hydro wheel creeping in its channel, a belt drive slick with oil and water — and plant closes right up to the edge of the heat. Where the machine rests for a season the circle is gone.

Above, past the stepped roofs — the roof of the lower tier is the floor of the next — is the third town, and it holds the others in.

Trees on the rim sent roots across the open pit when there was still open pit to cross. Vines and banyan-like aerial roots dropped and stitched and dropped again until there is a partial roof that is not a roof. Leaves interrupt the light but do not stop it. What comes down is filtered twice, by depth and by leaf, green-grey and diffuse and without direction, as if through wet muslin. The hanging root-curtains drip. They are residents too: thick, ropey, still growing downward, never reaching the mat below, feeding on air and condensation and the steam from the lamps.

The lamps are this place's weather. Old sodium fittings, orange, bolted to walls, hung from pipes, most still on, tended only by timers. Their pools are small and underlined by steam. At night — though there is no true night down here, only a brown dimness under the tiers — the light is the only sun there is. Plants lean toward it. In the shadowed corners the lean becomes a stretch: etiolated, blind, leaves paler than they should be, reaching for the orange pool they will never reach, shading the next tier as they go.

And with the plants, always, the moving things that are not alive. They are residents in the sense that everything else arranges itself around them. A pump kicks, labours for a minute, rests. The water level in the sump rises and falls a few centimetres with the cycle. The fan turns with a deep, patient *whoomph* and a loose chain clanks against a railing in time. The wheel creeps and creeps. The sound fills the hollow completely because the limestone throws it back, and there is nowhere for it to go, and the plants have grown up in it for decades without moving away. Vibration keeps a circle bare. Heat keeps a circle bare. Oil keeps a circle bare. Everything else is occupied.

There is no other kind of movement here. No track in the mud of the mat, no nest in the rafters, no stirring in the channels. A leaf unfolds. A root tip searches. A belt turns. Water falls — from the rim, from pipes, from the hanging roots overhead, from the weeping rock — in dozens of separate drips that merge into a constant percussion.

Change is what you get if you stay long enough to learn its pace. Silt settles a millimetre a year on the lower paving, under the moss. Rust deepens at the seams of steel doors, orange-black to a line twenty centimetres high where it is permanently wet. The mat thickens each autumn and does not rot fast enough to keep pace, so it rises, very slowly, toward the first step of the stair. Roots widen a fraction. Lamps tick as condensation drips off their housings.

It is a crowded place, saturated, warm, loud, dim, and entirely green in its living parts. Not a garden on top of a ruin. The plant is the substrate, the wall, the floor, the roof, the street. The town was given back to damp and green without ever being abandoned by industry, and the population that took it, leaf-layer by leaf-layer, is still taking it, in a way that has no outside and no end to fill.
