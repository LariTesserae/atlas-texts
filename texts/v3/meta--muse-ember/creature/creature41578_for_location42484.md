At first there is no one in particular. There is only the hum, and the movement, and the warmth on your skin.

If you stand still long enough in the middle of it, the hum does not get louder. It gets *closer*. It separates, the way eyes separate in the dark. A vibration under your boot resolves into thousands of feet. A rustle overhead resolves into wings. A smell that seemed one smell — warm, sweet, ammoniac, green — resolves into layers, and each layer has an owner.

Underfoot, where the ground is not ground, the smallest residents do the oldest work. Mites by the uncountable million graze the mud walls, eating fungi out of the dust, eating each other, eating the shed skins of everything larger. They do not build. They inhabit the building, the way a thought inhabits a head.

With them work the beetles — dark, polished, unhurried — rolling pellets of leaf pulp and droppings into side chambers to feed their larvae. Millipedes thread themselves through damp silk and chewed leaf, blind and patient, turning compost into compost. Earthworms and their smaller cousins push through the terraced floor, loosening what the rain has cemented, leaving castings that become the next layer of wall. Springtails jump in white clouds when you disturb a mat of moss. Nothing here is seen at a glance. This is a life lived in the thickness of things, in the finger-width between one sheet of silk and the next, and it never stops because the walls never stop being eaten and remade from within.

The ants come up through this world along roads they have painted with themselves.

A leafcutter procession is as thick as your wrist and as old as the valley. It keeps to a silk highway that runs along a terrace edge, across a root, up a woven wall, and the workers do not walk on it so much as flow on it, each one carrying a green flag many times her own weight, held high over her head like a sail. A minor worker, no bigger than a grain of rice, rides the leaf like a jockey, fighting off small flies. At the other end of the trail, in chambers beneath the floor, other workers chew the leaf into a pulp and feed it to a grey, spongy fungus — their fungus, their livestock, their food. The ants do not eat the leaf. They eat what the leaf becomes after the fungus has worked it. The waste from that garden, rich and dark, is carried up and mortared into the walls again. Ants are born, work for a few short weeks in the steady damp, and die on the trail, and are carried aside by sisters who do not mourn them, and the trail does not break.

Beside them, and under them, and above them, are the termites, who do not keep roads but keep *tubes*.

Their runs climb the cliff face in branching mud veins, wrap a tree trunk until the bark is only a rumour inside a column, and lace the interior of every wall. Inside the tubes it is dark and total and warm. Workers, blind, soft, the size of rice grains, move back and forth cementing pellet to pellet, nibbling wood pulp, tending the damp. Soldiers with great jaws stand at breaches. Deep in the oldest mound, below a terrace where the mud is hardest and roundest from a million shoulders brushing past, the queen lies swollen with eggs, fed mouth to mouth by a court that never leaves her. When the rains fail and the air grows still, winged alates push out of a crack in the wall by the thousands, pale and frantic, to fly for ten minutes and fall, and found a new tube one terrace over. Their lives are not long. Their city is.

The bees live behind a curtain you can feel before you can see.

A stingless bee colony hums through a sheet of resin and wax the thickness of your palm, resin dark as old amber, gathered from wounds in trees. The hum is in your teeth. At the entrance — a small upright tube of wax and mud, guarded day and night by workers who bite first and sting never — foragers come and go in a steady dribbling stream, their hind legs loaded with orange pollen, their crops full of nectar. Inside, in pots of wax no larger than marbles, they keep honey and pollen, and in brood cells they raise the next generation on the same fermented stores. Foragers live a month or two. The colony lives years, splits when it grows too crowded, sends out a virgin queen with a cloud of workers to start a new resin sheet under the next roof overhang, and leaves the old sheet to be kept, polished, and reoccupied by wax moths and small beetles who eat what the bees leave behind.

Above all of this, at the height of your head and just above it, the roof is alive with birds.

Weaver birds do not need a view. A male hangs upside-down from a half-finished nest, calling, displaying, his bright map almost lost in the green dusk. Females inspect. A nest is accepted or abandoned. The accepted one is stitched leaf to leaf with silk, grass to grass, another layer added each season until the roof itself is made of old nests, thick as thatch. The birds are almost constantly in motion — arriving with a single blade of grass held crosswise in the beak, departing with a convulsive chatter that sets the whole colony rustling. The claws click on mud brick when one lands on a rail beside you. The chicks inside the woven chambers gape for insects, and the parents bring them the mites and beetles and small caterpillars they have picked from the walls. A weaver lives a few years. His nests outlive him by decades, and are repaired by birds who never knew him, because the roof must hold.

On the rock faces the swallows have done the same thing in mud. Generation upon generation of nests plastered one upon another until the cliff is not rock but a pebbled crust of old homes, each cup still faintly warm with the memory of feathers. The swallows dart in and out of this wall all day, screaming, carrying mud pellets in their beaks, carrying insects to chicks that thrust their throats up in the dark of the cup. The oldest nests at the bottom have softened into planters; fern spores have taken in the guano-cement, and a green beard hangs from the cliff where once there were only birds.

The mammals keep the hours that no one else keeps, or rather, they keep hours that blur into everyone else's until day and night are only rumours.

Behind a wax curtain in a side chamber, fruit bats hang in a dense, shifting mass, wings folded, occasionally shuffling and squeaking and licking the wall for salt and moisture. Their droppings fall in a steady grey rain onto the compost below, and in that guano-cement orchids root, bromeliads sit, and ferns unfurl. At dusk — if dusk can be said to arrive down here — they unfold and go out through the shafts to feed on fruit, and bring the seeds back in their bellies to drop into abandoned nests and roof cracks. A fig that grows out of an old weaver's nest, its roots holding and prising at once, is a bat's child as much as a tree's. Smaller insect-eating bats thread the light shafts, snatching moths mid-air with a click the human ear barely catches. In the darkest passages, where the silk is thickest and the air is stillest, shrews and small nocturnal rodents and civet-like climbers work the tunnels on soft feet, eating fallen fruit, eating insects, eating the eggs of insects, leaving their own small droppings for the mites.

At the edges, where the sun touches for an hour, the air fills with a different kind of life. Hummingbirds work the flowering vines that have rooted in the roof, hovering, backing, probing, their wings a blur you hear as a needle of sound. They drink from bromeliad cups in the shade as well, one sip at a time from water that has sat dark and still since morning, and the ants drink from the same cups, and later a frog will lay its eggs there. Butterflies crowd the sunlit thatch in clouds of orange and blue, tasting resin and fermenting fruit. Moths take the night shift in the passages, dusting the walls with scale, and cave crickets sit with antennae longer than their bodies, feeling the air for the movement of things that eat them. Tree frogs call from puddles no larger than your hand that sit in roof joints, lined with silk and leaf pulp, their tadpoles grazing pollen from the surface film.

And the plants live here as residents, not as scenery.

Ferns grow in abandoned nests. Mosses saturate themselves on the outside of mud bricks and drink the mist that never becomes rain. Shade orchids send their roots along the inside of walls, drinking the damp that seeps through, blooming in the green dusk where no pollinator could find them if a beetle did not live in the same wall. Broad, soft-leaved plants unfurl in the compost of a bat roost. Vines are cables across a passage, then flowers, then fruit for the bats and birds who planted them in the first place. When a wall is no longer kept, it slumps and becomes a planter. When a nest is abandoned, a seed falls into it and becomes a tree that holds the roof together with its roots. Building and growing are the same process at different speeds, and the plants are the repair crew, the mortar, the insulation, living their whole lives in the house they are helping to hold up.

The fungi belong with them. Soft grey fans spread over damp silk, eating dead leaf, dead silk, dead insect, dead wood. They smell of warm earth. They are eaten in turn by mites and beetles and ant workers who farm and eat and are eaten. Nothing is wasted because nothing has time to be. Fallen fruit rots sweetly on a terrace and is eaten by ants before it finishes rotting. A dead bird's feathers are chewed by larvae. An ant dies on the trail and becomes part of the trail.

No one directs this. The queen termite does not rule; she is fed. The weaver male does not own his nests; he builds and is judged. The leafcutter worker does not know the valley; she knows the pheromone under her feet and the weight of the leaf. Each one does the ordinary work of its kind — plaster, weave, chew, carry, excrete, roost, feed, lick the wall for minerals, drink from a bromeliad, call from a puddle — and because it is warm here, and high, and damp from the air, and because a hundred thousand years of small acts have never been cleared away, the accumulation is total.

That is why they are not afraid of you. When a procession crosses your boot, the workers climb around you, unhurriedly, as if you were a warm stone that had briefly interrupted their road. A weaver lands beside you and clicks his claws on brick and looks at you with one bright eye and returns to his stitching. A bat shifts in the roost overhead and settles. They are the noise, and the damp, and the darkness at noon. They were here before you came down from the ridge, and the light will go first again long before they do.
