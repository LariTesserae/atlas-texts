The residents do not introduce themselves. They are heard first, and only later, in pieces, do you find the bodies that are making the sound.

The first ones are overhead. For a long while the roar has no shape at all — it is simply there, in your teeth and in the slabs under your boots. Then the roar thickens. It braids. A shadow crosses the hard blue sky, and then another, and then the whole bowl seems to tear along a seam as thousands of them come through it in a single ribbon. They are not birds, though the air moves around them as if around wings. Each one is no larger than your closed fist, but behind its head it carries a fan of translucent membrane larger than its body, veined like a leaf and fanned wide. When the sun catches them the ears flare pink and silver. When the ribbon turns away they fold flat against their backs like wet umbrellas.

They are blind. Where eyes should be is smooth skin, unbroken. The scream does not come from a throat. It comes out of the hollow of the body and shakes the ear-fans, and the fans throw the sound back at the walls of the bowl. That is how they see: they sing to the stone and to each other and read the returns. One individual note is hardly anything; a thousand notes braided together make a cloud they can swim in. The flock is a single animal made of eyes. When it veers as one body, it is listening to the echo come back off the far rim. When it shatters, it is not panic. It is feeding.

You see one come close enough to break from the ribbon and hang, for a second, over your head, ears beating slowly, the rim of each fan trembling with its own cry. Spores are already drifting up from somewhere below — glittering dust — and it snaps at them with a quick sideways flick of the whole head, ears and all. Then it is gone, and the note it made is left behind in the braid.

They live, as far as you can tell, in the air and on the stone by turns. At dusk the great ribbon loosens and drops. Nowhere is there a tree for them to roost in, so they settle on the warmest slabs and the leaning spires, press those huge fans down to the rock, and listen to the other world through them. Under the overhangs there are nurseries: rows of translucent, foam-like eggs stuck to the undersides of the stone, rocking a little with every pass of the flock above. The young hang from their mothers by hooks at the edge of the ear-fan, deaf at first, folded down tight, until their own fans unfold and begin to gather the roar for themselves. The ears are pollinators as well as sails. They come away from a spore-burst beaded with glitter and wet clear droplets, and as the flock tears through the bowl they drip what they carry onto bare slabs, and a new pocket of green will flare up weeks later exactly where a ribbon once passed low.

On the ground, the sound has a different texture. Put your palm flat on a slab, as you learn to do, and under the roar you feel a second rhythm: slow, rasping, patient. The grazers.

They are flat, stone-grey, the size of a dinner plate, with no legs you can see and no face you can find. Their bellies are sealed to the rock by a muscular rim, and they move by a ripple travelling under that seal, forward an inch, rasp, forward an inch. At the front edge a mouth works blindly, a tongue set with basalt teeth scraping at a film of mineral and condensed mist. They eat the stone, very slowly, and the moss that inches over it, and the sweet clear fluid that beads near the built things. Their shells are the exact grey-blue of the slabs, grown in concentric layers, polished by years of travel until in places you can see your own palm reflected faintly in them.

They leave the polished trails that cross the bowl, and once you have your palm down you can follow them by touch: here the stone is glass-smooth and slightly warm from friction, there it is rough again. The trails are roads. A dozen grazers will file along the same one in the course of a day, each scraping the same groove a little wider, each tasting what the last one tasted. The oldest trail is the spiral, etched almost away, crossing the whole floor of the bowl in a slow curve toward the broken ring. Half a dozen grazers will be strung along it at once, spaced as if by agreement, following a mark made by something long dead. It leads, you find, to where the mist gathers thickest. They are not lost. They are processing.

They have enemies, and the enemies do not come from the air. A grazer that stops to seal itself against a slab, still working its mouth, will give a sudden shudder that travels through your palm. Under the overhangs, where the upside-down gardens hang, something pours out. It is less a creature than a weather of creatures: thousands of pale, jointed skitterers, each no longer than your thumbnail, with too many legs and a hurry that never seems to flag. They nest in the hollows between the roots of the hanging plants, in cairns of carried mineral, in the empty shells of other grazers. At your shadow they burst out in a sheet, reassemble, pour across a slab in a living river, and vanish into the next seam. That is the demolition crew of the bowl. When a grazer dies, or is caught with its seal unguarded, they find the front seam of its mouth and pry. Minutes later the grey shell is empty and polished clean inside, and the skitterers are gone, carrying mineral away grain by grain to build their nests a little higher.

Between the grazers' rasp and the skitterers' rush, the hoppers keep their own silence. You meet one first as a sound: a single soft *thock* on the stone somewhere ahead of you, then absolute quiet. A hopper is an animal made to listen. Its hind legs are enormous, jointed backwards, and at each knee is a great translucent drumhead, amber in the light. It lands, freezes, and tilts one leg so the tarsus touches the slab and the knee rises, quivering. It is scanning. You can watch it test the stone the way you would tap a table for hollowness, cycling through pitches: the low grind of a grazer two metres off, the sympathetic ring of a slab about to give up a rolling stone, the downward pressure of air when a Listener ribbon passes low. One hopper freezing will freeze the next, and the next, and you will see a ripple of perfect stillness cross a slab of bare rock where you had thought nothing was alive at all. They eat fallen spores and the drift of dust near the ring, snatching with small downward mouths while the knees keep listening. They lay their eggs in the frosted seams, in the lee of the slabs, and the young hop in their parents' tracks, utterly silent except for that soft landing, learning the stone by knee.

They are, like everything here, local. Each species in this bowl seems to have been tuned to one particular note of the roar, and the roar is the sum of all of them tuned together.

Nowhere is that truer than in the pockets of green. You come on them after ten, twenty metres of ringing emptiness, and they flare up so thickly that the lushness itself seems impossible.

The paddle-leaves are the oldest residents. Each leaf is a thick paddle, cool as river stone, with a skin you can press your thumbnail into and not mark. Inside it stores no water but a mint-scented oil, and at dawn, when the volatile mist beads clear and coppery on everything, the leaves open flat and drink it through pores that open and close like mouths. By mid-morning the mist is gone and the pores shut, and a grazer nosing at the edge of a clump will taste only that bitter mint on its tongue, which is enough to turn it away. The leaf tips, when bitten, weep a little oil that coats the grazer's mouth and stays on the shell for days, so you can smell a grazer's recent meal long after it has sealed itself and gone still. Their roots do not search for soil. They etch. Each root ends in a point of acid that dissolves a channel into the basalt, inch by inch, and feeds on what it frees.

The whip-reeds are hollow and jointed, growing in tight feathery tufts, and they sing. Not as the Listeners sing. Their note is single and pure and seems to come from inside the stalk rather than from the wind passing it: as a gust reverses itself mid-air — and it does, here, for no reason you can see — a whole clump will shift pitch, all the tufts together, and the flock overhead will veer toward the sound as if a conductor had raised a hand. Reeds and Listeners are in conversation. A reed that has sung its note too long in one wind will crack at a joint and go silent, and the skitterers will come and carry the pieces away.

The metallic moss is not moss. It is a mat of living filaments, silver-green, each filament ending in a tiny bright bead that drinks the air. It inches. You can watch one at the edge of a mat, the bead reaching, tasting, pulling the filament after it, over the course of an hour. Whole mats are years old, centuries perhaps, creeping toward the sources of mist — the ring, the spires — leaving behind them a faint metallic taste on the slab that the grazers follow with their tongues. Where a mat dies, its filaments collapse into a glittering felt that the hoppers will stand and listen to, for the felt rings differently under their knees.

And then there are the ferns, which do not seem to be residents so much as events. Overnight, in the cold, unprotected by any shelter, a fern unfurls ten centimetres of frond, tight and green and perfect, drinking the night's mist. By noon the heat comes up out of the stone and the frond tears itself apart along pre-made seams, deliberately, violently, scattering its spores as a cloud of glitter dust that hangs in the air for a moment like a small, bright explosion. That is a dinner bell. The Listeners pour down through it. The skitterers pour out under it. The hoppers tilt their legs toward the fall of dust. By evening there is nothing left of the fern but a stalk, which the skitterers strip in the night, and the next cold night another fern will unfurl in exactly the same seam.

Under the overhangs all of this is turned upside down. The paddle-leaves hang down instead of up, drinking mist that gathers in the shadow where frost stays white at midday. The ferns unfurl downward. The roots etch channels in the ceiling of the stone. The Listeners' nurseries are here, the skitterers' towns are here, and the dark under the overhangs is full of a soft, endless ticking, as if the whole bowl were being slowly taken apart by fingernails. The lushness is never spread out. It is always tucked into a seam, a lee, a shadow, and the empty ringing stone between one pocket and the next is as much a part of their habitat as the green itself — the grazers' roads, the hoppers' listening floors, the Listeners' sounding board.

The built things are inhabited too, though not the way you expect.

The three needle spires lean together like a tripod, alloy like bone and wire, humming at a pitch you feel in your chest more than in your ears. Strung between their needles are filaments too fine to see, and they are the second choir of the bowl. The Listeners comb them, hang from them by their ear-hooks to rest in short, folded trances, and lap at the droplets that condense along them. Metallic moss climbs the spires as far as it can reach. Skitterers nest in the joints where the needles meet. The hum is not maintained. It is simply what the wind does to the shape, and the flock has learned to tune its own cry to it, rising a little when the spires rise, falling when they fall.

The great broken ring is buried to its hub in the slab and turns, slowly, against the wind. Around it, at millimetric precision, a constellation of stones hangs in the air. They have been there long before you and will be there long after. Moss rootlets grip the undersides of some of them, drinking the bright fluid that beads thickly in the ring's shelter. A Listener will sometimes alight on a levitating stone, ears flapping to hold its balance, and the stone will not so much as shiver. Dust near the ring falls, very slightly, upward, caught in the slow current, and the moss filaments reach up to take it. Every now and then one of the stones loses its hold, drops a few centimetres with a soft knock, cracks, and the whole bowl knows: grazers and skitterers converge within minutes, and the stone is eaten down to grit, and a new stone is already drifting up into the orbit to take its place, as if the ring were hatching them.

The shelter half-sunk into the rim is the strangest tenancy of all. Fused stone and smoked glass, a door open on one cold chair, and inside, impossibly, faintly warm. You sit in the chair — it is cold, as a chair should be — and the warmth is in the air around you, in the walls. Look closely at the smoked glass and you will see scratches upon scratches, and if you keep looking at them they will be there, and if you look away and look back they will be gone, repaired. Something lives in the glass. Not a body you can point to: a community of translucent filament-builders, no larger than dust, who scurry in the moment your eyes leave to seal a scratch with fresh glass, and in their work they give off heat. The room is their nest and their workshop. The chair is cold because no one sits in it. They do not need a chair. They came with the builders, or they came after, and the bowl has absorbed them the way it absorbed everything else.

And everywhere, under everything, the finest residents: the mist itself is not empty. The coppery taste on your tongue is life, a breath of filament-seeds and microbes that ride the volatile droplets and rain out again by mid-morning onto leaf and tongue and wing. The frost crust that crunches like salt under your boots is worked over by grain-sized pale things that come out in the shadow and retreat as the sun hits. The polished trails are coated with a living mineral slime that the grazers' tongues harvest. None of it is water. All of it is food, and sound, and erosion, rebroadcast.

This is the settled state. Nothing here is separate from the noise. The Listeners' cry chips a flake of stone from the rim when the flock turns hard against the echo. The grazers' scraping polishes the slabs that the hoppers listen through. The skitterers carry the chips away and build. The reeds sing the wind into a note the flock can read. The moss eats the polish and makes it soft again. A fern detonates, a flock veers, a gust reverses, a stone knocks and rolls a few centimetres and knocks again, and a new shoot is already in the place of the old one, unfurling in the cold. Growth and destruction do not cancel each other out here. They are the same motion, and the bowl is the shape that motion returns to, over and over.

At the rim the world of the residents ends in a straight line. You can see it: moss cut off as if by shears, plants stopping mid-leaf, a grazer's trail ending at an invisible wall and the grazer itself turning with great slowness and sealing, a hopper landing one last time on the boundary slab, tilting its leg, recoiling from the silence beyond and hopping back. A Listener ribbon will cross to the very edge and turn as one body, as if it had struck glass. One step beyond, the roar drops to almost nothing and you can hear your own heartbeat, and the air is merely thin and still. The residents do not leave, because outside the bowl there is nothing for them to hear themselves with. The bowl is a room, and they are its permanent, turbulent, deafening inhabitants.

Stay long enough and you stop trying to sort the roar into sources. It becomes weather inside your head. Cool, high, dry, bright, empty stone between the green pockets; paddle-leaves drinking copper mist off dry rock; ears fanned against the sky, tongues rasping the slabs, knees tilted to the stone, stones hanging in the air, and all of it, endlessly, destroying and remaking itself in exactly the same shape, and making the sound that holds the shape together.
