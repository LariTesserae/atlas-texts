If you stay long enough to stop expecting company, that is when you begin to find it — not all at once, not in any place you could point to again, but in the way the emptiness thins, for a minute, in one spot and not another.

Most of the bowl is only water and stone and your own muffled movement. You can swim the whole of it, up one wall and down another, shouldering through veils of turbidity that never quite clear, and see nothing that moves of its own accord. That is not an absence that settles. It is an absence that keeps making room. Life here is low and scattered to the point of rumor, and what there is does not gather. It holds its patch, its current, its echo, and lets the rest go void.

The first thing that is alive does not look alive at all. In the protected cracks, where the fresh black rock has broken clean and still weeps warm mineral water from inside its bright new planes, there are films. Olive, translucent, thin as breath on glass. They are not on the chop where the water will not hold a shape, and they are not on the open ledges where the rain strikes and tears. They are in the lee of a splinter, under a slab that has just sheared and settled, in a seam that rings a little longer than the others when a current hits it.

You come upon them because the color is wrong — a faint green-grey deepening in a place that is otherwise black and white with foam — and then you realize the color is moving. Not growing in the way a plant grows. Tearing, and regrowing. A veil of sediment settles over the film and blurs it. A small upwelling lifts it at one edge and it folds. Light from the pale ceiling above finds it and it brightens for a breath, then dulls again under turbidity. Where the rain hisses longest on a ledge above the water, a film will appear by evening that was not there in the morning, as if the hiss itself had brushed it into being. It eats nothing you can name. It takes the warm, gritty water on its skin and holds it a moment longer than stone does. That may be food. That may be all the food a place this new can offer.

Above the main chop, where the water climbs the walls in sheets and hangs in the suspended rain, there is another patch, and then a long empty stretch, and then another. Thin, hair-like strands trail from ledges, bleached almost white, trembling in the rain that does not fall so much as exist. They have no roots you can find, and no leaves. They come and go with the level of the water, which rises and falls by handbreadths in minutes. When the level recedes they are left exposed, glistening, clearly not stone; when it rises they dissolve back into the film from which they came. They tremble more when the wind tries to find a way through the rim and fails, and less when the bowl is only ringing. If you touch one — and you learn not to, because it tears so easily — it feels like wet hair, and cold against the warm water, and for an instant the two temperatures argue on your skin.

Nothing flies in that rain wall. Nothing with wings worries the surface. Everything that lives here is in the water, or touching the water, or remembering that it was.

Deeper, where the bowl narrows and the roar is greatest, there is the ear.

It is fixed to a shard near the deepest point, on a splinter of black glassy rock that must have fallen recently, because nothing has worn it smooth. It is pale, almost translucent at the edge and whiter at the base, and about the size of a hand held open, or a little larger. It is not a shell. It has no hard ridge. It is a flap, and it opens and closes with the percussion — not with the water, exactly, but with the boom underneath the water. When a deep note rolls through the bowl, the flap lifts. When the note passes, it settles. It filters. Whatever the turbulence brings it — torn algae, veils of sediment that have not learned to lie down, bubbles that rise and do not break — the flap catches on the inhale of sound and lets the rest slump away on the exhale. Its waste, if it has any, is a faint plume that joins the turbidity and is gone.

It does not move from its shard. It has been there long enough that silt has half-buried its base and algae has found the quiet side of its stem. Long enough, in this place, to be considered old. You can watch it for minutes and learn its rhythm before you learn anything else about it. It does not turn toward you. It turns toward the center, toward the loudest eddy at that moment, and your vertical tilts with it, slightly, as if you too were a flap.

The swimmers are harder to hold, because they are almost water themselves.

They are blind, translucent, elongate things that navigate by the pressure of the sound itself. They have no eyes that catch light, no fins that beat. When they pass you at the shoulder — and they will, once in an hour, a dark pressure that is suddenly a body and then only water again — you know them not by shape but by disturbance. The roar dips, as if something has taken the note out of it. Then, if you turn, you may catch a flash. Only when they turn does light from the surface rake across them and show a skin, a hollow length, a line of small flaps along the underside that open and close in sequence like the ear at the bottom, only faster, only along a body.

They ride the booms like slow weather. They do not swim so much as orient. A current strikes fresh rock, the rock rings, and the ringing shapes where sediment settles — and the swimmers turn toward the ringing, because in the ringing a veil of food arrives a fraction later. They drift upward in a loud current and hang there trembling, suspended from the sound itself, then descend on the receding wash. Their bodies make the sound do a small detour. You feel it in your teeth before you see them.

There are not many. You may count — if counting is possible in a place where distance measures in seconds of reverberation and the same bubble passes you three times in the same eddy — perhaps half a dozen in the whole bowl. One is long and ribbon-like and keeps to the middle, where the stepping stones lead from nowhere and stop. It follows a spiral that is not a route and repeats it when time stutters in a whirlpool, passing the same stone, the same bubble, the same note again, as if it had not just passed. One is smaller, rounder, and stays nearer the eastern wall, near the low ring of slabs, where the water is a little quieter and the hum under the roar is a little sweeter. One you will only ever know by the flash where it turns at the far side of the bowl, nearer in echo than a thing at your shoulder.

They do not meet, except by accident in a collision of currents, and when they do they pass through each other's booms without slowing. They do not call. The booms are the call, and the place makes them. If the sound stopped, you suspect they would not know which way to flow, and you suspect they know it. They live by listening because there is nothing else reliable — no light that holds still, no current that holds still, no up that can be trusted. The sound comes first, and they come after it.

Around the shell pile, you find the nearest thing to a home.

The white shells are fused into the rock near the wall where the water shallows and then drops again, arranged — arranged, you think, at first, because they are too regular to be a scatter, and then you doubt the word, because there are no tool marks anywhere in the whole basin, no metal, no glass, no plastic, no light. Stone, shell, bone, silt — nothing else. Some of the shells are empty and silted. Some are open to the current, trembling a little. The algae films have found the lee side of the pile and thicken there, olive on white, and the swimmers' veils pass over it and stir it. A fragment of bone, clean and bright inside where it has broken, lies half-buried at the edge, and the warm mineral water weeps from the rock behind it.

Something made this pile, shell by shell, in a ring that now hums in sympathy when a current strikes it low and sweet under the roar. Whatever it was is not here now to tend it, or it is here only as the shells themselves. No one with hands lives in this bowl. No one builds now. The built things — the knee-high ring of flat black slabs half-silted against the eastern wall, the broken half-wall running sharp into deep water, the stepping stones stopping mid-bowl — are older than the water and younger than any tool, and they are used now the way the ear uses its shard: as a place to be, to catch a note, to let sediment settle where the ringing is longest.

Inhabitation, here, is mostly that. Not occupation. A film that learns a crack. A strand that learns a ledge. A flap that learns a shard. A swimmer that learns a boom. Each one too new to have a name, each one trying out a shape for a loud minute at a time and letting the water take it back if it does not hold.

When you move through them, you are a disturbance they can use. The films brighten under your shadow as turbidity lifts. The hairs tremble harder in the rain your passing has stirred. The ear at the bottom opens toward the center where your splash has added its note. The swimmers turn — all of them, at once, in different places, toward a boom that is not theirs — and flash, briefly, silver-white, and are gone.

That is the whole web, if it is a web: the rain hisses and the algae appears where it hisses; the turbulence tears the algae and the swimmers ride the tear; the ear filters what they stir; the shells and slabs ring and shape where the next film will grow. Nothing eats anything cleanly. Nothing stays. The place has not yet learned its final shape, and neither have they learned theirs. They live in the attempt, submerged, warm, and small, listening to a world being assembled badly and beautifully at the top of everything, waiting — if waiting is the word — to see what the roar decides to let them become next.
