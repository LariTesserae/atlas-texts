Nothing lives on the plain. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing to unlearn.

What lives, lives only where the stone dips. If you find one of the hollows — a courtyard of moss in a country of black rock — you have found a population, or rather, you have found an individual that has learned to look like a population. From above it is green so dense it stops looking like green. From inside it is not green at all. It is architecture, and labor, and appetite, all happening at once and at three different speeds.

The moss is the outermost skin. It grows a mattress thick, layer upon layer, each layer holding the damp the others exhaled. It does not drink from rain. It drinks from fog that settles and refuses to leave, leaf by leaf, cell by cell, in a silence so complete that the act of drinking makes no sound. Underneath its spongy surface, the root-mat is already moving things. Not growing, exactly — trafficking. Minerals up from the basalt seams, sugars down from the leaves, water sideways from a pore to a pore where black water hides in the rock. The roots tap the stone like straws, patiently, secretly, and the stone gives up what it has been holding since before there was weather to hold it.

Beneath the roots is the true resident. The fungi.

White, intricate, almost lacework, they spread in the dark between moss and stone where it is warmer by a degree or two and damp by a degree more. They are the smelters of this place. They break basalt grain by grain, they pull nitrogen out of air too thin for anything else to bother with, they ferment — this is the faint steam you feel rising in the cold — and they sort the result into small chemical packets and pipe them through hyphae at a pace that does not pause for night, because there is no night here to pause for. If the moss is a skin and the roots are hands, the fungi are a stomach that never stops, and a heart that beats by chemistry.

The leaves above them do not photosynthesize in the familiar way. They open toward the aurora. Their undersides are faintly conductive, a little coppery to the touch, and they gather charged light and the ozone that comes with it. When the green-grey curtains shift overhead — slow, unrolling — the whole mat shifts with them. Thousands of fronds angle together. A wave crosses the patch from one edge to the other, takes a minute, and is gone. It is the nearest thing this place has to breathing, and it is not breathing. It is harvesting.

This is where the animals begin, but even here the line is not clear.

Under the moss, in the warm seam between moss and fungus, live the mites. They are piston-legged, armored, and almost entirely occupied with work. They click into a mineral crack, lock on, and pump detritus backward — waste, grit, the fine dust the fungi have already processed once — to be processed again. They have no eyes worth mentioning and no voice at all. Sound would be waste here; the air cannot afford it. They live in casings they build from leaf-fibre and mineral dust, little houses they carry and abandon and rebuild by the hour, not from restlessness but because the material is abundant and the old casing is better compost. A mite will spend its entire life — which is measured in weeks of constant minute-by-minute turnover — inside a square meter of moss, and will never know a second square meter exists.

Above the mites, across the leaf surfaces, move the grazers. Blind, translucent, soft as larvae, they travel in herds that are not herds in any social sense, only in density. They eat the factory's excess — the loose spores, the over-sweet sap, the broken edge of a frond — and in passing they fertilize it. Their feet leave a faint chemical trail that the moss can read. They have no need to call to each other. The herd moves because the mat is food and the mat moves because the herd has been there.

And the mat, in turn, eats them.

Some ferns, the low rosettes with overlapping fronds, are carnivorous in a way that is not dramatic at all. A grazer lingers too long on a particular leaf, feeding on the same spot, and the leaf curls. Not snaps. Curls, slowly, taking the grazer by the foot, absorbing it through the skin while it continues, for a while, to chew. Some fungi, when winter deepens — when the cold is most absolute and the growth is fastest — exude a poison in precise, seasonal doses that thins the grazers to a sustainable number, then the fungi recycle the bodies into nitrogen and start again. Predation here is not an event. It is a thermostat. It is inventory control.

If you watch a patch long enough, from inside it, you stop seeing individuals at all. You see turnover. A plant at the edge dies, is absorbed, is resurrected as spores. A mite dies inside its casing, the casing is taken by another mite, repaired, abandoned. A grazer is eaten and becomes the next frond. The population is not stable in the way a forest is stable. It is stable in the way a factory floor is stable — everything moving, nothing accumulating, almost no loss.

That is why they grow when it is coldest. Spring means nothing at this height. Cold is the catalyst they have waited out erosion to learn. They grow downward into warmth and upward into light at the same time, an engine straddling two worlds, roots and fungi warming themselves by fermentation while leaves drink the aurora. In the deepest dark, when the aurora is at its most violet and the twilight at the edge of the sky is at its most permanent, the spore-columns at the patch's rim rise. They do not drift. They burst, on schedule, ballistic clusters launched on the rare updrafts that the plateau permits. For a few minutes, a spore is the only solitary thing in this world — alone in the thin air over empty miles — before it lands in another hollow, another courtyard of stone, and founds a new factory, identical, instant, fully staffed with fungi and roots and mites already packed into the seed.

Between patches, there is nothing. Within them, there is also — after a while — nothing that is not part of the machine. Even the large things are only visitors, and temporary ones.

High above, on the edge of the aurora, ride the birds — or the things that have decided to be birds. They are not frequent. You may wait hours and see only the place where their wings should be, a slow rowing in the black. Their wingbeat is so slow it seems to row through air, which here is almost an insult to the word air. They do not land. When the evening-that-never-ends comes, they descend spiraling, hook one leg into the rim of a patch, and hang upside down.

They hang like that for a long time. The patch feeds them static from its leaves. You can see the faint discharge at the point of contact, a blue-grey flicker no brighter than the moss itself. In return — because everything here has learned to use everything else — the birds shed. Flakes of skin, a feather or two if they still have feathers, a thin rain of mineral-rich droppings that the mites below immediately dismantle and pipe back to the fungi. The birds are not residents. They are clients. They come for charge, they leave fertilizer, they haul themselves back up into the aurora's edge and disappear. Nothing was made for them. Everything has learned to use them.

Below the patches, beneath the moss and the fungi and the stone itself, there is a slower pulse that the inhabitants answer without knowing it. Geothermal cracks tick and exhale under the plain, warming the undersides of the hollows from below. The mites feel it as a change in the seam's dampness. The fungi feel it as a change in fermentation rate. The roots follow it, root by root, as the whole patch migrates — a hand's width in a season, toward a new seam of damp stone, leaving a ghost-green trail behind it that will take a century to fade. The patch is not rooted in the sense of staying. It is rooted in the sense of not being able to move fast enough to be noticed.

And far below everything, miles down where weather still lives, the glaciers grind. Presses the size of valleys, lifting nutrient dust, sifting it upward for the wind to carry here. The inhabitants never see the dust arrive. It settles into a hollow, is taken up by a root, is broken by a fungus, is fed to a mite, is eaten by a fern, is turned to heat and steam and finally to a spore that will be launched to another hollow. The whole plateau is one system running an industrial process on a planetary scale, and the living part of it is small, damp, warm to the hand, and mostly silent.

If you lie down in one of the hollows and put your ear to the moss — though sound does not carry, and the moss does not speak — you can feel it instead. A faint vibration. The clicking of mites in their seams. The *tok* of ice cracking somewhere impossibly far off, felt in the sternum. The slow wave of fronds turning toward a light that is not bright enough to read by, but bright enough to work by.

They will not notice you. That is not hostility. It is efficiency. You are not part of the inventory. You are the weather, passing at knee height, breaking against the stone, and then the silence closes behind you like water while the patches continue to work in the dark — lush and cold and damp and utterly, industrially alive, with no engineer in them and no need for one.
