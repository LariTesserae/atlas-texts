If you lie down in it — which you will, because the peat gives and sighs back up under your weight — the first lives are the ones you do not hear at all.

They are in the black surface of the bog itself. In the moss, springtails jump from your footprints as they fill bright with sky. Mites work the damp undersides of the sedge. Bacteria and fungi hold the peat together from below, patient and dark, turning wet air into food for roots. The lichen on the windward faces of the basalt are lives too, crusts of rust and sage and sulphur laid down in perfect maps that never quite touch each other, as if they had agreed, long ago, not to crowd. They grow so slowly that to say they are still is not quite true, and to say they are moving is also not true. They are simply lasting, the way the rounded ribs of rock are lasting, holding water in the palms of stone and giving it back a thread at a time.

The moss is the main animal of the place, if animal is a large enough word. It is a skin, worn velvet, settled hummocks and moss-filled gullies at rest. Along the windward edge it stands up in tight, erect tufts, and when the wind is right and wet those tufts make a note. Not a rustle. A clear, thin note, as if the slope were breathing through a reed. It changes pitch as a cloud passes over and thickens the air. Nobody explains it here. The moss does not need explaining. It is answering the wind, and the wind is answering the rim.

The frogs answer the moss.

They are small, stone-colored, skin like wet leather, and they do not live in ponds. There are no ponds deep enough to be called ponds. They live in the saturated moss itself, which is pond enough for a creature that size. You will not see them first. You will hear a croak from beneath the turf, from nowhere in particular, and then another, and then a chorus that keeps perfect, overlapping time — no one quite on the beat, no one quite off it, each keeping his own hummock. They keep their distance by song, as everything here does. Rest in the moss and they become stone again, indistinguishable from the wet basalt veined with quartz. Sing, and the whole bowl is populated from under your boots.

The plants that make the skin do not waste height. Sedges comb sideways in the wind, rattling like dry paper when it funnels over the rim and circles inside. Cushion plants are stitched tight to the ground, domes no higher than a hand, holding the heat of the sun against the perpetual cool. Dwarf shrubs grow to the knee and no farther, leaves thick as the end of a thumb, storing what the clouds wring out on the stones in the morning. The flowers sit in the moss instead of rising above it: yellow stars, purple bells, white trumpets that face down to keep their pollen dry and open fully only after the fog has passed, as if they are listening for it. They open and close with the unbuilding of clouds, sun warm on your back, wind cutting straight through your coat, breath visible even at noon.

On the flowers, when the sun is right, are the flies and the bees. They do not arrive in swarms that hide the flowers. They arrive in numbers that hum, a low, busy note that rises and falls with the cloud. They work the intervals — a half hour of bright air, then rags of fog, then sun again — and they have learned the bowl's strange weather better than any visitor. Their hum is the sound of the place being pollinated at height, too high, too cold, too wind-scoured by every lowland rule, and yet fed every morning by fog pouring over the rim.

Between the cushions run the shrews. Mouse-quick, they dart from moss to moss and vanish. Their lives are mostly underground in the peat, in tunnels the moss roof covers, but at the surface they become visible as motion: a thread across the green, a run that ends. They are prey, and the bowl is small enough that you can watch the whole chase happen. A raptor works the thermals off the leeward cliff all day, a dark shape crossing the entire sky in twenty seconds when the cloud sea below is a flat white ocean and the bowl is a sunlit island alone in it. It stoops now and then at a shrew, and the shrew's thread ends. Then the moss closes over again, and the dripping resumes, dozens of pitches from the weeping cliffs and from under stones and from the eaves of moss, louder as the world gets smaller in fog.

Above the rim hang the pipits. There are birds in flocks, and flocks of one. The pipits hang in the wind and pour out long, glassy notes, answering each other from far across the bowl so that you have to turn in a full circle to find them. They do not perch much. They sing to hold their distance, to say: this sweep of sedge is mine, that twenty paces of open moss between boulders is mine, and I am still here though you cannot see me for the fog at five paces. At evening they make their last long calls from the rim and are gone, and for a minute the bowl is only wind and water.

On the far hummocks, solitary, moves the grazer. It is like a small mountain camelid, hardy, alone for days at a time, moving slowly across the rolled saddle, cropping the knee-high shrubs, stepping carefully so its soft feet do not tear the peat. Its coat is wet most of the time. It does not huddle; there is nothing to huddle with, and no other grazer to be lonely beside. It keeps its own long interval. Then, suddenly, it sprints — not away, but at the fox that has learned the same bowl and has come low over the moss hoping to use the dripping to cover its steps. The chase is long and visible at this scale. You can watch it happen. The fox thinks better of it by the next hummock, and the camelid settles again to grazing, as if nothing had interrupted the slow business of being alive in a place that keeps everything wet and weathered and unfinished.

The fox is the other hunter, the one that has to work for its living where everything keeps its distance. It knows the cairn line, the windbreak hut, the stone ring whose centre has filled with moss now. Nothing built here will be finished, and the fox has no use for buildings except as cover. The birds now nest in the hut wall, a roofless windbreak of uncut stone fitted without mortar on the lee side, big enough for one person to crouch against the wind. The birds have taken it for what it is: a dry place above the seep, a wall that breaks the wind that circles inside the rim. They sing from it in the morning, small lives inside a human mark.

Humans, or whatever made the nearest kin of humans, have never lived here. They have only been scattered here, in transit. There is no village. There are four things, and perhaps a fifth half-buried: the line of ankle-high cairns weighting the sane path across the bog when fog makes the hummocks indistinguishable; the hut; the shallow stone ring for a fire that was never allowed to grow; the woven basket, half-rotted, filled with another stone; and a tool, a bone handle polished by hands, a flaked stone head lashed and unlashed a hundred times. No metal. No provision for winter. Someone who needed to cross, or to pray, or to wait out a storm, left evidence to weather at the same pace as everything else. Their lives here were brief, and largely silent — visitors lower their voices when they hear the whistling moss — and the place has taken their marks into its own long, established state.

That is the rule that repeats for everything that lives here, from springtail to camelid to pipit to raptor: water comes from the air and leaves sideways through the moss; the ground stays saturated but never floods; the sun is strong and the cold is constant, and both can change in a breath; the rim defines the world; distance is kept by song.

At night the temperature drops fast and freeze beads the moss with ice that will melt by ten. The sedge stiffens. The frogs start again, deeper. The springs upwell in the hollows, the tarn migrates a few feet across the peat as the season swells it, the stones tick as they cool. The lives settle into their own ringing reality — frogs under turf, lichen on windward stone, insects asleep in the closed trumpets, shrews in their tunnels, the camelid alone on a hummock, the fox somewhere under the rim — holding the small, wet bowl together until morning fog comes over the edge again to feed it.
