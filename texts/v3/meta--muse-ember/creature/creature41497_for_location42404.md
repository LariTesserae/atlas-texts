You hear them first, before you can pick any one of them out of the green mass.

Hammering in the lanes — slate snapped with a stone hammer, wood pegged with hardwood, because there is almost no iron here, only copper hand-worked into needles and pins. Market women calling from stalls shaded by tree ferns. Goats bleating from a terrace above your head while guinea pigs squeak from under the floor you are standing on. Dogs barking at the fog, at nothing you can see. Birds in the eaves starting up on schedule, hummingbirds scolding at the orchids, insects making a low, continuous roar in the warm air, and underneath it all, night and day, the irregular tick of water dripping from nets into clay jars.

That's who lives in Tawaq. Not one kind of creature, and not separated very neatly one from another. The town is too small for separation. Everyone leans.

The people lean first. Houses are not detached. Stone below, timber and wattle above, slate shingled and weighted with moss, and then a garden on the roof — potatoes, tubers, onions in shallow damp earth — and the next house's terrace is your ceiling. The next house's vine runs to your balcony. So the people who live in them share walls, share shade, share the same twelve slick steps up, and get winded on them. Even born here, even with lungs full of mist from infancy, you learn to pace. You carry a bucket — a jar with its lid tied down with string — and your heart gets loud in your ears. Your skin sweats and does not dry. Your clothes never quite dry. You do not wash, you do not bathe, you do not tear a leaf except the gardener who needs it. Children learn that before they can read, because water does not live in rivers here. It lives inside things.

They are ordinary, tired, funny, busy. Women mending the great fiber nets that comb the mist, seated in the lanes where the light is flat and green-grey and never sharp enough to cast a shadow. Old men with stone hammers, re-laying a wall in spring with round river-stones and flat slate gathered from the ancient slopes below, stones taken from a ruined house and cleaned by a grandchild. Roof-tenders who do not so much repair as coax the moss to root and hold the shingles down. Gardeners, the only hands allowed to break a leaf. Net-keepers, who watch the poles like sails between the houses, wring the cloths hung overnight, listen for the slowing bell that does not tell time but tells them the mist is thickening again.

Nobody arrives new. Nobody leaves very far. A house takes years to become itself. A terrace takes a lifetime for its wall to settle. So a family may live its whole life inside the settling of one wall. They keep their tools oiled with plant fat, they salt their dew-soaked greens against the damp, they light oil lamps in the market hall at noon because inside is dimmer than out — though out is never bright — and they blow them out at dusk when the frogs begin. The frogs know the hour better than the sun, which never really sets here so much as thins.

With the people, inside the same walls, are their other households.

Under the floor, guinea pigs. They live on warmth and scraps and squeak through the boards at market hours and at feeding hours, a whole hidden population under the stone ground-floor houses, kept for food and for company and because a body under a floor in a house that never dries is a useful thing. On the terraces above your head, goats, tethered where the potato vines are thickest, bleating down at the lane, climbing the impossible shared stairs as if they were born to them. One lean dog per few houses, barking at the fog that moves through the wall and takes the edge of the plateau with it, barking at voices that turn out to be goats, or children, or nothing.

In the eaves, the larger birds nest in the thatch of slate and timber and scream awake on schedules you can set a clock by. They are not pets. They are alarm, announcement, and opinion. At orchids, hummingbirds hover and scold, furious little proprietors, fighting over a doorframe flower as if it were a market stall. All day they work the orchids that grow not in a garden but out of doorframes and market stalls and cracks in walls, each flower a bright, damp mouth, each bird taking its dew and its nectar and leaving its quarrel.

And in the gutters and on the walls, in bromeliads big as buckets, in every perfect cup of dew a bromeliad holds against its own leaves, lives a frog no bigger than a thumbnail. It peeps at night loud enough to wake the street, amplified by the cup it lives in like a little earthen pot. Hundreds of them. A whole life — born, calling, mating, dying — may be spent without ever touching soil, in a teaspoon of water held inside a plant. You could live here a year and never notice dry ground underfoot, because the frogs are the ground, in miniature, everywhere above you.

They are not the only small lives. Insects make the warm air itself hum. Fungus softens fence posts from within and does its quiet work of unmaking, so that things can be remade. Lichen greens the steps and makes them slick, and everyone knows by foot where it is. Moss, on the north side of every stone — which here is every side, because the light never stays long enough to dry anything — holds roofs together. Ferns grow waist-high in the lanes, tree ferns spread over roofs so the slate is always in shade, as if the plants had been hired to cool the houses and shade the people and, not incidentally, to catch the cloud before it can be called rain.

Because that is the real household here: the plants are not decoration. Vines run balcony to balcony so that in summer the quarter is one continuous green mass with doors cut in it, and a neighbor can pass a tuber, or a gossip, or a cutting, without coming down to the lane. Orchids keep the doors. Bromeliads keep the frogs and keep the dew. Potatoes and tubers and onions keep the people, their roots shallow because there is nowhere deep to go, fed by soil that is deep and black and ancient on top of the dome — decades of leaves rotted into it, generations of the dead turned into it.

The dead are still residents. They are buried shallow in the garden soil so the potatoes will be strong, and a tree fern is planted over each grave, so the dead literally shade the living. Their shade falls on a roof, on a lane, on a grandchild's house built of re-laid stones from a house that fell before the grandfather was born. Names of the ancestors who sealed the cistern under the market square are gone. The cistern remains, and the town remains, older than any single life in it, changing by millimeters a century, a root thickening a mortar joint, a garden shifting a hand's breadth from one roof to the next.

At evening the light doesn't change so much as thin further, to a green twilight that could be noon or midnight. Lamps are blown out. The larger birds settle in the eaves. The goats quiet on their terraces. The guinea pigs go on squeaking under the floors, softer. The frogs take over the bromeliads, hundreds of thumbnail throats peeping in the dripping percussion of jars filling, drop by drop, from nets and roofs and dished stone lanes.

And up and down the shared stairs go the net-keepers, checking the poles in the increasingly thick mist, tying down another lid, telling a child in passing — you do not wash, you do not spill, this leaf is where we drink — while the whole of Tawaq, animal and plant and fungus and human and dead, stays damp, stays close, stays awake in its cloud, leaning on each other so as not to fall.
