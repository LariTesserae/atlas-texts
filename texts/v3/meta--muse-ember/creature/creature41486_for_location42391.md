At first, it looks uninhabited in the ways a living place is supposed to be inhabited.

There is no green to speak of. The stunted street trees stand in a line, their leaves thin and dusty, holding no shade, offering no nesting beyond a swallow's brief dart. The reeds in the drains are thin clumps. The salt grass on the dike crest is sparse. A place this wet, this hot, should be loud with jungle. Instead the vegetation is only a sparse wash, survivors clinging to the edges of pavement where the fill is not quite sealed.

The life is not in the trees. It is in the water film.

It starts there, on the canals. The water is warm and still and filmed. A crust of algae thickens it, pale green and grey, breaking only where a catfish turns just under the surface, mouth opening at the film. The catfish live in the cooling canals and the ditches, in channels too shallow and too warm for anything elegant. They do not roam. They turn in that same stretch, generation after generation, feeding on detritus and whatever the pumps lift and drop again. They are stunted, mud-colored, patient. The riprap along the sharp banks is unrounded and new, and the snails climb it, and climb the weeping concrete walls above it, leaving clean tracks through the condensation. The walls sweat all day. The snails live in that sweat.

Beneath the film, invisible, the bacteria and the rot are working. In the drains, in the flooded lots, in every permanent puddle where a road dips, there is a skin of life that feeds the algae, that feeds the midges. It is mundane and abundant and it never pauses. Nothing freezes. Nothing is flushed out. The water level is held exactly where it is, by the pumps, and so the whole shallow world is held with it — no flood to scour, no drought to expose the mud. Birth and death balance down to the size of a cell.

From that film, the sound comes up.

Frogs call from every drain canal. Not in chorus the way frogs do in a marsh at night, rising and falling. Here it is continuous and overlapping, one drain answering another, a male in one trench, another in the next, no leader, no pause, because there is no night for it to be a night chorus for. Their skin is made for this damp that never dries. They sit in water that is exactly their temperature. They call from clean, angular trenches with sharp banks, as if the trenches were made for them. Perhaps they were, without anyone intending it.

And above the flooded lots, where the standing water reflects the grey sky unbroken by wind, the mosquitoes rise in a visible sheet. You walk through it. It does not scatter the way an evening swarm will elsewhere; there is no evening to scatter into. Dragonflies patrol lower, over the same lots, over the steam vents, their wings catching the dim yellow-grey light. Midges swarm thick around the street lamps, which stay on under the eaves all day because the light never clarifies, and around the vents where hot moist air meets cooler metal and condenses. The lamps are on, so the midges are out, all day, humming audibly. They have no darkness to rest in.

Under the factory roofs, swallows dart and nest. Their mud nests are softened by the damp, remade and remade. They feed on the midges and dragonflies and mosquitoes, endless supply, no migration needed, no winter to force it. They live their whole lives inside the bound — far dike, far fence, far water tower — and never need to leave it.

At dusk that never quite comes, the rats run the pipe lines. They run them at noon too. The overhead steam lines march between buildings on steel trestles, and the rats use them as roads, sure-footed on pipes that sweat and drip. They feed on spilled grain from the depot, on refuse from the shops on main street, on the detritus the catfish do not take. Their fur is always damp. They have no predator large enough to check them, and no cold to thin them.

That is the fauna, and it is louder than the people, and it does not need the people. It feeds on warmth, on algae, on heat leaking from the machines. But the people are there, too, and they have fitted themselves into the same loop without ever quite rising above the animals in importance. They are not the owners of the equilibrium. They are its tenders.

There are not many of them. A small town spread thinly on a grid. Low houses and low shops along main street, windows glowing in the perpetual late afternoon. The pump house with its painted sign. The generator hall. The processing works. The depot, the warehouses. Between the buildings, mostly emptiness: wet paved yards, chained-off lots full of standing water, gravel hardstands where nothing is stored. The people live in that grid the way the frogs live in the drains — within a range so small you can see its far edge from almost anywhere.

They sweat constantly. It is not a season. Their skin, their clothes, their hair, are damp from the moment they cross the causeway and never dry. A towel hung in a house would not dry. A shirt worn at the start of a shift is wet by the middle of it. They move slowly in the heat that sits on the surface, conserving effort, wiping neck and forehead with a handkerchief that is already damp. They breathe the thick air in and out as damp. Their sleep is shallow and fragmented, because there is no quiet hour and no dark hour to mark it. The generator hall drones, the electric motors whine, the pumps thud in their pits, the frogs call, the condensation drips — all of it carried by water and concrete. Sleep comes in shifts, upstairs over a shop, in a low house with a fan turning, when the body decides, not when the light changes. No one with a watch would have any use for it here. They learn the pattern by dampness and by the cycle of a valve sighing.

Their work is not to make anything go faster. It is to keep everything exactly as it is. A man at the pump house checks the level — no higher, to flood the roads, no lower, to expose the mud. A man in the generator hall listens to the engines the way another might listen to an animal breathing, knowing each one's note. If a pump changes its note, someone comes to tend it. The pressure valves sigh on a cycle. The water tower drips from a joint, steadily, all day, in the same place. They wipe condensation from pipes before it drips where it should not. They clear a drain of reed before it chokes. They shout across a yard because the drone masks a normal voice, and a truck reverses somewhere across the flooded ground, its beep bouncing off wet concrete. Occasionally someone leans in a shop doorway under the eaves, in the lamplight, and watches the water in the street.

They eat and drink in those low shops. They live in houses among the warehouses like the swallows live under the roofs. Their children, if there are children, do not play in the flooded lots — those belong to the mosquitoes and the dragonflies and the frogs. They stay on the pavement, on the hardstands, in the narrow strip of dry concrete by a wall that sweats. Old people sit under the eaves where a lamp glows, watching the same puddle in the same pothole. No one arrives in a rush and no one leaves in a rush. Death is met the way everything else is met: quietly, and then the balance restores itself. A new tender takes the shift. The pump does not change its note.

And the machines — they are residents, too, in their way. The pumps in their pits, the engines in the generator hall, the fans turning in the heat-shimmer, the pipes humming. They are not frenzied. Much of it looks almost idle, but it never stops. It maintains the place. The engines make the heat that makes the steam that makes the condensation that makes the drip that feeds the puddles that the pumps lift again. The people feed the machines oil and attention; the machines feed the animals heat and water; the animals feed on algae and detritus and each other; the people sweat and breathe and leave waste and skin and hair in the drains. It is a closed loop, and everything in it lives at the same level.

Nothing migrates. Nothing hibernates. Nothing breeds in a burst and dies in a burst. The frogs call on. The midges swarm the lamps that are always on. The catfish turn. The snails climb the weeping walls an inch and descend an inch. The rats run the pipes. The swallows dart. The people tend and sweat and shout and sleep in the light.

If you left for a year and came back, you would not find a different town. You would find the same residents in the same places, damp to the same degree, humming at the same pitch. The town has not been taken by the jungle. It has been taken by something smaller and more stubborn: by equilibrium.
