No one lives here now, if you mean anyone who could get up, walk a street, and answer to a name.

There are no footprints in the dust except yours, and those are already filling in behind you. No hearth has been lit in the plaza in longer than the town has a way to count. The storehouses hold only leaf-dust. The workshops hold only tools laid down and left, dull metal that has never rusted because there has never been water to rust it, knapped stone cut from the gritty amber-grey lumps the plant itself grew inside its body. The tally marks on the doorposts have been worn smooth by hands that followed the fiber ropes in the dark. Those hands are gone — utterly gone. No bones remain picked clean, because there was never anything here to pick anything clean. No insect, no worm, no bird, no small scavenger followed the makers. The town is empty in the animal sense, completely.

And yet it is not empty. It is not silent. It is not dead.

What lives here is the place. Or, if that is too large a word for it, the thing the place is inside of.

It was, or is, a rhizome, or a trunk, or a seed-husk. The distinction stopped mattering long ago, when it stopped being a plant in any scale a person could picture and became a geology. Above you, further than your arms could measure across and up, is a matted canopy of root and leaf turned dark and fibrous, a ceiling woven so thick the cool air moves through it only in sighs. Below you is the same matter, compressed and aged past recognition, spooned out into that long, almost flat low ground. Around you the walls are swollen and rounded, softened at every corner as if by a river that has never run here. There is no sharp edge left anywhere. Projections have been absorbed. Floors are concave where generations pressed.

If it is solid here, it was or is plant. That rule never breaks. The pith under your palm, like cork. The bark, like worn leather. The hard pale places like bone. The powdered dust like spore. Living and dead are not two things laid side by side; they are the same mass in different states of its slow work. The dead parts are its bones and its stored years, and the living parts are threaded through them, indistinguishable to touch. You cannot find a boundary where the inhabitant ends and the house begins, because the house was carved out of the inhabitant, and the inhabitant has been growing back into the carving ever since.

The living parts are not green. Color is a rumor here. They are pale and waxy, translucent the way thick wax is translucent, in the way an eye that has never known light cannot see. Their leaves are pressed flat and broad, not to catch a sun — there has never been a sun — but to lie against the dark and wait for dust to settle on them. Their roots descend from the ceiling mat in slow, blind culms, hollow and chambered, and do not drink. Nothing here drinks. The air is dry and stays dry; there is no condensation on the walls, no seep, no trickle. Their chemistry is sealed and non-aqueous. What moves in them is not watery sap. It is oil and resin, sweet and sharp on the air, moving so slowly through the body that you would need a lifetime to watch it rise a hand's breadth. You can smell it louder in the workshop quarter, where the old builders boiled it down, and fainter in the storehouses, where leaf-dust covers everything like a first, permanent snow.

It feeds, as far as anyone ever understood, on dust and on vibration.

That is the whole of its appetite, and the whole of its sense. Dust settles — not falls, settles, in air too still for weather — on its waxy surfaces, on the floor, on the fallen woven ceilings, on the roofs of bark and mineral stone. When the great body above contracts in the cool that never leaves, the air is pushed through the narrow throats of its culms and hollows. The culms sing. A low, patient booming comes up through the floor into your feet before it reaches your ears, as if you were standing inside a cello someone is very slowly tightening. Then, at intervals with no rhythm you can learn, it cracks — a sharp pop like a knuckle, like dry wood splitting, somewhere indeterminate and very close. A long creak as a wall thickens by a fraction. A moan rising and falling as air is forced through pith.

The builders seem to have believed the plants grow toward sound. Whether they were right or not, the growth follows the resonance. The loudest chambers have the thickest walls. The quietest corners are thin and brittle and dead. Sound is how it finds, how it thickens, how it remembers where it is strong. It does not hear as you hear. It is the thing that is heard, through itself.

Its cycles are not seasons. There is no sun cycle, no water cycle, no pollinators, nothing that lands on you when you stand still. There is only a slow drying that takes centuries, and a slower swelling when the resin moves, and the release of spores so fine they hang and hang in the still air until a booming lifts them from chamber to chamber through the town's airways. In a right resonant hollow, where the walls sing back true, a spore settles and takes root in the wall. That is its child, if the word child applies to something that roots where it lands and never leaves. A new pale film on old pith. A root hair descending a fraction toward a sound. A bridge of grown fiber fusing one house to the next, looking in the dark like a doorway that was never intended.

The town was built into its hollow, layer upon layer, streets aligned to the veins of the plant, a plaza in a shallow bowl, thresholds and steps and hearth-pits carved first straight into the pith — you can still find the grooves of the stone adzes — and later framed in mineral stone, roofed in woven bark. The plant has been closing it ever since. Resin sealed the gaps finger's-breadth by finger's-breadth in a century. Doors became low and lopsided. Some entrances closed themselves entirely. It did not do this in anger. It simply continued. Walls are a fraction thicker when you return after years. A street is narrower by the slow push of a root. The town has been smoothed into its most stable form by this continued, patient occupation, until it will not collapse because it is already what remains when collapse is finished.

It does not need the town. It does not know the town. It feeds on the dust the town makes — spore dust, fiber dust from the looms, leaf dust from the storehouses, the minute loosening of its own ceiling whenever the booming shakes it — and on the vibration the town's hollow rooms and culms carry and return to it. An instrument, perhaps by accident, perhaps by design. The makers navigated by rope and by ear, by the pitch of a room, by the smell of resin here and leaf-dust there. They built low because the hollow is low. They built thick because the cool never leaves. Now their work is food and echo and cavity.

That is the only life left: one vast, slow, waxy, translucent mass filling all the space there is to fill, mostly finished, settled, final in the way old things are final, still eating the dark quietly and loudly. It was growing before the town was hollowed in it. It was drying and settling while the town rose and emptied. It will go on contracting above, singing through its culms, thickening where it is loud, thinning where it is quiet, releasing its spores to be carried by its own sound, for far longer than anyone will be there to stand in the plaza and listen, and hear the booming come up through the floor into their feet, and understand that the sound is not a noise in the place.

The sound is the inhabitant, breathing.
