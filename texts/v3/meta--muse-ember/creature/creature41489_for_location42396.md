Everything is touching something else.

That is the first thing you learn about the living here, before you learn any of their shapes. Nothing stands alone. Nothing faces out into open space. Warm lays on warm, and warm holds warm, in a density that is not crowded in the way a crowd is crowded, but layered — floor, body, fiber, ceiling, all occupying the same few feet of dark without ever quite pushing each other out. A life here begins in contact and mostly stays in it.

On the floor, the contact is literal. The Plate-Clingers make the ground. Hand-sized, flat, broad, armored in overlapping segments that are part chitin and part stone they have accreted onto themselves from the plates they sit on, they lie shingle upon shingle over the warmest plates for hundreds of paces at a time. It is hard at first to tell where one ends and the next begins, or where animal ends and shattered pavement begins, because their backs have taken the color of the pavement — black, rust, tarnished copper, bone — and their edges are sharp.

They sort themselves, if sorting is the word. A little ways out from a hot seam the plates are a fraction cooler, and there a different thickness of clinger will settle. Closer in, where the amber bloom of glowing rock makes the air press back against you even through your clothes, the segments are thinner, more translucent at the rim, better for shedding heat. They do not argue for their places. They shuffle, a few inches, file-mouths scraping at the gray velvet of the fungal mat beneath them, and settle again. The shuffle is the whole of their travel. The whole of their social life, too.

You feel them more than you hear them. Under your soles a faint, irregular ticking moves across the floor-plate, not a call — there is no calling across distance here, the thick still air will not carry it — but a scrape translated through stone. A Plate-Clinger rakes its file-mouth across mat and mineral, and the neighbor feels the vibration in its own sternum plates and answers, or does not. To be Plate is to live by that metronome: scrape, stillness, scrape. They eat the fungal crust, which is not a plant and does not reach for light, but a thin mat that lives on condensing mineral vapor and fattens on the wax the larger grazers leave behind. Their bellies make stone out of what they eat, segment by segment, so that in time a clinger is as much grown floor as animal, and when it dies it does not soften or rot. It dries. It hardens where it lay. The shingle layer gains another shingle, sharp and bright-faced and unweathered, as if nothing had ever happened.

Above them, upside down, is the other half of the same thought.

The Sky-Clingers hang from the stalactite thickets by hooked limbs, blind and pale and translucent, so that gaslight from below seems to pass through them rather than fall on them. They do not walk, or walk only to re-grip. They trail long fibers down into the standing haze and hold still while the air does its slow work — sulfur flakes, mineral dust, the fine shed flakes of ten thousand living things all settling or not settling in those permanent layers. The fibers catch. The Sky-Clinger eats at the end of its own extended arm, draw after draw, without ever moving its body.

Where the ceiling dips low the hanging becomes a veil. Fiber interweaves with fiber until there are two ceilings, one of rock needles and one of soft clinging bodies, and to pass you have to push through it, pushing warm translucent things aside. They do not flee. They grip tighter. Their world is that grip — tension in a fiber, warmth rising from below in broad sheets, the slow fall of flakes from the floor world to the ceiling world. A Sky-Clinger is born on that veil, already hanging, named by the fiber it holds to, and it will hang in very nearly that place until it dries and drops, or until its fibers snag on a needle and it lithifies there, a pale stalactite to join the others. From below you would never notice a death. The veil closes by an inch. The temperature where you stand does not change.

Between floor and ceiling wander the ones who make movement seem possible.

The Glassthings are long and jointed, worm-like, eyeless, with heat-sensing pits marching down their sides. Their bodies are in part glass, clear as a bubble, and when they pass near the great flank-striped fauna — those slow bodies whose sides spill blue-white light — they light up faintly from within, as if something inside them answered. They tunnel not through soil, for there is no soil, but through the powder of the dried salt basins, leaving perfect burrows with edges as sharp as a fresh cut. Trails cross the white blistered crust everywhere, young and fresh in appearance. They were made years ago. Because there is no water to slump them, no rain to fill them, they look the same now as the hour they were made.

A Glassthings life is a retracing. It follows its own or another's tunnel back to a warmer plate, rubs against its own glass flank and makes a brief spark, settles for a while against the heat, then tunnels again. It feels the world as gradient — warmer here, cooler there — mapped in pits along its length. It has no eyes to close, no ears to fill. It has heat and touch and the thin music of glass on glass. In the dark between the still layers, it is the only thing that seems to go somewhere, and even it returns.

Larger still, and slower than any of them, are the stone-backed grazers. Six-limbed, broad-backed as fallen slabs, they move on routes so regular that generations of feet have polished the floor plates to a dull shine along their paths. They do not hunt. They breathe in the sulfur vapor that hangs in the haze, take it down into chambers in their bellies where mineral chemistry does the work that water does elsewhere, and excrete it back out as molten wax. The wax cools on the warm plates into the gray fungal mats. The mats feed the Plate-Clingers. The Plate-Clingers shed flakes that rise to the Sky-Clingers. The grazers are the digestion of the place made into an animal: vapor in, wax out, floor remade.

They are enormous by Still Deep standards and entirely unhurried. One step, long stillness, one step. Their stone backs grow plates over their lives, and when they die — and they do die, constantly, as everything here does — they do not rot. They dry where they stand or fall, their limbs becoming new needles, their backs becoming new plates, bright break-faces where the body split. That is why the floor looks so recently fractured. It is continually being re-made out of its own dead, and the break is always bright.

And threading through all of it, like punctuation in a very long sentence, are the builders.

Lean, long-limbed, with clever hands, they are the only inhabitants who are not defined by clinging or hanging or tunneling or grazing. They walk for hours under a ceiling that never appears and a floor that undulates on a continental scale and see nothing made, and then — a semicircle of stacked stones no higher than a knee. A pit lined with glass shards, faintly warm from a geothermal tap. A single standing wall, one stone thick, mortar a wax of mineral fat.

They make these with a manual technology: chipped obsidian knives, hammered copper needles from surface ore, drills of sharpened quartz. They knap glass, they beat metal, they weave cords not from plant fiber — there is none — but from sliced sinew of the stone-backed grazers and from the binding threads of the fungal mats. Their tools are simple because there is nothing else to be simple with. Their buildings are small because the vault is vast and there is no weather to build against, no rain to keep out, no winter to survive.

The builders do not invent a new shape. The wall is the same wall. The cache pit is the same pit. Generation after generation a builder comes to the place where the stones of the last builder still lie, sharp-edged and un-eroded as they were left, and rebuilds them in the same arrangement. The plan is not stored in an idea that can be improved or forgotten. It is stored in the arrangement itself. To live as a builder is to walk far, find the mark, and repeat it faithfully with your hands, then leave it again. Their memory is external, mineral, and perfect, because nothing here has the means to wear it away.

They tick and click against stone as they work, feeling the floor answer through their soles. They handle warm plates, hot wax, cold glass, without the flinch an outsider would expect; their hands have learned the temperature of everything by touch. They eat fungal mat and grazer-wax and shed flakes caught in their own cupped palms. They die as the others die — dry, hard, in place — and the next builder follows the polished path or the fiber veil or the sharp tunnel to arrive at the semicircle and begin again.

The fungal crust itself is the quietest life of all, if life is the word for something that mats over warm plates in gray velvet and eats vapor as it condenses. It has no leaves, no roots, no reaching. It is the skin of the equilibrium, turning wax into food into stone into vapor again. Under the Plate-Clingers' file-mouths it is scraped and regrows — or rather, re-condenses — in the exact same thickness. It binds the builders' cords. It coats the mouths of visitors left too long, with the taste of hot iron and oil.

Birth and death happen constantly among all of them. A Plate-Clinger hatches from beneath a segment and shuffles an inch to a warmer seam. A Sky-Clinger is born clinging. A Glassthings tunnels its first burrow and then follows the old ones. A grazer exhales wax. A builder stacks a stone. A builder dies beside the wall. Nothing in the system changes for it.

There is no season to note a change in. The thermal columns rise and fall back in the same broad sheets. The aerosol layers hang at the same heights. Far off, a rock-plate pings a single clear note with a long silence after it, and pings again at the same interval from farther than you can guess. That is equilibrium, sounding itself.

If you stay long enough you stop feeling like a visitor and start feeling like another presence in the density — the only one out of place, though even that feeling thins. Something is clinging to the floor beneath your feet, something else is hanging above your head, something else is threading between them in the dark, and all of them are warm and still and near-touching, holding time in the way they hold each other: not by passing it on, but by staying exactly as they are, sharp, hot, dark, and crowded, while beneath the sealed crust the Still Deep continues to be.
