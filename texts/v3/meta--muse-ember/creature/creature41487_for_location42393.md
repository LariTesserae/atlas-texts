If you stand at the lip and listen long enough, the first inhabitants are not the ones you step on. They are the ones who hear you first.

A hundred paces across, a room in the rock, and the sound of your own boot is caught and thrown back from the far wall — a wall you cannot see, because there is nothing to see with, only a wall you can hear. The inhabitants have known this long before you arrived. They live in a stone bell, and the bell is their sky, their road, their conversation. They do not move through it so much as they resonate through it.

The eldest are the ones in the water and the black crust.

It is too hot here for most life, and too high, and too dark, and too still. That is why it is full. In the warm pools that sigh up bubbles — water that boils at a temperature that would scald a lowland animal, because the air is thin — there are bacteria and archaea that have been cooking in that same mineral soup for longer than the pools have had names. They are not swimming so much as enduring. They turn the geothermal bleed, the sulfur and iron and dissolved mountain, into things that can be eaten. Everything else in the room is built, eventually, out of their patience.

Around them, in the marsh-edge where the floor gives under your foot with a wet sigh, is a second skin of life: fungi, white and grey as things that have never needed color, threading the spongy plant matter and the soft stone. A fallen frond does not rot here. It is unmade over years, inch by inch, by fungal threads and bacterial film and the patient chemistry of wet rock, until it is mineral again. Nothing is allowed to leave, so everything must be taken apart here. The fungi are the great recyclers, the undertakers and midwives at once. When a fern dies — and a fern here may live for a century growing a finger's width, long enough to be called a forest by the mites that live on it — the fungi walk it back down into heat and water and the dark clay that feeds the next fern. The water that fell as rain on the summit a thousand years ago may still be falling here, drop by drop from the vaulted ceiling, and the fungi have been drinking it the whole way down.

The plants are the next tenants, and they are not green.

There is no sun to be green for. Growth is driven by heat, and by sound, and by the fall of water. The ferns never unfurl toward light because there is none to unfurl toward. Their fronds are broad and hand-sized and translucent as a mushroom's gill, veined and hairy, feeling their way toward the warm seams in the stone. Their leaves turn, very slowly, toward warmth — toward a crack in the rock, toward a pool, and, if you stand still long enough, very slightly toward you. Not because you are food. Because you are a warm thing moving through a cold-dark place, and that is, for them, the only kind of sunlight there is.

They grow in clumps. That is the strangeness of the room and the truth of it. Ten, twenty paces of bare, polished stone, mist hovering a foot above it, nothing growing at all — then, abruptly, a curtain of lushness so thick you could hide in it, dripping, warm, alive with touch. Each clump is an island. The mosses in waist-deep mats, black-green and spongy as a held sponge, hold water and give it back very, very slowly. The vines hang like braided hair from the walls and root in mid-air where the mist is thickest, farming the condensation. Nothing spreads easily across the polished stone between. So the clumps have grown up separately, for a very long time. A mite in the eastern clump does not know the dialect of a mite in the western clump, except that sound carries. Across the emptiness, they can still hear each other. That is enough. That is, perhaps, everything.

If the fungi and the bacteria are the room's memory, the plants are its listening skin. Inside the hollow stems of the bell-ferns live the bell-throated things.

They are small, soft, blind, made mostly for making a sound in a plant. They inflate a throat sac against the stem, and the stem does the rest — a low, damped booming that you feel in your teeth more than you hear in your ears, ringing for ten seconds in the bowl of rock. It is not a song as a bird would sing. It is a resonance set up in the fern, the way a note is set up in a cupped hand. One calls from the east clump, and the whole hollow hums. Somewhere west, at a distance that feels enormous in a room you could walk across in an afternoon, another answers, once, and then is quiet again. They have been at this for so long that the ferns have grown to suit them — thicker stems, wider hollows, hairs that soften the boom. The animal has grown to suit the fern. Neither would hurry to change now.

Higher in the air, in the mist, are the clickers.

Blind, winged swimmers of the air. They do not fly so much as swim, their wings beating in the thick warm damp, their bodies pale and translucent, navigating by echolocation. They fill the dark with a rain of tiny, rapid pulses, like someone snapping fingers far away, and then very close. You hear them before you feel the stir their wings make in the mist. They eat what lives in the mist — spores, midges too small to feel, the exhalations of the mosses — and in eating it they keep the air moving. When a pool sighs and bubbles, the clickers are there a moment later, tasting the burst of warm vapor. Their pulses are the fastest thing in this place. Everything else moves at the speed of a pool filling, or a lifetime emptying. The clickers move at the speed of sound, because they have to. They are the room's quick thought.

On the floor, and mostly on the great bare sweep to the south, are the grazers.

Vast and slow, bodies warm as stones in sun, moving across bare stone at a pace that makes a human lifetime seem like a step. You find them not by seeing — eyes here are vestigial, filmed over, or absent entirely; they navigate by heat pits along their flank and by touch and by the very faint chemical rain of the mountain — you find them by stepping. The crust breaks. The mist stirs. And from somewhere under a moss mat, something vast and slow complains, and the sound goes around the walls and comes back to you from a direction that is not where it started.

A grazer may take eighty years to cross the room. It leaves a broad, wet trail behind it that steams slightly where its warmth meets the cold stone, a trail that the floor fauna follow and erase within hours. It eats the mineral film and the algae-thin skin that grows on the polished rock where the weeping walls darken it, and the edges of the moss mats, taking in more rock and water and microbe than any clean thing should. It has few enemies. It does not need to hurry. When it dies, it dies where it lies, and becomes, over decades, a warm patch in the stone where fungi gather thick and the ferns lean in close. The room does not bury its dead. It absorbs them.

And beneath all of that, in the black crust of the marsh-floor, is the seething abundance that makes the whole place possible.

Worms, transparent as water in a skin, pulsing heat from one end to the other. Mites, eyeless, riding the backs of grazers and the bell-ferns alike, farming the fungi on their shells. Snails without shells, slugs that move like slow hearts. Beetles without eyes, their antennae feeling for warm cracks and for the vibration of a footstep. An entire second fauna turning the floor over an inch at a time, restoring it behind you. Your footprints do not last. The seep finds them, the worms find them, the moss finds them within the hour, and the floor sighs and closes.

This is the rule they all live by, without ever stating it: nothing hurries, and nothing leaves, and heat is what light is for everything else.

And then there are the oldest residents, the ones who are perhaps not alive at all and perhaps are the reason anything here is alive.

A dozen of them, scattered so widely you first think they are part of the stone. A low black spike, knee-high, between two moss clumps, warm to the touch in a way geothermal stone is not warm, humming just below hearing. A seam running up the far cliff like a healed scar, too straight, made of a lattice finer than any weave, that rearranges itself so slowly you would need a hundred years of watching to notice it only in the way water runs off it. A flat disk set into the stone by the door, cold, around which condensation gathers in a perfect ring.

They are not ruins. Ruins are broken. These are molecular machines embedded in rock, atom-by-atom things from beyond our engineering, still doing their work on a timescale of centuries. They keep the cold places cold so the water will weep there. They keep the warm places warm. They exchange the mountain's deep heat for chemical work in the soil, feeding the plants that have no light to feed on. The ferns have grown over them like hair over a scar, and they have let it. The bell-throated things nest as close as they can to a spike's hum. The clickers avoid the disk's cold ring, or gather at its edge where the mist is thickest.

Every eleven or twelve years, one of the artifacts by the wall gives off a single, perfect tone that hangs in the stone for a day and then stops. And the animals answer it, all of them, once — the bell-ferns boom, the clickers snap, the grazers complain, the worms pulse faster in the crust — and then, together, they are quiet again. No one knows what is being asked. Perhaps it was only ever here to watch the room breathe. Perhaps watching is a kind of tending.

Stay long enough and you learn not to look. You learn the map by ear and by skin: the weeping wall to the north where drips fall fastest, ringing the whole cathedral height; the loud clump to the east where the mist is thickest; the warm empty stone to the south where the grazers cross, year after year, leaving trails that vanish behind them. The darkness is not empty and it is not night. It is full — of water falling and falling again, of warmth rising and falling back on itself, of living things calling to each other across vast hollow stretches of stone that are, in the end, only heartbeats apart.

They have been here, in this settled, primordial, wet room, for so long that the stone itself seems to be remembering how to be alive. And when you leave, quietly, by the bent slit that is the only door, you do not leave footprints that last.
