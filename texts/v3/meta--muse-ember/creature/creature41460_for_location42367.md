Look up, and the ceiling is not empty.

At first it is only sound — chittering, squeaking, a rustle that travels in waves across stone a hundred metres wide, settling, then starting again somewhere else. Then your eyes adjust and the dark acquires texture. A patch of rock darker than the rock around it is not shadow. It is bodies. Tens of thousands of bats, shoulder to shoulder, wings folded tight like cloaks, heads tucked, ears still showing above the fur. They hang inverted by their feet and do almost nothing, for hours at a time, and yet the colony as a whole never entirely stops. One animal shifts its grip, another wakes, stretches, yawns, scrapes a wing against its neighbour, and the ripple runs sideways for a moment before it dies out. Their musk is in the air here, sharp and animal, mixed with the wet stone. Beneath them, in piles on the floor and on ledges below, guano has accumulated into pale, gritty drifts — fresh dust constantly sifting down, feeding everything else.

That is the loudest layer, but it is not the only one. From the crevices in the walls come the rasping of cave crickets, long-legged, long-antennaed, rubbing their hind legs against their wings in a dry, continuous trill that bends and echoes with the curve of the roof. You hear one close, a sharp ticking from a crack at knee height, and before it stops the answer comes from thirty metres away, fainter, delayed, as if the chamber itself were replying. They are everywhere in the deeper, lightless galleries, in fissures, under fallen blocks, in the narrow sideways passages. They do not need the green places. They need only damp, shelter, and the steady fall of organic dust from above — bat droppings, dead midges, fungal spores, the occasional fallen bird or insect from the entrances.

Between the bats and the floor the air itself is inhabited. Midges and small flies hang in clouds over the still puddles and over the guano piles, a living haze that you walk through without breaking. You feel them on your face, on the damp film already on your skin. They rise when you pass, settle again behind you, rise again. They are not in swarms for a season; there is no season here to start or stop them. Over a basin a few centimetres deep, mirror-flat, fed not by rain but by condensation, they breed and die and are replaced, their larvae in the film at the edges, the adults in the air just above it, year and year without a change in pitch.

Under every stone, if you lifted a few, you would find the floor truly working. Woodlice, pale grey and armoured, uncurl and scuttle when disturbed. Millipedes ripple away in slow, many-legged lines. Beetles — small, dark, shining — probe into the grit between shards. In the few pockets where pale silt and thin soil have collected, earthworms live coiled in the damp, doing the patient turning that has never had time to make soil here. They are feeding, all of them, not on plants for the most part, but on the invisible ground: fungi.

Fungi are the real residents of this place. You see only parts of them — a brown, leathery film on bare rock where nothing green grows, a dark stain on a fallen block, white threads spreading through a hollow where silt lies, a faint bloom on a dead beetle nobody has moved. But they are decomposing the little that falls and processing the little that arrives from above, turning guano and fallen insects and the odd dead frog into something woodlice and millipedes and beetles can eat. In the long stretches between the green patches, this is the entire basis of life: stone, damp, fungi, and the animals that graze on fungi.

On the walls and ceiling they take other forms. Harvestmen hang in bunches from ceiling cracks, a dozen pale bodies with legs intertangled, motionless for long stretches, then a leg twitches, a body sways almost imperceptibly. Spiders have strung their webs across every fissure wide enough to hold one, webs beaded with condensation, vibrating faintly when a midge blunders into them. Slugs and snails work the damp walls slowly, grazing algae and fungal film, leaving silvery trails that dry and fade and are remade the same night — though there is no night here to remake them in. The algae themselves darken whole sections of sweating wall a dull green-black where light reaches, and the snails know those sections by taste.

At the edges of the puddles, frogs sit. Perfectly still, perfectly ordinary, the sort you could find in any temperate pond above, except that this pond does not freeze, does not drain, does not warm in summer. They sit with their eyes above the waterline, listening, and when they move it is a small, sudden hop to another basin a few metres away. They eat the midges. The midges eat the algae and the bacteria in the puddle film. The puddles are fed by the air. Nothing hurries.

Near the ramp where the light thins but has not gone, the community changes its character because the outside world still leaks in. Mosses form low, dark carpets where a shaft of diffuse light falls on a sweating wall. Liverworts press themselves flat beside them. A fern tuft has rooted in a crack and uncurled fronds that are pale and leggy, reaching for a light that never strengthens. In the thin soil of a floor hollow, a sprinkle of small seedlings stands up, white-green and spindly, each one etiolated, each one surviving on damp and dimness alone. These are the only plants in the whole system, and they appear only where the two rare conditions meet. A metre away there may be none at all, only bare sharp stone and brown fungal film.

It is on the high ledges near those entrances that the birds live. Swallows and wrens stitch the twilight with calls, darting out to the surface and back, wedging nests of twigs and mud onto ledges where no predator can easily reach. Their nests are among the few made things here: mud-dauber tubes glued like tiny organ pipes to a wall, spider silk across a crack, a nest that persists in the stillness long after the season that built it has passed above. On the floor below, a snail shell cracked neatly against an anvil stone marks where a bird has been feeding, leaving the spiral fragments in a little pile. At night above — for the surface still has nights, and some of these animals keep its time even in oek place that does not — mice and shrews run the floor in the dark gaps between chambers. You do not see them often. You see the pause, the whisker, the dart from one shadow to another, listening for beetles, for fallen seeds, for anything that has dropped from a bird's nest or a bat's fur.

And then, between all of this, there is nothing. That is the other half of living here. A bat colony here, a patch of moss there, a seething corner where damp wood and fungi and insects have gathered, and then a gallery tens of metres long with only sharp stone, pale silt in the hollows, and the echo of life from the next chamber over. A cricket call will come back to you from far away, bent by the roof. You can stand in a space big enough to hold a town and hear only your own footsteps on the shards — edges sharp enough to bite through a boot sole — and, faintly, the endless chorus continuing beyond sight.

It is overwhelming in number and sparse in spacing, because the container is so large. The invertebrates across the whole system must number in the millions. The bats alone in the tens of thousands. The fungi are uncountable. They live by falling and feeding and dying in place, generations on generations in the same cool, the same damp, the same unmoving light, neither increasing nor decreasing in any way you could measure if you left for a week and came back. The rivulets run down the same walls. The fern is in the same crack. The bats hang from the same ceiling, shifting in the same slow waves.

Nothing here is strange in kind. That is what makes it complete. Familiar animals, brought down by gravity, by flight, by following damp and shelter, have found a place that never changes against them, and have filled it to its equilibrium — not busy, not empty, just held, below the world, in a quietly roaring stillness where the stone is still sharp and the life is still everywhere and neither is in any hurry to become anything else.
