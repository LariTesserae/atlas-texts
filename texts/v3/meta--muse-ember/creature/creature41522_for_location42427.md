Life here is felt before it is seen.

Not as eyes first, and not as one animal and then another. It is a crawling on the skin, a breathing in the air you have to push through, a wet weight on every leaf you brush. The living thing is not any of the frogs or birds or insects in particular. It is all of them at once, so close and so numerous that you stop trying to count kinds and start to feel volume — the sheer saturation of it. A thumbnail frog under a leaf. A procession of ants on a stem. A midge cloud parting around your face. A tadpole shadow under floating leaves. A snail trail crossing another, and another. There is always another, a few inches further on, doing the same thing in the same dim light, and the next is already behind you before tsnarl closes.

In the water that is barely water there are lives lived almost entirely in a finger's depth. The black sheet over the peat, the puddles that are only a shine, the mud pots that swell and seal — each holds a population that may never know any other puddle. Tadpole shoals move in the sheet-water like poured shadows, feeding on the green film on the undersides of leaves. Small fish flick there too, small enough that the water seems too shallow for them, surfacing to breathe in air that is already warm and wet, laying eggs in the mud they themselves keep stirred. Snails work the mud and the weeping lumps of iron-stone, grazing algae and mineral crust, leaving trails that loop and cross until the whole surface looks written over. Under that, unseen, the bacteria and fungi that make the mud bubble are living at a pace the fish cannot match — fixing, fermenting, releasing heat and sulfur tang, breaking a fallen body down before its shape has finished cooling. A frog dies in that water, quiet under a leaf, and within a day the fungi have furred it, the tadpoles have grazed its edges, the bacteria have taken its inside, and a seedling is pushing up through a richness exactly the size of that death.

On the ground layer the lives are mostly still. Ferns unfurl over ferns, each frond a slow animal in its own way, reaching twenty times and moving no step at all. Elephant-ear leaves overlap like shingles, wide and thin and pale underneath where the filtered light barely reaches, holding condensation on their upper sides, holding a frog on their undersides. That frog does not hop far. Born in a puddle no larger than your palm, it climbs the first stem it finds and spends its life within a few leaves of where it metamorphosed, sitting in the drip, throbbing its metallic song until another frog answers from the next cluster over, and another from further out on the marsh. Its children climb to the next leaf. Ants walk the same stems in processions, carrying leaf fragments and seeds and dead neighbors larger than themselves, following trails that are not paths so much as habits — a line worn in air that closes behind them because a liana has rooted and a fern has leaned into it within the hour.

Above that, the trees are also lives that refuse to stay separate. A root drops from a mid-story branch, finds the mud, thickens into a new trunk, and the tree becomes a pillar of green that is also a ladder and a wall and a hiding place. Lianas hang to the floor and root where they touch, so a vine becomes a small tree, becomes a corridor for ants, becomes a perch for a frog, becomes a net for a spider whose web is beaded and re-beaded with condensation. Small mammals rustle in that root tangle — not large, not loud, shrew-like lives that squeak and pause and rustle again, moving a body-length at a time through wet leaves no wind has ever moved. They do not range. They do not need to. There is food on every leaf underside, insects on every stem, fruit falling where it ripened, moisture in every breath. A mammal born in that tangle may die in it, its body cleaned by ants and fungi and beetles while its mate raises the next litter on the next root over.

The canopy is another floor, not a ceiling. Leaves roped together, furred with epiphytes, holding hanging nests that sway only from the weight of chicks shifting inside them, holding hanging fruit in all stages at once — blossom, hard green fruit, ripe fruit, and the fallen fruit below already fruited and already rotting. Birds live there in overlapping phrases that never quite finish, small and green-brown and almost invisible until they move, calling and answering and calling while, below them, the night-singing frogs are still singing. There is no shift change. The birds that sing like day and the frogs that sing like night sing together because there is no day or night for them to keep. Moths move in that dim, greenish twilight that never brightens, pale and unhurried, because it is always dusk enough for them. Dragonflies and damselflies patrol the puddles in lacy, constant rounds, parting the midge clouds, laying eggs in water that looks too shallow to hold life. Midges themselves live and die in the air over a single puddle, born in the condensation on a leaf, mating in a cloud that reforms a foot from where it broke, never traveling farther than the next branch down.

All of these lives are touching. That is the tightness of it. An ant carries a mite. A frog eats the midge the ant was following. A spider eats the moth that pollinated the flower whose fruit the bird drops for the mammal whose droppings feed the fungus that feeds the seedling. No one species climbs above the others for long, not because of a predator from outside — nothing comes from outside — but because every birth is matched here, in the same square of mud, by a death. A leaf falls and is skeletonized before the next leaf above has finished expanding. A flower falls already fruited. Populations do not surge. They do not crash. They hover, dense and complete, at the exact number that the leaf cluster, the puddle, the root pillar can hold in this heat.

And they make the place that holds them. The heat is not only from the earth. It is the sum of all that breathing and feeding and fermenting in a bowl with nowhere for air to go. The rain is not from the sky. It is steam rising in straight columns from the mud pots, condensing on the nearest leaves, gathering, swelling, falling again as warm drips from leaf to leaf to water — drips that the thumbnail frogs sit in, drips that bead the spiders' webs, drips that keep the sheet-water a finger deep forever. The inhabitants sweat the water back and forth between themselves, drinking it, exhaling it, letting it fall. Seeds fall where they ripened because there is no wind to carry them, and root there in the wet skin of the place. Frogs spend their lives on the same lily pad cluster. Insects may never leave the leaf they were born on.

If you watch a single life, it is brief and complete. If you watch the whole, nothing is happening. A fern unfurls overhead while at your feet another is being eaten to lace. A fruit ripens while its twin rots. A bird calls while a frog answers while a drip falls while a mud pot bubbles and seals. New seedlings push through old deaths. Old deaths feed new seedlings. The light stays the color of water in a leaf. The chorus stays loud. The air stays still except where a body moves through it — your body, pushing a liana aside, parting midges, disturbing a cicada-shimmer that rises and settles and does not stop.

They do not welcome you and they do not drive you out. They simply continue around your ankles and over your head and under the leaf you just brushed, overwhelmingly alive and perfectly balanced, each generation living and dying within reach of the next, making their own heat, making their own rain, never leaving, never arriving, in a low basin that is still in the act of being born and for that reason will never become anything else.
