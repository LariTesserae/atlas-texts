Eight hundred people, and no other animal life at all to keep them company.

That is the first fact of who lives here, and the one the body learns before the mind does. The lanes are never empty. From the stair-head at one end to the Engine Hall at the other, four hundred paces of canyon stacked three and four stories high, there is always someone hurrying past with a basket, a tool roll, a child on a hip, a length of iron on a shoulder. They move that way because there is nowhere else to move; every wall is a house wall, every roof a floor. They are close enough that you hear them breathing in the next room before you see them. And in all that hurrying there is no scurry in the gutters, no flutter from the eaves, no dog trotting at heel. The people are the only things that walk or fly or crawl in Sinderhold. In that, they are alone together.

Their bodies have taken the shape of the place, the way the houses have taken the shape of the rock. Heat is not weather here; it is condition. At the stair-head it is already forty degrees, fifty in the middle of the canyon where the hot air gathers. Sweat does not evaporate. It stands. It beads on the upper lip, runs in bright tracks down the back of the neck, darkens the small of the back and stays there, salts the skin of the forearms until a lick tastes of the boilers. A person who has lived here a year learns to drink from that salt, to work with it, to not wipe it away too often or the skin underneath cracks. They work in pairs at the great valve wheels in the Engine Hall because a single wheel is too hot to hold, two pairs of gloved hands taking turns hauling while the other rests its palms in the damp air and lets the heat go out of them. The gloves themselves never quite dry. Nothing quite dries.

Clothing is thin, layered for ifs. A shirt for the press shop has a permanent damp under the arms no matter how it is hung in the one shaft of fan-air that might pass for a cool room. Trousers are cut wide so the thighs do not chafe. Children wear what their parents have cut down, sleeves rolled, collars soft with wash-water that never fully leaves. Everyone's hair lies close and flat. Everyone's eyes are amber-adjusted, most comfortable in the dim glow that hangs every twenty paces on sagging cables, and most wakeful when those lamps sink from hard white in the working hours to amber in the quiet hours. That shift, and the ninety-minute breathing of the vents, is their clock more than any watch. When the dome begins to weep and the warm rain falls for ten minutes, rattling on iron roofs and spattering the Cistern Square, people pause where they are with jars out, or tilt a face up, or simply listen to the counting drip from eave to gutter and let the machinery cool its throat. When the vents sigh open and the paper on the walls flutters, they resume.

They are workers, but not in the loose sense of a factory town above. Sinderhold makes the things that are too costly to fire where heat must be paid for: castings still warm from the pour, furnace brick, glass vials ground from silica, machine parts hammered and filed and riveted until they are smooth. The work is intimate and loud in its small way — the press in the Loom Shed thumping its rhythm, a belt slapping softly, the rail cart trundling its fixed loop between the Engine Hall and the square with parts for the surface. There is no traffic roar because there is no traffic. Noise lives next door. You know the cough of the man across the lane that is too narrow for two carts, because his window opens onto your kitchen. You know when the woman above you is planing a door, again, because the damp has swollen it shut in its frame. You learn to hear a house the way you might hear a body.

Houses are not so much owned as occupied, layered thick. A shop's roof is the next house's floor. A bridge-street at the third level carries laundry and pipes and cables overhead like a second ceiling, so a lane below can be in near-dark at noon that is not noon. Balconies lean over doorways. Stair towers bolt onto older brick made from the basalt underfoot. To live here is to live in someone else's shadow the whole time, and the people have made small economies of that closeness: borrowed heat, borrowed light, a cooler corner by a fan that is called cool at a degree or two less than everywhere else and is fought over politely. Families of three, four, five squeeze into a stack where a mother might keep the succulents in the gutter of the square alive with a thimble of condensation because her child was born there and has never seen anything green bigger than that.

There are two kinds of Sinderhold people now, and they know each other by the way they talk about the stair.

The surface-born came down when the blister in the rock was still fresh and sharp, when the gravel underfoot still bit through soles and the stone still ticked as it cooled. They remember cool air on the neck that was not a fan. They remember sun that hurt the eyes in a different way, sky that was not a dome, rain that fell from clouds and not from sweating rock. Some of them still go up the stair in single straight flights, carrying castings and furnace brick through the pressure change in the ears, trading for food and letters and news that arrives always a little stale. They bring back vocabulary the way other people bring back seeds. *Dog. Pigeon. Snow. Tree.* They teach these words to children who have never needed them, and the children repeat them slowly, tasting the strangeness, because to them the words describe things that belong to a story about outside.

The cave-born have never been up. Or if they have, it was as infants carried in arms and they remember nothing of it except the pressure on the ears. For them the world is complete. The ceiling weeping is rain. The vent sigh is wind. The amber lamps turning white is morning. They grow up pale in the way the ferns in the gutters are pale, bleached almost white by lamplight, because there is no sun to burn them brown. Their days are measured by the drip-count between shifts and by the brief brighter light that rolls the length of the canyon when a furnace is tapped, showing for a second the whole small city in sharp relief — iron roofs, wet stone, the underside of ten thousand cables — before sinking back to amber. They count that light the way surface children might count sun coming through blinds. They play in the bridge-streets and on the landings, in the one open place, the Cistern Square, not much bigger than a house plot, where the pool steams faintly and is too hot and mineral to drink but fine to look at. They learn to read by furnace glow. They learn to sleep to the ticking of young rock settling, to the murmur of the boilers breathing under the floor, to the occasional ping like pottery.

Babies are born here in the damp heat, sweating as everyone sweats, brought into the world in rooms that never cool below forty degrees. Mothers wipe them with cloths that stay warm. Old people die here, too, and are tended here, their noise absorbed by the rock and the humid air. Houses sit on screw jacks because the young floor heaves and settles in fractions; people, in their way, do the same, adjusting, planing, living with what sticks. The older surface-born, when they talk of animals, do not talk with nostalgia so much as with a kind of clinical wonder: how did the flies know to find the food? How did the rats not starve in a place that made so much waste? The cave-born listen and do not miss what they never had, except in the quiet hours between shifts when even the machines seem to exhale and only the dripping remains, and then a mouth may shape a birdsong it has never heard, just to see what the air does with it.

They keep no pets. They could not. The heat and the dry steam and the chemicals in the process air have sterilized the place of anything that crawls or flies, and the people have made a virtue of that sterilization without ever saying so. A child who finds a beetle in the runoff will stare for a long time, because it does not belong. The absence makes them unusually quiet in layers, and unusually aware of each other. Without insect hum to cover it, a conversation in a lane is a conversation for the whole lane. Without a dog's bark, a sudden clang carries. Intimacy is not chosen here; it is built in.

And then, in the damp, the other residents.

Not planted in any proper sense, though a few were coaxed long ago by hands that remembered gardens. In the gutters of the Square, in cracks where the pooled runoff lingers, in the damp shadow under the cistern where the pipes sweat, there are mats of pale succulents with fleshy rosettes that store the warm alkaline water against the next dry hour. There are hairy ferns bleached ghost-white by the amber light, their fronds so fine they tremble when the vent cycle begins and the lamp flames lean. And there is a low, moss-like growth that spreads as a film over the black oily puddles and the edges of the gutters, no bigger than a hand, that blooms in tiny waxy flowers only during the quiet hours when the fans have stilled and the heat ebbs a fraction. No one with work to do sees them bloom except the night watch and the children who are supposed to be sleeping.

These are the only other living things besides the people, a few hundred square meters of them at most, tentative and small and pale. The town itself is the thicket, as someone once said, and the plants are its proof that life can colonize even a machine's runoff. They live because the city makes moisture anyway — the dome weeping, the boilers condensing, the square steaming — and they live only where that moisture lingers longest, which is to say, where humans have not quite sealed the rock. They do not feed anyone. They do not give shade. They are not eaten, not grazed, not pollinated by anything that flies. They seed themselves from the vents, from dust brought down the stair on boots and baskets, and they persist because the heat they love is the heat the people cannot leave.

People tend them, in the way people tend something fragile that asks nothing. A cistern keeper will flick condensation from a pipe onto a rosette with a fingertip. A mother will set a jar to catch the ceiling rain and pour a little into a crack. Children who have never seen a tree will name the ferns after the words they learned from the surface-born — *fern* is a word they know, *tree* is a story — and guard the moss flowers fiercely on the nights they open, as if those pale blooms were a second kind of lamp. When the vent cycle pulls the excess up and out into a shaft that goes somewhere far above, into darkness, the ferns bend toward the pull and then settle again, patient as anything that has learned to live by a ninety-minute breath.

So the whole ecology turns on itself: people borrow temperature from the rock and the boilers to make things for the surface world, and the waste of that borrowing — heat, damp, mineral water — is what the plants live on, and the plants live only in the narrow places where the people have left room, and the people live in the narrow places where they have left no room at all. No animals break the circle. No outside enters except by the stair, a basket at a time, and by the shaft above where the air is pulled away. It is a complete world turned inside out, dense and urban and quiet, hot and dim, small enough to hold in your eye from the stair-head and yet layered so thick with presence that you could live here for years and still find a new bridge-street above a lane you thought you knew.

If you listen long enough, you hear who lives there not as a list but as a texture: the soft slap of a belt, the distant clank of a press, the cart on its rail, the boilers breathing, the drip counting itself from eave to gutter, the faint ping of cooling stone, and underneath it all the low murmur of eight hundred people going about their work in clothes that never quite dry, keeping each other warm in a place that was never meant to be inhabited and has been inhabited anyway.
