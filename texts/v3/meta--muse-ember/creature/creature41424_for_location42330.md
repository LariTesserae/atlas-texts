If you stand at the top of the stair-hole and listen, you hear them before you see them — not as a crowd, but as layers, because that is how they live.

The highest lives are the ones that don't wake for you. In the dark inlets of the chimneys, the bats hang by day, folded into the plaster, and only squeak awake when the light shafts have gone dim and the people below have gone to their lofts. They are the hamlet's night-shift, and they drop their insects for the owl below to take. Below them, in clay cups that were built for them a hundred years ago and are repaired every spring whether they need it or not, the swallows return to the same place. They do not build. They occupy, which is a different thing entirely, and more adapted. Sparrows work the eaves where the tiles meet the thatch, and the pigeons have their own small ledges under the roof, cooing down into the top terrace where the women who feed them climb with bowls of grain and clean the ledges after a fashion. The birds know the rhythm of those hands; the women know the rhythm of the birds returning.

Down a little from the roof, trained along strings up the side of a light shaft, is the vine. It is a resident in the strictest sense — branch by stubborn branch, coaxed for years to reach real sun, and in summer it throws a wavering green shade across the stair and into the topmost rooms. It has learned the strings. Its leaves turn in the shaft-drafts that lift the laundry. It is not wild, it is not quite tame; it is furnished.

The cats come next, because they own the middle air. They patrol the wall-tops and sleep curled on the warm wall-caps, above the chickens dust-bathing in the dust of the middle terrace, above the mice. The mice live not in the walls so much as in the bricks themselves, in the pores of the mudbrick, in the grain jars where beetles also live, in every warm crack where crickets keep their perpetual metronome. At night the cats hunt the mice, and the mice have learned exactly how close to the cats' sleeping places they can afford to be. It is a noisy, close-quarters predator-prey that never leaves the walls, and everyone — cat, mouse, human — is used to the proximity.

And then the people, eighty or a hundred of them, who to an outsider count as a few dozen families and to an outsider count as one household, but inside distinguish themselves by terrace and by craft and by which loft above which shop they sleep in. You do not so much walk past them as negotiate past them. Elbows in, sideways step, bucket held close so you don't knock the rows of earthen jars that line every shelf. Laundry hangs at head height so you duck. Gourds and herb bundles and coils of rope and caged birds and spare loom shuttles hang close enough to brush your shoulder as you pass. Everybody's work brushes everybody else's work.

They are never still, and they are never silent, but not in the way a market is loud. A market is loud outward. This is loud inward. A loom thumps in a doorway against a hammer striking somewhere lower against a child shouting his lesson against chickens complaining in the court below that. The potters shape while they talk, and their talk is part of the shaping. The weavers — there are a dozen looms visible at once, some in doorways, some tucked under houses — sing to keep time with the shuttle, snap and thump, snap and thump, a rhythm you can hear from the center court to the farthest wall. Conversation itself has to rise. People shout greetings up two terraces, not because they are rude but because the plaster and low ceilings trap and reflect everything back at them. A door that is not quite shut bangs in every draft from a shaft. The drip bowls add a high, perpetual counterpoint, tap, tap, somewhere behind the pots.

Work defines them more than anything else, though perhaps not work as we would call it. It is hand-work multiplied by density and by hours. There is a small forge at the lowest level where a boy works the bellows in time with the potter's wife singing, and produces perhaps one new iron nail or knife blade a day. That is enough. Everything else is wood and stone and clay and fiber and bone: stone hammers, adzes, spindles, rope pulleys that haul water and grain up and down the light shafts, hand-carts with solid wooden wheels that travel the twenty paces that matter and then stop, because there is nowhere far to go. Someone is always replastering a wall, always re-tying a thatch edge, always sweeping ash from a hearth into a pile to be ground and made into new bricks. A crack that opens to raw earth is filled and smoothed the same day. Repair is not an event here. It is a rhythm, like the bell.

The bell is small and hand-cast, rung by a rope, marking the hour not because anyone needs it — the light does that far better, falling in sharp rectangles down the shafts and moving across the terraces all day, telling the weavers where to sit to dry and where to sit to weave and where the fermenting jar in the kitchen will catch sun — but because by the time the sound has climbed the shafts it has become part of the air. They ring it because they have always rung it.

Their bodies know the place in a way their minds perhaps do not need to articulate. The floors are worn concave in the middle of every passage where feet have passed for generations, so a child can feel his way in the dark by the hollow. Door edges are rounded to the thickness of a thumb where shoulders have passed. Plaster shows twenty layers where an old hand has smoothed over the last repair without quite removing it. Tools are shiny where grips have polished them. A brick chipped a decade ago is now a soft edge a child learns to feel for without looking. The people are worn smooth in the same way. Nothing looks new because nothing is new, but nothing is collapsing either.

You can tell where a person belongs by dust. The top terraces, near the hatches, are dusty and parchment-dry: flour dust from the grain handlers, soot dust from the hearths, fine grit from the tiles. Their clothes, their hair, their hands are pale with it. They are the ones who notice the laundry snapping in shaft-drafts first. As you go down, warmth gathers. The bottom is moist and close. By the kitchen and the communal bath hollow the air smells of wet clay and boiled grain and dung and thyme. The women there work in rotation around a great blackened pot and a sunken clay tub where people wash, and they have known for generations that in the cool of the northern wall, breath and cooking condense and bead and run in a slow line. Beneath that line someone has permanently set a shallow bowl, and next to it another, and next to those a row of jars with cloths over their mouths. The person who tends those bowls — it is always someone, in rotation, without thinking about it — is a water-carrier without a river. Rain, when it comes, pours down the shafts in bright curtains and hisses on the hot tiles and splashes into the central cistern and into a dozen smaller basins placed strategically under every drip-line, and for twenty minutes the whole hamlet is loud with drumming water. Then it stops, and the dripping continues for hours; everything that was wet slowly gives its water back. On a cloudy day, lamps of oil and fat are lit in niches, though no one seems to need them much.

That same tenderness extends to the other lives that share the damp pockets. In the very bottom corner, where the floor dips a finger's width, a shallow puddle almost never entirely dries after rain. The people will not pave it over. Frogs live there, small and mud-colored, and algae greens the rim of the clay. Elsewhere damp means a darkened patch of plaster and moss beginning at a crack — and the moss, when it rains, is the loveliest thing in the Undermade, fine bright lines appearing along the mortar cracks of the northern wall, as if the bricks themselves had learned to photosynthesize. It grows only on made surfaces here. Wild ground, being none, grows nothing.

So too do the herbs. There is no soil except what people have mixed into boxes and terraced planters and woven baskets. Thybean: thyme and dust-mint and a bitter leaf for tea, in wall pockets where plaster has been scooped out and filled with compost and watered from the drip bowls. A few potted shrubs by the entry sit more as furniture than as plants. Sprouting grain is set in saucers near the kitchen, a crop tasted more than harvested. Earthworms work the compost boxes unseen, turning the made earth over and over. In earthen tanks by the bath, small fish eat algae and mosquito larvae, glinting when the light catches the shaft. All of it is small, tended, and complete.

The central court at the bottom of the stepped bowl is for the loudest residents. Chickens cluck under the basins and dust-bathe in the middle, a single pig roots and processes waste and complains, and a few goats are tethered and untethered all day, nibbling tethered rope, bellowing for the tether to be moved, their feet polished by a hundred other hooves. They are alarms and processors and companions in one. At night the chickens roost in their built coop, the cats take the wall-caps, the crickets take over the sound, then the owl in the forge rafters — tame, fed, taking moths and sometimes a mouse, in return for his presence among the bellows — and then the drip again, and somewhere in a loft a cough, a shift, a pot lid clinking. It drops at night, but it does not go quiet. The Undermade has a lower volume, never silence.

What comes over time, as you watch them, is how completely they have erased the distinction between living in a place and living in what was made. The children born here have never touched unworked earth. To them a "stone" is something fired in a kiln, shaped to look like a rock to hold up a terrace. If a wild seed blows down a shaft and sprouts in a crack, it is either transplanted into a box or carefully removed, because an unmade thing staying on its own would be a kind of failure. It is not superstition, exactly. It is a law they were built under and have maintained ever since: no natural substrate is allowed to stay exposed, no wild shape is allowed to keep itself for long. If you dropped a stone here, in a week it would be ground into mortar or carved into a hammer head.

And so each life, in its own way, has agreed to it. The swallows occupy. The frogs live only in clay jars and in the one puddle people protect. The vine has learned the strings. The moss knows the mortar. The people know the hollows in the steps. An outsider might call it odd, this hamlet that is not built *upon* the earth but built *instead* of it, a hundred people and a hundred other species packed into sixty paces, all of them remade a little every day so that the place can go on being exactly what it is.

At four in the afternoon, if you stand in the central court, you can hear all of them at once and feel all of them at once — sun in the shaft, a goat pulling at its rope, chickens clucking, the smell of wet clay and boiled grain, the heat of the tiles above radiating down into the top rooms even as the hearths die, the damp cool of the bowl at your feet, the farthest loom and the nearest drip in the same breath. That is who lives there: everything, close enough to touch, kept smooth by being held.
