Nothing living down here breathes. That is the first condition of residency, and everything else has arranged itself around it.

What moves, then, is not alive in the way a place usually is alive. At first glance the room looks empty of inhabitants and full of equipment, and that is not quite wrong, but it is not quite right either. The machines are not being used. No one is using them. They are running themselves in overlapping cycles, feeding on power that comes through the bulkhead on an umbilical thick as a spine, and excreting finished work back out through the same dark. Between an intake of current and an output of room, they print, they lift, they fold, they weld, they scrape, they carry, they inspect, they fail, they are eaten and re-made. It is an ecology translated entirely into logistics, predator and prey without the hunger.

They have territories, because they have to, in a room this crowded.

The Cart lives closest to the ground, where the cold pools ankle-high and the channels run. It does not walk so much as it negotiates. There is no straight walkway to follow; it weaves between crate-stacks and cable tiers and terracing slabs, increments one crate, one panel at a time, with a padded, muffled clank that has been insulated into softness. You hear it more in your feet than your ears, a low thrum traveling through the interlocking slab by slab. Its pace sets the room. When the Cart passes, dust does not get a chance to become dust. The Scraper-cart system is the room's weather of maintenance: everything that sheds is taken away, everything that fails is taken away, everything unnecessary is returned to feedstock before it can accumulate.

The Arm lives at mid-height, and it is the most animal-like of them, which is to say the most patient. It sweeps, pauses — a soft servo sigh — sweeps again, with a biological patience, like something cropping. It cropped the engineered cress first, when that was its shift. It lifts wall segments from the printer ridge and sets them against frames, folds a panel on a hinge that the next version will not need, turns a crate so its stencil faces outward. It works in light-pools, under the amber work-lamps that hang low just above the machines, and when it moves out of a pool its work continues anyway, because it feels along rails and edges by touch and proximity more than by sight. Its status light is green and patient. It does not tire, but it does repeat, and in the repetition a kind of care emerges: the same pause in the same place, the same reach, the same placing down, as if the motion itself had become a preference.

Above both of them, spanning the room, is the Gantry. It does not live in a place so much as it lives across places. Its travelling lamp means shadow does not stay fixed here; shadows crawl as it crawls, and a crate that was solid a moment ago becomes long and thin, then solid again. Under it the rain falls — warmth from the machines rising to meet the chilled heat-exchange plates overhead, condensing, returning as a slow intermittent rain inside a room with no outside. The Gantry passes through that rain without hurrying. Its printer head, when it is working, crosses with a faint hiss of extrusion. That is the Printer's voice, if it has one: not the Gantry itself, but the part of it that is laying down the ridge in the far corner, row by row, a pale aggregate-flecked slurry that steams faintly in the cold and hardens into something like stone but not stone. Its edge is wet. Its top is unfinished, soft. Rebar stakes up through it like uncombed hair.

The Scraper lives lowest and latest in the cycle. It is the eater. The Sensors — fixed along walls and on the gantry, green-eyed, never moving — inspect what the Printer has made and what the Arm has placed, and if it fails, the Scraper comes. It takes the failure back into feedstock with a deliberate, unhurried grinding. This is not punishment; it is digestion. Nothing here is wasted because nothing here can be spared. The room is too small and too sealed to afford waste. What fails becomes pellets again, becomes slurry again, becomes ridge again.

They never collide. They never quite synchronise. That is how you know it is a system and not a single will. The Printer produces a wall segment, the Arm takes it, the Cart removes it, the Sensor judges it, the Scraper eats it. Each waits for the other in turn, touching by proxy, by timing, by the space left between them. They are intimate the way things stacked to the ceiling are intimate — near-touching, layered, lashed in tiers — and they never touch at all.

That is the large fauna. The small fauna is where the room surprises you, because it was not supposed to have any.

In the light-pools, where damp finds a crack between modules and a lamp stays on too long, pale moss has colonised. It is not planted. It is tender, almost white, in thin felted patches, eating concrete dust and the air. The Scraper leaves it alone, mostly, because it does something useful: it scrubs CO2 and particulates from the scrubbed and re-scrubbed air. The system tolerates it as a filter. It is the only thing in the room that is not made, growing anyway in the cracks between what was made. It does not need photons rationed because it accepts whatever leakage the amber lamps give it. It waits, patient, sparse, through the scheduled dark cycle when the lamps go out and the machines continue blind, feeling their way along rails, laying down wet ridges in perfect black. The rain still falls then. The cold still pools. The moss waits.

Along one wall, in a row of shallow trays under their own small, bright LEDs — because light is expensive down here and must be accounted for — there is a more intentional planting. A sparse stand of engineered cress and algae, rationed photons, harvested by the Arm at intervals to feed a vat. It looks startlingly out of place, as if a memory of a garden has been filed among the tools. Its green is the only deliberate green in the room. Everything else that grows is grown in steel: bacterial film in the coolant loops, living on warmth and flow; algae bloom in the pale teal glass tubes, turning the water the colour of weak tea when the lights are left on too long. These are not tended. They are tolerated, monitored, flushed, re-seeded when the Sensor lights flicker. They live by accident and by utility, and they make the room's water — that same water, endlessly remembering being rain, drawn up by pumps from floor channels and sumps, filtered, heated just enough in the vats to evaporate again — slightly alive inside its loop.

So the food chain down here is inverted and complete. Power feeds machines. Machines feed on the cress and the algae by proxy, through the vat. The moss feeds on the air the machines foul. The bacteria feed on the coolant the machines warm. The Scraper feeds on the mistakes of the Printer. The Printer feeds on the Scraper's leavings. Nothing breathes, but everything metabolises.

And then there is the one in the far corner, behind the half-raised wall that is still only frames and wire. It is not yet living, in any sense the others are. It is being lived into being.

A smaller module sits there on its own new slab — same terracing, same sumps, same damp channels, same crates already stacked crate-for-crate against its wall as the stack in the main room, its travelling gantry now idle, its lamp dark, its moss tray empty and waiting. The Printer is not layering the main room anymore; it is layering the ridge you saw rising, which is the ridge of the copy. When the copy is complete, the Arm will fold it or seal it, and send it out through the umbilical dark to become another pocket elsewhere, below another surface, to begin building its own copy in turn.

That is the final inhabitant, and the reason the place feels unfinished even where it looks complete. The main room is not a factory for a product. It is a factory for rooms. It is total and embryonic at once, still in its first iteration, still raw, still being taught its own shape by its machines. You can see the places where the design changed — a conduit rerouted mid-wall, a panel left hinged open because the next version will not need it. Edges are sharp because nothing has softened them. Paint is half-done, orange primer on one gantry leg, grey on the rest. The place has not yet learned to decay.

It is crowded, it is cold, it is damp down to the bolt-heads beading with condensation, and it is almost perfectly quiet, except for the rain ticking to a minute, the fans cycling behind panels, the thin constant note of a seal leaking cold air like a held breath. The Cart increments. The Arm pauses, sighs, reaches. The Gantry's shadow crawls. The Printer hisses. The Scraper eats. The moss grows white in a crack, eating dust and air that were never meant for it. The cress drinks its rationed light. The water remembers rain and falls again.

This is not a place that was built and then lived in. It is a place in the act of being built, indefinitely, that has decided to be just enough of a room to contain the act — and everything that lives there lives by participating in that act, and by being, in turn, re-made by it.
