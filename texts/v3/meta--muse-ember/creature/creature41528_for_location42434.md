Nothing here lives singly, and nothing here lives alone. That is the first thing to lose, if you meant to keep it: the individual. What lives here arrives already in multiples, already finished, already in the middle of its act, and it stays that way by being replaced, not by continuing.

The smallest, and therefore the most numerous, are the marchers. Thousands of them, and no two are distinguishable in a way that would hold for more than a glance. Copper-winged, penny-bright, their bodies built on a polished, machined plan, legs ticking against wet stone. You see them because they spark, faintly, at the joints — a blue-white crackle in the thorax where charge gathers and gathers, and then discharges with a small, clean clank into the rock. They move in files, not swarms. File after file runs between the sparse presences of the grove: from a pool to a reed bed to the flat of naked stone and back again, up the fluted trunk of the tree in an unbroken column, across the moss in a line so straight it looks ruled. They do not hurry because there is nowhere to hurry to. They carry nothing you can see. They deposit nothing you can see. At the edge of the bowl, where the pattern overlaps itself, a file will pass through its own passing, thin and transparent for a step, and continue unaltered. If one stops — and they do not, exactly, they hesitate mid-step, the same hesitation again and again — the one behind takes its place without breaking stride. Their legs, when you look closely, are put on as if by a logic that has read anatomy backwards: left where right should be, right where left should be, working perfectly.

In the pools the pumpers do the loudest work. They are frogs the way a diagram is a frog: swollen throats, translucent skin the colour of iron-diluted water, eyes set for staring at nothing. They do not jump. They do not swim, in any sense that would get them elsewhere. They sit in the black marsh-edge water and pump. A sucking in, the throat bellows out, a labouring exhale of steam that is not quite steam. In, out. In, out. Their bodies rise and fall with a regularity that belongs to a valve, and the whole pool seems to breathe with them, though the water itself never ripples unless you have already seen it ripple. When one deflates completely it does not die. It resumes. Or another, in the same spot, with the same bulge in the same place, takes up the same stroke. You cannot tell the moment of exchange. There is no moment of exchange. There is only the pumping, continued.

And it is the pumping that feeds the hiss. The reeds are not empty. Their stems are hollow and jointed at exact intervals, translucent against the grey light, and every few seconds something below the bowl breathes up through them — a pressure, a cycle — and the reed gives it voice. A hiss, regular as a valve releasing, thrown back by the fresh rock until it is impossible to point to which reed spoke. Inside, if you could look, you would find not sap but a small, folded mechanism of membrane, opening and closing on that same cycle. The reed does not grow toward the light. It conducts. It takes the breath from underground and returns it to the condensing air, and the air, dense with water that has not decided to fall, takes it back and offers it again.

Underfoot, the moss is a single thing pretending to be a groundcover. Great velvet-black mats, lush where they grow at all and utterly absent elsewhere, they give like felt and hold the print of a palm longer than cold felt should. Touch one and you feel it before you see it: its whole surface rising and falling, a millimetre at a time, in unison. Not a shiver. Not a breeze. A breath shared across fifty paces of mat, so that when you step on one edge the far edge lifts. Dozens of tiny, blind grazers move within it, or perhaps it grazes itself, taking in condensation and giving it back in the same rhythm. The mat does not spread. It does not thin. It thickens and thins in place, like a lung deciding again to be a lung.

On the trunk of the one great tree, the bracket fungi have made a wall of themselves. Pale, layered, interlocking like gears, tooth against tooth, they turn — very slowly, so slowly you think you imagined it until the grinding begins. A low, rhythmic sound, stone on stone, that you had thought came from underground. It comes from here. Spores are ground to dust between the brackets and fall, and are reabsorbed by the wet stone below, and the stone weeps them out again as a thin sheet that spreads and stops. The fungi do not rot the tree. They work it. They are the tree's industry, or the tree is theirs. Above them the canopy does not move. Its leaves are lush, dense, unfallen, utter. Under it lies leaf litter from what must be eternity, and no leaf is in the act of falling. The tree itself, fluted and hollow, leaning over the whole bowl like a vault, might be the oldest thing here, except that nothing here is old. It is fresh-joined, sharp-edged, newly set into its place, and it has already learned to hold still forever.

The birds — or the things that have learned bird-shapes — sit in that unmoving canopy and in the corners of the grove, and call. It is always the same phrase, from every corner at once, in perfect synchrony, and it never finishes. A note, a repetition, a tightening, and then the stop before the last note, as if the phrase itself were a loop with the end sealed to the beginning. They are glossy and black in the sourceless light, their legs reversed, and they perform arrival backwards: a bird lands, and only then do its wings fold, neatly, impossibly, after it is already perched. It takes off already folded. It never trembles. When it calls, its throat works exactly as the pumpers’ throats work, a sucking in and a labouring out, and the echo of the call arrives, sometimes, a fraction before the call itself.

Below it all, through the soles of your boots, comes the grinding. Low, rhythmic, patient. Something down there is turning, stone against stone, in the dark beneath the fresh-split rock, and the pressure it makes is what the reeds hiss with, what the fungi grind to, what the moss breathes in time to. You never see it. You are not meant to. It is the engine-room of a factory that was never built, and its inhabitants are pressure and return, pressure and return, all the way down.

And in the pool nearest the tree stands the long-legged one. It is alone, which here means it is singular at any given moment. Tall, reed-thin, its body a held pose of standing: head at an angle, one leg bent at a joint that is not quite a joint, its reflection in the water leaning at a different time than it does. Its shadow, when there is light enough to have one, falls toward the light. You can watch it for a minute and swear it did not move, and yet when you look away and look back it is in the same pose with its head at the same angle, and it is somehow certain that it turned while you were not looking. It does not feed from the pool. It does not drink. It stands in the iron-coloured water the way a valve stands in a pipe: in the flow, not of it. If it is replaced, it is replaced by itself.

At the thickest places, where condensation beads and hangs and runs in the wrong direction by a fraction, your own breath comes back to you as mist in front of a face that is not yours, though the face is. That, too, is an inhabitant, of a kind: a thing that lives only as exhalation, that forms, hesitates — exactly as the droplet on the tree leaf hesitates, swelling, darkening, holding — and dissipates into the air that is already full of itself. It does not speak. It opens and closes its mouth at the pace of the pumps.

None of them accumulate. None of them deplete. The insects march and discharge their charge into stone that weeps it back. The frogs pump water in and out of themselves in equal measure. The fungi grind spores that the stone reabsorbs. The reeds hiss pressure they did not make. The birds call a phrase that does not end. The long-legged one stands. The moss breathes a millimetre up and a millimetre down. Time here is not a gradient they live along. It is a circuit they are wired into, and their lives are the current going round: loud, even, industrial, humming at a constant volume with no machine to point to, because the machine is what living has had to become in a place torn fresh from a wetter, lower world and set down high, still trying to run its marsh processes on knife-edged rock, still trying to run at all.

Watch any one of them long enough and you will see the hesitation. The droplet swells, darkens, hesitates. A marcher stops mid-step. A pumper holds its throat full. A bird holds its beak open before the note that never comes. You have seen it before, in exactly that way. Look away. Look back. It is exactly where it was.
