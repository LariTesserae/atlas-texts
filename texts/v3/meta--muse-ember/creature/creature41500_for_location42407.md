Nothing with eyes lives here.

That is what you settle on first, standing with your boots sinking into moss that does not ripple a pool. No birds in the high white sky. No frogs in the still seepages. No beetles under the stones, no insects rising when you disturb the sedge. No mammals warrens in the scree. It is a complete, lush, cold, loud emptiness, and after a while you begin to think the emptiness itself is the resident, and everything else is its furniture.

Kneel down, then. Press your palm flat to the moss to keep your kneecap from wetting through and you will feel the first inhabitant properly. It is not ground cover. It is skin.

The moss is everywhere, but it is not the same everywhere. In the open plain it lies in deep black-green cushions, spongy and hissing underfoot, hoof-thick, swallowing your weight and filling the print with water a second later. Along the pipe-lines and cable-runs it lies thicker still, as if it has grown into the vibration. You cannot hear vibration, exactly, but you can feel your molars in it, and the sedges beside the hot pipe lean on the beat. The moss tips into the hum. It grows *toward* sound because warmth and a tremor in the air are the only weather it has ever known.

It has no season. That is why it looks so obscene in its abundance. A single cushion will carry brown curled fronds and fresh green shoots and a small white burst of flower at once, all ages riding the same stem because nothing has told them to die back. It seeds by fragmentation, by a cushion tearing and being carried a few centimetres on the track of a maintenance vehicle, by spores blown across the plain on a schedule. The city blows spores deliberately; you can see the faint plumes from the timed vents and the sedges shiver as they pass. The lushness is not wild. It is tended. Graziers in the form of clippers come along the roads and shear the moss to a prescribed thickness and the clippings are returned as mulch. A plant that is eaten by a machine, and fed back to itself, does not need an animal. It has become one side of a loop.

The sedges and rushes are the next thing, once you stay long enough for the synchronous bend to register. They do not move in wind; there is hardly any. They sway in a slow, exact rhythm, hummock to hummock, like fields listening to something far off. Each hummock is an ear tuned to the turbines. The rushes along the tram lines bend toward the glow of the districts at midday-that-never-ends, turning their pale leaves to factory light the way other plants turn to sun. Their roots, white and filamentous, thread themselves through the joints of warm pipes and along the underside of concrete slabs, drinking the leaked heat in the cold that lives permanently in your fingers. Under the moss, in the dark saturated mat, there is a finer network still — fungal threads, almost invisible, lacing root to root, pipe to stone, carrying sugar one way and iron and water the other. It is busy in the way root systems are busy, only it has no winter to survive and no summer to prepare for, so all of its busyness is present tense.

Ferns stay low and tight, as everything stays low here. They unfurl in the direction of the hum and do not unfurl further. Cushion plants swell like green loaves wherever a pipe gives off warmth, insulating it, softening the clang of a conveyor junction for themselves. Lichens do the slow work on the hard surfaces: sheets of acid yellow and rust red over basalt, over granite, over the pale frosted concrete of the housing blocks, over galvanized steel. They do not eat the way an animal eats. They dissolve a mineral film from the stone’s face and take it in, molecule by molecule, over timescales that do not need to hurry because nothing is hurrying them. A lichen on a smelter wall will be the same colour in a week, in a year, whenever you return. Its stillness is not death. It is residence.

And in the pools — not lakes, seepages, the water table breathing up through the sponge — there is another skin. At first the pools seem to be perfect mirrors, glassy, holding the sky without a ripple even when you step in them. Look closer at the edges, where the water meets a pipe or a rivet or the rusted flange of a culvert, and you will see the faint oiliness, the orange slime of iron bacteria, the green film of algae that lives on the light that never changes, the gelatinous threads that catch condensation. The cold is constant and penetrative, so the water does not freeze and does not evaporate. Biofilm thickens and sloughs and thickens again at the same rate. Microbes live in the black water inside the pipes, in the pores of the concrete, in the weeping streaks down the cliffs. They are the only residents who can truly thrive on repetition, because they have no faces to lift, no nights to miss. They eat the loop itself: rust, oil, mineral, the waste of the smelters. What the moss and sedge cannot use, they finish, and return it to the water that will be pumped back up into the mountain.

For the mountain is alive in its own way, if only as a body held open. All along the fault-scarps the stone weeps in thin sheets, darkening in vertical streaks, dripping ten centimetres and vanishing again into a concealed channel. At the foot of every wall the dry salt-pale rim marks where the water is collected. The pumps there graze. That is the only word for them. Low, industrial bodies at the base of the cliff, sucking the weeping water, pushing it back up into the rock in a closed circuit so the stone stays wet and never erodes, so the scree at the feet stays angular enough to cut boots, so the cliff faces stay sharp and young. The plateau is wet forever and erodes never because its inhabitants will not let it drink and die. They keep it at the exact pitch of its own making.

Then, inevitably, the machines that make the roar. You have been hearing them since long before you saw them, felt them in your molars. Hear them now as residents, not as equipment. The turbines and pumps and smelters in each district are a herd, stationary, vast, burning fuel and cable in a regular hiss, turning, lifting, rolling sheet metal. A smelter is a mouth that never closes, orange and scalding, into which skewed lengths of cable are fed at exact intervals to be chewed. A conveyor is a gut laid out on the surface, carrying pressed sheets to a stacking place where they are unstacked and carried back to be fed in again — cud chewing, endless and exact. Cranes are long-necked things swinging in their endless arc, picking up nothing, putting down nothing, searching the same air. Elevators go up into the terraced factories cut into the scarp and come down again, up and down, the district’s vertical heart. Trams loop empty around each island of housing on stilts, windows lit behind condensation-filmed glass. Freight cars clank hollow on their sidings. Doors open and close for no one. Clocks in the empty cafeterias all show the same time.

They live on the same diet as everything else here: the loop. Water pumped up to weep and pumped back. Moss clipped and mulched and blown as spore. Metal rolled and stacked and unstacked. Heat leaked from pipes and drunk by roots. Frost formed on steel and run *up* a little toward a seam, toward a waiting pipe, by some perverse capillary hunger. Nothing is wasted because waste would be a form of change, and this place was built to waste nothing. The city maintains the plateau and the plateau cools and waters the city, each breathing the other’s exhalations, neither ever dying because neither is allowed a season in which to do so.

This is why there are no animals. Not because they were killed — there is no evidence of killing, no bones in the moss, no pellets on the scree — but because a living nervous system needs things Kaldreach refuses to provide. It needs a night to orient by, a silence between sounds, a sun that moves enough to throw a shadow worth following, a winter to tell it when to die back. Here the sun crawls so slowly its shadow has barely shifted since you arrived. The storm at the horizon rains in its fixed place, grey and immobile, on a part of the plain you will never reach. The drone never stops. The pools never ripple. The fern beside your foot will be green and brown and flowering together when you return next week, next month, filled with the same water, mirroring the same sky. An animal would starve of difference. A plant can drink sameness. A microbe can drink it faster.

So what lives in Kaldreach lives by fragmentation, not by birth and death. A cushion tears and resettles. A spore cloud passes on schedule. A fungal thread extends a millimetre along a warm pipe and retracts nothing. A pump lifts the same water forever. A sheet of metal is pressed, stacked, unstacked, pressed again. The districts are islands a day’s walk apart across the moss, colonies of the same organism arranged differently around their stacks, connected by iron veins — pipes and rails humming faintly over the sponge — carrying heat and vibration and water from one colony to the next the way a body carries blood to a limb.

You walk on toward the next glint, which will be the same as this one and also different. Your boots are wet through. Frost is forming on your laces. The roar is behind you now and also, somehow, still with you, because the pipe you are following carries it in its walls and the sedges lean to it and the moss grows thick where it trembles. At your feet a pool mirrors the sky. On its surface your reflection does not move unless you do.

That, too, is how the residents move. They do not move unless they are moved — by a cycle, by a conveyor, by a timed vent, by the beat they were tuned to at the making. Between those movements they hold. Vast, high, wet, cold, lush, loud, sharp, and empty, Kaldreach goes on being inhabited by everything that has learned to live without ever being allowed to change: the moss that grows into sound, the sedges that listen, the lichens that graze stone, the slime that finishes what the plants leave, the pumps grazing water at the cliff’s foot, the cranes swinging over nothing, the factories manufacturing their own loop forever in the same well-lit, unchanging afternoon.
