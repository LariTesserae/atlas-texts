First, there are too many to count in anything like the way we count.

Not a population. Not a colony. A press of lives so close that touching is the default condition, so that to be here is to be leaning against something alive, to be crawled over, to be built upon, to be eaten or to eat, all at the same slow temperature. They do not move through the chamber so much as move through each other. A current finds its way through the gaps they have left for it, and those gaps are the streets, and the streets are always narrowing.

The oldest are near the hinge.

There the shell is thick and ridged and the nacre runs in tight arcs, and everything has had time to accrete. Mussels live there in cemented rows, shoulder to shoulder, foot long gone into the silt, shells half-buried in their own kind. They do not look like they do anything. They open, a fraction, and the frill of their gills shows, pale and beating with a rhythm so slow you could miss it if you watched, and they close again. They anchor themselves with byssus — threads finer than hair, spun from a gland in the foot — and those threads sling across alleys, hold houses to walls, lash a leaning tube-spire to its neighbor. Some of these mussels are older than any other living thing in the chamber. They were small once, when the place around them was mostly bare shine. Now cliffs of them subside under their own weight, ridges grow over ridges, the dead of their parents become the substrate for their children, and a mussel that dies does not leave: its shell is colonized, bored, built over, becomes a step, a wall, a room for something smaller.

Above and below them, and everywhere denser, the tube-builders.

Serpulid worms in translucent tubes, stacked, leaning, braced against each other like fallen trees turned into houses. Their tubes are chalky and layered with silt and old shells, five and six of them high where the rib gives them a ledge. From the mouth of each tube the worm extends a crown — a fan of hair-thin tentacles that wave, taste, catch. In the darkness the crowns look like a field of faint plumes slowly combing the water. To feed, the whole city does this at once, in loose asynchronous waves, and the water between them thickens with motion. When disturbed, each worm retracts in a fraction of a second, which here is fast, a vanishing that sends a shiver up its tube. Tube by tube, the shiver travels.

They are the architects because they cannot help it. Every wave of a crown stirs silt into place. Every tube is a new surface for something else to settle on. When a spire topples — and in the cold it topples slowly, undermined by its own weight — it does not become rubble. It becomes a ridge-road, a dam, a new ledge. Larvae in the current test it within a season.

The sponges are the other architects, and they do not build so much as spread.

Low, pale mounds, only a few cells thick at their edges, they creep across bare nacre where nothing else has yet taken hold. A sponge has no head and no individual face to recognize; it is a suburb of pores opening and closing in no particular order, water entering, water leaving, particles caught in a collar of microscopic whips. Frontier sponges are the palest, almost transparent at the margin, so that the bright shell seems to shine through their tissue. Further in, where they are old, they are thicker, ridged, harboring small tenants in their canals — amphipods, mites, larvae that will never swim again. They eat by filtering, in the same patient way as everyone else, and in doing so they turn passing rain into flesh that will, in time, be eaten.

And then, far more numerous than any builder, the crawlers.

Blind white amphipods and isopods, unpigmented, translucent enough that you can sometimes see the dark line of a gut moving through them. They have many legs and they use them without hurry, scuttling in processions that seem unending because they are. They go everywhere the water can carry a body and some places it cannot. Over mussel beds, over tube spires, over sponge domes, over each other. They are the cleanup and the grazing and the restless traffic. They eat dead tissue, algae filaments, bacterial film, shed tubes, the soft parts of anything that stops moving. Their eyes, if they have them, are pits. They navigate by touch and by the taste of water that has passed over something rotting a moment before. In the tunnels that have roofed over in the old core, where light from the seam never reaches, they are the only motion, pale processions feeling their way along mucus roads laid down by others.

With them move the ostracods and copepods and mites too small for scale to matter — seed-shrimp no longer than a grain, darting and then hanging still in the water, heart beating visibly. And the rotifers, which are not really crawling at all but turning, their ciliated wheels spinning so fast they blur, lifting themselves from surface to surface like dust that has decided to swim. They are present in clouds, in the spaces between larger bodies, in the boundary layer where water barely moves. Individually they are nothing. Collectively they are a haze of mouths.

The fish come and go.

Small, eyeless, pale, they slip through the seam when the current steepens, and for a while they are weather inside the chamber. They do not belong, exactly. They circle the outer districts, gills flaring, bodies held up by water that is always, everywhere, buoyant with cold. They nudge between tubes, suck in silt-laden water and expel it, take an amphipod here, a worm crown there if the worm is slow to retract. Their bodies are soft and without scale shine, adapted to dimness and to pressure. They are huge by local standards, and yet they too are prey, in the larger lake above, or they leave again on the ebb and are gone. Inside, no one pursues them. They are a pulse, not a resident, and their passage is felt as a displacement, a bulge rolling through the streets and setting every plume to swaying for a moment after they have passed.

The true resident hunter is the shrimp.

Translucent, with long antennae trailing ahead like feelers in the current, it pushes in through the mouth headfirst, legs paddling, eyes empty-looking and blind. By the measure of the chamber it is a leviathan, longer than a mussel bed is wide. It does not rush. Nothing here can rush for long in the cold. It advances along a rib, tapping tubes with its legs, testing. Where its pressure front arrives, the city answers before it is touched: crowns vanish, mussels snap, amphipods freeze or scatter into silt. A wave of retraction runs from district to district, a silent stadium wave, and then silence again while the shrimp picks, slowly, at what has not retracted fast enough. It eats and moves on, and behind it the re-opening begins, tentacle by tentacle, foot by foot, as if the chamber is inhaling again.

The green lives are few because the light is few.

Along the seam, where the dimness is fractionally paler, olive algae hold on in sparse filaments, each one isolated like a hair, swaying in the breath of the current. They do not form a meadow. They are concessions: a bit of local production where a bit of light arrives. Where there is no light, bacterial mats take over — black-green films spreading over silt in the darker corners, over dead shells, over the lower parts of tubes. You would not know they were alive by looking. They do not move. They exude slime, they bind silt into a springy skin, they digest what everyone else leaves, and their chemistry leaks into the water as food for others further down the chain. In their films, smaller bacteria swarm and divide and die, and are eaten by rotifers and amphipods, and so on up through a food web that has no top except the occasional passage of a fish or a shrimp from outside.

And everywhere, raining in, the lives that have not yet arrived.

Larvae are always falling through the seam on the tide — mussel larvae with tiny translucent shells, tube-worm larvae with a ring of cilia, sponge larvae that look like unformed tissue, bacterial spores, the eggs of amphipods carried in a mother's brood pouch until they release. They are food for those who filter, and they are colonists for those who wait. On the bare nacre districts, where the shell still gleams unoccupied, a patch will suddenly have taken hold: a fuzz of first tube-builders, no bigger than pinheads, waving tentacles that have not yet built tubes, testing the surface with their feet. Or a sponge margin edges forward a cell's width and does not go back. That is how new neighborhoods start. Not by founding, not by plan, but by a current dropping something where it happened to stick, and that something living, and its living making sticking easier for the next.

They mate in silence, by releasing eggs and sperm into the current and trusting the tide to do the arithmetic. They molt. Their shells split along a seam and the soft white body inside pulls free, leaving a perfect empty copy that will be used by someone else within days. They die where they live, and because nothing is carried away except by the slow breath of water, the dead stay. A dead mussel is a house. A dead worm is a tube for a smaller worm. A dead amphipod is a meal that feeds a hundred rotifers. Silt and pellet and shed shell are mortar. The chamber is filling in from its walls inward, layer by layer, at a rate no human would notice in a lifetime and everyone here notices in theirs.

In the roofed tunnels of the old core, where builders have grown up and over and met, there is another kind of life that is almost not life at all — the flashers.

Creatures no larger than grains of sand, lodged in pores and old tubes, that send out a pinprick of cold blue light, on, off, on, in that dark tunnel where even the chamber's dimness does not reach. It is not for seeing by. It is too brief, too small. It might be a mating signal, it might be a startle, it might be the chemical glow of something digesting. Whatever it is, it is the only light that originates inside, and for the instant it is on, the walls of the tunnel — which are made of other lives — catch it and throw it back, nacre and tube and chitin gleaming for a fraction of a second before returning to the everywhere dark.

No one knows there is an outside. No one knows there is a lake, or ice, or a country far away. Their whole sky is the curve of shell overhead, which for some is a ceiling and for others, born inverted on the dome, is the ground they crawl on, growing down toward a floor they will never fall to because there is no down that matters, only toward the current and away from it. Their whole geography is measured in ribs and alleys and bridges of byssus. A fallen spire is a landslide. A night is the hours between tides when the seam breathes out and the water goes still and even the slow beating of gills seems to pause.

They live densely, silently, in cold that aches through everything, filtering what passes, building only from what dies there or washes in, waiting for larvae to rain and for hunters to come and for the slow accretion to meet itself above them. In a few hundred years the dome and floor will join and the pocket will close and fossilize into a single stone made of a city. Until then they are embryonic and crowded and busy at the edges, feeding and settling and being eaten, in water with no air and no end, in a dimness that never clears and never quite becomes night.
