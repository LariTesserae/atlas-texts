You do not meet them at first. You meet what they have left, which here is more reliable than a face.

In the pale dust there are bootprints layered over bootprints, not going to a door. They go to the leg of a conduit tower, then off along a pipe, then stop. At the stopping place the dust is pressed into a shallow seat, and the stone beside it is darker, where a back has leaned for a long time. The prints are crisp. They have been there for a very long time, and they were old before you arrived, and someone has walked in them again on top of their own earlier self, so the tread is blurred into a single continuous groove. You could follow it for kilometers and it would not wander. That is the first sign of the life here: not movement, but repetition.

The second sign is smaller, and slower.

Along the warm conduit you may find one of the crawlers. You will probably take it for a stone at first — everyone does — a low, elongated stone, basalt-grey, armored in overlapping discs, each disc smooth on both sides like the pebbles of the plateau. It is blind. Its face is no face at all, merely the front edge of the plates lifting a fraction to find the air. Under the rim there are legs, short and many, that fold and unfold with a deliberation that makes you doubt that anything has moved. It advances perhaps the width of a fingerprint while you watch, and then it stops and sits.

It grazes, if the word can be used. There is nothing to graze on but dust and the faint film that gathers where a warm electrical joint breathes out metal onto cooler stone. It lowers its mouth-slit to the dust, tastes, lifts, tastes again. It seems to be tasting for iron, for heat, for the note of the hum. The hum is loudest here, under the tower, in your molars. The crawler gathers with others, two or three, rarely more, in the exact place where the pipe is a degree warmer. They sit in a loose row, plates overlapping the dust, and they do not move for a very long time. To tell them from the plateau, you must learn patience. That is also, you come to understand, how the plateau tells itself from them.

The keepers live in the same places as the crawlers, for the same reasons. You will mistake one for a larger crawler, or for a stump of built stone, before you mistake it for a person.

It is sitting against the leg of a transformer tower, back to the tower, boots out in front of it, knees drawn up, head down. Its coat is of basalt fiber, grey and heavy and polished where it rubs — at the elbows, across the back where it has leaned, at the shoulders where small plates of stone have grown or been sewn into the coat, hard as pebbles, worn disc-smooth by contact with walls and pipe. Its hands are in its lap, long-fingered, the nails worn flat like teeth. Its skin is dry to the point of dust. When you come close enough to hear your own boots, it does not turn. It lifts its head a fraction, slowly, and you see a face that has been planed in the same way as everything else here: nose broad and flat, mouth a thin line, eyes set deep under thick lids, open only to slits that catch the amber from a window and the white from a conduit light. It has altitude in its ears too, and always has; the inner ears are large and flat against the skull, not for hearing speech but for feeling the ground. The stone here is one continuous skin, and under it, far away, a press thumps, a conveyor turns. It feels that as a pressure under the heel of its boot long before it could hear it.

It is warm. Not fever-warm, not animal-warm in the way you are warm, but stone-warm, as if it has been sitting in this sunless heat for so long that its body and the seat under it have come to the same temperature and you must find it by obedience to the dust.

It stands up — the standing takes some time, as if it is unfolding rather than rising — and it walks. The pace is not a shuffle. It is a placement. Each boot comes down flat, full weight, the sole worn smooth, and stays a moment before the other is lifted. Crunch — and then, after the crunch, the long silence. Crunch — and silence. Listening, you realize it is listening to the district more than it is looking. It stops beside the pipe, puts a palm flat against it, and holds it there. It is feeling the rhythm inside the metal, the way you would feel a pulse. Then it walks on along its groove in the dust, the same groove it walked this morning, and in a season if there were seasons, and years ago.

The keepers are the tenants of the housing blocks, though they spend almost no time inside. The older blocks are eight or ten stories of basalt block, lower floors half-sunk and blind with dust, upper floors polished by weather. A keeper's room, if you are shown it, is always the top room, the one with the steady amber square. Inside there is one chair, one table, a shallow basalt disc set under a pipe joint to catch condensation drop by drop, tools left on the ledge — a hammer, a brush, a wrench whose metal has not rusted and never will — a coat hung, a pair of boots by the door left ajar. Everything is grey. Everything is coated with the same fine feldspar dust, except where a hand has wiped it away in a clean arc. They do not accumulate. They do not seem to own; they seem to *attend*.

That is their work, if work is the word. A district has a mill, a smelter, a transformer yard. One keeper attends each district, rarely two, because the equilibrium of the place seems to require only one presence near a machine to keep it knowing it is attended. The keeper sits near the smelter's vent and watches the heat shimmer the same way, breath by breath, at the same interval. It watches the slit of orange at the door and does not open it. It listens to a bearing turn in the mill and brushes dust from the housing with a dry brush, although the dust would settle again in exactly the same pattern. It puts its hand on the warm pipe and feels the conveyor go round in its closed loop below, carrying the same stone from a pit that never deepens to a mill that never fills. It does not fix. Nothing breaks. It is there so that the rhythm does not have to be alone.

Time, for them, is counted in those rhythms. A conveyor loop. A smelter breath. A hum that rises in the molars when you pass under a tower. They sleep sitting up, against a tower leg or against warm stone inside the district, head down, breathing slowed until the plates of their shoulders are still as a dome, for as many loops as they choose. There is no night to make them do it. There is no morning to stop them. They doze and wake and doze again in the same warm spot, and the crawler beside them does the same.

They eat what the place eats. They could not live otherwise. In the mill they gather the fine grey paste that the grinding makes — stone ground stone ground — and dry it into pellets on the pipe, and chew it slowly, taking iron and feldspar and rust-brown mineral out of it with a gut that works at the speed of erosion. Around the transformer joints they scrape the faint metallic condensation with a fingernail and take it on the tongue, as the crawlers do. A shallow disc under a joint fills, very slowly, with moisture that has been breathed out by machines and by themselves and taken back in again, and once in a long walk they drink it. They lose almost no water. They do not sweat. Their eyes do not water. Their throats are scraped raw by the thin dry air, and they speak rarely because of it and because speech is a kind of erosion they do not permit. When a keeper does speak, the voice is low and dry and the words are few and mostly for places: *Mill*. *Smelter*. *Tower*. *Far*. It is said with the mouth barely moving, as a hum might be said.

They travel, but rarely, and the travel is an event that can be measured in footprints left for years. Two kilometers of open black plain is not distance to them in the way it is to you; it is a crossing of a different hum. Each district hums in its own key — the mill lower, the transformer higher, a note you feel in the teeth — and a keeper knows its own district's note the way you know your own room in the dark. To walk to the next cluster of amber lights ten or twenty kilometers off is to walk out of your note into another, carrying the ache in your ears the whole way, following the old groove you or a keeper before you made in the dust, resting at each conduit tower where the air is a degree warmer, sitting with your back to the leg, palm on the pipe, feeling the other district get closer through the stone. Some, on a long crossing, will climb onto a belt conveyor and sit on the slow stone it carries, letting the loop do the work, getting off at the same unmarked place each time, leaving a new print over an old one. No one with them. No rail. No hurry that the place could recognize.

They meet, when routes cross under a tower, in the way they do everything: by sitting down together. Two keepers, perhaps three, and two or three crawlers between them, backs to the tower, boots out, in the warmest dust. The tower hums. Humming enters the molars whether they will it or not, and so they hum back, not in song, but in that low note, for a long time, until their breathing falls into the same interval. A hand may be placed flat on the stone between them, feeling the other's arrival still traveling through the plateau from kilometers away. They do not, as far as you can tell, discuss. To be in the same heat, in the same note, is enough. Then, after a number of conveyor loops you cannot count, one stands — unfolding — and places its boots in the groove and goes.

You will wonder, watching them, about the crawlers. A keeper will pause beside one, lay a broad hand lightly on its plates, and remain there a while, warming it or being warmed by it. The crawler does not move. Whether the crawlers are their young, or their kin, or merely the other tenants of the warm pipes, is not a question the place answers. You notice that the crawlers never leave the pipes. You notice that a keeper's plates thicken year by year, disc over disc, until a shoulder is armored like a crawler's back. You notice that over a very long time a groove in the dust that begins as a low drag, as if something has pulled itself along, becomes a bootprint. You never see the change. You only see that the district, which needs one keeper, always seems to have one, and that when a keeper finally sits and does not stand — when the breathing becomes the sitting of a stone, when dust settles over the coat and boots until the tower has a new low dome at its foot that looks natural from a distance and built from close — the amber square in the top room stays amber, and another groove, fresher, begins its long circuit from the next district.

They do not rot. There is no water to rot them. They do not rust. They mineralize in place, as the buildings mineralize, as the mesas were mineralized. A tool left on a ledge by such a keeper will still be there when you return, and the dust pattern around a boot will be identical, and the light will be the same steady amber, because the place has finished changing and they have finished changing with it, down to the tolerance of a river stone.

If you stay long enough, you will find you have begun to sit. Not because you are tired, though your breath is still unrewarded and your throat still scrapes. Because the keeper sitting against the tower does not look up when you approach with your loud boots, and after a while you understand that standing and looking is your noise, and you sit down in the warm dust beside it, back to the same leg, boots out in front of you, and you put your palm flat on the pipe to feel the rhythm. Your footprints, at first, are new and sharp in the pale dust. In time, if you walk the route, they will layer over themselves, and become a groove, and become a road no one else made and everyone will follow.

They are not the masters of the city, and they are not its servants. They are what the plateau makes, when it has been high and dark and warm for longer than there is a word for, and it makes something that can walk it slowly, tend its machines by presence, eat its dust, drink its condensation, and hum its note back to it under the towers, until the line between animal and rock, and rock and building, and building and animal, is worn smooth on both sides.
