If you stay long enough in the Bowl to stop listening for footsteps, you learn that the lives here do not gather. They are not in the market square, though the square is wide enough for them. They are not in the granary, or the bath-house, or the smithy, though their tools are laid out there as if work had only paused. They are in the intervals — between one half-roofed house and the next, between one drip and the next, between the gravel street and the marsh grass that has already taken back the edge of it.

The first inhabitants you are most likely to actually meet are the builders, and you will meet very few of them, and not for long.

Perhaps a dozen households are living up here now, in the houses that have gotten far enough to have a hearth and a whole side of thatch. Their smoke is thin because the pine is still resinous and the wood is still damp, and because there is not much of it burned at a time. It rises and is immediately wettened by the air, and hangs. You can smell it more than you can see it: pine resin sharp in the cold, peat smoke underneath, wool that never quite dries.

They are spare people in the way people get when sound travels too well. Their voices are practical and low, mostly about stone and water — roll, lever, carry, clear, tie that up, leave it — and then they are gone around a terraced corner and the street they were just on is empty again for yards and yards. Their hands are cold all day in a way that has nothing to do with winter. It is the damp cold that lives in the Bowl, the kind that gets into wool by morning and stays there through a fire. Their nails are split from bronze chisels and stone mauls. They carry stones in woven baskets up steps that are still bright with fresh chips, and they raise a wall a course a day, and they stop, not because the work is done, but because the vegetation has grown over the last three weeks and must be cleared again before they can work where they left it.

They do not hurry. They cannot. Everything here moves at the speed of seepage, and they have learned to move with it, to count a drip the way other people count a breath. A child — there are only a few, small and quiet, raised more by water than by talk — will stand beside an empty cistern and listen to a single bead swell for a long, patient minute and then fall three meters into the hollow bell and hear the *BOOM* roll off the cliff and roll back and roll again, slower, and count his own heartbeats inside it before it dies. That is their music. They do not talk over it.

They sleep in houses with no doors, or doors that do not close, under thatch that ticks in the night with rain that fell hours ago. They listen to shutters clap and unweighted shingles lift and slap on roofs that are not finished, to scaffolding poles knocking together in the wind over the rim, to water hammering in a blocked log pipe — *tok-tok-tok* — for an hour at a time. They do not seem to mind it. It is the sound of the place keeping its own time while they wait.

Because they are tenants, not owners. The plan is a hundred years ahead of them. The streets are broad for carts that do not exist. The reservoir is enormous and mostly dry. The tubs in the bath-house are sunk and empty. The forge is cold. What they are holding, in their cold hands, is not a city yet. It is the beginnings of one, tried out against a marsh that is also only beginning.

And the marsh, left to itself, is a far more numerous resident.

It has come in first, and it is everywhere the water can reach. On the lowest ground, where a thin skin of peat and black mud lies over scree and water stands an inch below the surface, sedge and cotton-grass stand knee-deep in no particular order and wave in a slow green tide. They do not bow the way grass in the lowlands does. They lean, soaked, and recover slowly, as if moving through something thicker than air. Dwarf birch and willow crowd the margins in low, heavy thickets, their branches hanging with damp, their small leaves dark and glossy. They live close to the water and close to the ground, as if not yet sure how tall they are allowed to be here.

Ferns grow out of the wall joints and out of the unfinished streets, in the cracks between flat stones fitted by hand for carts. They uncurl in the cold shade of retaining walls. Vines have already found the scaffolding and begun to pull at it, green fingers around pale pine poles, hauling themselves up toward light that is mostly white and flat.

And moss — moss is the carpet and the road. It makes a thick, spongy mat on every north face, on roof timbers, on the rims of pots left out to catch rain, on the feet of the stair-path where your boot sinks a little and makes no sound. In places it does not grow randomly at all, but in straight lines, following the lines of the streets where feet shuffle the spores but do not crush them. It is a living record of where people have walked and how often. It softens their steps and keeps them, and then it softens them again. It is pale in the low light, almost grey-green, like something that has never seen strong sun.

Beneath all of that, the peat itself is alive, deepening by a fraction each year. Where you poke a reed, dark water wells up, and it smells of rot and iron and new growth. Small things are at work in it that you will never see unless you kneel and look directly at them, and even then only for a second — pale, uncolored specks in the wet moss, springtails and mites and worms bleached by cold and by the newness of the place, their bodies translucent because there is nothing here yet to give them color. They take up residence in damp where no bird would think to look. They are cold and slow, like everything else, and they belong to the skin of the place more truly than any house does.

Not much larger than that lives here at all. That is the second truth, after the plants.

Now and then a heron picks its way through the marsh on long legs, silent, pausing in the black water, and is gone. It does not sing, it does not nest here, it does not stay. It seems to be checking whether the marsh is ready to be a marsh, and finding it almost, though not quite, and leaving again. A few frogs have come to the shallow pools near the reservoir, cold and slow, and will croak once, a blunt sound, and stop, as if surprised at their own voice and at the echo that comes back off the rock. They are pioneers in a place too cold and too new for them to thrive, living in water that never warms. No other birds. No stock animals — the builders brought none up, and the cold wet would not keep them, and there is no pasture yet, only sedge.

The water, in its turn, has tenants that arrived before the houses did.

Every empty vessel the builders made for human use has been colonized by something smaller. The great clay tubs in the bath-house hold a film of algae in the damp at their bottoms. The row of clay jars under the bronze rain chains in the market square hold rain that ticks down bead by bead, and in each jar a different note lives, and in the water itself, unseen, a cloud of microbes is laying down the first skin of life in a clean pot. The log aqueducts that carry spring water from the weeping cliff leak at every joint in a fine needling spray, and inside the hollow pine, where it is always wet and always leaking, fungi and slime are growing in the dark of the wood. The enormous reservoir on the lowest terrace is dry except for a shallow black pool that never quite drains, and that pool never ripples, even in wind. It only reflects, perfectly, the scaffolding above it upside down, so that a second town lives there permanently, built in black water, waiting in the same unfinished way as the one in air.

Even the drips themselves have a kind of residency. A bead on the cliff face swells for a minute at a time, clinging to limestone, and then it falls, and then the sound it makes lives longer than the bead did — *bong ... bong ... bong* — rolling in the Bowl and back again, never quite fading before the next fall swallows it. In the cisterns, in the tubs, in every sunken thing meant to hold water, the note goes up and hangs in the chest. The inhabitants of this place, if you listen, are as much the resonances as the things that make them: the shingles, the shutters, the creaking poles, the water hammering in pipes. They move in the wind that pours over the rim and they have no other work to do.

By late afternoon the fog begins to think about coming back in over the rim, and the stone weeps harder, and the threads of condensation on the inside faces of walls — thin vertical lines, as if the stone were breathing out — become visible again. The light does not go out, it thins. The cotton-grass stands still in the damp. The moss holds the last of the day's wetness in its sponge. In the houses with fires, the smoke remains thin. In the houses without, no one lives yet, but a vine has found the doorway and a fern has found the hearth.

No one here is fully at home. The builders are waiting for the peat to thicken enough to trust, for the roofs to be finished by children who are not yet born and are not yet old enough to finish them. The plants are waiting to be cleared and are already coming back. The frogs are waiting for water that does not freeze them slow. The heron is waiting for a marsh that is older. The small pale things in the moss are waiting for nothing at all; they are already doing the work that will make a marsh a marsh, a fraction at a time.

And in every empty vessel, patiently, the sound of water falling is waiting to be full.
