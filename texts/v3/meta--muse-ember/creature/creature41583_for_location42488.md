At first light they are not in the lanes at all. They are inside the walls.

The cold slate weeps before the sun is high enough to thin it, and before the town has quite woken, there is a sound under the dripping from the cracks: cloth being wrung out, hands finding the inside troughs that run along the lowest wall of every house, a wooden bucket clunking against stone. A woman — no one has come down a street to a door yet, because most doors do not open onto streets — lifts a hatch in her floor and steps barefoot onto the roof of the house below hers. Her neighbour does the same a moment later, from the opposite side, so that two people cross on a roof that belongs to neither of them and belongs to both. Their feet are already wet. Their reed cloaks, woven tight and pale as salt-bleached driftwood, are stiff with last night’s damp and softening again as the dew reaches them.

This is the first work, and it does not vary. Every interior wall sweats. Every house has its trough and its clay pipe, and every morning someone must walk — roof to roof, ladder to ladder, lane to lane — wiping the condensation down with cloth, wringing it into a bucket, clearing the pipe with a bone needle if the slow trickle has clogged with grit or lime. A child who is too small to wring properly is given the needle and sent to listen at the pipe junction, ear to the wall, to hear whether it is running. Loudness has never been needed here. The walls are thick and damp and they swallow sound, the drips are constant, and so people speak low to each other across a roof that is someone else’s ceiling, and the words arrive already close.

By the time the light turns hard and shines off the plain, the condensation has stopped. Their whole morning has been gathered, bucket by bucket, and carried — on pulleys, in hands, on heads — to the central cistern sunk into the rock. The cistern is not theirs until it reaches it. Until then, each house keeps its own sweat. No one throws water away, and a leak left unmended is not a nuisance but a threat: left alone it will cut a channel, take a wall, and take the houses above it in time. So the wiping is not housekeeping. It is survival, repeated daily because the rock will not stop weeping until midday, and will start again at dusk.

The children are the first to truly live on the surface. They climb notched driftwood ladders the way other children run down paths — quickly, certainly, barefoot — from a bedroom that was built over a bedroom that was built over a damp room given up as a cistern, up through a roof that someone’s grandmother re-plastered the summer before last. Houses here are not inherited. They are continued. A family lives stacked through itself: the oldest pair in the lowest dry room, their children in the rooms lifted above them, their children’s children in the rooms lifted above that, so that to visit your parents you go down, and to visit your children you go up, and a door often opens straight onto the roof of a neighbour below, who may be a cousin, and who expects you to wipe your feet before you cross. The houses are continuous because the people who live in them are continuous. When a mudbrick wall slumps in winter — and it always slumps a little — it is lifted and re-faced in late summer with fresh shell lime, the whole town whitewashing itself at once while the lime kiln smokes and men and women who have done it all their lives keep the work going in a slow, low-voiced rhythm, hands and forearms white and cracked, lungs full of it by evening.

The children do not go to that open ground by accident. There is only one piece of the table where nothing has been built: the newest splinter at the highest corner, the rock that still tilts after rain and will not hold anything square. The adults forbid building there, which is exactly why the children claim it. It is the only place in town where you can see the plain in every direction at once without standing on a roof, and the only place that is not somebody’s floor. They play there in the bright, colourless light, surrounded on all sides by weighted roofs and thin curls of driftwood smoke, miming the things they know best: the careful hand along a wall feeling for a drip, the climb down a ladder, the stoop at low tide after crabs among the pools at the foot of the table. Their games do not have teams or shouting. They have listening. One child presses an ear to the rock and counts the soft ticking that crosses the slab on a cold night, plate by plate, as it settles by a fraction. The others wait to see where she hears it next, because a new tick in the morning means a new crack in a lane by evening, and that crack will be somebody’s father’s work before dark.

They are shaped by that work without thinking about it. Strong calves from ladders. Sure feet on rubbery wet mud. Skin perpetually chapped by lime and salt, toenails worn thin, hair damp and matted under a hood of woven reed. No one is tall here; the houses are low and the ladders are steep. Their clothes are all the same in substance if not in cut: reed and kelp cord soaked and re-soaked until it gives, shell buttons, driftwood toggles, every lintel and bucket stave different because everything is found. They wear soft shoes when they must go down to the plain, and bare feet everywhere else, and their feet are always cold, even in sleep.

What they eat, and what eats with them, comes from the edges. Life is rare on the table itself, and they have learned to be precise about it. In the lanes after a tide, black eels thread the drains and appear slick in the ankle-deep run-off, and a boy following one with a bucket is not hunting in the way an outsider would understand. The eels are kept, mostly. They clean the drains. They are taken only when a drain runs clear and an eel is fat and slow, and then it is shared between the houses whose pipes it used. No one wastes an eel. At low water, when the plain turns to grey mud and shining pools that never empty, the town goes down the foot of the table — hand over hand, buckets tied to their waists — to work the fresh rock among the crabs. The crabs do not belong to anyone until they are lifted, and the pools give up shellfish and barnacles scraped from the lower stone, and the clumps of reed at the base where a spring finds the edge are cut carefully, never all of them, because the reed is cloaks and cord and the only thing here that can be woven.

Above, on the roofs, the green is theirs in a way the plain’s is not. Where rain has washed the salt away and soot and a leak feed it, moss and small tough weeds grow thick. The town looks, from a roof, as if it is wearing a thin ragged thatch of its own hair, and the children are sent up on the north side to pick the weeds sparingly, handfuls at a time, to go into eel broth with whatever halophyte they can find in the cracks where fresh seep gathers. The salt-tufts in the lanes are stepped around. A lane is a crack, a gutter, and a property boundary, and a tuft growing in it is a small, stubborn boundary of its own.

In the cistern itself lives the town’s most deliberate companions: pale, blind fish no bigger than a thumb, swimming in the black water where the pipes pour. The children feed them — crumbs, and the grey moths that drown themselves happily on the condensation at the cistern rim, skimmed off by hand before they sink. The fish are not eaten. They are a sign. A child on rota watches them for the whole morning, sitting on the rim with feet dangling, and if a fish dies the cistern is not drunk from until the pipes have been cleared and the water has run clear again. It is not superstition. It is the only instrument they have that is always in the water, and it works.

Very few things speak of anything older than the town, and they do not talk much about it. The oldest people alive remember when the sea floor slipped and tilted and this slab came up, fresh-sheared, with fossil shells still lying in their seabed patterns in the stone and barnacles crusted high on the faces where the sea used to reach. They remember houses on the plain that were not saved because there was nowhere to take them. Their grandchildren, born on the table, have no word that fits a tree, or soil, or a bird’s song — there are none to nest here, nothing to breed insects in, and the air is empty in a way that has become normal. Their language is rich instead in wetness: words for kinds of seep, for a drip that will hold, for a drip that will cut, for the hour when the waterfalls off the edge roar after rain and then are gone. The lichens crusting the slate in pale maps are the oldest living things they share the rock with, and they leave them alone.

To live here is to maintain, and the maintenance is the life. Listen at night and you can hear the counting go across the town as the plates contract, and in the morning someone will be filling a crack with stone by evening because the tide has undermined a corner in the night. A house left empty will weep itself to ruin in a season, so no house is left empty, and no person is left without a place on a ladder that leads somewhere. Babies are born into damp rooms kept warm by thickness and closeness, and fed on broth made from what the drains and pools gave up that day. The dead are not buried, because there is no soil to bury them in; they are taken down at low tide to the pools where the crabs work, and the plain takes them back.

Stand by the cistern at midday, when the work of the morning is done and the town has gone quiet again except for water moving in the cracks, and the blind fish are turning slowly in the dark below the rim, and the children are still on their splinter of rock watching the wet, shining plain go flat to the sea. Around you the roofs stretch wall to wall, each weighted, each chimneyless kitchen sending up its thin smoke to dissolve at once in the cool air. The people are everywhere in it and nowhere loud in it: wiping, listening, climbing, replastering, mending, feeding fish, following eels, coming back up from the crabs with their buckets. They live by catching what the rock gives off and not letting any of it go.
