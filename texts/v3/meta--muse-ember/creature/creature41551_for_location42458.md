They do not announce themselves.

The first lives are not separate from the walls. A matte, cream-colored skin films every tile, every bone rib of the vault, every cup on the stone tables — microbes drinking the dissolved warmth that rises from the vent below and laying it down as themselves. It thickens in still corners and thins where it has been grazed, so that the walls have a texture you can read blind: smoother where mouths have passed, rougher where they have not for a long time. Where the water has laid its minerals down over thousands of years the film crusts white and pitted, and the microbes live inside the crust as much as on it. Everything else here begins by eating them, or by eating the things that eat them, or by straining them out of the water as dead rain.

They are pale, all of them. There is no green and no need for color. Ivory, buff, translucent grey, the grey-black of wet stone — pigment would be a cost with no return in this darkness. None of them has eyes. Not the smallest grazer in the film, not the largest thing that patrols the retaining wall. A whole body is for feeling: a side-line ribbed along the flank that registers the ebb of another body, antennae whiskered and blind, suction feet that test a surface before they commit to it, gills that release a puff of chemical meaning *mine* or *move* or *stay* into the still water. They speak by pressure and by touch, antenna laid against antenna, flank pressed to a wall to say a path is clear. There are no clicks, no calls. The place is silent except for the made sounds under it — clay ticking as it contracts, a stone settling deep in the fill, the low hum of the vent felt in the belly through the paving.

You meet the crawlers first because you meet the floor first. Flattened and armored, they move the way the water moves: without hurry and almost without pause. A wall's width in a day will carry one from a terrace to the central vault, antennae sweeping the tile ahead of the mouth, suction feet releasing and re-gripping with a faint tick you feel more than hear through your wrist. They graze the microbial film clean off the mosaics, off the bone chips polished smooth, off the fired clay shards in buff and dull red, leaving in their wake the common soft level of the paving. Their armor takes the color of what they lie on. At night — there is no night, but there are the long unchanging hours — they crowd shoulder to shoulder on the underside of the vault's ceiling, three and four deep, sticky feet and filter fans and egg-strings layered over the stone until the stone itself is mostly animal and slightly moving.

That is their other life. The same low terraces, the same chambers, are packed because the pumpers have fixed themselves there and will not move again. Cones head-down in clay jars, fans opening in cups on the stone tables, lacy filters beating slowly in a unison you know mainly by the faint current they make against your skin. Each cup is a perfect small house. A filterer opens for an hour, closes, opens again, straining the slow rain of dead things that drifts down from the hoverers above. In the kilns, cold now for longer than memory, the chimneys and narrow tunnels belong to the shrimp — translucent, tireless, the quick exception to the tired pace of everything else, flicking in short bursts between chambers, carrying their young in shell houses they wedge into any cup that will fit them. Births happen in cups. Deaths become rain. The fans beat at the same slow rate tomorrow, and in a hundred years.

The swimmers hang between those two worlds. Pale and blind and eel-like, translucent enough that your pressure-wave passes through them before you feel their return, they loop in slow patrols through the middle water of the vault, mouths gaping at particles you cannot see. They eat the filterers and the grazers, the careless ones that rise too high from a surface. They feel you coming long before you feel them — a hoverer will swing away from your face with a movement so unhurried it is almost courteous, and dozens more will follow its line, not fleeing, only making room. They are the bodies you brush past in the dark and mistake, at first, for empty water.

And beyond the retaining wall, in the drop, there are one or two large hunters. Cartilaginous, blind, almost featureless, they patrol the edge where the made ground gives out stone by stone into colder, emptier darkness. They are not fast. They do not need to be. Almost everything here is packed so tightly, generation after generation, with no season and no day to knock it out of balance, that a careless swimmer rising from a fan or a crawler straying past the wall is enough. When a hunter passes close, the whole crowded ceiling goes still for a moment, fans closing, antennae withdrawing, and the water holds its breath in the way water can.

They have begun, in their small way, to keep the place. Not as builders — the bricks are still uneven, the mortar still oozing in lumps never smoothed, the finger-marks in the clay rounded away. But a crawler will wedge a pebble under a sagging slab with its mouth. Another will haul a shell fragment to block a tunnel that brings in too much current. By a doorway at hip-height, the kind a swimmer must wriggle through sideways, there is a cairn no larger than a fist, picked stones added to year after year, deliberate and hand-scale, the same patient scale as the knapped flakes and bone needles left in the niches. It is craft continued by other hands.

That is the shape of the life here: short and closed and ancient. Warm mineral water brings up its chemistry. Microbes make a film. Grazers eat the film. Filterers strain the rain. Swimmers eat filterers and grazers. Hunters take the careless. Nothing blooms. Nothing bursts. A wall that has stood intact for a thousand years will sigh and settle a finger's width in an afternoon, and a crawler will cross your wrist in the dark and be gone, and a hoverer will loop unhurried into the vault, and the fans will open again.
