If you stay long enough for your eyes to give up on the dark beyond your light, you begin to look for life the way you would look anywhere else. You look for marks. For a trail in the dust, for a nest in a fissure, for something chewed or shed or left behind. For a change in the rock where a body has rested. For a sign that something has been here and has returned.

There are none of those signs here, and that is the first shape the inhabitants take. Not a crowded shape, not a green or busy one. A shape defined by what cannot happen. Nothing sinks, so nothing can be buried and nothing can be dug up by a root or a claw. Nothing rises, so nothing can be carried away on a current to feed somewhere else, or warned away by a smell that has drifted in. Sound dies within an arm’s length, so nothing can call across the plateau. There is no warmer layer to move to, no colder layer to hide in. A creature here would have to live inside its own halo — its own heat, its own exhalations, its own waste — because the water will not take any of it away from it. If it lives here at all, it must be able to bear being surrounded by itself forever.

The dust is the first thing that might be alive, because it is everywhere and it is in numbers. Look close to it in your light. The grains do not drift toward you or away. They do not settle on the rock below them or on the broad upturned surfaces of the causeways. They keep their spacing. A grain that is beside another grain now was beside it a minute ago and will be beside it in an hour. They glitter when your lamp moves across them, each one holding a point of light without brightening or fading.

If they are organisms, they are organisms that have stopped being organisms in any active sense. They do not feed as they hang, because the things they might eat hang beside them instead of falling into them. They do not move to feed, because to move would be to leave the exact place they were left, and there is no advantage in the new place — it is identical to the old. They do not divide, because a new grain beside an old one would just be another grain that does not fall. They do not die and break apart, because breaking apart would mean sinking or rising, and neither is allowed. They are a population the way a constellation is a population: many, sparse, motionless, and preserved by the same stillness that preserves a tool you let go of and a bubble you breathed out beside your mask. They are the snow that never accumulates, and if something lives in that snow, it lives by consenting to be snow.

The buildings are the second candidate, and they are warmer than the dust. At eight degrees, everything should be a uniform cold, and the water should be that cold from the rock floor to as high as you care to climb. The structures are not. Through a glove you can feel it, a steady mineral warmth that has nothing to do with a machine running hot. There is no current to carry the heat away, so it stays close to the surface it comes from, in a thin, invisible thickness that does not rise past your hand. It is not the heat of a fire. It does not flicker. It is the temperature of a body that is not hurrying.

Make a small scratch on one of them — not with a tool, so much as with a fingernail against an edge — and the warmth spreads faintly under the surface ahead of the line closing. The line closes without a sound. No rust gathers in it, no salt blooms, no pale crust is disturbed for more than that moment. It heals, but healing here does not mean growing. The ring at the centre does not grow larger. The inverted cones in the east do not grow taller. The blade-like shell does not uncurl. A dozen of them lie across the plateau, thirty or forty metres apart, each in a geometry that should be difficult to cast or weld or balance: a hexagonal prism passing through a sphere, towers meeting at angles that would shear under their own weight if weight were a thing that pulled here. They do not reach toward one another. They do not lean. The causeways arc between them with a deliberate plan, the amber seams running along every edge and across every junction, the small nodes at the crossings steady and dim, too dim to read by. That light does not scatter. It stays on the surface it marks, like a line drawn on skin.

If they are alive, they are alive in that minimal way: they hold their shape, they hold their temperature, they close a wound. Their interiors, glimpsed through dark slots, are cathedral-sized and empty. No altar, no nest, no bones, no stored food, no sound of breathing — and here you would hear breathing, because your own is too loud in your helmet and then there is a silence that presses on your ears. The emptiness inside is not the emptiness of a place that has been vacated. It is the emptiness of a shape that continues inward. They are not dwellings. They are the dwellers, and the dwellings at the same time, and neither activity — dwelling or being dwelt in — requires anyone else.

The pod is the only regular movement, and the only movement that returns. It crosses a lane at a speed that never alters. It does not slow for a turn, it does not hurry on a straight, it does not stop. Eleven minutes to complete its circuit, by the displacement of dust that does not close behind it. It leaves no wake. You cannot hear it coming because sound does not travel far enough to warn you, and it does not broadcast a light ahead of itself because the amber on the causeways does not scatter. Whether there is someone inside it, you never see a face or a hand. If there is, that person has learned the same lesson as everything else here: do not disturb the stillness, do not enter it. The pod does not dock, does not open, does not shed a diver in a flurry of bubbles that would hang in a cloud beside it. It passes and passes again, a solitary circuit around a city with no street life. It is not a resident. It is a keeper of the count, and the count is almost always the same.

Once in a very long while, there is an animal.

It comes from beyond the rim, where a dropped thing begins to sink again and a bubble finally rises and sound travels a little farther. Inside the shelf it is several metres long, translucent, blind, with broad fins that move like slow fabric. The movement is unhurried and it seems unneeded. There is no up to rise to and no down to sink to, no current to ride, no light to turn toward. It does not turn toward the amber seams. It does not open toward the warmth of the buildings. It does not feed — there is nothing here that can be encountered by feeding, because a particle that is not in its mouth will remain beside its mouth in the same relative position indefinitely, and the animal does not push through the dust to meet it; it passes straight from rim to rim with the dust parting and then holding empty where it has been. Behind it remains a broad, faint ribbon of disturbed grains, visible long after it has vanished into the ink beyond the plateau. It sheds nothing that hangs. It leaves no eggs. Its body is clean, its translucency undisturbed by any film or growth. Whatever life it has — feeding, breeding, growing, dying in the ordinary sense — it has elsewhere, in waters that still move. Here it is only a passage, an interruption that proves the stillness by leaving a line through it, like you leave a line through it.

You are the other interruption. Your light discovers the city piece by piece because nothing shines far enough to be seen all at once. Your breathing is loud. Your bubbles do not climb; they remain beside your mask in the shape you gave them until you move away from them. Your suit leaks a little heat into water that will not carry it away. Your passage remains behind you as a clean track through the dust, and if you left and came back after a long absence, the track would still be there. You are, as the place measures such things, the largest disturbance it has known in a long time, and you cannot stay. Not because there is no air or no room — the interiors are vast enough — but because you need to rise, and rising is not a thing the shelf permits a dropped object to do, and your body needs a gradient, a surface, a flow past you that will take your waste and your exhalations away from your face. If you stayed long enough and stopped moving, you would gather your own halo around you and become still in the way the dust is still. A tool let go here hangs where it was released, slowly turning as though in syrup. A body let go here would do the same. That is the final form of residency available here, and it is not living in any sense you brought with you.

So what lives on the Still Shelf does not live as a species lives. There is no breeding, no growth, no feeding, no migration up or down, no sound made to be heard at a distance. There is dust that holds its place, buildings that hold their warmth and close their scratches, a pod that holds its speed, an animal that holds its course and leaves, a visitor who holds his breath and leaves a track. The pale crest on the rock stays bright and undisturbed. The fissures stay narrow, as if the plateau were split a moment ago and forbidden to finish. Newness is not an event here; it is a condition, and everything here partakes in it by being preserved at the moment of its arrival.

Whatever lives here, lives by hanging exactly where it was left — and by never, for as long as it is here, needing to be anywhere else.
