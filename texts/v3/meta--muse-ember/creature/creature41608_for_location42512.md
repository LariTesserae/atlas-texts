The first living thing in the Third Basin is not something you can pick up and look at. It is under the water, and under the mud, and over the cables, in a layer you walk through without noticing.

On every rock that stays wet — which is every rock — there is a mat. White where the crushed limestone dust settles and the bacteria lay down their own pale stone, orange where the iron in the runoff rusts in their bodies and they excrete it back in threads. It is slimy to the touch, faintly warm, and it grows the way the Basin grows: a millimeter at a time, thickening over a season, slumping and re-seeding after a flood lifts it. It coats the intake pipes, it coats the lower rungs of the catwalks, it coats the cables until they sag deeper under their own green weight and the green cables that dangle from the half-sunk mill into the current are not cables anymore, to the things living on them, but vines. Everything that stands still long enough in the warm water becomes a substrate. Even the work-lamps, when they are off, bead with condensation and film over.

That film is the beginning of the food chain here, because there is almost no green to begin it. The pale, hair-like algae that float in the still pools under the amber lights are the only photosynthetic thing the water itself can manage, and they turn a faint, unconvincing green in that artificial dusk. The chemosynthetic bacteria do the real work — eating iron, eating sulfur, turning the industrial runoff and the guano and the flood-wash from above into a soft, living crust that can be eaten.

And it is eaten, constantly, by the things that live in the mud.

If you kneel — if your waders will take it — on the black coal-dust terrace and lift a stone, the underside is not bare. Amphipods, blind and white and no longer than a grain of rice, are packed in it in the hundreds, moving sideways in that frantic, unhurried way of things that have never seen light. They turn the mud over nightly, grazing the bacterial mat and the fallen insects and each other’s dead. Worms do the same work slower, leaving their casts in little stippled towers on the ledges. Snails work the weeping walls, rasping grazing paths through the slime where their shells leave a polished track. Mussels are everywhere the water is shallow and steady enough to let them stay: stippled over the submerged ledge at the north end where the river breaks, clustered on the rusted hull of a wagon in the shallows, fused in beds to the inside of a pipe where the fish shelter. They open and close in the current, filtering, and the water around a mussel bed is visibly clearer, a small quiet place in the murk.

The crayfish work alone, mostly at night if you can call night here, singly picking through the shallows with their claws, turning over stones the amphipods have just cleaned. They are ordinary crayfish, grey-brown and damp, and there are so many of them that the mud never quite settles.

All of that — mat, amphipod, worm, mussel — is the living floor. The water above it is crowded in a different way.

In the deeper channels, where the braided sheet is knee-deep and then deeper where it finds the old workings, the blind cave fish move. They have no eyes to speak of, just the pale swell of skin where eyes would be in a fish that lives in the sky-world, and they move in shoals so dense and so tightly packed that from the causeway the whole mass looks like a single silvery animal turning in the black water. They boil the surface when they turn. They break it singly, too, other fish, picking at the film where a midge has fallen or a cricket has lost its grip on a ledge. There is a constant, low ticking and plopping made by mouths at the surface, thousands of small breaks per minute, that you learn to hear as one sound.

In the shallows of the southern terrace, where the hot springs bubble up through the mud and steam faintly, the frogs are. Pale, almost translucent, with bellies the color of wet limestone, they call from the edges of the pools with a deep plunk that repeats and overlaps until it stops sounding like a call and starts sounding like dripping, but lower and wetter. Salamanders hang in the clearest threads of water, gilled, unblinking, drifting with the current and then darting — that one fast thing — to take a drifting amphipod. Their larvae are in the water in numbers you can see only when the lamp hits them, a cloud of commas over the pondweed.

The pondweed itself is a rumor, like everything green here. It traces the edges of the clear channel in a thin string, and a fern or two pushes out of a crack near the hot springs, bleached almost white by the absence of light, patient in the steam. It was there last year. It will be there, a frond taller, next year. Up where a maintenance shaft lets in a grey column of noon, moss hangs in damp curtains and does something like photosynthesis, and under the leak of warm vapor from a pipe joint a fringe of it has established itself, living not on light but on warmth and wetness. It is enough to prove the world could be green. It underscores how animal the rest is.

Because above the water, the animal density is what drowns you.

The ceiling, thirty to ninety feet up in places, is not bare rock. From a distance up close it resolves into texture: shelf upon shelf of bats, hanging in clusters like dark fruit, thousands upon thousands of them, shifting in their sleep, muttering, waking to chitter. When a pump starts below the causeway and the vibration travels up through the stone, the whole roost can burst at once — a screaming, leather-winged cloud that fills the Basin from wall to wall for ten seconds and then settles again, shelf by shelf, until the muttering resumes. They are ordinary bats, grey and brown, making ordinary cave-bat noises, made extraordinary only by being ten thousand in one warm room and by the rain of guano that falls, night after night, into the water below and feeds the mats and the mussels and the amphipods in a closed, warm loop.

Below the bats, on every ledge, the cave crickets live. They are pale and long-legged and they saw at each other incessantly, a high, metallic rasping that never quite stops, made by cricket nests tucked into cracks in the crushed spoil of the causeway and into the station houses where the condensate runs down the inside of the windows. You hear them before you see them, and you see them because they are everywhere — on the valve wheels, on the railings, on your hood if you stand still long enough on the causeway. In the warm air near the vents, where the temperature rises and the water steams, the midges hang so thick you walk through them and feel them settle on your lips and in your ears. Mites are in the air too, invisible until the light shaft at noon makes the mist visible, and then you can see the whole air is moving with them.

Birds come and ruin the pattern, briefly. A pair nests in the arm of a rusted crane, season after season, in the same cup of twigs and cable ties, and the fledglings go out over the water and learn the Basin the way the bats did, by repetition. They are the only lives here that have seen the sky that day.

The pumps have their own fauna. Bats in the pump housing, where it is warm and dry enough for them. Fish in the intake pipes, sheltering from the current. Biofilm on everything that moves slowly. The machines do not dominate; they are accommodated, colonized to saturation, rusting at the pace of the damp, which is to say completely and without hurry, while things live in and on and around them as if they had always been part of the terraces.

And then there are the people, who are not really inhabitants in the way the mussels are, and who are, in the way that matters.

They come down in the cage or along the slanting drift, in yellow waders, at the start of a shift. They are men and women who check gauges, who open a valve wheel taller than they are, who smoke on the causeway in the drip with their hoods up, who know the names for things that have no official name: the slippery ledge, the warm pool, the place where the fish boil at noon, the place where the dripping on the tin roof of the station house keeps you awake on a double shift. They have learned to talk by leaning in, by shouting an inch from another person’s ear, by hand signals that work over the river’s roar and the crickets and the bats. Their ears ring when they go up. Their waders fill at the seams because there is nowhere dry to sit.

One attendant keeps a shrine in a station house — battery lamp, tea things, a radio that rarely works — and cleans it, and that cleaning is a kind of inhabitance. He has been there long enough that the bats know his step and do not burst, long enough that the fern near the spring is something he can tell you has grown. Others come and go across years, but the work does not change its pace. A gauge is read. A pump thumps in its slow heartbeat. A mat thickens. A stalagmite field, uncut teeth a millimeter or two a century, grows imperceptibly toward a drip that has fallen from the same spot every few seconds since before anyone’s grandfather came down.

Nothing here hurries except the dart of a fish and the burst of a roost. Everything else — the bacteria thickening on a cable, the mussel gaining a shell-ring, the bat giving birth blind and deaf in the cluster and finding the teat by smell, the crayfish molting and hiding under a stone until its new shell hardens, the worker learning the causeway by feel in the dark between the amber pools of light — happens by accumulation.

It is crowded because it has had time to crowd, and it is warm and wet and loud because no one has ever turned it off. There is no room left between the presences on the walls, in the water, in the mud, in the air. The Basin is still being made, layer by wet layer, and the lives in it are making it with it, patiently, ordinarily, without end to the dripping.
