If you stay still, the crust begins to move.

Not by walking. Nothing here walks fast. It moves by opening. A mouth no wider than a pinprick in the side of a tube, and then another, and another, until the tube-wall itself seems to breathe. In the cold the pace is so slow you could swear nothing has happened, and then a plume unfolds — a small, translucent fan, cream-colored, a little ragged at the edge — and beats once, twice, and waits. Behind it, deeper in the lattice, something clicks. A shrimp, perhaps, snapping at nothing that you can see. The sound comes through the water and through the stone-that-is-not-stone and into your ribs.

That is the first inhabitant, and the last distinction you will be able to make. The smallest lives you will never see except as a movement: the skin of microbes that coats every fresh break, the amphipods no longer than rice grains that pick their way through tube mouths, living for a few weeks in a chamber built by something else, cleaning it of detritus by eating it. They are everywhere the crust is, even in the hundred empty meters between towers. You find them by the faint disturbance they leave in the marine snow where they have dug in. They do not build. They keep house.

The builders live at the very tip of their building.

A scaffold worm does not lay a tube and then move into it like a hermit crab. It lives at the lip. Its soft body stays inside the newest, palest segment, its feathered plume — not a plant, not a leaf, but a crown of gills — waving out to meet the current and catch what the current carries. Behind it, the tube hardens. Year by year the animal secretes more, gnaws and re-secretes, fusing its work to the work of its neighbors and its ancestors, until the individual is a small living point at the growing edge of a great, blind, mineral mass. It will die there, likely in a decade or two, of a slow failure, of being overgrown, of being eaten through the wall by a borer. Its tube will not die. It will be vacated.

That is the pattern for everything that lives here: the animal is brief, the room outlives it.

Sponges are the second builders, and the slower. Where a worm-tower leans strongest into flow, a sponge will settle over the downstream side and begin to grow upward and outward, not in a stalk but in a woven, vase-like thicket, its pores pulsing with a rhythm even slower than the worms'. Water is drawn in through a thousand small mouths and expelled through a few larger ones, and in passing it is stripped of snow and plankton, of the larvae of things that will never swim up to the surface. A sponge can live for centuries. It can be half-eaten by a sea spider and close over the wound in a season. It does not move. It does not chase. It makes the tower more porous, more sheltered, more itself. When it dies, its skeleton of silica spicules remains, a lace inside the carbonate lace, and something else will grow through it.

Between and among these two, the polyps have their stations. They look like anemones lifted from a warm sea and bleached by absence of light — no color, or only the faintest cold blue when startled, translucent enough that you can see the gut moving inside. Each is a single mouth on a stalk, waving a tiny plume to filter. They come and go across the surface of a dome in generations, colonizing a fresh fracture after a collapse, then being overgrown, then wiped away and returning again. To watch one is to understand that there is no vegetation here, and never was. Everything that waves is trying to eat the falling snow.

And because the builders make rooms, the tenants arrive — not by building, but by inheritance.

No fish here digs a nest. A young eelpout, pale and soft and eyeless in the strict sense, hovers in the lee of a pillar until the current changes, then slips head-first into an empty tube mouth that is exactly the width of its body. It stays. It will stay for most of its life, its tail undulating just enough to keep water moving past its gills, darting out to snatch an amphipod or a shrimp that strays too close, then retreating. When it dies inside — and they do die inside, curled — another eelpout's juvenile will find the same tube a year later and take it in turn. Shoals you see scattering like ash are not shoals in the open-water sense. They are families of tenants, launched from adjacent chambers by your passing, hovering for minutes in a tight, trembling cloud above the dome, and then filing back into their holes one by one. They are never far from a hole. To be far from a hole is to be prey.

The same is true for the shrimp that lives deep in the lattice and clicks in the darkness, for the small blind crabs that do not so much dig as gnaw. A crab will walk the pavement on legs as long as its body, feeling ahead with antennae that have no use for light. It will find an old tube mouth choked with hardened mucus and sponge spicule and work at it patiently, hour after hour, widening it. It does not know it is a builder, but it is. For a few years that widened chamber will be its apartment. When it dies, a snail will graze the algae-less film from its walls. When the snail dies, the chamber will be empty and ready again. Nothing is sound here. Everything is re-used.

On the surface of the plain, between towers, there are walkers who belong to the emptiness itself.

The blind crab — squat, white, almost translucent at the joints — moves at a pace set by water barely above freezing, six legs stepping, two claws held out like feelers. It patrols a territory it will never leave, perhaps ten meters of dome and rubble, picking at marine snow that has settled, at the edge of a sponge, at a dead worm's plume. It has no eyes to speak of. It knows the shape of its ground by touch, and it knows it because its ground is the accumulation of its ancestors' work. The sea spiders are slower still, almost absurdly slow, their bodies no larger than a coin and their legs ten times that, picking their way over the tubes. They are predators, in a way — they will settle on a sponge and slowly suck its tissue through a straw mouth — but even their hunger is unhurried. A brittle star will sit with its arms raised, waving them in the current like a net for snow, and when a particle touches, draw it in. A basket star will unfurl at night — if night can be said to exist here — and become a moving thicket.

None of them thrive in the gaps. The gaps are where they are exposed. A crab crossing fifty meters of bare pavement is a long, blind walk with no cover, and every animal that crosses knows it by the pause before it starts. Most never do. The towers are islands of safety, and the pavement between them is not a road. It is a connective tissue for larvae and for snow, not for adults. Abundance is local because safety is local. You do not meet here; you meet on a single tower, in thousands, shoulder to shoulder in the lattice, and then nothing for an hour's swim.

Above the towers, in the water that is only slightly darker than the water they displace, hang the fish that do not live *in* the crust but *over* it.

Eelpouts in numbers, cusk with their long chin barbels trailing, a pale grenadier that has come up from the abyss behind you to hover, motionless, over the upstream edge where the plumes are thickest. They are not fast. In this cold, no muscle can afford to be fast for long. A predator's lunge is a short, expensive thing — a sudden bend of the body, a mouth opening around a tube mouth — and even then it rarely succeeds, because prey has a hard wall behind it and the predator has a body too large to follow. The whole system is arranged by that simple geometry: soft, small, and many inside; large, soft, and few outside. The fish hover in the current like kites when the internal wave comes, all their fins and all the plumes bending the same way, and when it passes they settle back onto their pillars with a faint, shared sigh of settling dust.

The deepest inhabitant is the one you are least likely to see, because it does almost nothing.

Behind a low dome on the downstream side, in a chamber polished by generations of use, an octopus will take up residence. Pale, almost gelatinous, its skin able to shift from smooth to papillate in a way that makes it disappear against the spongy crust. It will brood. It will lay a single clutch of eggs in the tube, guard them for years — four, maybe more, in a life stretched by cold — and never eat again. It will not leave. Its arms will curl and uncurl around the eggs, cleaning them, keeping water moving. The rest of the plain will go on filtering and collapsing and regrowing around it, and it will die there, still holding its chamber, and its body will become the food for the amphipods that take its tube. In a place where individuals are brief beside the crust, this one has chosen to be brief in the other direction: to spend its whole life in one room, making a single, enormous investment against the snow.

And then there are the ones who come from elsewhere, because the summit is not sealed.

From the slope behind you, where the ground drops a kilometer into darker water, a sleeper shark may rise, slow as a landslide, to mouth at a tower and leave. From above, days or weeks apart, the rain of larvae and snow from the surface arrives, and some of it becomes plumes, some of it becomes crabs, most of it is simply eaten and turned into more crust. From the plain itself, a collapsed tower brings its own catastrophe: a silent slide, a cloud of pale dust, a sudden exposure of fresh interior. Within weeks, pale new tubes sprout on the break, eager and unprotected. Within years, the debris becomes a new foundation. The animals that lived on the old tower are mostly dead or scattered, but their tubes are now rubble, and rubble is habitat. The builders eat the abandoned and build with the eaten. Nothing here affords waste.

This is the life, then, if you can call it individual at all. A worm at the tip of its tube, a sponge pumping centuries away, a fish in a borrowed room, a crab gnawing a room larger. A predator that cannot enter. A mother who never leaves. A whole population turning over inside a pavement that does not turn over at all, except to be worn, bored, and smoothed. They are all pale because color is a language for light, and there is no light to speak in. They are all slow because the water is cold and food arrives as a slow rain. They are all translucent, blind, or dim-eyed because the world is made of touch and current.

They do not live *on* the place, the way a bird lives in a tree. They live *as* the place, secreting it, gnawing it, sheltering in it, dying in it, and leaving the shell of themselves for the next. Look ahead. Another dome rises out of the dusk, rounds off, disappears. It is already inhabited, has been inhabited for thousands of years, and will be inhabited long after the particular mouths you see opening and closing now have closed for the last time.
