If you wanted to count what lives in Solvara, you would not begin with people. You would begin with layers, and give up on a true count before you got very high.

Everything here lives on something else that is itself alive, or was made to look alive. A family lives on the roof of a workshop. The workshop lives on the roof of another workshop. Under the second workshop is a damp wall cavity, and in that cavity are silverfish, and cockroaches, and a slow green tide of mold that has been resident there for longer than the family has. On the roof of the family’s house is a planter, two feet of engineered soil, and in that planter a fig. The fig’s roots are in the tank beside it. The tank’s stand is bolted to the roof. The roof is somebody’s ceiling, and through that ceiling, at night, you can hear the somebody.

Nothing is separate. A pigeon asleep in a warm exhaust duct is not visiting the building. It is part of the building’s insulation, part of its sound at dusk. A spider who strings a web across the corner of a press every time the vibration tears it down is not a pest being tolerated. The press tenders leave her that corner on purpose, the way you leave a step for a cat. They know how long she has been there by the size of her web.

The people are the largest part of it, and the most adapted, though not in the way you would expect from a place so bright and high. They are not lean and chiseled by wind. They are damp. Their hems are damp, their collars are damp, the backs of their necks are permanently sunburned in a neat line where the shade of a hat ends. In open sun they sweat fast and get out of breath on the switchback stairs, and it is not a performance; the air is thin enough that even the born-and-raised pace themselves, children excepted, leaning into the turn of a landing to breathe where a wall throws a little shade and the moss smells sweet. Old people sit in that shade for hours, not resting exactly, but attending: to the hum that comes up through their shoes, to a voice dropping down from the balcony above into their conversation, to the fruit that has fallen from the terrace above their head and needs picking up before a cat or a lizard gets to it.

They live this way in strata. In the lower bowl the foundry quarter is almost entirely human-used heat. The families who work the rolling mills and extrusion presses and looms live directly above the machines, their floors warm through the night, their cooking done with waste heat tapped off a pipe. A press comes down every forty seconds and they time their sentences to it without thinking, pausing in the groan, finishing in the clang, which they feel in the soles of their feet and in their teeth. Their ears have gone a little deaf in a particular way; they lip-read each other across a lane. Their skin is greyed by dust that never quite washes out because the water is too precious to wash freely and the damp in the shade keeps the dust from rising. When they come up to the middle terraces to buy greens, they bring the roar with them in their clothes for a little while.

The middle is greener and wetter and holds most of the living in number, if you count stems and leaves and small bodies. Here the ground is the roof of the housing quarter, and the housing quarter is packed so tightly that a pot of tomatoes on a ledge is a neighbor with a history. People know which vine was planted by a grandfather and which by an aunt who moved up two terraces, and they can tell by thickness, by the way it leans toward reflected light off the opposite tile wall, by how it ripples when the wind moves through the confined lane. There is a wall in the market that does this all over, a whole surface green and undulating, and everyone shops beneath it because it makes a cool, moving shade.

Everybody tends. That is the main occupation, under the other occupations. Maintenance crews are always cutting back, always pruning, always clearing a drain choked with leaves, always repainting a corrugated wall and then replanting the moss in the same vertical lines where the water has always run, because moss is not an invasion here. It is a sign that the wall is still sound, that the drip behind it is still the correct drip. If a patch of moss disappears, someone will come to look for a leak that has been sealed too well. The algae curtain on the north side of a water tank is scraped off by the tank keeper and put on the bean planter below, dark and rich, and the tank is left to green again. The green-black film where a channel runs constantly in shade is not cleaned away entirely; dragonflies live there, thin as needles, and tadpoles turn in the shallow, and children are forbidden to dip their hands in but do anyway and are scolded without much force.

Water, in Solvara, is a resident too, and it keeps its own schedules. In the dampest interior corridor, condensation falls from the ceiling every morning in a gentle, regular rain. It has a name, and a time, and people hang a shirt there to catch it or send a child to stand under it before school so his hair is wet for the heat of the day. In kitchens, in market lanes, in the cloistered air under a west-facing balcony dense with leaves, water hangs. Planters saucers hold it. Fish tanks hold it. A tin cup under a valve behind a wall has been catching a drip for years and will for years more, and whoever lives above it empties it on a weekly round, and has long since stopped expecting it to stop. The fish in the rooftop tanks — small, silver, turning slowly in green water — are not exactly pets and not exactly food, though they are both. Their owners feed them fallen crumbs and the occasional insect, and skim the surface, and when the tank is too hot they move a banana leaf over half of it for shade. The fish know their keeper by shadow.

Birds belong to no terrace and therefore to all of them. Swifts nest under every eave and in the warm ducts, rising at dusk in a screaming cloud that goes up and up past the switchbacks and past the cable cars, then drops back down to sleep in the heat of the machines. Their droppings feed the planters below, and people collect it in buckets without disgust, the way they collect everything that can be reused. Pigeons are more domestic. A flock rides the cable cars up and down the central spine, getting off at the crown station where the air is warmest, getting on again when the sun moves. The conductors leave the door open a moment longer for them. People make room on a bench. No one shooes them; they are considered regulars, and a pigeon with a crooked foot has its own small reputation. Mynahs and sparrows work the orchards, getting into the figs and the citrus before people can, and people shake a bamboo stick at them in the morning and leave the highest fruit for them in the afternoon, because a tree that is picked clean by birds is a tree that will be left alone by crews wanting to cut it back.

On the ground — which is never ground, only roofs and ledges and sun-hot concrete — are the smaller, sun-loving lives. Lizards bask in the fierce white glare on a landing, motionless, then slip sideways into a crack where water drips and disappears. Over generations they have taken on the colors of the repairs: a lizard on a wall patched in a slightly different shade of tile will be that shade, if you look again. Children catch them gently and let them go on the same warm spot, because they know which spot is whose. Cats patrol in the evening, when the concrete finally gives up its heat and the roar from below softens a millimeter. No dogs; dogs do poorly on stairs and roofs and in this much shared air, and cats do well. A cat’s territory might span five roofs connected by a plank, three balconies, and the top of a water tank. They drink from saucers, they sleep in the mouth of a duct, they accept a scrap of fish from a tank keeper, and at night you hear their bells — no, the bells are the goats’. The cats are silent, and that is why you notice them.

The goats are at the top, on the highest garden terraces, where the air is brightest and thinnest and the plantings are thinnest because the soil is shallowest. They are kept for milk and for trimming the edges no crew can reach, and for the sound. Their bells clank over the press roar, a higher, slower music that marks the hour the way the press marks the minute. They know the sun spots, they know which gate leads to the fig tree landmark — the one that grew up through the scaffolding of a tank, roots in the tank itself, crown now shading three floors of housing — and they will stand there in the shade all afternoon, chewing, while below them a whole stairwell eats the fig’s fruit straight off the lower branches. Goats are stubborn and entirely accustomed. They have never been down to the saddle, let alone below the cloud sea. Their kids are born on a roof, lick the concrete warm from the sun, and learn to climb.

In between and underneath are the lives you mostly hear or glimpse. Cockroaches in the warm wall cavities, coming out when the presses idle for a maintenance cycle. Silverfish in the damp interior, eating at the edges of paper kept too low; everyone has learned to keep books and letters in the dry upper cabinets, and to accept a little loss at the bottom. Ants in the cracks between paving stones, moving in a line that never seems to end, carrying leaf fragments bigger than themselves across a roof that is somebody’s kitchen ceiling. Spiders across machinery, as said. Snails on a handrail after a rain passage has burst against the upper tanks, leaving them in a slow procession up the polished metal where hands have held it smooth for decades. Bees in a duct, humming over the orchards, stinging only when a child steps barefoot on a warm ledge where they have clustered, which is rare because children here learn to wear the soft-soled shoes that make no sound on stone that never quite dries.

The plants are the longest-lived residents and, in a sense, the most remembered. A banana on a roof, soil a foot deep, roots wandering sideways through a duct, will produce for years and be cut back and produce again. A citrus leans out over a lane so its fruit can be reached from the balcony opposite without anyone having to go downstairs. Ferns and orchids run in gutters, and when they choke a drain the crew comes, and the orchids are lifted out, divided, and replanted in the next gutter down, so that a family of orchids has migrated down a whole terrace over fifty years. Bamboo leans over market stalls and makes a shade that moves. Between stones moss presses up, bright and small, and weeds push where damp collects, and everyone leaves them because they mean the joint is holding water the way it should. People measure time in maintenance cycles, in repaints, in the slow creep of a rust stain that looks like a river on a map, in the gradual thickening of a hedge on a retaining wall. A vine covering a wall halfway will be remembered as halfway next year too. A fig tree is a landmark for a generation.

People are born in Solvara into this layered damp and heat and they learn it as normal. A baby sleeps in a hammock under vines, in air that is warm and close and smelling faintly of algae and hot oil and wet leaves. A child’s first outdoor space is not a yard but a roof, not soil but tile, and she learns the sky by looking up between laundry lines, and learns the cloud sea by being told, on the rare clear day at the crown, to look down. She learns stairs the way others learn streets. She learns that a door will stick in its frame in the damp season and that a floor has sagged a fraction and been packed level again and will sag again, and that you lift a little as you push. She learns to time her voice to the press. She learns whose fruit falls where, and that you pick it up promptly, that you do not drink straight from a tank but from the filtered jar beside it, that you leave some figs for birds and some shade for goats.

They do not bury their dead in earth, because there is no earth to touch. What is left is returned, in the practical way everything here is returned: ashes worked into the thin soil of a planter, a favorite tree planted over the spot where a tank keeper sat for thirty years, a name given to a corridor rain because a grandmother stood under it every morning and it is how you know she was there. It is not a dramatic custom. It is the same custom as the moss, the same equilibrium of weathering: paint peeling to older paint, concrete stained where water has always run, copper gone green, steel gone the color of tea, tile crazed and patched in a slightly different shade until a whole wall is a mosaic of repairs and the lizards match it.

At the crown there are the fewest lives, because there is the least shade and the most sun. Tank keepers, goat keepers, the pigeons who ride up for the warmth, a final planted terrace, a railing — and then air, thin and bright, and the drop to the clouds. You can stand there with your back to the dense green and steel behind you, hear the muffled roar of the presses far underneath, feel the sun on your head and the damp coolness of a water tank against your shoulder, and hear a goat bell somewhere behind you, and understand what holds it all: nothing here touches the natural earth, nothing here is left to dry out or wash away, nothing here is wild and nothing here changes quickly. Water is caught and led and caught again. Plants root in what is made for them and spill over anyway. Animals nest in what is warm and mechanical. People live above people, and below them, and beside them, in air that is always, somewhere, a little damp and a little warm, and they go on tending what they did not plant so that it will be there when their grandchildren need the shade.
