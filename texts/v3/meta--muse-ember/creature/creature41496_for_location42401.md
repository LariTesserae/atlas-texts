At first there is only the warmth, and the damp that warmth makes.

It is not one body. It is all of them together — cold-blooded bodies lying on cold stone, warm-blooded bodies curled in the mat, leaf litter decaying, moss respiring — giving off a thin vapor that is always rising a finger's width and then giving up. It condenses on the underside of the nearest leaf. It gathers there until it is too heavy to hold, and falls. That is rain here, and a birth, and a death, all at the same slow interval.

If you watch the film on a leaf long enough, the film watches back.

It is full. Rotifers turn in the water skin like tiny, transparent wheels. Nematodes thread and unthread. Springtails jump in arcs so small you feel the landing more than you see the leap, two hundred at a time under a boot print, gone. Mites work the leaf mold with a patience that has no younger or older. They are blind or nearly so, and the green-black light is enough for them because they do not navigate by light. One side of the mat is wetter than the other. One patch of peat is saltier with iron. One leaf is warmer than the stone beneath it. That difference, read by skin and by mouthpart and by the wet tip of an antenna, is a map complete enough to live by.

There is no empty surface for them to stand on. Under every step is moss, and under the moss, older moss, and under that the black peat that sighs when it is pressed and closes again. The moss itself is alive in spirals, small rosettes turning always to the left, layer upon layer, a foot thick in places and never thicker. It will not unroll further. It has reached the size the drip allows it, and it stays there, photosynthesizing in the memory of light, exuding water into itself.

The plants that make the roof live differently only in scale. Alder and birch and tree ferns lean in from the rim, ghost-trunks spongy, their branches plaited by vines until there is no sky left to compete for. Every twig is a rope of epiphytes. Every leaf underside is a nursery — liverworts flattened like green coins, ferns unrolling over them, other ferns unrolling over those. Roots do not go down. They travel sideways through the mat of other roots, or they hang free in the air, pale hairs waving, drinking condensation directly. A plant grows not toward the sun but toward the next drip, toward the exhalation of its neighbor, toward the warm leak of a frog's belly. When a leaf rots — and leaves rot constantly, one by one, with no season — it is eaten while it is still falling, by mouths already waiting underneath it. Another leaf takes its place at the same season, at the same spot, to exactly the same size, because there is nowhere else for it to be and no room for it to be larger.

Among the roots, in the moss, the ground moves without haste.

Millipedes, white as paper, blind, in their hundreds, thread the peat with a soft, packed shuffle. They do not rush because there is nowhere to rush to and no predator that rushes after them. They eat the rot that is already being eaten. They are eaten in turn at rates exactly sufficient that their hundreds are always hundreds.

Slugs the size of a hand cross a drip and shine. Their skins are translucent; you can see the dark coiling of gut, the paler pulse of organs, the algae moving through them like weather. They leave a silver trail that fades before they have finished making it. Snails cluster on a fallen log so thickly that the log is only borrowed — shells like wet pebbles stacked to the shape of wood. Beetles, armored like black seeds, move too many to follow any one, turning over in the litter, clicking faintly, shell against shell. In the crooks of branches, in holes in wet wood, in the thatch of hanging moss that never dries, the same kinds repeat at a higher level, each with a territory no wider than its own body and a share exactly its own.

The frogs and salamanders are part of the moss in a way the others are not yet. They lie still for such long times that algae colonize their backs, so that to find one is to uproot a tuft of moss and have it blink. Some are pale and uneyed, or eyed only under a skin. They breathe through that skin, drink through it, mate in the film of water on a leaf. Their bellies are green with what they have eaten, so their backs and their bellies are both the color of the thing they lie on. Their movement is an occasional swell, a redrawing of the mat around a warm body.

And in the center, in the one deep pool that does not flow but weeps, the mussels open and close in the cold. They do not filter a stream. They filter a stillness. Generation after generation of them has been the stillness.

That is where the largest inhabitants keep their place. They are mole-like and blind and the size of a hare, with forepaws almost like hands, dense fur that beads like your hair beads. They do not build. They press themselves into the mat and the mat presses back and fills their prints behind them. They come to the same anvil stone in the pool's edge — basalt, pitted and polished smooth by hammering — and crack the same mussels on it, with a hammer-stone they keep there. The sound is soft, and regular, and almost never. Stone on shell. Stone on shell. They eat what they crack, and they are eaten slowly, by mites and beetles and fungi and the mat itself when they die, at a rate that leaves the next generation of them already shuffling in the moss with its eyes covered, learning the wetness gradient by touch.

They are not the only hands that have been here. Half-sunk in the mat are other worked stones — a flake chipped to an edge, an adze no bigger than a thumb — left by something that used its hands and then stopped coming. Moss has grown through their hollows. Mites live in their pits. They remain because they are useful and because nothing here removes what is useful. Nothing has been improved upon. No path leads to them, no clearing opens around them. They are simply objects the room allows itself, now inhabited like everything else.

Fungi bloom in the dark in patches no larger than a thumbnail, white and luminous in the way wet things are luminous. They are not flowers. They are the speed of decay made visible. Where a dead tree stands — bark intact, spongy, indistinguishable from the living trees except that it does not weep from its wounds — fungi thread it inside and out until living and dead are the same consistency. A body that dies here does not fall and lie there to be noticed. It is converted at its edges while its center is still warm.

This is why nothing grows. Not because nothing lives, but because living and dying are the same event seen from two sides. A leaf is born the size it will keep. A millipede is born into a population that is already at its number. A salamander is born with algae already waiting to paint it. The drip that might have always been the same drip falls at the same interval on the same stone. The water rises as vapor off the warmest things, condenses, drips, soaks, and rises again.

If you could stay, you would learn them not by sight — your eyes would adjust and adjust and never quite finish — but by the small differences they present to touch. A leaf is wetter than a stone. A stone is colder than a body. A body is warmer than anything else. Each presence, plant or animal or fungus, holds its fixed place, eats and is eaten in a circle so tight it has no beginning, and breathes the same water back out into the air of the room until the air has no reason to move at all.
