If you walk for a day, you will likely see nothing move.

That is not because nothing lives here. It is because nothing here needs to come out, or go anywhere, or become something else in order to be alive. The first mistake is to look *between* the presences for inhabitants, in the green distance, as if life were a visitor to the architecture. Here the architecture is the life, and the ground is a life, and the water going through both of them is a life, and only very late, if you are still long enough, will you separate the smallest of them from the house that holds it.

Start underfoot.

The mat is not ground cover. It is a body laid flat and stretched to the horizon. Root-cord, stem, and flattened leaf are woven so tight that it feels like decking, spongy and bright, warm and saturated and faintly sweet, like a greenhouse floor held forever at the moment after watering. Push it aside and there is no earth, only more weave, and water, and more weave springing back.

Its living is in that springing back. Your footprints fill themselves as you lift your feet, not because it is soft like a marsh, but because it is full and elastic and wants to be flat again. That want is its whole motion. It weeps from every leaf and stem, beads and re-beads, takes the drip up again into itself, condenses it again out of the air that sits on it. It transpires exactly what it absorbs. It is warm through your soles while your face is cool in the thin wind, and both are constant. Individual leaves do not age. The edge of a new leaf is eternally new, slightly translucent, and on the mat there is nowhere a new leaf ends and an old one begins. There is only the weave, emerald and green-grey, with nowhere for anything to fall or rot or be buried. It lives by staying wet, by staying warm, by staying exactly as full as it is.

What rises out of it is not built on it. It is the mat folded upward and held in one posture.

The tower a few hundred meters out is a life in that sense. Twenty meters high, hollow, its walls braided in spirals from living stems, its dome of overlapping leaves opened just far enough to let light in and no farther, its seams showing where one whorl grew into the next like basketry. The doorway is an arch in the braid, clean-edged but not cut. No cut ends anywhere. No skin of bark. A smooth, wet, pale surface where new growth is permanently new.

It lives by a circulation you can hear before you can see clearly. Water enters at the base, travels up inside the living wall, and weeps out at the top. On the way up it sings — low sighs in the hollow, high pings as drops fall through the braided wall, a constant counterpoint of plinks that never rises or falls. The tower is a resonator because it is hollow; its own leak becomes its voice. It has no mouth to open. The doorway does not lead anywhere in particular. Its interior is empty vertical space under a dome that never quite finishes closing. Its life is to hold the spiral, to hold the dome half-open, to pass water upward and let it out in sound. That is all its biography, repeated without variation for as long as it can be noticed.

Farther out, the arch is another life, or possibly the same life divided against itself. Two great braided ribs rise, curve toward each other over a narrow channel, and stop a hand's width apart. The gap has always been that width. The tips are frayed into soft rootlets that curl, reaching, never arriving. If the tower lives by rising and sounding, the arch lives by reaching. Nothing closes it, because closing would be a completion, and completion does not happen here. Its energy, if energy is the word, is spent in the almost-touching: rootlets curled in damp air, feeling the other side across a distance that will never be crossed.

The bridges live by spanning, though no one crosses them. Long, bowed, hollow underneath, with a walkway of flattened living leaves that do not wilt under a foot that will never come. The baskets live by holding without holding: immense open cups with latticed walls, always brimming, never overflowing and never emptying, because the wall leaks exactly as fast as the mist feeds it. The stair-tower lives by leading up: each step a broad stiff leaf in a perfect spiral to a platform whose railing is a row of half-unfurled buds.

Those buds are the closest thing here to youth, and they are embryonic forever. They have been half-unfurled for as long as anything here has been able to notice. They do not grow up. They do not fall. They hold the railing and look, presumably, toward the cloud bank in the east, which is the same shape it was an hour ago, under a light that does not move appreciably and is never quite noon. There is no birth to follow them and no death to clear them away. The population of lives on the plateau is fixed at the moment of emergence, sparse as a constellation, each vast individual separated by hundreds of meters of green emptiness and visible from the next because nothing obstructs the view.

Between them run the drip-lines, thin living cables sagging low across the mat, carrying beads of water along inside them, out of sight. One ticks the same rhythm, twenty times a minute, loud enough to turn your head, then forgotten because it has not changed in the next minute either. They may be veins, or root-cords, or umbilicals linking the constellation. They carry only water, in a closed warm loop — condenses, drips, is taken up again, condenses again. They do not carry messages. The towers do not answer each other. Their songs overlap in the air and never harmonize, because none of them ever varies a note.

And then, inside the houses, there is the other kind.

By a basket-plant, if you are very still, very long, you might notice it: a pale, translucent thing suspended inside the hollow, hanging from a single thread of stem. It does not move toward you. It does not move at all, except for the slow, barely perceptible pulse that is its breathing, which is also always the same. It filter-feeds on the condensation as it forms, letting the mist come to it, beading on its surface and popping off, letting the brimming cup below feed its small weather system. Whether it is a separate inhabitant or an organ of the basket — its tongue, its heart, its lung grown inward — is hard to say, and the place does not help you separate them. Everywhere here, plants do the work of ground, house, bridge, reservoir, roof, road, and instrument. Perhaps this one does the work of a tenant as well.

It lives in the interior of a plant that lives on a mat that lives in water. Its world is small: the curve of the cup, the thread above it, the drip falling through the wall, the sweet leaf-air. It has never crawled, never swum, never left. In an ecology that is total and almost empty at once, motion has become optional, and almost nothing has chosen it. It is not hiding from you. Hiding would be a change of position.

The drops themselves have the briefest lives of all, and the only lives that cycle. A leaf weeps, a bead forms, pops off, falls, strikes the shallow sheet and rings a hollow stem, is taken up by the mat, condenses again on a stem that sweats. That is a whole life: beading, falling, vanishing into green. But even that does not progress. The pattern is the same at the first minute and at what would have been the second.

There is no soil fauna beneath them. No burrowing, no grazing, no warm bodies crossing the mat in the daylight. No seasons arrive to interrupt the pulse. No night relieves the light. The mat stays warm, the sound stays loud, the buds stay half-open, the arch stays a hand's width from closing, the tower keeps singing its leak in low sighs and high pings. Everything transpires what it absorbs, everything leaks what it receives. That perfect equilibrium is their knowledge, if they have any. They do not need to hunt, or flee, or finish. They do not decay and do not leave bones. A frayed tip is not damage; it is growth held at the instant before completion.

So whoever lives there lives as postures held forever: the mat springing back to flat, the tower rising and resonating, the arch reaching, the basket brimming and leaking in balance, the bridge spanning an emptiness no one traverses, the stair leading to a platform where the young never unfurl, the pale thing breathing in the hollow, the drip ticking twenty times a minute in a cable no one reads.

You do not interrupt them. Your footprints fill. The tower's pitch does not change for your arrival. The suspended thing does not turn toward the sound of you. They have no way to register an event, because an event would be a change, and change is absent. They are new, in the raw, translucent-edged sense, and they will always be new — lives that began once, sparsely, by growth alone, with no tools and no cuts and no burnings, and that have been in their first minute ever since.
