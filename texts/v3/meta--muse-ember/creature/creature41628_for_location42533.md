The first to notice you are the ones who never move toward you.

They are low, broad-footed, half-submerged in the sheet-water that stands a finger deep over the tiles. Their feet are spread flat against the clay as if the ground were a skin they were meant to keep pressed down. They do not croak. They blink, slowly, and their blinking is not their own — when one blinks another, farther off, opens, and another, and another, in inverse intervals, as if a single eyelid were passing from body to body across the shallows and never arrives anywhere twice. Beside them the fish turn in the clear water in small, exact circles. They vanish under a leaf, still turning while hidden, and reappear at the start of the circle, complete. They make their own current because the water has none.

The turtles stand on the dry tiles with their necks out, perfectly still except for the faint rise and fall that you cannot see, and do not try to cross the plain. The white birds stand knee-deep at the edges of the pans, one wing half-open, in the middle of a lift that has been half-beaten for as long as you can look. Herons and fowl stand with them, crowded wherever there is water and green, eyes open and unmoving, covered in the same bright glare as everything else. The lizards lie flat on the hot walls, flattened into the brickwork, indistinguishable from the sun-warmed clay except for the eye. The dragonflies hang at fixed points in the air, like punctuation, not resting but held there, at the end of a line of channel or over a blossom. The darting things under the water go through their darts and return to the place they darted from.

The insects are a multitude on the blossoms — beetles and flies and bees in numbers too great to count — and they cover the flowers so completely that the flowers seem to breathe under them, a slow swelling and shrinking that is not wind. Every tree is in flower and in fruit at once, and the insects work the flower while the fruit hangs ripe beside it, and nothing is taken away. Nothing falls. Nothing rots. The blossom does not close. The fruit is picked and is there again, or the picking hand is always halfway to it.

The green dwellers are residents in the same way. They did not come here. They were placed, in sunken urns, in raised beds inside reed fences, on trellises of lashed poles, with a channel aimed precisely at each root. The trees are trained over frames until their canopies meet in high, dense umbrellas, and vines are grafted and woven into walls and roofs so that the thatch and the leaf are one tissue. The grasses rise in ordered clumps inside the basins in huge glossy blades. They are fully unfolded and fully green and fully ripe, and they do not stir, because there is no wind. They weep with the rest of the place: beads form continuously on the undersides of the leaves, grow large, and shrink back into the surface without ever falling. They are not growing. They are being kept at their fullest, tended by primitive means into a second nature that has locked itself.

Between the green, when you walk far enough over bare tile and empty basins and walled enclosures that contain nothing but wet clay, you come upon the others.

They are few, and they are spaced the way the houses are spaced. A lone hut of mudbrick and thatch, a granary on short stout legs, a cluster of three or four houses around a water-pan, a fragment of wall enclosing a field of reeds. At the cluster you will find them already there. One crouched at the edge of the pan, hand in the water, smoothing and re-smoothing the same clay tile. One standing on a straight channel that goes to the horizon and ends, holding a basket, in the middle of a stride that is always the same stride, carrying nothing that fills or empties the basket. One stacking mudbrick, one uneven brick at a time, into a wall that is already uncracked and complete, placing the same seven bricks in the same arrangement that you saw a few strides back, and will see again. Their clothing is mats of woven reed soaked dark, hide lashings at the wrist and ankle. Their skin is beaded with condensation that forms and swells and is reabsorbed and never drips. They smell of hot wet clay and green leaf, because they have been pressing their hands into both, always.

They do not speak. There is no birdsong and no speech here, only the occasional tick of clay contracting, a soft suck as a foot leaves the wet tile, a muffled thunk from a basin wall, each arriving once from a direction slightly wrong, and then the damp pressure-quiet returns. Their gestures are like the animals' gestures: one gesture, held. The croucher crouches. The carrier carries his basket along the channel and is, a little farther on, carrying it again. The stacker stacks. They watch in the same way the frogs watch — abundantly, silently, without turning to follow you as you pass, because turning would be a new gesture. Footprints in the wet clay stay dark and sharp behind them, and they step into the same prints as they step out of them.

They live in the huts that are grown as much as built. The vines are grafted into the roofs. The thatch does not decay. Inside, the granary holds grain that is always full; beetles live in it in such numbers they are part of the grain's texture, and a keeper stands with his legs visible below the granary floor, motionless in the shade that is not cooler than the sun, only greener. The low basins between houses are deep enough to stand in, knee-deep, like the birds. No one bathes. No one drinks in a way that lowers the sheets; a hand scoops, a mouth drinks, the water wells up cool where a stick was pushed into the clay, and the sheet remains a finger deep and perfectly reflective.

There are young. Small figures crouch where the large ones crouch, small frogs beside large frogs, chicks held under the white birds' half-open wings forever. They do not grow into the larger form, and the larger do not grow old. There is no old here, no bones, no mound that is not a stacked earth mound inside reed revetments. No one migrates. No one dies in place. They eat the fruit that is always ripe, or they do not eat in the ordinary sense, but take in light and wet heat as the plants do and sustain themselves at the same teeming equilibrium, taking and receiving the same breath over and over. They were put here, as the plants were put here, in shallow pits and lined ponds, with stone and bone tools, piece by piece, carried into place. It looks as if they arrived yesterday, already complete, already in flower, and then stopped.

And in the standing water there are other residents. The reflection of the sky is a deeper blue than the sky. The canopy reflected is taller. Sometimes there is a reflection of a roof or a tree that is not standing there at all, standing just behind you, where the keeper should be. The inhabitants cast soft, multiple, contradictory shadows — one to the north, one to the south, one falling upward across its own leaves — so that you can never be certain where the body that casts them is. When you step in, there is no ripple, no lapping. The reflection does not break. It waits for you to step out.

So they live there the way the place lives: not as a population that counts and changes, but as a condition held in place by hand. Frogs blinking out of phase, fish circling an invisible center, birds mid-beat, insects breathing for the flowers, trees rooted in urns and leafing without season, keepers crouched at pans and stacking the same bricks, all of them wet and bright and perfectly still, repeating across the flat because the makers ran out of variations and the place now copies itself out of habit, and they with it, until a cluster of walls and green crowns far off on the white glare, when you come to it, is the same cluster again a little farther on, solid and empty and lived in.
