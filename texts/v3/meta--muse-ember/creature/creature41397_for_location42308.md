What lives in Urmesh does not move to meet you. It waits, rooted in heat, and you find it the way you find the place itself — first as warmth underfoot, then as a smell, baked clay and crushed green leaf, then as color against the dust.

Down low, in the bowl of the floor where the pumice dust lies thickest and bare for great distances, it begins in seams. You have to kneel to see it properly, because it does not stand up to be counted. Mats of broad, paddle-shaped leaves lie flat against the stone or push up against a wall, against the slumped mortar of a house, in the corner where two paved streets meet and the plates have cracked. They are dark green-black, almost black in the deep shadow at the edge of the dome, and where the light-plates are thin and the dim dusk is strongest they turn burgundy at the rim, as if the heat has scorched the edge of the leaf and the leaf has accepted it.

They are thick. Waxy. Made to catch very little light and to lose almost none of what they make from it. Inside there is no water to be lost. There is a hot, turpentine resin, the smell of it rising when a stem is bruised or when the midday brightens a fraction and the leaves unfurl. In the morning they open toward the translucent mineral in the dome, slowly, without hurry, each leaf turning its broad face up. In the evening they fold, broadest leaves creasing over the others, holding their resin close through the cooling hours. Their roots do not drink because there is nothing to drink. They grip. They take hold of a piece of pumice, they pry into mineral dust, they split a paving plate with a patient, widening pressure and take their living from the stone itself, from the air that the vents sigh in and out, from the heat that never leaves the floor.

And they are not alone in the low places. Between one house and the next there may be fifty paces of naked dust and a single clump — ash-tongues, tufts of dry fern that are not ferns and do not curl for water. They stand in a pale, papery star, dry to the touch, silent until a current crosses the plaza and then they rustle, all at once, like rain that never falls. Then they settle again. They live by the same rule, further out in the emptiness, as sparse and as deliberately spaced as the houses themselves. The city was built that way, and the population that remains has learned the pattern.

Lift your eyes from the mats and the vines are already overhead. The kiln-vines do not respect buildings. One has grown into the mortar of a wall and the mortar has grown around it, and neither has yielded for a hundred years. They tangle up the stone ribs of the dome, climbing toward the light-plates, seeking the strongest of that perpetual greenish-brown dusk. A tangled mass will cover a kiln throat blackened generations ago, will spill over a granary door warped shut, will follow a rope bridge long after the rope has rotted. Where they find a loom left standing in a house, they push through the floorboards and curl over the warp threads, leaf resting on wood, stem resting on thread, and the wood does not split and the vine does not stop. It is a cohabitation, settled.

Higher, or rather wider, are the resin-trees. The trunk is no thicker than a wrist, no thicker than the handle of a tool left in the dust, and yet the crown is vast and overlapping, overlapping the next crown and the next, like a roof made of shade. From below, in the dim light, they appear to be holding up the dome. They drip. Slowly, all through the day, a bead of amber sap forms and falls and ticks on a clay pot below, on baked earth, on a shattered glassy plate. The sound carries in the animal-less night far more than it should. The sap inside the leaf and stem is their blood and their water and their defense, turpentine-sharp, hardening in the pale glow where it falls. In the midday, when the floor plates expand and crackle with snaps like fired twigs rolling across the bowl, the trees make a faint ticking of their own, swelling.

They flower the way they live: without visitors. No insect comes, no bird brushes them, no hand gathers. In the low seams, where a shoot has pushed up through a paving crack in the deepest dark of night and split a plate to take the light that is there, a sulfur-yellow flower opens. Waxy, spicy, self-contained. It sets itself. The center thickens, the petals close at evening with the leaves, and a pod begins to swell. You can hear it in the quiet, if you listen through the vent pipes sighing and roaring with the thermal updraft. It ticks. It ticks for days. And then, with a flat pop that echoes off the dome and rolls to the perimeter wall, it bursts.

That is how they move. By explosion and by wind in the vents. Seed on the hot air, seed in the slow spirals where dust lifts off the hottest floor. A seed lands fifty paces away in naked dust, finds a seam, grips pumice, and begins again — a mat that will lie flat for years before it sends up its first flower. Nothing else is needed. The place keeps itself and they keep the place, in equilibrium, nothing new and nothing freshly ruined.

At night they are still, except for contraction. The walls tick as they cool, the kilns tick, a house creaks in its exact lean, and far out in a corner a new shoot forces its way up. In the morning the leaves unfurl again toward the plates. The vines release a drop of sap here and there. A pod pops. A resin-tree ticks against a pot.

That is the whole population of Urmesh. No heartbeat, no wing, no foot in the dust beyond the old hand-prints baked hard in the kiln row — the last sign of animal life, and already softening at the edges. What lives here lives green and burgundy and amber, from heat and mineral and dim light, in vast empty spaces held in shape by streets, and it does not need anything to come back.
