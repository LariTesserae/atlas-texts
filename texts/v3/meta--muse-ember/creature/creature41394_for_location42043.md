At first, it seems almost no one lives there at all.

The city is mostly empty. The houses stand far apart, the streets are too wide for the few feet that cross them, the plazas are larger than any market needs, and the fields inside their walls are ripe and untouched. You can look across three valleys and see the same pale roofs and stone pens and come back to the bench you left and find everything exactly as it was. It takes a while to realize that the emptiness is not absence. It is sparseness. Life here does not crowd, and it does not announce itself.

It lives low first.

The green mat is where most of it is. Cushions of moss thick as a mattress, grasses in which your feet sink and do not bend the blades, ferns unfurled to a size they will keep. Between the sharp stones the green pours in, wet and cool to the touch, and in that damp it is quietly full. Snails inch through it so slowly that the silver of their trail is still there tomorrow, and the day after, the snail itself only a little further along, its shell damp and bright, its feelers raised as if listening to the still air. Beetles move in the grass, shiny and unhurried, black-green shells catching the flat daylight, making no sound against the stems. They are not hiding. There is nothing here that needs them to hide.

In the hand-pools, tucked between tilted slabs where condensation has gathered and stayed, the frogs wait. No larger than your hand, the pools are perfectly still and reflect the sky, so for a moment you see only your own face in the water, and then two eyes. They are grey-brown, mottled like wet stone, their eyes just above the surface, blinking seldom. They do not croak. They do not swim, or if they do, so little that the mirror never breaks. The water does not need to move for them to live in it. It only needs to stay damp, and it does.

That is the condition for everything here. The moss does not spread, the ferns do not grow larger, the berries on the waxy shrubs stay glossy and tight forever, neither falling nor rotting. Something has trapped a little soil in a crack, and the plant has filled it exactly, and stops. The fauna that live in that green have taken the same bargain. They grow exactly as much as they die, they consume exactly what they make, and so they can afford to be abundant without being noisy.

The small mammals peer from burrows in the moss, whiskers forward, fur sleek with the air's moisture. A marmot will sit upright in the sun on a warm slab, forepaws tucked, head turned a quarter to the light, and when you look away and look back it will be in the same posture, perhaps a degree further into the sitting, as if the sitting itself were its work. The ground birds are the same. Ptarmigan-like, mottled grey and white and stone-brown, they stand on the fracture planes and are the stone until you are almost on top of them. They do not flush early. Camouflage is enough. Why waste the energy of flight in a place where nothing is chasing?

Above them, in the air that never moves, the moths hang. There are enough of them to make a dust in the still light, pale wings folded, drifting no farther than the width of a house. They do not buzz. Nothing buzzes here. The insects are present in numbers, but they have no reason to call to each other across a distance, because no distance needs crossing. The grass is thick with beetles. The air is thick with moths. The silence holds them both.

The larger animals are domestic, and they too hold still. In the stone pens at the edge of town, sheep and goats stand in wool so thick it seems to keep the plateau's cold and damp out of their skin. They chew, jaw moving in a slow circle, unhurried, their bells silent — not broken, simply never rung, because a head raised to chew does not swing enough to strike. They are not wary of you. They look at you with the mild, unfocused attention of animals that have never been startled by anything that meant it. Their pens are clean, their fodder is green and exactly enough, their fleeces are full. A sheep standing in a pen three valleys away will be standing in that same pen, in the same damp smell of wool and moss and stone, for as long as you care to return.

The birds that live with people do the same in miniature. Swallows and swifts nest in the eaves, wings folded tight against their bodies, tails protruding from under the turf roofs. Occasionally one makes a tiny adjustment — a shuffle, a turn of the head, the same adjustment back and forth — and then is still again. They do not sweep and dive in the way swallows do in a windy place, because there is no wind to ride and no need to chase. The moths are already hanging there, in their dust, waiting to be exactly what the birds make of them.

And then, last and sparsest, the people.

You find them the way you find a chisel mark still pale on a lintel, or a flint hammer lying where it was dropped, still sharp. One here, one there, between structures built for far more of them than there are. They are dressed in woven wool, undyed and heavy with the damp air, leggings and tunics and cloaks pinned with wood and bone, no metal to catch the light. Their hair is tied back against the cold. Their sleeves are wet from brushing through the moss and fern. Their breath shows, briefly, even with the sun on their necks.

They do not hurry, and they do not seem to be waiting for anything to finish. A man stands at a doorway hammering at a lintel that is half-carved — a line, a spiral begun — and will remain half-carved, his adze rising and falling in the same arc, the chips at his feet exactly as many as his work requires. A woman carries a basket between two houses that are a long walk apart, her pace even, her basket woven of fiber and full of something she will set down in the same place she set it down yesterday. Someone tends the kiln from which smoke rises in a thin straight column that goes up and stops, like the bead of water under a ledge. Someone else stands by a condensation wall of woven fiber strung between posts, watching the damp gather in bright beads on the waxy leaves below it, beads that swell and do not fall.

They are organized, after a fashion. There are quarters for craft and for storage, for animals, with fences and gates and wells cut deep into the rock. But the organization is not pressing toward completion. It is pressing toward balance. A field of crops stands at perfect ripeness inside its low wall, and no one harvests it, and it does not spoil. A workshop has a charred interior and a tool still warm-clean in the hand's sense. A granary door is small and thick and pegged, and is opened and closed to exactly the same degree by the same damp hand.

They do not age as you would recognize it. The young stay young. The old do not get older. A face you see from a ridge will be that face, in that wool, at that same distance from that same lintel, when you climb the ridge again. They do not die, and they are not born in any hurry either — or if they are, the birth is part of the same equilibrium, a life arriving exactly sized to the space left by the moss it replaces. There is no sound of children playing, no argument, no call across a plaza. Sound here is rare and spaced: a single bird call, clear; the tick of a stone cooling; the tap of a hammer far away. Your own steps on the wide slab street are too loud, and you learn to make them softer.

That is what lives there, in the end. Not a wilderness, and not a city, but a plateau where the rock is newly split and will not soften, the water beads and will not fall, the green is lush and will not spread, and every living thing — frog in its hand-pool, snail on its silver road, beetle in the unsinking grass, sheep chewing in its pen, swallow folded in its eave, person with a basket between two far houses — has found the exact size of its life and holds it.

From the highest bench, toward an evening that does not change the light, you can see them all at once if you know how to look: the glittering pools, the pale roofs in their valleys, the green mat closing over the last paving stone, the bead of water still swollen under the ledge by your foot. Nothing has moved to meet you. Nothing has gone. Everything that lives there is there, in its place, alive in the stillness, consuming only what it makes, and making only what it consumes, forever.
