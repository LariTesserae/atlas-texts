They are not animals. If you come looking for a who — ears, eyes, legs, anything that might turn toward you or away — you will find nothing. No insects in the flowers, no birds hidden in the thicket, no lizards flattened on the hot stone to drink the heat. Nothing here moves under its own power.

What lives in the Sinterhouse moves the way a factory moves when left to itself: by pressure, and heat, and expansion. Slowly. And then, all at once, in a pulse you will almost certainly miss.

They live in islands. Three on the floor, and a fourth that grows in mirror image from the ceiling, though there is no mirror. For meters at a time there is only bare, shining mineral floor, empty enough to hear your own pulse, and then suddenly a thicket so lush it contradicts everything around it — leaves overlapping leaves, layer upon layer, in greens almost indecent against the grey and copper ground.

That sparseness is not an accident. They cannot spread the way things spread where there is water. They have never known water. No root here hunts for moisture. Roots go down and sideways after heat, braiding themselves tight around veins of hot rock that run under the floor, and they breathe in mineral vapor the way other plants breathe air.

What runs in them instead of sap is a clear ruby resin, thick as warm honey, that does not dry. Brush a stem and it weeps and stays wet, a bead that catches the light. The leaves are broad and waxy yet dry to the touch. Some are ribbed like corrugated iron, some are enamel-smooth and slightly translucent, so the ordinary white-gold daylight from the slit entrance passes through them and lights the veins from within. This is their first work: to be fins.

A young shoot is almost entirely intake. Its leaves angle, day after day by increments too slow to watch, to catch the band of sun where it falls. Their surfaces are made to shed heat as much as to take light, to hold a temperature on the leaf that the vault below is constantly trying to push past. They smell of hot resin and scorched sugar and faint ozone when the sun is on them, like a workshop left running in an empty field.

As the root finds a hotter vein, the plant changes its work without ever quite deciding to become another thing. The root swells and contracts over the course of days — a bellows cycle so patient you could watch for a whole day and see nothing but the same swollen knot — driving mineral salts up into the stem, calcining them there, breaking them down and laying new material in layers, cell by cell, like a pipe being printed in hot metal. This is smelting. The plant is not feeding on soil, because there has never been soil. It is making its food out of rock that has never been soil, cooking nutrients out of raw slag that is still in the act of becoming floor.

The stems thicken to hold what the roots have made. The ruby resin is stored under pressure in jointed reservoirs, and hours or days later a joint somewhere in the thicket pings, very small and metallic, and vents a thin hiss of hot vapor. It is the nearest thing they have to a voice, and to breathing out. The air in the pocket moves mostly when a plant decides, reluctantly, to release it. Otherwise the air is still and hot and thin and metallic, and the dust that gets into a visitor’s lungs stays there, because there is no water to clear it. The plants have no lungs to clear. They take the vapor in through the same pores they will later vent through, in a rhythm governed not by need but by pressure rising past an unnamed threshold.

No one plant keeps one job. Intake, smelter, reservoir — these are phases, and phases in a place that has not yet agreed on its own rules. A leaf that was half-formed will be half-formed again next week, and then, in an afternoon when you are not looking, complete — bigger than the stem that bears it, ribbed differently because the crystal pattern underneath it has shifted while it was waiting. Individual leaves do not repeat. The thicket is not a colony of finished shapes. It is a process that keeps assembling itself, layer upon layer, and has not finished becoming a species, or a garden, or a factory.

When they flower, they do not open for pollinators. There are none to open for. The flower opens like a furnace door, glowing orange at the throat, and sheds its pollen as a brief, silent spark. The spark arcs across the empty mineral floor to the next plant in the island, or sometimes, improbably, to the next island, and cross-pollination is electrocution. It is quiet, precise, and impersonal. Where the spark lands, the leaf edge singes faintly and the smell of ozone sharpens for a moment, and a seed begins — not on a stigma, but deep in a pressurized node in the stem, where heat and resin and a sudden charge have done the work that elsewhere is done by touch and tongue and wing.

The seeds are older than their parent plants. You cannot always tell one from a pebble, because here a pebble is not quite a pebble. It is a nucleus, growing. A seed lies in the hot floor, made of layers that seem to predate the plant that made it, as if the template was laid down in the rock before the rock had finished setting. It waits for a heat threshold it has apparently always known. Some wait years while the parent thickens and vents and calcines around them. The pocket itself is older in the middle than at the edges, and the seeds keep that same staggered time.

The ceiling garden is the same garden continuing, root to root. Hanging leaves angle down toward the light bouncing off the floor; roots go up into the vault in a mirror that is not a mirror. At the meeting point in the middle of the room the two halves do not touch. They interleave, which should be impossible, and direction up there behaves as badly as it does down here — walk east across the floor and you arrive back from the west, having passed the same thicket twice from opposite sides, and uphill and downhill are the same slope. The plants have never needed to know which is which. A root that leaves east may re-enter west and find the same hot vein again. They are comfortable being their own opposite.

They do not die the way things die where there are animals and rot and rain. When a plant’s pressure fails, or its smelter root cools as a vein shifts and seals itself with fresh glass, it does not fall and decompose. It calcines in place. Its leaves go brittle and translucent, its resin is reclaimed by the heat and by its neighbors, its mineral layers become indistinguishable from the floor that is still setting around them. A crack that was open an hour ago is half-sealed with a lip of fresh glass; a footprint fills in like cooling concrete deciding to forget you. A dead thicket becomes new floor, and new floor becomes a place where a seed older than its parent is already waiting.

That is why the islands look both profuse and provisional. They are engines growing where engines should not be able to grow, and gardens behaving like factories. Their roots print pipes in hot metal. Their leaves are fins. Their flowers are furnace doors. Their breath is a vented hiss. Their speech, if you stay long enough to listen in the almost perfect quiet — except for the tick and ping of cooling slag and the occasional sigh of a plant venting — is the echo arriving a fraction before the sound finishes leaving your mouth, delayed by a coldness you cannot feel.

They have no hurry whatsoever to finish. Heat builds in the vault and barely falls, muffled day and night, and they grow in pulses separated by long, patient stillnesses. Nothing here will last in the form you see it, and nothing here has lasted yet. The floor is still setting. The thickets are still assembling themselves out of material they smelt themselves.

For now, that is who lives in the Sinterhouse: not creatures, not artifacts, but three lush islands and a fourth hanging upside-down, running like a factory, grown like a garden, with no water, no animals, and no hurry to be finished becoming a world.
