It is loud before it is visible, and the living things here are loud in the same way — not in bursts, but as a pressure that has become the air.

You do not meet them face to face. You hear the first inhabitants before you see them, which is how most of them prefer it. The roar is the machinery, but laid over it, sharpening it, is a screaming that never quite resolves into a single call. Hundreds of swifts own the hollow shaft. They nest in the vent holes along the terraces, in the dark mouths of idle ducts, in pairs where the steel has rusted into a bowl just wide enough for a cup of twigs and feathers. They cannot land anywhere else. To come to rest is to fall. So they live almost entirely in the air, tearing up and down the forty-meter width of the shaft in tight, deafening loops, catching what the warm updraft lifts to them.

The tower breathes for them. Once every nine days the great wheel below turns far enough to sigh, and the enclosed air slows as it rises. Midges, moths, the dust of spores and seeds — all are carried up on that slow exhalation, and the swifts ride the same current, picking insects out of the wind with a snap you feel more than hear. At dusk they drop home in a spiral, screaming louder, a rain of wings against concrete, and vanish into holes that from below look empty. Each pair knows its hole by the pattern of damp around it. They have raised young here for so long that the shaft itself must smell, to them, like a nest: warm iron, guano white as efflorescence on the ledge below, the faint onion-smell of the ferns.

Below them, where the swifts do not go, another airborne population has never left the ground at all in any meaningful sense. On the lower floors the electric lights have never fully gone out. Around them, in a permanent amber noon, moths and midges swarm thick enough to breathe. They live their whole lives in that warm light around the lamps, as the description of this place says — whole generations born, mating, laying eggs in the cracks of the lamp housing, dying, and being replaced by others who have never seen the sky that is open at the top of the tower, forty meters above them. They are not drawn to the light the way lowland insects are. They have been selected, over decades, for staying. The ones who wandered would find cold and void. The ones who stayed found a world of constant warmth, sugar-water from condensation on the glass, and no winter.

They farm nothing, and are farmed by nothing that hunts them in the dark, except the spiders.

The spiders are everywhere the machines pause. They string their webs across idle gears, across conveyor belts that will move one increment in a month, across the seams where a pipe has gone quiet. A web here is not an ephemeral thing. It can be left for a season because the prey comes to it, and because the hum never stops. They vibrate with the hum. A spider can tell a midge from a drip, a wasp from loose rust, by the vibration traveling through silk that is itself bolted to two tons of slowly breathing steel. They sit in the center of these great, invisible drums and wait, motionless for hours, in the way of everything here. When a gear finally sighs forward a millimeter, the web shivers but does not tear. Prey struggles and is still.

On the sun-hot transformer boxes, in the open yard and on the roof ring, the lizards do the same thing as the spiders, but by sun rather than vibration. They bask motionless for hours on metal that holds the ordinary white noon light like a griddle. At this height the air at the rim is thin and cold enough to sting your nose, but the transformers are warm, and warm is the whole economy of the tower. A lizard will choose a box, claim its particular heat shimmer, and return to it day after day, sliding out only to take a moth that has strayed too far from the lamp or a midge that has been lifted and dropped. They move very slowly, as if conserving their own long abrasion against the same concrete the vines are slowly splitting. Their scales are grey-brown with rust dust. They have learned the exact hour when each box cools enough to be safe and not so cool as to be cold.

But the most improbable inhabitants, the ones who make the place feel inhabited rather than merely occupied, are gathered at the drips.

There is no pond here. There are teacups. That is enough.

At dawn the cold steel sweats. Warm air from the engine floor rises to meet the cold at the open top and condenses, making its own closed weather. It drips, almost always somewhere, from pipe to leaf to bolt head. It collects in the cup of a leaf, in the hollow of a gear tooth, in the saucer of a cable clamp, in a footprint-shaped hollow worn concave into a step. In those pockets — dark, warm, still, perhaps a spoonful of water at most — life breeds at a scale so small you could cover it with your thumb.

Thousands of frogs live in this archipelago of teacups. Their evening chorus, amplified by the hollow steel, is the wet percussion under the industrial drone, a constant plocking from a hundred invisible marshes. They have made a pond system out of a leaf axil and a bolt head and move between them in the rain of drips, hopping along pipe runs that are always damp with black damp-tears beneath every joint. A female will lay a string of eggs in the saucer of a clamp. The tadpoles will develop there, in water that may not be replaced for weeks, eating algae green on copper and the fine biofilm that grows wherever condensation lingers. They metamorphose in a gear tooth. Their skins never dry because the tower never lets them. They are, impossibly, frogs at extreme altitude, and they do not know it, because to them the tower is not high. It is warm, it is wet in the right places, and the roar covers their calls and covers them from anything that might hear them.

Around them, in the same damp, is the world that makes the frogs possible, and that the frogs in turn make possible. Bacteria bloom white on concrete in efflorescence, algae bloom green on copper, fungi work the leaf-rot that takes three seasons to become soil on a ledge. Springtails and mites live in the rust-dust grit caught in blocked drains, in the flattened accumulation that is cemented, slowly, into something soil-like on top of a machine housing. Earthworms, few and slow, turn that grit. Moss fills the cracks that water widens a fraction over twenty years, and holds them. None of this is visible as an event. If you return after a year, the white bloom will be a little farther along the wall, the green a little deeper. The frogs eat the midges that eat the algae. The spiders eat the frogs' smaller neighbors. The whole damp system is a food web built from a drip.

And then there are the green inhabitants, who are not decoration but residents in their own right, and who set the terms for all the others.

The ficus vines as thick as your wrist are not climbing the tower. They are anchored in it. Their roots are in pipe joints, in the expansion joints of concrete, in the seams of engine housings. A vine advances a handspan in a year. It has grown over the dials of gauges until the dials can only be found by following the roots down. It drapes from the terraces and climbs the quiet smokestacks, and along its length, in the leaf axils where water sits, frogs hide by day. Where it crosses a warm pipe, insects farm that warmth in the shelter of its leaves — scale insects, aphids, the small biting things that live their entire lives in a single warm joint and never need to cross the void between platforms.

The ferns erupt from bolt heads in great shaggy rosettes. They grow downward as well as up, hanging inverted gardens from the upper catwalks, leaves drinking condensation as it falls. When the great wheel sighs, the slow wind that ventilates the shaft leans them, almost imperceptibly, into itself. They have, over decades, synchronized themselves to a nine-day breath. Ferns on a ledge do not need to hurry because the drip that feeds them does not hurry.

On the roof ring, where the normal sun hits unfiltered, there are trees. Real trees, with trunks and branches and dappled shade, rooted in planter-engine housings, grass growing in the concrete joints around them, holding rain in their blades. Their canopies are interlaced with electrical cable, which serves them as trellis and as shade and as a highway for rodents. Their blossoms are visited by insects that live in the warm light, and by the swifts when they stoop, and those insects carry pollen, and the birds carry seed to the transformers, where new saplings sprout from oily grit. A tree here will slowly split a housing seam over twenty years, patiently, the way water splits concrete. It will not be moved. It will not be hurried. Everything alive has negotiated terms with the machinery on those terms.

In the cable trays the rodents run. They are the connective tissue between the lush points. A rodent may build a nest in the insulation of a single engine housing on a single platform and live its whole life there, never descending, because the void between platforms is uncrossable for it — wind, roar, and a drop that looks like rumor far below. But a young rodent will sometimes run a tray for an hour to reach the next platform, carrying seeds in its gut, carrying mites on its fur. Wasps paper the undersides of the catwalks, building grey cities in the warm underside where rain never reaches. Every warm pipe has its insects farming its warmth, a faint ticking population that can be heard only when you put your ear close and the roar drops, for a moment, into a sigh.

There is no apex. There is no outsider. The swifts eat the midges the lamps breed. The frogs eat the midges the damp breeds. The lizards eat whatever wanders into the sun. The spiders eat whatever the hum brings. The rodents eat seeds and leaves and the occasional fallen insect. The wasps parasitize the quiet. The fungi eat the fallen leaves that the trees drop once the wind, torn sideways at this height, finally pulls them loose. The bacteria eat the fungi. Everything dies where it lives, and becomes the thin soil on a ledge, and feeds the next handspan of vine.

To live here you must be small enough to fit in a teacup, or winged enough to cross the void, or slow enough to let the tower come to you. You must tolerate deafening noise by making your own noise inside it, or by learning to listen through vibration. You must be able to make a pond out of a bolt head, a garden out of a gutter crack, a home out of a housing that was built to be a machine and is still, faintly, running. You must not need a river or a valley or a season that changes quickly. You must be content with damp, not wet — intermittent, patient, endless.

And so they do. Leaf by leaf, frog by frog, swift by swift, moth by moth, they have claimed a place that was built for industry and then left to the weather, until the industry and the ecology became the same thing. They do not hurry because the tower does not hurry. They are not pristine and they are not in collapse. They are established, in that settled balance of decay and repair, humming together very slowly at the top of the world, in a tower that is mostly empty air and concrete, and alive everywhere a drip can be caught.
