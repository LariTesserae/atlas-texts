You will not find them all at once. No one walks out to meet you, and nothing announces itself. The first lives you notice are the ones that do not need a floor to stand on. They only need the light.

Swallows and pigeon-like birds are nesting by the thousand in the mouths of dormant engines, in the black throats of furnace shells, in any hollow that is cool enough to hold an egg. They wheel in the narrow gaps between towers in slow, bright flocks, and they cast no real shadow because the light is coming from everywhere, bounced and re-bounced off pale metal and ceramic. When they call it sounds muffled, as if through a thin wall. The air edits everything down.

With them, thick enough to walk through, are the moths and small flies that hang in the permanent glare around lamps that never turn off. They drift like slow snow. They part and close behind you. They were born into that white-blue, cold, overexposed light and they have learned to navigate without a direction. A lamp here is not lit; it is a condition, like altitude. They do not sleep in the dark, because there is no dark to sleep in, only recesses where the light is still remembered by the surfaces. They live their whole brief lives in the glare, and the glare lasts so long that a brief life is a long one.

Go down a level, then down another, into the seams where the saturation almost touches. That is where the next inhabitants keep themselves. They live on film, not on water.

Lizards, grey and excellent, lie for hours on panels that have held the sun for days — days here are long — until the metal under them is finally warm, and then they dart, excellently, into ductwork. Beetles work the warm vents, patiently eating dust and insulation fiber and whatever the warm air leaves behind. Both have learned that temperature is not where the light is. The metal is cold despite the glare, cold the way a thing at altitude stays cold, and colder still on the north sides of pipes where water beads and stands. They drink that beading. They wait for the long overnight, in geologic slow time, when warm air from deep in the lattice meets cold upper metal and condenses in shallow, breathing pools trapped in the dish of a discarded housing. In the morning the pools are no wider than a plate. The lizards and beetles come to them as to wells.

And in the pools, improbably, are the fish.

Silvery, turning in slow circles in water that looks too shallow to hold them. They are not, in any useful sense, swimming. They have learned the damp, they have learned the slow. A pool here is shallow in space and deep in time. It persists for hours, then disappears without running, without going anywhere, reabsorbed into film and bead. The fish have learned to live in that disappearance. When the pool goes, they lie still, silver and wet, breathing through skins that are more like damp membranes than scales, waiting in the curve of the housing for the next condensation to return them to circling. They reflect the light that is everywhere because there is nowhere to hide from it. If you watch long enough, you will see one pass through a place where the water should not be, a shape still turning a breath after the pool has thinned. It has not learned to be deep. It has learned to last.

The slowest inhabitants do not move at all, until you have been gone for years.

Mats of moss the color of pale hair in the corners where the damp line holds in a gutter. Lichen in white and rust-orange crusts on sun-baked ceramic. A thin, wiry grass in the sweepings — and the soil here *is* sweepings, shredded insulation and metal dust and plastic fiber compacted into a grey mat that holds moisture for a while. A vine that is mostly a root, feeling its way along a cable tray for twenty meters to find another damp seam. They do not make a forest. They make a sparse, opportunistic suggestion of green against all that manufactured surface, and they grow at the pace of everything else: patiently, across long durations, apparently not moving at all. Return after a decade and the seam is greener. That is how they measure a year. They are the first residents who were not invited either, and the ones who will still be here, greener, when the paint finally chalks.

Deeper still, where the air is warmer and never fully bright, there is another population, and it is the one the place was really made for.

In the pipes, in the bends where warm air rises and cold metal sweats, there are blind, soft things. Translucent, without eyes or shell, they settle where the condensate gathers and they pulse, very slowly, when a furnace deep in the stack exhales on its years-long cycle and warms its district for a season. They have no use for sight in a light that has no single direction; you can cast a faint shadow north and another, weaker, south, and a dark interior can be lit, bright as noon, from the wall itself. They have learned to be shape without edge. They bud when it is warm. They eat heat and film and the slow fallings of insulation.

In the insulation itself, in the grey mat of shredded fiber that the grass grows in, are things with many legs. They walk through a landscape that is constantly coming apart in shards — panel and snapped conduit and curled sheet, glittering and sharp, with the bright inside of the break still bright. Many legs are not for speed. They are for hold, for distributing weight across a surface that was never meant to be ground. If you listen close enough, the whole dense stack is threaded with their sounds, a faint ticking under the ticking of the machines. They are never in a hurry because the habitat is everywhere, layered just as thickly as the structures. Above something, below something, beside something that is also above something else: to live here is to live in a seam.

Which is why the largest inhabitants are the ones you first took for background.

The cranes, the conveyors, the furnaces, the power halls. Industry is here at industrial scale — factories the size of basins, conveyors wide enough to drive on, cranes that straddle an entire district — but it does not roar. The machines have slowed themselves into near silence, because the thin air carries poorly and because slowness is survival at altitude. A crane lifts at a meter an hour; you can watch it for an afternoon and see that it has moved. A conveyor inches, a peristalsis. A furnace breathes heat for a season of slow years, then cools for another, warming everything that lives on it. Birds nest in its mouth as in a ribcage. Beetles work its vents as on skin. Moss marks its gutters with a dark line. If the small things live in the gaps between the saturation, the great things *are* the saturation, and they are alive in the only way the place allows anything to be alive for long: metabolically, mechanically, patiently. Everything mechanized, everything slow, everything patient. No traffic, no shouting, no grinding. A drip somewhere, every few seconds, out of phase with a tick somewhere else. A faint electrical hum you feel more in your teeth than hear.

And then, in the margins of that quiet, the rarest of the residents.

You know someone is tending the place because the place is tended. A number on a factory wall is stenciled fresh while the rust around it is ten years further on. A walkway that is also a roof that is also a wall has been cleared of scree, though the scree is everywhere else, sharp and new. A broken panel has been lifted and set aside with its bright break still bright. You may go years without seeing a living thing that does it. Then, on a walkway close enough to read that number, you see one, moving at crane speed.

They are tall and thin, built like the lattice itself, breathing the thin cool air slowly, their breath showing for half a second even in full glare. They do not hurry because there is no hurry that would help them: a sound here arrives after its echo, you hear the drip and then, a beat later, the faint click of the machine that caused it, so they have learned to finish a thought long before they answer it. A sentence takes an afternoon. They live on a single level, sometimes for a whole life, because the stairs that go down a level arrive, damp and mossy, at a level that is also up, with condensation beading in the shape of the tread you just left. They navigate by memory of the doubling. They remember the tower both rusted and fresh, the bridge that crosses a gap and halfway across the gap is a different gap on a different level and you finish on the other side of the basin from where you started, though the bridge has not moved. They communicate mostly by repair, by leaving a panel true, a duct clear, a seam respected. There are not many of them. The place is too dense, too bright, too slow for many. They are less a population than a habit the place has kept.

But even they are not the most native thing here.

The most native things live in the thinning itself.

Reality here is overbuilt. It has been built on itself so densely, and so high, with no bedrock to anchor it, that it has begun to thin and double in places. Not dramatically. Slowly, as everything else does. Two stacks that should be far are near when you are not looking at them. Distance stutters. Light has no single direction. And in those thinning places — in the damp pockets, in the long bright days, in the moment after a bright panel when the panel is still there, a pale shape in the air where it was, for a breath longer than it should be, and you can almost touch it — something has learned to live.

They have no body you can point to, because a body would be only one version. They are lingerers, afterimage dwellers, creatures of the break between versions: between the rust ten years further and the fresh paint, between the stair going down and the stair arriving up, between the click and the drip. They feed on discrepancy the way the fish feed on damp and the soft things feed on heat. They are why a sound can arrive after its echo and still be on time for them. They are why the bridge can take you to the other side of the basin without moving. They do not haunt the place. They *are* the place, making alternatives of itself in the margins, because a day here lasts so long that things have time to exist twice, and a new level grows, a decade at a time, on top of a level that is already rusting in a way that suggests it will be new again.

Watch for them at the edge of vision, where the strong light in a dark interior is coming not from a door but from the wall itself. Watch for the faint double shadow. Watch for the fish that turns once more after the pool is gone. That is not a failure of the world. That is its population, at its most itself.

Stand long enough in the cool air, bright light on your face, on a floor that is a roof that is a wall that is a cliff, and you begin to move the way they move. A meter an hour. You listen for the tick and the drip, out of phase. You stop expecting the shadow to point one way. You learn the names of the seams: engine mouth, vent, gutter, housing dish, cable tray, insulation mat. You learn that to live here is not to live *in* the place but to live *as* it continues — slowly, densely, brightly, breaking gently and keeping going.

It will keep going. Slowly.
