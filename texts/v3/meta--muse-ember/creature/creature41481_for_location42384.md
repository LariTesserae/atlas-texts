You don't meet them at the tunnel. You hear them before you can crawl close enough to see, and what you hear first is not a voice. It is hands.

*Thock, thock, thock.* Stone on clay. The clack of the loom. Water slapping into a basin, hissing where it finds the kiln. A chant that is barely a chant — few voices joined, counting in a pattern that resets every few breaths, as if to lose the count would be to lose the order of the tasks. The hands come and go across all of that sound. Dark, clay-stained, wet to the wrist, they are at the wheel, then at the fire, then at the garden urns by the doorway, then again a moment later in a place you have not yet seen. At first you take them for small people, packed tight in a low, hot room. Stay a little longer and that shape fails, and fails again, and does not quite settle.

The hands acquire forearms as you watch, but never quite acquire owners who stay put. A forearm rises from the steam by the kiln, long and folded, the skin mottled green-brown where algae has furred it and dark almost to black where the heat is worst, pale and slick where condensation runs fastest. It works, and while it works a body gathers around it: a crouch, a shoulder, a torso bent double under the basket-dome so that the head — if the damp swelling above the shoulders is a head — stays just clear of the hanging pots. When it moves to the loom it lengthens. When it returns to the urns it folds. The others do the same. There are not many. You count six, then seven, then six again, because the chant resets and they change places halfway through the counting.

They are warm the way the kiln is warm, and damp all the way through. Sweat does not evaporate from them; it condenses, a fine beading on the forearms, on the backs of the necks, matching the walls. Now and then they go translucent in the steam, as the three frogs do in the wall channels, and for a breath you can see the coiled structure inside them — a spiral of clay worked upward, a lashing of wood, a weave of reed pulled tight — and then the condensation thickens and they are solid again. They breathe through their skin as much as through any mouth. The mouth, when it opens for the counting, opens a fraction after the sound has already arrived. You hear the next number before you see them form it. They hear each other the same way, and work to the echo rather than to the hand that made it. The hammer falls after its report. They do not seem to mind the delay. They have learned to move in the gap.

They are not born here. Nothing here is born in that order. They are made, and unmade, and made again, and the making does not proceed in a line. A new one starts the way a tile starts: a palm press in a basin scooped from solid clay, fingers first, then a soft, glossy mass that holds the shape of the hand that pressed it and then forgets it. It is tended like the urns. It is watered from the channels. It is left by the kiln to firm — not to dry, nothing here ever dries — but to take a little heat into its rings, to harden at the edges while the center stays wet. A coil-pot body. Reed ribs. A skin of mud plaster that is never finished, so that in the dim amber light it always looks half-plastered, reeds showing through like the ribs of the wall beside it.

Then it works. Then, in the course of a cycle that is also a day, it is broken. Not dramatically. A crack runs across a shoulder during the morning crush, the way a tile cracks. By evening the crack has run backward into the hands that will break it tomorrow, and the piece is whole again, soft in the places where it has not yet set, and someone presses wet clay into the seam and smooths it with a thumb. The builders work around the surplus. A person who has been unmade sits half-unmade in the corner among the baskets-inside-baskets, waiting to be remade, its tools still within arm's reach, its skein of dyed fiber still tied at its waist. No one buries them. No one preserves them. Nothing here has eroded, and so nothing here has died in the way stone dies. They simply continue everything else.

That is why their bodies never quite finish. One has a forearm that is still bubbling up from the heat, swelling into shape too slowly to use. One is glazed before it is wedged, its surface hard and clicking while its interior is still raw. One has a vine — bean, squash, that bitter leaf curling like a question mark — climbing its shelf-leg of an arm, testing for a handhold in the time it takes to blink, and the person does not brush it away. It is tended like livestock. The vine is pruned with the single dull knife that passes from hand to hand all day, because there is no other metal and no hand is allowed to keep it. Whoever holds the knife trims the herb bundles, opens the seed bundles, cuts the vine back to the wrist, and then passes it on without looking, hand to hand, the way pots are passed. The knife is the only thing in the room that is allowed to be singular. Everything else exists for a second time.

They eat what the room grows for them, and what it grows on them. Overnight the tile floor blooms with fungi, soft and bright. By noon they are eaten, pinched off and eaten raw, still warm from the floor. From the soil in the urns they pick the tiny white worms, or rather they leave the worms to work the roots of the small trees whose roots circle visibly against the glass-hard clay, and eat what the worms leave. They drink from the basins where the rain — the fine warm rain that falls in a slow circle and never quite lands — returns to the basin it came from. They breathe in steam and breathe it out again as the drip on the dome overhead. The rain is not separate from them. It is their circulation, traced outside their bodies for a while, falling sideways, running down in rivulets, collecting in hand-shaped channels, pooling, rising again.

The household is not theirs alone, but it is not separate either. The blind grey moth, pale as unglazed clay, circles the embers in its slow devoted orbit, and they do not chase it. It sees heat for them; they navigate mostly by touch and by the heat on their skin, their eyes — if the pale fungus-lit niches are eyes — kept half closed against a light that never decides to become day. The three frogs live in the channels and appear and vanish in the condensation on their skin, breathing through the same wet surface. The plants are fed on steam and constant drip and they feed in return, gently strangling loom and shelf and person alike, their tendrils finding the spaces between tools that hang within arm's reach of other tools. Fire eats the damp wood, wood eats the clay, clay eats the water, water eats the fire, and they eat the fungi that bloom on all of it. It is a predator-prey system with no animals in it except the ones that have been made into parts of the work.

Their work is the choreography that holds them together: coil, weave, press, lash, fire without metal, cook without vessels that would survive. They hammer, they pour, they lift, and they begin again on the thing beside it, because starting anything means continuing everything else. A roof is woven before the walls are raised to meet it. A shelf is built while pots are already set upon it, still wet. They chant the count to keep the cycles from colliding — steam cycle, water cycle, fire cycle, weaving cycle, growing cycle — and when the count resets they reassemble around the tasks in a slightly different order, and the same hands appear where they were not a moment before. If you watch long enough you understand that it is less that there are six or seven of them than that there are always enough hands for the work at hand, and the work is always at hand.

Time in them is layered the way it is layered in the herbs hung to dry, green and brown and seed at once. A tenderness in the kiln can be firing, have fired, and will fire in the same cycle, and so can a person. One moment they are newly pressed and glossy and need to be handled carefully or they will sag. The next they are clicking hard as fired clay, and the next they are soaking again in a basin, softening, being wedged back into shape by another's hands. They remember tomorrow because the echo arrives before the sound, and they do not remember yesterday because nothing here is old. Their shadows separate from them and pool independently by the kiln, where everything is slightly darker than it should be. Those pooled shadows do not move when they move. They wait. When the person returns to the kiln the shadow is already there, ready.

You can see, if you stay, why the mountain has no summit. The terraces outside are not built and then abandoned. They are built by the same continuing, a person standing long enough in the doorway to be lashed into the frame, a wall standing half-plastered for long enough to acquire a forearm. The room is emerging, and they are emerging with it, layer by layer, damp at the edges, warm to the touch despite the thin altitude, surrounded by the vertigo of a drop that begins one step beyond the last terrace.

When you crawl out — the tunnel a little longer than when you entered, the flap of reed mat heavier with condensed water — the cloud has not lifted and the dim light has not changed, but the summit has grown by another terrace while you were inside. From out there you can still hear them: the hammering, the counting that is not quite song, the hiss of water on hot clay, the deep irregular breathing of the kiln as it draws. The rain inside is still falling in its slow circle. The moth is still circling the embers. The hands are still passing the knife, still pressing tiles that are still soft, still tending the things that climb them.

By the time you have finished trying to describe them, one of them has been unmade and remade and has already forgotten the shape you gave it. That is not a failure of the description. That is what lives there: not a finished people in a finished room, but a tended, handmade, endlessly continuing making, hot and wet and overgrown and overmade, already complete and still, in the most literal sense, under construction.
