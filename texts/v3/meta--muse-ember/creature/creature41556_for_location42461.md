At first, it seems that nothing lives here at all. That is the first way the place misleads you, and the most honest one. The ground is bare mineral crust for metres at a time, grey-brown silt hardened, iron-rusted, blackened with grit, cracked into broad plates with rounded edges. No grass lifts from it. No scrub. Nothing green. The eye keeps sliding across it looking for a sign of life and finding only more of the same packed, sorted, settled earth, until the light takes it at the horizon.

Then the smell reaches you. Warm mineral, a faint coppery tang, and under it the close, mushroom smell of wet felt. That is the first inhabitant announcing itself without showing itself. Follow that smell to where the damp is, and the place comes into focus, slowly, in layers.

Around the pockets of water — the shallow saucers where warmth gathers a centimetre or two deep, sometimes no larger than a hand, sometimes spread into a long irregular sheet that reflects the sky like a hole cut in the ground — the ground changes its texture. This is where they live. Not everywhere. Only where the dampness lives, and only where the dampness stays. The moisture here does not travel. It belongs to its hollow. And so do they.

The black ones are there first, if you know where to look. A black glaze on wet clay at the pool edge, smooth as lacquer, as if something had been poured and allowed to set rather than grown. It does not shine properly. It gathers light and holds it in a dull, depthless way, like the glassy slag-pebble in the silt beside it. But it is not stone. When the faint tremor runs up through the pool from below — the water hardly ripples, it only shivers — the glaze flexes with it, almost imperceptibly. It breathes with the pool. Above the larger pools the mist hangs a hand's breadth thick and never disperses, never lifts into fog, and the black felt at the margin seems to breathe that mist in and out with it, taking water and giving back warmth in exactly the amounts it took.

A pace away, where the ground is a little drier but still warm, the ochre sheets begin. Rust-orange, leathery, folded over themselves like insulation or tarpauline left out in the damp for years. They are dry to the eye and damp to the smell. Their edges curl up slightly, as if trying to trap the vapour underneath them. When you crouch, you can see that the white silica skin of the silt has been pressed flat beneath them, sorted finer there than anywhere else, as if the sheet has been slowly ironing the ground it covers. They do not spread quickly. They do not spread at all, in any direction a plant would understand. They thicken. They re-form themselves where they already are.

And across the silica skin in other places, pale grey-white fans spread in a flat radial pattern, filamentous, almost hair-like at the edges, solid enough in the centre to crack where they dry. That cracking is the most alive thing about them. A dry day — though there are no days here, only the same pale, even light — a dry spell in the air, and the fan splits along a line. A thin line, a pale scar across the white. Then the damp returns from its pocket, and the fan heals at the crack without changing it. The scar stays. The two halves knit, but the crack remains visible, remembered in the tissue. A fan can be healed and still carry the map of every time it dried.

It is hard to say how many kinds there are. Three, perhaps. Black glaze, ochre sheet, white fan. Or it may be one kind of life wearing three faces, depending on how wet the ground beneath it is, how much heat comes up through the silt at that exact spot. They have no leaves to photosynthesise with and no roots to drink. They feed, as far as one can tell, on the slow exchange that happens between silt and damp and warm air — on warmth and vapour and minerals themselves, taken up a fraction at a time and given back as crust. The terraces of mineral that build up in low steps across the plain, the graded bands of pebble size that look sieved by a machine, the pools that receive and the crusts that supply: the fungi are part of that sorting, or they are sorted by it. It is impossible to tell where the mineral process ends and the living one begins. They grow exactly as fast as the silt dissolves beneath them, and dissolve, in a sense, exactly as fast as they grow.

When they make spores — and they do, rarely, without flourish — the spores fall straight down. There is no wind here to carry them, only a steady, low air-movement that goes nowhere. So a colony stays where it was born. A spore lands a millimetre from its parent, on the same warm silt, in the same damp pocket, and waits there, perhaps for a very long time, until that same pocket breathes it in. Nothing accumulates. A spore that lands and does not germinate becomes mineral again. A spore that germinates becomes felt again. The family piles up on itself, in place, for an age.

In the pools themselves there is another skin, thinner still: a pale greenish sheen, a fungal bloom spread across the water's surface. It does not break the reflection. It lives in it. The tremor from below passes through it and is lost. Condensation beads on the undersides of the darker stones at the pool edge and stays there, neither running off nor evaporating away, and the bloom seems to feed on that held water as much as on the pool below it. The whole damp pocket — water, mist, stone, felt — is one small, closed household, and it is also, repeated across the basin, the whole population.

Life besides the felt is rare to the point of rumour, and smaller.

If you stay still long enough — and stillness here feels less like waiting than like being accepted into the equilibrium — you may see the grazer. It is no longer than your thumb, blind, translucent, so pale you can almost see the silt through it. Legs like pale wire. It lives at the edge of a damp mat, exactly on the boundary between wet and dry, and that boundary is its entire world. It does not cross into the wet. It does not wander into the dry. It travels along the line between them, with a slowness that feels geological, a few millimetres at a time, then stops. It dips its head to the fungus and pauses for a very long time. The pause is the point. It may be feeding. It may be listening to the tick-tick of mineral contracting somewhere out of sight, or to the low roar that never changes, which you feel as much in the soles of your feet as you hear. Then it moves on, wire legs placing themselves with great care on ground that has been in the same shape for longer than anything should be allowed to keep.

There might be one in a hundred metres. You may walk an entire day and see none. It does not appear to need the fungus, and the fungus does not appear to need it. It is tolerated, the way the mist is tolerated over the pool. A small, blind thing that has learned to live in the seam of another thing's life.

High up, so distant it seems more a disturbance in the light than a creature, there is sometimes the winged one. Something with wings, looping on a thermal that is always there, always the same temperature, always blowing in the same direction. It never descends. It never lands on the felt or the crust. It does not cry out. Its loop crosses the washed grey-blue sky and returns to the same place in the sky, as if the air had a track in it. You may not see another for the rest of the walk. Whether it eats, or what it eats if it eats, cannot be guessed from the ground. It does not need the place below, except for the air that holds it up without change. The place does not need it either.

That is the relationship, between all of them: needlessness, perfectly maintained. The fungi take warmth and vapour and give back sorted mineral and held damp. The grazer takes a little of the edge and gives back nothing that can be measured. The winged one takes the thermal and gives back its shadow, passing once, briefly, over a white fan, which does not react. Nothing is coming or going. The pools evaporate exactly as fast as condensation returns the water. The mist does not grow and does not lift. The roar under the world does not swell or fade. It is loud, and it never changes, and everything that lives here has arranged itself to live inside that loudness without ever raising its own voice against it.

You could leave for a year — in any other country a year would change everything — and return to find the same mist a hand's breadth over the same pool, the same black felt at the same edge of the same crack, the same scar healed across the same white fan, the same grazer perhaps a few millimetres further along the same boundary, the same winged loop in the same empty sky. Not because nothing moves here. Heat is moving. Water is moving. Minerals are moving. The tiny grazer is crawling. The felt is breathing. It is simply that all of it, accounted together, arrives back at the same place it started, forever.

An old, finished landscape, still working.
