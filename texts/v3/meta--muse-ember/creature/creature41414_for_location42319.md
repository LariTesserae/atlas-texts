No one lives here the way a city is supposed to be lived in.

You can walk a whole morning inside the built layer — up terraces, down through covered passages, along a channel that runs in a groove in the stone at your feet — and see no one at all. Most doors are shut. Most houses are soaked through and cold and empty, their stone-slab shutters closed against a mist that has not decided whether to be rain. The architecture is saturated, as if it were waiting for tenants. It is not, most of the time, occupied. It is tended.

That is the first correction. The people you do see are not residents in the ordinary sense. They are keepers at intervals.

You will find one, eventually, at a channel. A figure in heavy dark wool, standing slightly sideways in an alley too narrow for shoulders, head bent. The wool is never dry. It hangs in a damp weight over a smaller, stooped frame, patched at the elbow and hem with darker cloth, the colour gone to the colour of wet stone itself. No bright thread, no ornament that would hold water. Hands emerge from the sleeves — broad, slow, chapped from stone-dust and cold, nails cut short. When they work they do not hurry. Every step on slick rock is tested with the toe of a thick, bound shoe first. A fall here would not be a fall onto soft ground; it would be a fall onto a city that is also a quarry.

They are low-voiced, and not especially because of secrecy. Sound dies here in half a breath, in mist and thick wall, and raising the voice feels wasteful. What you hear more than speech is cloth rustling, a soft shush as an arm lifts a stone, a low word or two exchanged across a courtyard well, the phrase ending before the echo can return. Conversation, when it happens, is practical and brief: the state of a culvert, the sheep seen on the south moor, a wall leaning a degree more than last season. There is no market cry, no call to gather. Households are miles apart across open stony moor that is still part of the same urban layer, and a visit is a day's walk in weather you can watch change ward by ward.

They do not cluster where the building is densest. They live, when they live at all, on the edges of the middle districts — near the high, dry points. The granary, the shrine, the lookout: those dry places on the highest ground are where goods can be kept above the seep, where a body can be kept above the damp. A family will have a one- or two-room ground floor sunk half below the street, a hearth that is almost never lit — the chimneys are low and give off almost no smoke, and most houses go cold for weeks — and an upper doorway that opens onto someone else's roof. In winter many of them leave the plateau altogether, down the weather-softened steps to lower country where wood is plentiful and stone dries. In spring they return, because spring is when the water table rises and comes up through floors, and a channel not cleared in the morning will silt up by afternoon.

What they do, all day, is keep the water moving correctly. That is the life. They clear a finger-wide channel of grit with a small stone, they re-lay a pavestone the frost lifted in January, they brace a retaining wall with packed earth and carry a flat stone up three terraces to fill a hollow where a jar should sit. They count — not numbers, exactly, but states: this cistern is sinking, that hollow holds black water that will not evaporate, this doorway has been painted over by lichen another centimetre. They are slow because the work is continuous and will outlast them. Children, when you see them, are the same size for a long time and learn the drainage before they learn anything else: which street floods first, which passage echoes with a drip every few seconds, which west-facing wall sweats first at dusk. There are very few children. Most of the figures in doorways, keeping out of the mist with their backs to the stone, look old in the way wet stone looks old — not worn away, but settled.

They eat from storage. Jars in niches, grain in the granary above the seep, dried meat and curd from the sheep. Food is cold more often than hot. They sleep by daylight, because the hollows for lamps are never lit. At night the condensation returns and the city goes uniformly dark and damp, and they are inside, motionless, listening to the streets sound slightly louder in the cold.

The animals that live with them are in keeping with that scale. On the moor between districts, far apart, move the sheep and the shaggy goats. They are dark, heavy-fleeced, and their wool never quite dries either; the fleece hangs in wet curtains over the ribs. They do not bleat much. They move at walking pace, heads down, cropping the stiff tufts and rosettes, pausing a long time between mouthfuls. No bells. They are not milked in the modern sense so much as tended for wool and occasional curd, and they are let out to graze ground that is mostly construction — terraces, scree, wall-cracks — because there is no pasture and no intention of making any.

Alpine choughs nest in the empty niches, in the hollows above doors, in the underside of a covered passage where the drip keeps time. Black birds with dull red beaks, they are the nearest thing to a chorus and even they are mostly silent, an occasional click or a soft wing-rustle as one slips out into the mist. A raptor will cross high once in a while, silent as the city, and then be gone. A lizard, dark from saturation, will flatten itself at noon on the one upper surface the thin sun can actually warm, and lie motionless for an hour, breathing barely visibly. You will not see it if you are walking at a human pace.

The true permanent population is lower down and smaller.

In every cistern and peat-stained pool that does not evaporate but slowly sinks, there is pond life you have to look for: water fleas pulsing in the cold, caddis larvae dragging cases of fine stone through the channel water, water mites, a frog's spawn in spring blackening and then clear. The pools are too acid and too cold for much else, and that is enough. In the thin threads spilling over terrace edges, algae make green, black, and rust-brown strands that sway and do not sway. On the walls the condensation film that forms at dawn and runs off at noon is, for a few hours, a habitat in itself — a skin of water a hair thick, inhabited by rotifers and tardigrades and bacteria, drying to nothing by the time the sun has warmed the upper stones, returning at evening on the west-facing side first.

And over everything, slower than the people, the lichen. Crustose, foliose, no higher than a thumbnail, painting wet rock green and black and dull rust. It covers a doorway in a decade. It covers a handprint worn smooth in plaster if no one wipes it. The cushion plants in the scree, the rosettes in the wall cracks, the dwarf heather no higher than an ankle, the stiff grass that bends more than it sways — they are the forest of this place, cropped by wind that crosses the plateau unbroken. They grow an inch in a cool summer and are set back in an hour, and they persist because they, like everyone else here, have learned to hold water and not require it to be kind.

That is the census, if you want one: a few hundred people, seasonally present, spread over a city that could hold thousands, tending a hydrology their ancestors built. A few dozen sheep and goats, far apart. A handful of birds, a lizard here and there. Clouds of animals too small to count in the water, and a continent of lichen and algae that will still be there long after the last channel-clearer has left for the winter.

They do not dominate the place. They are not loud in it, or fast in it, or even, most days, visibly in it. They move through it the way the water moves through it — seeping, dripping, sinking, re-emerging two terraces down — keeping the stone from quite falling apart, and being kept, in turn, by the stone.
