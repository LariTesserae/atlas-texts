Almost everything that lives here lives underfoot first.

If you put your hand down in the mud, or step, or kneel, you find it before you see it — there is no seeing — a softness that is not mud. Bodies, layered. Large as a hand, warm as a stone held in sun for a long time, they do not hop or scurry when your weight comes on them. They flow. They give and give again, shift sideways with a slow sigh of silt, and then, while your boot is still there, begin to shift back, rebuilding their shallow nests in the shape of your print. A hundred paces will take you over more of them than you will ever count, translucent in places, damp, blind, breathing at a rate so slow you have to stay still to feel it. They are everywhere the water is shallow enough to sit, in the hollows, along the rims of pools, in the cracks that have not yet filled with anything. They have no need to hide. Nothing here has learned speed.

In the water above them are the ones that brush your legs.

Long, pipe-thin, blind, they turn away at the last moment and slap a warm side against your shin as they go, or they gather — curious, involuntary — in dense shoals that part around your boots and close behind you as if you had not been there. The feeling is not of fish darting. It is of something patient passing, in a current of its own. In the warm pipes they lay their eggs in layers, larvae upon larvae, dense as gravel in the curve of the metal, where the water is held at bath temperature by heat that has passed through something hot and old below. They hatch without hurrying. They grow over seasons that have no light to mark them. The cold places will make your toes ache; to them it is only a slower place.

On every warm surface there is another layer of living.

Slugs with the texture of wet rubber climb in overlapping curtains up the sides of concrete blocks, up the weeping rock faces, down again, leaving a fine, patient trail that the condensation immediately begins to erase. On the pipes — the hot pipes running through the black water, the layered pipes, the cables thick as a wrist — animals cling in rows, as if to a reef, their bellies to the metal, taking the heat straight into themselves. Small blind crabs have taken a half-sunken conveyor for a causeway, walking its still belt out into the marsh and back, their feet feeling for silt and for the faint electrical prickle in the air. Above the water, just above it, hangs a cloud of insects so fine that you do not hear them or see them, only feel them as a tingling on your lips, a faint warm static against your cheek, a breath taken in and discovered to be alive.

And sometimes, out of the black, a bird.

Slow birds, soft-winged, with enormous blind eyes, fly into you in the dark. They do not startle. They land on your shoulder out of curiosity, settle their whole weight there for a moment — longer, perhaps, than you think — and then leave, their weight gone before you have decided whether it was real. They feed, so far as anything here feeds, at the top of a chain that begins with silt washing off the raw new scarps. Silt feeds the filter-feeders in the still pools. The filter-feeders feed the things that flow like amphibians in the shallows. The amphibians, mating in water where old chemical traces make the surface buzz faintly against their skin, feed the birds. No part of it needs light. No part of it makes much sound.

The plants are few, and in their fewness you notice how they live.

Reeds, black and tough as cable, grow not in soil but in the seams where concrete meets rock, their stems leaning persistently toward a light that is not there. A pad of moss, pale grey and felted, spreads thin as skin on a wall. A fern, or something fern-like, folds its fronds away from your fingers with a soft, tired sigh, as if it were listening rather than seeing. That is the way of them. They grow toward warmth, toward seepage, toward the hum in the pipes and the faint electrical field that lifts the hair on your forearms. They take minerals from the water, heat from the metal, a slow current from the air, and they do it slowly, a stalk adding perhaps a ring in a decade. And they do it in copies. A plant will grow in a perfect duplicate of its neighbor, stem for stem, mirror reversed, as if made by a machine that learned to copy badly. In the reeds, a clump grows in a spiral, and two clumps later the same spiral, reversed, patiently working its way up out of the black water.

The buildings are inhabited in the same layered way.

In the shed two valleys over, with its corrugation rusted through, animals nest in warm, wet straw that was never straw, pressed close in the dark where the air is warmer still. On the leaning watchtower, its ladder broken halfway up, something large and slow occupies the top. It does not move away when you approach. It only breathes, a long warm exhalation you feel on your face in the damp, and stays. In the pump-house a great wheel turns — one revolution perhaps a day, perhaps a week — its paddles lifting black water and setting it down again on the other side, and the animals that live on its rim ride that slow lift, unconcerned, their bodies beaded with moisture. A machine here is not separate from habitat. A machine is a warm thing that moves, very slowly, and everything that can use warmth uses it. Pipes within pipes. Nests within nests. Bubbles rising off the warm metal into water that is already air.

They live, mostly, by touch and by heat, because those are the true senses here. They have no use for eyes, and those that have eyes have enormous, useless ones, overshadowed by skin that feels temperature a moment before anything else. The air is too full to hold any more moisture, and at night — there is no night, exactly — everything is wet: lashes, sleeves, the backs of animals, their own backs, yours. It rains sideways off metal, off rock, off their skin, in a slow weeping that never quite starts. They are adapted to being saturated. The quiet is not empty; it is saturated, the sound a crowded place makes when everything in it has learned to move slowly and to make almost no noise at all.

And they repeat.

That is the wrongness, and it lives in them as plainly as in the water. You brush past a hand-frog — soft, blind, translucent — and feel it again a few paces on, identical, its skin at the same temperature, its breathing at the same rate, as if the same individual had been copied forward along your path. You count six warm bodies along the rim of a mud hollow, and when you come back there are seven, and they are all the same size, and none of them remembers being new. A plant you touched with its fronds folded away will be there again, mirrored, on the other side of the causeway. A warmth reflected back at your palm arrives a moment late, as if your own heat had been kept and returned. They do not seem to mind this. Or perhaps to them, this is simply what living looks like when it is done slowly, in the dark, over a very long time: to be present in two places at once, to arrive late on purpose, to be copied, reversed, and copied again, and to go on with the cycle regardless.

For they do go on. Blind fish spawn in the warm pipes. Amphibians mate in the shallow black water. Slugs climb their curtains. Crabs walk the conveyor. The reeds lean. The large thing on the tower breathes. The wheel makes its next turn, lifting water from a lower blackness to a higher blackness, for no product anyone can name, and the animals ride it, and the mud under a boot-print rebuilds itself, and then slowly forgets, and then slowly remembers again.

They are not many kinds, in the way the surface world counts kinds. They are many instances, layered and warm and blind, clinging and flowing and gathering, making a density under every step. That is how you know the place is inhabited — not by a call or a track or a light in a window, but by the fact that you cannot move without displacing something, and that something, displaced, will in time come back to exactly where you were, faintly out of time, and settle again.
