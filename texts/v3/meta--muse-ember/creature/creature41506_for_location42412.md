If anything lives here, it does not at first announce itself by moving.

You look, out of habit, for the usual signs: something crossing the floor on its own, something turning toward the light, something that lifts, settles, startles. There is nothing at the high slits. The wind outside never gets in, so nothing rides it. The shelter of the cabinets and housings is empty of nests, webs, droppings, tracks. The cold and the dryness and the pressure of noise have drawn a line and nothing with eyes or wings or a pulse has been allowed to cross it to finish a life.

What remains is quieter than that, and smaller.

In the terraces, sunk flush so they do not interrupt the engineered ground — one planter here, one thirty metres away, another farther still, out where your voice would not carry — there are the tufts.

They are not much to see at a distance. Gray-green, stiff, no higher than your ankle, they read as a smudge on the pale pads, as a shadow that has been painted in and left. Up close they hold their shape. Each mat is the same radius. Each rosette repeats the same arrangement of leaves, leaf over leaf in a flat, patient star. They are clones, not a population in any wild sense. If you measured one against another you would find the same count, the same curl at the tip, the same dull waxy dullness that keeps the stripped air from taking them apart.

They were bred for this exact room. For constant white-yellow light that never moves and never warms. For air that is dry in the mineral sense, recycled and old, with nothing to drink in it. For a floor that never stops shaking under them at a pitch that would deafen anything that listened.

They do not drink. They feed, in the way the description was engineered into them, on light and on mineral vapor alone. There is no water to carry anything in or out. No puddle ever forms and no leaf ever browns at the edge from thirst, because thirst has been designed out of them as thoroughly as rust has been designed out of the steel. They do not flower. They do not shed. They do not turn toward anything because there is nothing to turn toward that changes. They do not grow, either. Year after year, visit after visit, the mat is the size it was made.

The only motion they ever make is not theirs.

Beneath the vibration — the constant, deafening, flat pressure of noise that lives in your sternum and never rises or falls — the leaves tremble in place. It is not a sway. The air inside never learns to move, the dust in the grooves never drifts, so nothing here is ever brushed by a draught. The shiver is borrowed entirely from the floor plates. The plants are still, and the world shakes them just enough to prove the world is still running.

That is their whole life, insofar as it can be called one: to remain at peak condition, unchanging, soft only by comparison with the razor edges around them. A well-made model of softness. Sparse, solitary, spaced at measured intervals like punctuation across a very long, very empty sentence. They do not spread. They do not seed. They do not die. Having been selected to do this perfectly, they have stopped doing anything else.

Around them, in the rest of the emptiness, are the un-living, and they too have taken up territories the way animals would, if animals were allowed.

A fan on the far ridge spins with a blur that is always the same blur. It does not speed up when the engines are at load, it does not slow when a hatch is left open, because load and openness are not variables here. It is an eye that never blinks.

On the far terrace a conveyor carries a single, finished engine housing in a perfect loop. It arrives at the same stop in the same position, makes the same faint click, and turns again. It is pacing, but its circuit is so complete it never arrives anywhere new.

An overhead crane is parked mid-span, its hook hanging still. It holds its breath forever. The switchgear cabinets stand like cliffs, silent and sealed. The stored turbine housings are boulders that will never roll. None of them are crowded together the way a working floor would crowd them. Each is set apart, exhibited, left at its most efficient instant and held there.

They do not eat, but they hum. They do not breathe, but the intake labyrinth around the chamber breathes for them, drawing the outside wind down and across tuned resonators so the roar never breaks. They do not reproduce, but the conveyor reproduces the same moment forever.

Together, the tufts and the machines make a kind of false ecosystem: green things that do not grow, and metal things that do not work, sharing a huge, cold, dry hall that is made to look like a landscape, and held, needle by needle of frost on the glass, at the exact age it was commissioned.

That is who lives there. Not many. Not varied. One species of stiff, gray-green mat, copied and spaced and left to tremble under a noise that never changes, among machines that circle and spin and wait in perfect, unmoving suspension.

They will look the same when you come back through the hatch, and the same when no one is there to see them at all.
