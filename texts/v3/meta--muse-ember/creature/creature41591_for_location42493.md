If you ask who lives here, the plain answers first with the hiss. No one lives here on purpose. No burrow holds, no nest persists, nothing makes a sound on purpose, and there is nothing with a light or a smoke or a road to mark a home. A footprint fills. A gravel bar builds itself centimeter by centimeter, trembling, and then slumps sideways in a sigh. Whatever lives here must live without staying.

## The Incidents

Once in a long while you see one, and what you see is never the same twice.

A blind, eel-pale thing knifing through a shallow channel, gone before you are sure it moved. It is longer than your forearm and thinner than the trickle it travels in. It has no eyes, no pigment, only a translucency that catches the pearl light from below and turns it a pale, interrupted grey. It holds its course only while the channel holds its course, which is a few seconds at most. Then the trickle braids away from it, finds a new line between pebbles, and it is left stranded in a film too thin for its body, and it folds back into the film.

Or a cluster of small, clicking burrowers in a pocket of wet sand where the water has been still for maybe a minute. Their backs are armor plates of polished fleck, each plate catching that brief iridescent green-brown before the light swallows it. They vanish by burrowing in two directions at once. Not metaphorically. One half of the cluster drives down and left while the other drives down and right, and the two disappear into a place that was packed a moment before and is now, impossibly, sand again.

Or something with too many legs under a slab, quick as a thought. You lift — or the water lifts — a half-buried stone, and for an instant the underside is crowded with fine, beaded limbs, all gripping, all letting go at once. They are not legs for walking. They are too many, too uncoordinated, and they vanish when the slab settles back, because the slab was only ever resting on them.

They are not a population. They are incidents. The plain does not support a world. It tolerates visitors who have learned to be mostly water themselves. And yet there is no outside for a visitor to come from. No horizon, no beyond the mist where a true elsewhere could send them. The weather is manufactured inside the plain, born in one quarter and dying in another. The incidents must be manufactured inside it too.

## The Hangers-On

The green, such as it is, hangs on in the same way.

A grey-green thread of algae stretches a meter or two in a backwater that has held still long enough to be noticed. A clump of pale reeds — hollow-stemmed, jointed, colorless as bone — stands in a small hollow, every stem humming faintly in the wind, every root clinging to nothing more substantial than a film of mud over stone. A lichen crusts a slab, soft as felt. A moss hangs, not from a tree, but from the lip of a crack, a curtain of tiny damp fronds.

None of it spreads. Roots cannot find purchase in a ground that floods uphill for a moment, boils, re-forms. Light is too dim for anything ambitious. The water carries iron, and salt after a long stillness, and the slow leach of stone. Plants that can live here are plants that can detach and start again, and so most of what you see is in the act of starting again.

They are not much, and they are everything the plain has for a beginning. They make the film on the stones. They leave the iron behind when they tear loose. They are food, not in the way of grazing, but in the way of passing over: a body that drags across an algae thread takes some of it, and is tinted afterwards.

## The Betweeners

Stay long enough — hours, which here feel both longer and shorter than hours — and your breathing syncs a little with the hiss, and the packedness stops looking passive.

Pebble on pebble on pebble, overlapping like scales. It is saturated, occupied, no room left between presences. Scrape with your hand and a thin film of water wells up instantly. You have not dug a hole; you have disturbed a surface. The water table is the surface. The plain is a skin, and the skin is weeping.

That weeping is a breathing. Air is warm, body-heat warm, so full that water condenses on everything, gathers in beads on the upturned faces of the gravel, runs in threads down the sides, drips perpetually onto the stones below. Evaporation from the open water, condensation on the stone, the two exchanging places so quickly the plain seems to breathe in place.

Something lives in that exchange. Not in the water. Not on the stone. Between them, in the film.

Call them — if you need a word where they have none — the Betweeners.

Each one is about the width of two pebbles and about the thickness of the film itself. Wedged between two real stones, its upturned face is exposed to the air and its underside is pressed to the silt, and from a pace away it is literally indistinguishable from the gravel it sleeps in. Wet ash in color. Polished flat on the side that faces the water, because that is the side that works.

It has no legs, ordinarily. No eyes. No mouth you could find. It has one pore, on its upper face, whose lips soften and open and heal like the lips of a crack. When a bead forms above it, the pore opens a fraction and takes the bead in. When the air thickens and evaporation rises, the pore opens again and lets the water out, as mist. Inhaling condensation, exhaling evaporation. That is its whole metabolism of breath, and it is the plain's macro-breath made small, a million times over.

It feeds through the same pore, and through its skin. It sits, face up, and lets the film of iron and algae and bacterial slime that settles on every stone during a stillness run down into it. It digests slowly, by dissolving. After a meal of algae it flushes, briefly, that iridescent green-brown. After a meal of iron it leaves the stone behind it darker and colder for a little while — the darker channel that remains after the water has left it, as if remembering. That darkness is not a trail, exactly. It is a place where the film has been grazed clean.

It moves by letting itself be moved. A trickle alters its course every few seconds, finds a new channel, abandons the old one. The trickle turns the Betweeners over with it. Most do not resist. One side swells with water taken up, tightens, then releases, and in releasing it pushes its neighbor stone a fraction. That fraction is traveled through the packedness as pressure. A kilometer away, a crack opens and you feel it in your sternum, subsonic. Some of that pressure is the plain settling. Some of it is this.

When enough of them swell together, the gravel bar builds. Centimeter by centimeter, all its stones trembling. That trembling is not only water. It is hundreds of them, coordinated by nothing but sharing the same bead cycle, pushing in the same direction because the same trickle has just turned over them all. And when the bar slumps sideways in a sigh, they slump with it, dissolving into the general wet, each one re-wedging itself between other stones in its new configuration, which is recognizably the old configuration with a slight variation. Time here is broken into moments that repeat with slight variation. So is their colony.

They reproduce the way the plain reproduces. A mud dome boils up in a slow, silent exhales where they are packed, and subsides, and in the boiling they are pinched in two. Or a crack splits the gravel in a branching pattern through a colony, fills, seals, and the seal cuts a single Between-er into two, each half closing its new pore and beginning again, each remembering the other as an echo in pressure. Kingdoms are not founded. Configurations are repeated.

That repetition is why, walking a straight line, you will after a while see the same configuration of stones on the horizon in three directions. Turn around; it is behind you too. You are not seeing the same colony three times. You are seeing three colonies that have restarted the same pattern, in mist-walled sectors that fold back on themselves, the way storms hatch overhead, mature, and die of their own density, depositing their water on ground that was already wet. North will not hold still under your feet because their colonies orient not by north but by the direction the trickle is running this second, and the trickle will not hold still either.

And they disperse the way the plain disperses. When the local weather decides to commit, rain falls and then, in one sector, begins to fall upward, beading on the undersides of stones, climbing back into the air as mist. That is their migration. A juvenile film, thin enough to ride a bead on the underside of a stone, climbs, lets go in another quarter, and lands as a fresh start on a stone that has never known it. Born in one quarter, circling, dying in another. They are weather made slow and flesh made weather.

This is how the incidents resolve.

When a channel deepens for a few seconds and runs straight, a whole packed group can unfold into the water column and knit themselves into one long, eel-pale swimmer, blind and translucent, feeding as it goes on films and on younger Betweeners. It is not a different species. It is the colony in its swimming phase, holding a single shape only as long as the water holds a single shape. When the channel bends, it gives up the shape and folds back.

When water shallows into a still pocket of sand, they armor. They take up flecks of polished stone and silt and hold them to their backs with mud, plate by plate, until a cluster clicks against itself as it burrows — in two directions at once, because the cluster, pinched by a crack, is dividing even as it escapes.

When a slab covers them, they branch. The too-many-legs under the stone are fine, beaded filaments they exhale from their pores to grip the underside where rain is beading upward, holding place against a trickle trying to turn them over. Quick as a thought, because the slab will settle again in a breath.

Eel, armored clicker, many-legged gripper: not visitors. Phases. The plain does not need them to be separate. It needs them to be changeable, because the plain is changeable and stable only as a waterfall is stable.

## Their Works

What you took for artifacts is their architecture, which is to say, their habit of packing.

The line of ankle-high stones, one every two paces, running unnaturally straight for four hundred meters, then stopping, then resuming a little to the left: a highway they have smoothed by repeatedly swelling and settling in a line, packing the gravel under their bodies into a firmer course for trickles to follow. Water takes the middle section away. They do not rebuild it the same. They resume a little to the left, with variation.

The shallow, square pit, its corners still faintly right-angled, filled to the brim with clear water that reflects a sky different from the one above you: a nursery. A place where they have cleared the pebbles enough that the film can thicken to a depth where a juvenile can open its pore to the brighter light — light reflected from below. The brighter sky with edges in the puddle is not a sky. It is the underside of the film, lit from below, where they can see. Your own figure standing a little to the side of where you are in that reflection is them, also in the film, also standing to the side, bending the light.

The mound, stepped on one side, melted on the other, wide as a house: a colony mound. Many of them pushing pebbles up the same gentle slope, the stepped side the side they can climb against the trickle, the melted side the side the trickle takes every few seconds. It sits for a minute in the dim light and collapses back into slurry, and later, recognizably, rises again.

The single upright slab, pierced near the top with a hole worn smooth: an old slab they have kept worn as a door. Water and their beaded grip have worried the hole until it is smooth. Nothing passes through it on purpose. Everything passes through it by accident, which is the only way anything moves here.

There is nothing here with a tool. These are not tools. They are the faint suggestions left when a packed, living gravel has finished a shape and let the plain take it back.

They have predators only in the sense that the plain is their predator. Turbulence tears algae, un-packs colonies, floods their pores with iron-heavy water, strands them in films too thin to breathe. They die by dissolving back into the general wet. But they do not vanish. The darkened, cold channel remembers their shape, and the next trickle that finds that channel — and the plain rehearses channels exactly where they were — fills it again, and they resume.

If you put your foot down, you displace a dozen. You come away cold, though the air is warm, because your hand has touched their breath. Your footprint fills not with water but with them, re-packing themselves into the depression, healing the crack your weight opened, their lips softening over it.

They are older than the water that polishes them and younger than the moment they were last turned over. They make no sound on purpose; the clack of pebbles in the wind is their bodies clicking as they swell, and the hiss of condensation forming on a million stone faces is, in part, a million small pores opening and closing. You hear the plain before you understand it because they are the plain, heard at the scale of a film.

Stand here long enough and you will stop trying to count them. You cannot get lost by distance because distance misbehaves. You cannot get lost by direction because north will not hold. You can only keep walking, and keep hearing the drip-field, and see the surface, which is enough, which is endless: pebble, water, pebble, water, a Between-er taking in a bead, letting it go, being turned over by a trickle that will braid away in a few seconds, being remembered by a darker channel, being eaten by its own swimming phase, being carried upward in rain that has decided to fall the other way.

They have no need of you to witness it. The plain, vast, warm, dim, ancient, packed, goes on doing it — creating and destroying its own small forms, weeping and absorbing — and they go on with it, between stone and air, between sound and cause, between the brighter sky in the puddle and the pearl gloom above it, in the thinning where land and mist and reflection give up distinguishing themselves, and the wet flat continues living, under a sky that sweats back.
