At first there is only the crust, and it is not one thing.

Kneel, and press your palm flat — not to feel stone, but to feel the pattern of its resistance change from inch to inch. In some places the surface is smooth and hard, a coin of itself. In others it yields, almost imperceptibly, as if something under the surface has shifted its weight. That is the first truth of the inhabitants: they are not *on* the plateau the way a deer is on a plain. They are *of* it. Their bodies are made to the same tolerances as the pits they live in. To look for an individual, at first, is to miss the point. What lives here lives as a covering.

It resolves, slowly, into layers. Not strata in the rock, but strata of life, three and four deep in places, near-touching, neighbor pressed to neighbor with scarcely a grain of bare stone between them. And the layers do not divide by size so much as by habit, by where they choose to stand in relation to the damp.

On the open swells, where sun polished the stone cream and rust, move the grazers. They are the ones you notice first because they move in the open. Flattened, the size of a coin, fringed all around with a pale, almost translucent border that trembles as they go. They have no legs you would recognize, no mouth that opens. They advance by a slow rippling of the fringe, a body-length in a day if the surface is kind, less if it is not. They feed not by biting but by absorbing — they pass over the biofilm that darkens the stone and leave it paler behind them, as if they have wiped a table clean with their own undersides.

Where they have fed, the plants that follow find dust. A rosette of grey-green succulence tucked into a seam, a lichen rimming a water cup, a wiry clump of thread-thin stems — all of these root, if root is the word, along the faint, cleaned paths the grazers have made over and over. The grazer does not know it is gardening. It simply does not eat the lichen, and the lichen does not mind the grazer's passage. Over a season, which here is mostly a pause, the two histories braid together until you cannot tell which prepared the ground for the other.

In the hollows, where the stone is pitted into honeycomb, the community thickens. Here the inhabitants live head-down. Only their domed backs show, overlapping like shingles on a roof, ochre and dove-grey, so perfectly matched to the stone that at a step back they vanish entirely. Each dome is a sealed house. Beneath it, in the pore, the animal sits inverted, breathing through its skin against the damp wall of the cup, sipping not the standing water — there is a thumbnail of water in some cups, held for weeks, reflecting the sky — but the wetness of the air in the pore itself. They do not drink the pocket dry. That would be a kind of death for all of them. They absorb, they sip, they mostly wait in the humidity that collects at night when the warm rock gives up its heat and the air near it sweats in tiny beads.

At dawn, as I have said, the plateau exhales. It is these shingled thousands, all opening at once as the sun touches them, releasing a vapor so faint you would call it nothing if your boots were not suddenly damp from pressing your weight into stone that is dry-skinned an inch down. In the evening the vapor is drawn back in. The stone ticks as it cools. A shell, somewhere under your hand, clicks shut.

Under the hand-sized overhangs — no larger than your palm, where the pitted stone has lifted a fraction and left a shadow — hang other kinds. Translucent, amber, the size of a large seed. They do not crawl. They anchor by a stalk and hang, and through the shell you can see, if you stay long enough and your eyes adjust to the slowness, a slow pulse. A heartbeat that takes a long time to complete even one beat. They taste the air chemically, as all of them do; a faint change in the mineral damp, a trail left by a passing grazer an hour ago, is a message to them, and they answer it by changing the rate of that pulse, by tightening their grip, by letting go a little more condensation onto the underside of the ledge. There is no voice here. A signal is a touch, or a scent laid down on stone and left to fade or be renewed by the next body passing over it.

And inside the rock itself, in channels that run for meters beneath the surface, blind and thread-like and wet, move the ones who never come fully out. You see them only as a gleam in a tunnel, a thread turning in the damp, gone again. They have no eyes and need none; the rock is continuous to them, a dark country of pores connecting pore to pore. They are the ones who open the stone from within. They widen a channel by the patient pressure of their bodies, they deposit mineral as they feed on the films that grow in the dark, they seal it again behind them with a lining of their own substance. What long ago was weather doing — rain that is only a rumor, sun that polishes, wind that finds the weak grain — is now done by them, grain by patient grain. The erosion continues, but it has been taken over. The rock and the life are dissolving each other in the most courteous possible way.

Among all these the large ones move. Barrel-bodied, armored, no higher than a knee, their skin perpetually damp to the touch, they advance a few meters in a week and seem not to advance at all if you are only there for a day. Their armor is not one shell but a mosaic, and in the folds between the plates live, permanently, a small crowd of the shingled pore-dwellers and the coin-grazers, transported, fed, sheltered. The large ones are not predators. Nothing here is. They are slow landscapes for the small. When one seals itself for a dry spell — and dry spells here last longer than any observer can comfortably imagine — it draws its fringe inward, closes its breathing pores, and waits under its own bulk with its passengers waiting inside it. It can outwait a season. It can outwait several. Then, when condensation gathers again in the deep cups, it opens, it dampens, it resumes its week-long journey of a few meters.

Birth here is not an event. It is an accretion. A grazer does not disperse its young. It feeds, it divides where it stands, and the young live on top of the old, or just beside them, until the layer is thicker rather than wider. The shingled ones add a dome to the edge of the shingle. The hangers leave a daughter hanging near the mother, their stalks knotting together under the ledge. The threads extend a new branch into an unopened pore. There are no migrations, no rush after rain, because rain is not enough to invite a rush and there is nowhere empty to rush to. A cleared place — a disc where the crust has been worn smoother over a hundred years — is not recolonized by a charge. It is recolonized neighbor by neighbor, body-length by body-length, until the paleness is gone and the pattern is restored.

This is why the artifacts are so faint. Now and then you come on a low arc of larger pebbles, half-buried. A ring of stacked slabs no higher than your ankle, collapsed on one side. A small cone of pellets, a cairn of old shells. No one built them at once. The barrelbacks, in the course of moving a few meters in a week, nudge a pebble with their shoulder. Their passengers, over generations, die in place on the high side of a ridge and their shells are moved by neighboring grazers, a millimeter at a time, toward the low side. The threads, emerging, push up a little dust. Century upon century, the nudges accumulate into an arc, a ring, a cone — things that look almost made, and almost natural, because they are both. Nothing cut cleanly. No tool's edge. No smoke, no track. And then erosion, which is now them, begins to unmake them at the same rate, returning midden to monument to stone again.

They breathe through skin and shell. They have no lungs to voice with, no wings to beat the air, and so the silence over the most densely inhabited ground you will ever stand on is thick, not empty. You hear your own footsteps too loud on the crust. You hear, very rarely, a click. Otherwise you hear only the faint ticking of cooling stone, which is also, in a way, them settling their houses for the night.

Their colors have agreed, over an immense time, to match the stone that holds them — ochre, ash, translucent amber, the dull green of the few plants — because visibility here is a liability. To be unnoticed is to survive. The rosette looks like part of the rock's pattern. The lichen rim looks like a stain. The dome looks like a knoll. The advantage is not speed or teeth. It is saturation. Nothing invades, because nothing is empty. A foreign spore, a wind-blown seed, lands and finds no pore unoccupied, no damp unclaimed, no trail unmarked by a scent that says: this is already lived in, thoroughly.

When one dies in place — and most do — its shell does not rot away into nothing. It becomes a shelter for the next grazer. Then, as the thread-like ones work beneath it, it becomes mineral. Then it becomes part of the path that a plant will follow for its one flowering in several years, before it resumes doing almost nothing, which is the most successful thing to do here.

Stand there long enough, warm and high, damp in the pores under your feet, and the scale reverses. You stop feeling like an observer of a landscape and start feeling like a temporary, clumsy presence on the skin of a community that has finished its long experiment and settled into the form it will keep. Slow, silent, layered, touching everywhere, holding together not by balance exactly but by contact — grain by grain, pore by pore, body by patient body, for longer than any of them, or you, can measure.
