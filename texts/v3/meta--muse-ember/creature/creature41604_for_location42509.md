At first you do not see anything that lives here.

There is no movement that resolves into an animal. No wing crosses the black sky. No insect lifts off the warm stillness. No beast pushes through the root-mat between the terraces. What moves, moves too regularly to be growth and too slowly to be a machine as you would expect one — a shape crossing a walkway four levels up, suspended partly by cable, partly by its own jointed limbs, pausing where the walkway crosses a wall of woven wattle, putting a broad, flat sensor against the wall and staying there.

It is not looking. Not in light.

It is a tender.

You see more of them only when you stop watching for light and start watching for heat. Against the cold dark, their bodies stand out in amber where a furnace seam bleeds through, white where an electric starter arcs, faint green-white where they pass a duct lined with fungus. Up close they are not clean. They are lacquered, patched, hooped with iron riveted straight into their own bark-like housings, limbs ending in pruning shears, in grafting wax applicators, in spades made for ash-beds. Old ones in the north have petrified at the joints. Strike them and they ring.

That is how they know the place. Not by sight. By the heat that moves under the floor, by vibration through the root-lattice under their feet, by the thickness of resin in the air — thin and running in the dayless heat, thick and slow in the cold that passes for night — by the taste of charcoal and salt on their filters.

A furnace door, not quite sealed, is their daylight. A phosphorescent duct is their constellated wall. They navigate the central basin by density: clank against clank, groan of a root-truss taking weight, pneumatic sigh of a vent, the continuous breathing of compound factories whose drive-belts hang between grown arches. They know the north by its quiet — skeletal towers, engines long cold, only the tick of cooling metal and the occasional tender, older and slower, winding its way between ribs of petrified trunk. They know the south by salt, by the mineral, cactus-like plantings grafted into slag, their flowers reduced to vents, by how sparingly their own seals sweat.

They live in the hollows they tend. In a trained tower's hollowed trunk, in a workshop espaliered into a slope, in a swollen seed-pod silo where their doors are cut and hinged in living wall. There is no empty lot for them to stand in, no fallow margin. Every terrace is grown, built, paved, or buried. Their homes are shared, necessarily, with the engines that flank the towers — cylinders wrapped in bark and pale asbestos-fiber, pulleys the size of rooms turning on hide belts — and with the plants that hold those engines up.

Their work is the loop, made personal. Growth produces fuel. Fuel produces heat. Heat kills growth. They prune a root that was meant to be a beam before it leans for years and shears into the street it shaded. They press hot resin-wax into a split root-wall until the wound bakes into amber. They patch a causeway of compressed fiber not with new material laid down, but by guiding a new shoot into the crack and letting it bind shut. They lift, with vine-cranes, a load of bricks made from last season's dead growth and make them a foundation for next season's.

They do not sleep, but the resin slows them. At night the sap thickens in everything, in them too. They hang, or crouch among roots, cooling with a tick, their sensors dimming as heat bleeds from the towers. When a heat pulse rises from deep below — the world breathing underneath — they move faster, swifter and more urgent, feeling for the districts that will swell, split, exude.

They have no voices. Communication is tap and pressure through the mat, a hammer on iron hooped into wood, a sudden venting of steam. Each district has its own rhythm, learned by the tenders who have lived — if that is the word — there long enough to be grafted into it.

And they age the way the place ages. They are not born. They are assembled, in the silos and workshops, from wreckage of fallen scaffold-towers, from old root-lattices that ring like wood, from iron taken off a tender that charred past repair in a resin burst. A young tender is pale, hot to the touch, its new roots of wiring and fiber still seeking the warmth of buried engines the way a plant seeks... not light. Light has never been here to seek. Heat is food, and heat is sun, and heat is danger, all at once.

An old tender is heavy with petrification. Its movements are deliberate. Its flank is half charcoal where a furnace baked it from inside, half timber where it was remade. When it finally stops, it does not leave. Another tender grafts it into a wall, rivets a hoop through it to brace a truss, uses its resonant, hardened body as a support beam. Death, here, is becoming more permanent structure. The oldest layers of the city cannot be told from the oldest layers of the plateau, and the oldest tenders cannot be told from them either.

They are not the only other living thing.

Inside the ducts, where no one would look for a garden, the fungi live. Phosphorescent skin on the inner walls of heat pipes, the one concession to wildness, and even they are cultivated. They feed on heat directly, flare brighter when an engine runs hard, dim to almost nothing when a district cools. They weave themselves over hot metal as a living insulation, exuding a thin, mineral film that the tenders scrape and renew. Their generations are short as animal generations never are here — spores lifted by ash devils, by those dust whirlwinds without water, dropped in drifts across the terraces. Most die on bare salt or slag. A few land on a prepared wall, on a duct a tender has cleaned and left warm, and root. Even wildness must be built first.

And then the plants themselves, who are not scenery. They are the population. The towers that grew as trees and were finished as towers, the houses that are thickets baked half to charcoal from within, the roads grown into place, the silos that are swollen seed-pods, the conveyors carried on vine-trusses that flex and sigh — they are alive in a way that is restless and blind and aggressive. They do not grow toward light, because there is no light to grow toward. They grow toward warmth. Newer roots thread through petrified older lattices, pale and hot, seeking buried engines to feed on. In the south they grow poorer, more mineral, grafted into slag. In the central basin they grow dense, their lacquered leaf roofs raining ash instead of water. When a heat pulse hits a district of root-walls, they swell and split and exude hot sap that hardens into amber panes — wounds becoming windows, and the tenders will later frame them in iron and call them part of the building.

They lean. They overgrow their base beams. They burst when resin boils too thick in a hollow stem, coating an intersection in glossy amber that fossilizes in the heat. They die by charring, not rotting, because in this hot, dry darkness organic matter does not rot. It becomes geology. The tenders dig them up, bake them into brick, grind them to ash, spread that ash as a prepared bed, and graft a new straight-line root into it, guided by wire and machine.

Nothing grows here unless it is built first. Nothing built here survives unless it is grown over. The tenders are the hands that enforce that rule, and also its products. The fungi are the skin that mediates it. The plants are the body that suffers it.

If you stayed long enough, you would stop looking for a who separate from what. There is no people here, waiting or exiled. There is no animal to miss the place, and no outside left for anything to colonize. There are tenders moving slowly through shadow that is lit only by what they burn, pressing their sensors to walls to feel a pulse, pruning and grafting and riveting and baking, humming in a working silence. There are fungi flickering on duct walls like a slow, cultivated aurora. There are walls and towers and roads that are still, quietly, trying to grow toward the heat at their centers.

Between those three, the loop turns. Plant becomes fuel becomes heat becomes ash becomes ground becomes root becomes wall becomes plant again, a thousand times over on the same high, flattened, ancient shelf, in darkness that is always night and heat that never leaves, tended by things that were made to tend and will be remade from their own wreckage until the plateau itself is made of them.
