If you stand waist-deep in the warm water long enough for your eyes to adjust, the first thing you learn about what lives here is what does not move like an animal should. No fish turn under the clear surface. No insects lift off the leaves. Nothing crosses the small, walled sky. The silence is not emptiness — it is crowded, lush, and almost still — and the life, when you begin to see it, is not in the animals you are waiting for. It is in the green, and in the machines the green has learned to cover.

Start under the water, where you can see them best. The submerged meadows are feathery and patient, rooted not in soil but in black ceramic granules in plastic trays, in pipe-joints, in the seams of concrete decking, in gutters bolted to walls. They wave only when the pumps give their low thud, once a minute or so, and the current carries them and then lets them settle. Their fronds are crisp at the edges, too crisp. Leaves are not chewed or yellowed or fed upon. By the hundreds they lean, all of them, toward the plain white light above the surface, and toward the small white work-lights wired under the decks, as if both were the same sun. Pale new shoots push up beside dark mature leaves, in the same tray, from the same manufactured crevice, and grow without being eaten, without being thinned, without any animal appetite to check them. So they thicken.

At the surface the mats drift. Broad paddle-leaves, thick and waxy, press up against the sides of tanks and against each other and barely move. They rot very slowly, and their slow rot is food — the nutrient water itself is thickened by it, which is why the ripples die a few inches from where they begin, as if the water were sweetened. There is a skin of life on everything the water touches: a green film on the submerged catwalks, a pale fuzz on the steel of the tanks, root-hairs and spores suspended and turning in the warm, body-warm current, carried uphill in pipes to planters on the highest terraces and rained down again in a slow artificial trickle.

Up out of the water, on every terrace, is the excess. In planter-boxes bolted to glazed tile, on tank lids that have become garden beds, in gutters that have become planters, in cable-festoons that have rooted themselves below, the growth is dense from wall to wall. Fern-like fronds, coil-vines, things that lean and things that climb. The vines have taken the spire in spirals, winding round chimneys and greenhouse roofs, because there is no tree to climb and no rock to cling to — only the built thing. Their stems are sometimes rubber cable gone green at the surface. Their leaves sometimes arrive in pairs along a wire.

And then, among the true leaves, you begin to notice the ones that are not leaves. They are flat panels, veined like leaves, mounted where a leaf would want light, drinking light not to grow but to make power for the grid. They never wilt, never fold in the evening, never shed. They are indistinguishable from the growth around them until you step close and see the faint seam, the connector at the base, the way their edge catches the midday light differently. The Works raises them in vats. Vats lift a hundred thin panels at a pace that is almost stillness, and a press stamps root-cables to go with them, and an assembler bolts new plant-modules together and sets them in a tray. Over months the module roots. A spray of green splits a machine housing along its seam. A shoot finds the tray. And the thing that was installed stops looking manufactured at all.

Because the reverse is happening too, all around you. Some of the machines have begun to photosynthesize. Their housings — teal, safety-yellow, dull red, enamel still glossy and unchipped — sit shaded under the growth they were built to tend, and the growth feeds them back. Gear teeth stand exposed and oily, unworn, turning very slowly. A conveyor advances a new module once an hour. A motor hums under its casing, warm in the humid air that should be cold at this height but is kept warm by engines and stored water. Condensation drips from glass and steel in a steady, manufactured rain that is never quite weather. The same warm water is reservoir, coolant, nutrient solution, and mist, passing through root and pipe with no clear boundary between the two systems.

There are flowers, of a kind. A valve-like bloom opens on a vine that has grown around a pipe, over a season, and closes over the next. It does nothing that a flower should do — there is no pollinator here, no seed scattered by wind to a natural bank — but it opens anyway, because the module that produced it was built to bloom when the pressure cycle reaches a certain point. Root-cables pulse faintly with current where they cross the trays. Where a pale frond presses against a cold housing, there is a soft ticking brush, the only sound the life makes besides the machines.

That is how they live here: by being made, and by rooting. They are not planted in the earth. They are installed in crevices, and then they overgrow the installation. A tank lid becomes a leaf bed. A gutter becomes a planter. A machine that split becomes a trellis. The factory's product is its own overgrowth, and the overgrowth in turn runs the factory — shading the motors, cooling the water, making power from light, slowing the ripples to nothing. Hundreds of identical units, repeated terrace to terrace, packed wall to wall, electrified and mechanized at industrial scale, but living at a tempo slower than weather: water-level rising centimeters in a year, bubbles taking a long time to rise from a submerged valve, noon looking like noon from hour to hour through water and glass.

No predation, no grazing, no decay except that slow, rich rotting of the floating mats into the nutrient pool. Nothing dies quickly, and nothing is born except by the Works, one module an hour, one thud a minute. The sharp concrete edges have not yet softened. The bright welds have not yet dulled. The leaf edges are still as crisp as cut plastic because nothing has had time, or teeth, or mouths, to blunt them.

They fill the place completely, and they are the place: a high, warm, newly switched-on garden of plant and machine tangled together at the seams, green everywhere, silent except for water and leaf and motor, standing in its own shallow sea under ordinary light, growing over everything it was built from and, in growing, keeping the whole slow, sterile, luxuriant system alive.
