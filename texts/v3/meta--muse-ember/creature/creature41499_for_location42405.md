What lives here first is not an animal at all, and it does not live *on* the place so much as it *is* the place.

You stand on it before you see it clearly — a mat, hand-thick, springy, waterlogged, giving under a boot with a soft suck. In the pale stretches it is still unfinished, a bright green fringe only a few centimeters wide blistering up over clean, wet granite. Behind the fringe it thickens and darkens and sags. Ahead of it, there is only shining rock. The whole plain, several kilometers of it cupped between ridges, is in different stages of that advance.

The principal resident is sphagnum moss. Individual shoots are almost too small to count, each topped with a pale, star-like head of curled leaves, each leaf full of large clear hyaline cells that hold water twenty times the plant’s dry weight. It lives not as a single plant but as a colony that never really dies at the bottom. The top grows a millimeter or two a year toward the light; the bottom goes black, sour, and stops decaying properly. That is the quiet work of the place. Sphagnum makes its own water acid — pH dropping to four or less in the pools between the hummocks — and that acidity, and that wetness, excludes almost everything else. Bacteria slow. Leaves do not rot. A boot print fills with dark tea-colored water and stays there. Under two centimeters of water, then five, is more sphagnum, then peat that is barely peat yet, then stone.

It makes the only topography there is. The hummocks are sphagnum that has grown a little faster or a little higher to escape the water. Hollows between them hold still, icy water even at noon. A sedge root thrown across a channel is a bridge that can bear weight. There is no soil that is not made of dead tissue underneath living tissue.

Among the moss, thinly, are the other plants that can tolerate it. Flattened sedges stand in loose clumps, twenty meters apart in places, their roots knitted so tight into the mat that they hold the surface together where the moss alone would slump. Cotton-grass lifts itself a foot above the mat in summer, thousands of white heads shivering together in the wind and making that constant static you hear under the streams. Cushion plants press themselves flat to the stone, tight as a hand, to keep warm and out of the wind — saxifrage, moss campion forms, things that live for decades at a few centimeters high and flower all at once in a brief, disproportionate color. One species of moss dominates for ages, then one sedge, then one cushion plant. They do not crowd. The cold, the water rising around every root, and the altitude forbid crowding.

Ahead of the moss front, where the rock is still bare, other things live first. Crustose lichens paint the granite in dull orange and grey. Green and black algae make the wet slabs slick. Bacteria colonize the condensation on the orange steel of the huts and the pipes, building films that the sun bleaches by noon and the mist re-wets by evening. It is the same frontier, only microscopic: life laying down a little organic matter where there was none, so that moss can follow.

Below the living surface, in the part that never sees light, are the fungi and microbes that do the slow chemistry. Fungal hyphae thread the dark, wet mat, breaking down what little falls in, sometimes putting up a small, pale fruiting body after rain — rare, and gone in a day. Deeper still, where the peat is saturated and oxygen does not reach, methanogens and other anaerobes work without air and release tiny bubbles that rise through the tea water and pop at the surface. This is an ecosystem that preserves more than it decomposes. That is why it is building itself upward instead of wearing away.

The mat itself is inhabited at a scale you would miss if you were looking for birds. Springtails jump in the moss heads. Oribatid mites graze algae off sphagnum leaves. Nematodes and rotifers swim in the film of water held between leaves. In the pools: diatoms, desmids, water fleas drifting in water so cold they move slowly even in full sun. These are the only true crowds here, and they are invisible unless you kneel and look.

Animal life you can see with your eyes is rare to the point of absence, and most of it is passing through.

A ptarmigan nests on a hummock, its mottled brown so like the moss and stone that you walk past it. When it explodes up, it is startlingly loud for one second — wings, alarm call, gone — and then the wind and water are louder again. It raises chicks on the invertebrates in the mat. There are no other birds to make song, and nothing to sing over anyway.

Frogs come up from below the cloud in wet springs to spawn in the shallow pools. You hear them clicking more than you see them. The tadpoles develop slowly in water that never warms. Most years only a handful survive to hop back down the far slope.

Dragonflies are the only insects that can afford to be conspicuous. On the warmest afternoons, when the sun burns your face and the wind is momentarily still, a few patrol over the water, thermoregulating in the sun, dipping to lay eggs. Their larvae live a year or more in the pools, under the sphagnum, before the adult gets its few days.

Once in a long while, a sheep track comes up from down below, old hoofprints already refilling with water, moss creeping into the edges. No sheep stays. The forage is too thin, the ground too wet, the winter too hard.

And laid thinly over the top, almost to invisibility, are the other residents. They are not in charge, and they are not permanent, but they hum and cycle and sweat like living things.

Three low orange huts, half a kilometer apart, bleached on the south side. A generator shed whose floor has heaved because the moss has grown up under it and its door will not shut. A relay mast on the highest bench with guy-wires that sing in the wind. A weather station on a pole, solar panel tilted up, recording the white light and the cold. A small turbine that turns too slowly for the altitude, ticking around, never stopping, never fast. A pump in a basin half a valley away, cycling on and off with an industrial tone that carries too well across hard, wet, tree-less ground. A pipeline running in a straight line down the far slope, bright when it catches the light, beading with condensation long after any rain. A half-finished concrete retaining wall at a channel crossing, already being undermined by living roots. Rusted drums. Tire ruts that the bog takes back within a season.

People come to live in them briefly — a technician for two nights to swap a battery, to check a water level, to note that the pumps have dropped the level an inch and the sphagnum has already re-wet itself in a week. They zip their jackets against the wind even in sun. Their breath fogs at dawn. They leave, and the mist comes back into the hollows before dark.

Everything here keeps its own time. The moss grows. The turbine turns. The channels shift after a freeze. The peat-cutter parked at the edge of an experimental cut sits rusting very slowly while moss softens the black face of the cut and creeps back over it. The light goes from white to a slightly less white, and the wind, and the water spilling over bedrock with no banks, and the pump hum, continue without variation into the night.

Nothing about it is strange, piece by piece. But standing in the middle, on a plain made entirely of living ground, you understand the census finally: plants, fungi in the dark, microbes in the acid water, stone, and very thinly, machines and the occasional animal passing through. It has just started, it will take a very long time to finish, and it does not need any of the rest of us at all.
