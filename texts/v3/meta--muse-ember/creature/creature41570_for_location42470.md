If you stop walking, the sound does not stop with you. It gets louder, and with it the first answer you form about this place — that nothing lives here at all.

It is a reasonable answer, for anything that lives the way you understand living. Nothing green. No moss on the wet stone, no lichen crust, no biofilm in the clear pools. No tracks in the shallow sheet of water, which parts when you step and closes behind you without clouding. The water stays sterile and bright, as if the constant upwelling of dissolved rock keeps sweeping it clean. On the surface, in the open, this is a pre-life country, or a post-life one. Minoral only.

Wait long enough, though, and the answer begins to change its shape. Not because something comes out to meet you. Nothing does. But because the crowd you mistook at first for boulders refuses to be only stone once you have stood inside it.

The columns are everywhere, knee-high to above your head, shoulder to shoulder in places, leaning a degree or two in no particular direction, running in low ridges for hundreds of paces and then breaking and resuming along the fracture planes. They weep in a precise way. Water beads on their vertical faces and does not move until it is too heavy, then falls pillar by pillar, tube by tube, with a chime. Inside almost every one it stands at a level, dark and still. On some flat tops there is that tiny perched pool, round as a borrowed sky, with no inlet, creeping higher by a hair over a year.

An empty tube cannot keep a level like that, or tune the way these do. A drip strikes inside a hollow and rings, and the pitch is governed by how far the water has risen. As the water rises — a millimetre in a month, a centimetre in a decade — the note sinks. The whole basin is slowly tuning itself downwards, and you could stand here a day and hear the same pattern repeat without variation, and return in a year and find it a fraction lower. That is not geology behaving randomly. That is something being maintained.

Look close at a single column, as close as you can get without your shade falling across it. The fracture face is glassy, fresh, vesicles open. The water inside is not running. It is held. Outside, on the face, a bead climbs, imperceptibly, a fraction up against gravity, then slips and falls with a tick louder than it should be — louder because the hollow amplifies it, perhaps, but also because it falls onto something that is not quite empty.

Most of the pillars, you said to yourself at first, are empty. That was true to the eye. You looked down mouth after mouth. Dark water, dark stone, nothing. A forest of houses with no one home.

Then there was the ribbon.

Once, in a deeper pool between two columns, a pale translucent ribbon uncurled from the shadowed mouth of a tube, no thicker than a finger, blind and eyeless, and hung in the water filtering. It stayed perhaps a minute. Then it withdrew. In another pool a few paces on, rings expanded with no drip to cause them, as if something large and soft had turned over just beneath the surface and decided not to come up.

You did not see an animal. You saw a part of something, left out for a minute and then taken back in, as a tongue is taken back, as a hand is withdrawn. That is the only exterior you are likely to be given. The rest of it lives inside the stone's water, and only rarely shows itself at the mouth.

What would make a thing leave its tube for a minute? Not to hunt — there is nothing to hunt, nothing in the open water but its own reflection of the grey ceiling. Not to flee — nothing here is fast enough to chase it, nothing here hurries at all. It leaves to exchange. Inside the tube the water is its own, held and re-held. At the mouth it meets the shared sheet, the shallow sea that runs between the columns, the water that is constantly renewed with dissolved rock. The ribbon hangs there filtering that other water, tasting it with its whole surface perhaps, taking in what the upwelling brings, and then withdraws before the minute is up, not from fear but because the tube is its proper place. Its world is vertical and narrow and dark.

You begin to picture the interior, and the picture must be allowed to stay provisional. No eyes, because there is nothing to see inside a hollow column under a sourceless sky. Translucent, because it does not need opaque skin where the stone already stands guard. Soft enough to press against the inner wall and seal it, so that the water inside is partly outside it and partly itself. It does not walk. It does not need to. It filters, it grows, it holds its level.

And it builds, very slowly, the very thing it lives in.

A column is not a rock it found. It is a shell it has precipitated and mineralized around itself, a hair at a time upward, year after year. That is why some tops are snapped flat or jagged — growth stopped, or broke, or finished. That is why the tiny pool on top creeps higher: that is not a pool made by rain. That is the living tip, still wet, still soft, holding its water with surface tension above a head of stone. It is why the water inside rises as the note falls: the body filling its own tube displaces, grows, takes in more of itself, and the air above it in the hollow grows smaller, lower. A tone a fraction deeper each year is a sign of age, of fullness.

If that is true, then the forest is two things at once, and most of it is the second. It is a town and a graveyard in the same stones. The shells outlive the soft tenants by centuries, perhaps by millennia. A tube that looks empty to you may be a dead shell that still rings when a drip finds it, because dead stone rings just as well. Another may be young, uncolonized, waiting for a bead to carry something down a fracture and lodge it. Another may be occupied, but its tenant is so still, so perfectly matched to its own held water, that you will never see a ribbon if you stay only a day. The living ones in any one basin are not hundreds. They are sparse, solitary, far apart — one here, then nothing for a long stretch, then one again. That pattern you noticed in the built things is the pattern of the life itself.

The built things, then, are not houses for something else. Scattered thinly among the columns: a ring of upright slabs, waist high, incomplete, its interior clean of silt; a low wall of stacked stone twenty steps straight, then stopping for no reason you can see; a single squared block in the middle of a pool, a little taller than the others, its top pocked with shallow repeated marks.

They look like someone began something and, slowly, lost interest. Or like a species with no tools thought once, loosely, in stone, and did not think it again. If the tenant in the tube only rarely puts its ribbon out to filter, what might it do in a far rarer exterior phase — a phase a human stay will almost certainly never witness? Push. A body that presses against its own wall can press against a loose slab. Over a season that is a century, a slab can be nudged a finger's breadth. Over a life, a ring can be nudged into a circle, a wall into a straight line. The interior of the ring is clean because the tenant sits there in the sheet water filtering it, keeping its small clearing clear. The wall stops because the tenant that made it died in the middle of pushing, or because one push took longer than its life and there was no second tenant to finish the thought. The squared block, taller in its pool, pocked with repeated marks on top — that may be where it rubs. Where its mineralized tip drags again and again against the same stone, leaving its sign in the only material it has. Not writing. Not a monument. A repeated touch, remembered by basalt because basalt is the only memory this place keeps.

This is where the strangeness you noticed while standing still begins to belong to the tenant, even if it does not give up its last secret.

The beads that climb: if the ribbon, or the soft tip, travels outside the tube up a fracture, drawing water up with it to keep the upper growth moist, a bead would climb against gravity and then let go, and the hollow would make the tick louder than the bead deserves. The beads that simply hang and fall, pillar by pillar, are tenants drinking, or letting go, each on its own slow timer, so the field is a chorus of independent clocks.

The pool that does not ripple at once when a drip strikes it, the ring expanding a full second later as if the water is remembering: a drip strikes a surface that is not only water. Beneath it is a soft dome, a body under pressure, transmitting the blow down the tube to a tenant that tenses a second later — a reflex slowed to the speed of this country. The ring is not the drip. The ring is the answer.

The rings in the open pool with no drip at all: a tenant in a wide mouth under that pool, turning its weight, deciding not to come up. Or the wandering phase, if there is one — a larger, softer body loosening in the sheet, testing the water between tubes before it settles in a new one. It does not break the surface. It only displaces it.

And the perched pool on top of some columns, perfectly still while the air around it is full of falling drips, your own shade not falling across it at all: that one does not surrender. Perhaps because it is not a flat surface in the way a pool should be, but a convex living dome holding its film of water, bending the flat grey light around itself. Your shadow is diffuse in this sourceless light; it falls on the stone around. It does not fall on the tip because the tip is not stone, and not quite water, and it is maintaining itself against the air. You can put that down as an anomaly, or as the clearest sign that the top of a column is a mouth.

Reproduction, if this slow solitary life reproduces at all, must travel the way everything else here travels: through stone and water, not across land. The ridges break and resume along fractures, and a daughter may be nothing more than a fragment pinched from a ribbon, carried up a fracture in a climbing bead, lodged in a crack a pace away, beginning to hollow and mineralize a new tube beside the old. You would never see it happen. You would only return in a decade and find a new note in the chorus a little higher than the others, a water level a hair higher in a tube that was empty before, a ribbon filtering in a pool that held nothing but reflection last time.

That is what lives here: not a population moving through a landscape, but a landscape that lives in column form. Soft tenants inside stone tubes, blind, translucent, minute-long filterers at the mouth, holding their water, building their shells upward a hair a year, sinking their own tones as they fill and age. A few at a time per basin, most houses empty or dead, their shells still singing because they are wet. Now and then, in a phase almost too rare to call a phase, a tenant pushing a slab into a ring, a wall, a pocked block, and then dying with the work unfinished.

You will not see it live in a day. A human stay is a single held breath. You can only stand still, listen to the drips find the hollows, hear the field tuning itself down, and understand that the roar you heard before you saw the land was not wind alone, and not rain alone. It was the breath of everything in the forest, taken in and held in stone, released pillar by pillar, a tick at a time.
