At first it seems there is no one there at all.

That is the trick of the bowl. You drop through the slit in the black teeth and the plain disappears, the dripping starts up on every side of you, and in front of you is only water and mist and wet green, spaced far apart, as if someone has cleared a room and left it empty on purpose. It takes a while to understand that the emptiness is not absence. It is spacing. The inhabitants are not gathered. They are stationed.

The true residents are the ones you cannot see, and they outnumber everything else by orders of magnitude before you have even listened.

Under the surface of the central pool, in the springs where the water is forced up by pressure from below, live the iron-oxidizers. They are not animals, they have no faces, no movement you could track with your eye. They live in the cold, black, iron-rich water where it comes up through the cracks in young basalt, and they breathe what would kill everything else — dissolved iron, hydrogen sulfide, the heavy fizz of carbon dioxide pooling low over the pool.

In return, they build. Working in continuous, silent shifts, they lay down rust. Thick ochre mats spread across the shallows like felt. Hollow pipes and chimneys rise from the vent rims, armored in orange scale, growing a fraction even while you watch. When a mat grows too thick it collapses, sloughs off in black, slaggy rafts, sinks. They coat the sedge stems, the pebbles, the edges of the pool in their own manufactured waste. It is a smelter without fire: water in, gas out, metal down. They do not eat the bog so much as they process it, and the bog, in turn, is kept acid and oxygen-starved and exactly cold enough for them to keep working. Their work creates the only hard, permanent architecture in the place — not walls, but mineral crusts, sulfur rims, rust pipes — and everything else lives in the lee of that chemistry.

That chemistry sets the terms for the next inhabitants, the ones that hold still.

The sphagnum is not scenery. It is a slow animal, a colony animal. Carpet after carpet of it quilts the tussocks and the margins of the pool, deep and quaking underfoot, green below and rust-red where the flat light touches the tips. It drinks the condensation off the walls, it holds water like a sponge that never wrings out, it acidifies everything around it and keeps the peat from rotting too quickly. It filters the iron water as it rises, trapping particles, releasing the gases back upward in tiny, patient bubbles. Beside it, in tight bright spirals, clubmoss creeps. Sedges stand in dense, shivering brushes, each tussock a separate island, ringed by open water. Bog myrtle and dwarf birch crowd only along the rim, low thickets whose leaves are always beaded, roots down in the seep.

And in the iron-stained shallows, sundew spreads its sticky stars flat on the moss. It is a plant that hunts. Because the bog is rich in everything except nitrogen, it catches midges on flypaper hairs and digests them slowly, leaf by leaf. Bog cranberry runs in thin lines along the flow-lines, where water slips from rock to pool, following the same routes the minerals take.

They do not move, but they are not passive. They are the filters and the traps and the slow pumps of the system, turning light into tissue and tissue into peat, holding the water in place so the vents below can keep firing on schedule.

The air, when you notice it, is alive in a way the ground pretends not to be.

Midges hang in clouds over the warmest patches — which is to say, over the patches where the water is a fraction less cold, where the peat exhales a little more. Thick enough that a mist around your face is not just mist. They live hours to days, they do not build, they rise in the dripping light and fall again, and they are the transfer station between the plants and everything that eats.

Dragonflies patrol in short, darting circuits, never far from the pool, landing on a sedge tip, launching again, their wings catching what light there is. Water beetles stitch the surface from below, diving and surfacing in a regular rhythm, trapping a silver bubble for air, leaving a fine wake that closes behind them. In the moss, you do not see the tiny mammals, you hear the tunnels — a rustle, a quick run, a pause — shrews and voles following heated runways between tussocks, eating worms and beetle larvae, never emerging fully into open air. They are the only warm-blooded residents who actually live here year-round, and they are small enough to disappear into the thickness of the carpet.

Then there is the sound you cannot avoid: the frogs.

One per tussock, as if assigned. A frog in every shallow margin, in every wet hollow between sedge clumps, throats inflating, croaking with a rising, revving insistence until individual voices merge into a wall. They are not loud because they are social. They are loud because the bowl is a drum and the water between them carries sound perfectly, and because there is nothing to absorb it. Each one holds its station in the green, eats the midges that land near it, is eaten in turn if it stays too still in the open. Their eggs, when they lay them, are laid in those same shallow films, in the acid water the sphagnum keeps just survivable. Tadpoles hang in the shallows like punctuation marks, motionless, filtering water that is already being filtered.

Above them, briefly, the birds.

A snipe lives here by territory, not by flock. You will not see it sitting; you will hear it burst up from a tussock with a *whup-whup* of wings and a bleating call like a steam release, then it will drop again a few paces away into sedge and vanish. It eats frogs and worms and anything soft it can probe for in the saturated peat. Its whole life is concealment in a place that is already concealed.

Ducks come through in pairs, low and white-chested, across the pool. They land with a rush, they take off again, they do not stay. They are commuters, not residents — they feed on beetles and seeds and the surface film, they bring nutrients in on their feathers and feet and leave again before the mist thickens. For a minute they are the largest animals in the bowl by far, and then the bowl is still again, empty-seeming, one frog per tussock.

That is the pattern of life here: abundance without crowding. The cold keeps everything small, or short-lived, or both. Nothing builds a nest you can recognize, nothing makes a burrow that lasts, nothing accumulates except mineral. Leaves that fall are drawn toward the vents, stained orange, sunk. A dead frog, a dead bird, is found by beetles, cleaned, sunk. The midges die and fall into the water and are eaten or they feed the sundew. The waste of each station becomes the input for the next station down the line.

And there is, finally, the one inhabitant who does not belong to the cycle: you.

You are the only made thing in it. Your boots leave a print that the peat swallows in minutes. Your breath adds a momentary warmth that increases the condensation on your face and then is lost. You do not feed anything here, you do not get fed, you are not stationed. The frogs do not stop for you, the vents do not pause, the iron bacteria do not notice. When you leave through the slit, there is nothing to close behind you.

The residents are perfectly adapted not because they are tough, but because they are interchangeable parts of a machine that was never intended to be inhabited. The bacteria make rust. The moss makes acid and holds water. The sundew makes nitrogen out of midges. The midges make food out of air and light. The frog makes sound and eats the midges. The snipe makes a sudden noise and eats the frog. The water rises, cools, sheds its gas, sinks, is charged again, and rises.

Nothing lives there for long, and nothing lives there alone.
