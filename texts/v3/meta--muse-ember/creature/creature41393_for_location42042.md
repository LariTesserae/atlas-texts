At first, from the rim, it seems as if no one lives here at all.

The water is too warm and too still, the rock too new, the green too unanimous. What looks like a shore is a spire rising out of a lake that has no shore, what looks like a roof is a garden, what looks like a garden is a roof of the house below it. Only when you stay long enough for the mist to thin — and it thins only for a breath at a time — do you realize the texture on the spires is not rock alone. It is inhabited.

Not in the way a city on dry land is inhabited, with people moving *through* it. Here people move *within* it, the way a plant moves within itself: by adding layer upon layer and then persuading the layers above not to close.

A family, and then the lineage that claims them by a knot in a rope, lives stacked because the basin gives no other direction. The ground floor is basalt, quarried by hand, set without mortar or with shell-lime, and it stands half in the water. Its lower walls are always wet, darkened to the tide-line that is not a tide. Above it, timber and bamboo and woven fiber make a lighter room, and above that a roof that is not a roof but a raft of earth held by reed and root, planted with root vegetables and broad leaves. That garden shades the doorway of the family next door, whose own ground floor is a meter lower down the same spire. From the water you come up a ladder cut into the warm stone, hand-deep and slick, and you do not know if you are entering a house or leaving a garden until the woven mat shutter closes behind you.

Inside, the thick walls hold the day. There is no drying here. Clothes never dry. Skin never quite dries. The people who live in the High Sump are small-boned for the damp, or they have become so; their hair is kept short and bound with reed fiber, their skin carries a permanent sheen and a faint smell of resin and hot stone, their voices have learned to be low because sound does not carry past fifty meters and mist swallows the rest. They speak as if not to disturb the water.

Fire is not a thing a house has. It belongs to the cluster. One hearth-house per neighborhood — one per spire, one per raft-field, one per submerged shelf — holds the communal fire, and that is the only warm, dry air in the basin. Smoke rises through a narrow flue and is instantly softened by the low cloud. People come to it to cook, to warm joints, to sit with their backs to the heat while the rain makes its single continuous hush on the roof.

They eat what the water and the mats give slowly. Algae cakes dried on low platforms, root vegetables pulled from floating gardens, the occasional trapped fish from a basket trap set in a spring pool. They cook slowly, communally, in clay pots blackened thin. No one hurries because there is nothing to hurry toward. A child can watch a fern by the eaves unfurl for a week and understand that a crack in the basalt below the house is doing the same thing on a different timescale. Both are heaving. Both are being made.

The people are not one kind, because the neighborhoods are not one kind.

On the spires, where the black rock breaks the surface in knife-edges, live the stone families. They are the quarrymen and re-levelers. They know which blocks are still warm and cannot be sat on, which veins of pale mineral will split clean, which foundation course from a previous generation must be lifted a hand's breadth because the ground rose beneath it. Their children learn the city not by street names but by knots: this platform lashed in a double reef to that stilt belongs to this lineage; that sagging rope bridge, spliced with a sideways splice, belongs to another. A log left underwater for a generation comes up mineralized, harder than stone, and is valued precisely because it has lived in the water long enough to stop rotting.

On the raft-fields in the shallower coves, where there is no rock to build on, live the garden families. Their houses float. Not boats — houses that have accepted floating as a condition. The platforms are lashed registers of reed and bamboo, anchored to rock with rope that is constantly renewed, patched with new growth, planted at the edges with mats of small-leaved plants. They move a few meters in a season if a spring shifts, and the whole neighborhood moves with them, gardens and chickens-less yards and hearth raft all in careful relation. Their work is weeding, which here is not clearing but persuading: a garden left unwed for a month is a thicket, and a thicket left for a season is a new shelf.

On the submerged shelves, where the mountain slopes simply disappear down into jade-green opacity, live the shelf families, the most amphibious of all. Their ground rooms are built *into* the water, with underwater thickets rooted in the tops of sunken walls. Inside, the air is cooler and the light is greener. They tend the spring pools — clear, bubbling, steaming faintly — where the water upwells and warms the stone underfoot. The spring watchers know the breathing of each pool by its bubbles. One cove where rain falls sideways in a slow sheet from a hot spring's steam, wetting only the underside of a staircase, is theirs, and they step carefully up the dry side.

All of them live with the same condition: the city is embryonic because the mountains are. A doorway begun by a previous generation lies on the bottom of a clear pool, perfectly preserved, its placed stones still straight, its plan abandoned not because anyone died but because the rock heaved a few centimeters and the plan no longer made sense. The people do not mourn such things. A building here has a lifespan not of centuries but of a slow, patient decay that must be renewed by the living. Houses are re-leveled. Mats are rewoven. Vines are cut back from windows or, sometimes, allowed to seal a door that no one uses anymore, because a sealed door is a wall that costs nothing to maintain.

If the people are the second presence, the first is plant.

Life arrived here recently and nothing has checked it yet. Everything grows as if for the first time. Giant ferns unfurl from eaves, broad-leaf vines run along rope bridges and root in mortar and in window slits, moss grows on the north side of everything and then, after a while, on the south side too. At the waterline every structure wears a fringe: floating mats lashed into garden rafts, reed beds, algae drying. Below the surface, near stilts and foundations, there are weed-forests rooted in stone, holding leaf to leaf in stillness, swaying without a current because there is no current to sway them.

Some plants here do not behave like plants in a settled country. In the older, deeper parts of the basin, where the water is darkest, abandoned foundations are covered in a slow, spiral growth that does not rot. It climbs in perfect, unhurried coils, preserving what it covers. The people leave it alone. It is not food and it is not building material; it is what the water does when it is left to itself long enough.

Other vines grow *down* from bridges, rooting not in soil but in air and water, forming living curtains that harden, attachment by attachment, into new land. A bridge between two spires can, over a lifetime, become a shelf because the plants hanging from it decided, cell by cell, to become a shore. The gardener families watch for this and help it or cut it, depending on whether they need a shelf or need the span.

This is why the boundary between built and grown is hard to hold. A door left unopened for a season will be sealed by growth — not overgrown, *sealed*, as if the house had closed its own eye. The settlement is not cleared; it is continuously persuaded back, and the persuasion is a full occupation.

Animals, by contrast, are a rumor of the usual world. That strangeness is part of the place. Warm water and exuberant plants should mean abundance, and here they mean almost the opposite.

You will go days without seeing a large animal. Fish are sparse in the dark, still, jade-opaque water. In a spring pool you may catch an eel-like shape, blind or nearly so, feeling its way between warm stones, and then it is gone. Traps come up empty more often than not, and when they do not, it is often a trap full of silt and a single small swimming thing that lives in the shallows and breathes the surface film.

Birds are almost absent, lost in the cloud layer that never leaves. What flies are midges in drifts above the water, moving as a single breath, rising where a spring steams and dissolving where the mist thickens. In the floating mats there are amphibians, translucent and slow, that hold to the underside of leaves. That, and the small swimming things in the underwater thickets, and the eel in the pool, is the fauna.

At night the city does not glitter. There are few lights, and those few bloom in the damp like fungi — pale, unhurried glows from the hearth-house and from algae cakes and reef mats left out to catch the last dim light. The darkness over the water is complete and close, and the sound of the place is correspondingly muted. Paddles move without splash. A raft laps a stilt pylon. A hot spring breathes. A stone clink comes from a spire across the basin, late, as if traveling through water rather than air. People listen for these small arrivals the way other people listen for bells.

Strange things persist if you stay long enough to notice them, and the inhabitants have learned to notice them without naming them important. Mist moves through houses and condenses on inner walls, running down in rivulets that join the lake without ever having been a river. Water that evaporated from one spire rains back a few hundred meters away onto another. A child learns quickly not to expect a river, a tide, an outlet, or a dry season.

They are born in the upper rooms, in air that is already damp and faintly sweet with algae. They are taught the knots, the splices, the places where the ground is still too warm to sit, the pools that bubble faster before rain. The old live near the communal hearth, where the air is least saturated, and tell where the foundation courses lie under the clear water and why they were abandoned. When someone dies, the body is wrapped in woven fiber and resin and set into the water near the foundations, where the spiral growth will take it slowly and the mineralized wood and stone will keep it. It is not a cemetery. It is a continuation of the building.

By dusk — if dusk can be distinguished from the long, dim afternoon that has been going on since morning — the spires disappear one by one into mist. What remains is the nearest cluster of wet basalt and green, the warm water lapping at its base, the soft endless rain closing over it all, and the living things of the High Sump settling back into the one condition they all share: being made, slowly, and not yet finished.
