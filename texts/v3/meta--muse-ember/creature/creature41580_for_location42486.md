At first you would say nothing lives here.

There is no burrow to find, no nest, no den, no house with a light in it. The pans are too wet to hold a footprint, the channels too fast to hold a body, the concrete too slick and too cold for anything that wants to stay still. A heron can stand in the central pan for an hour and catch nothing, and leave without a sound, because there is nothing there for a heron to be a resident of. It only visits the surface.

Living here does not present itself as a creature. It presents itself as a coverage.

Put your hand close to the water in a still pan, not into it, and you will see the skin. Black-green algae, suspended perfectly even, thin as breath on glass. It is not in the water so much as on it, a living film that rides the level up and down with the pumps, concentrates at the edges when the pan drops, spreads and dilutes when it rises. In the channels the film has another form: a bright, almost luminous streak of green on the polished stone where the flow has run clear in the last day, dulling again as the brown slurry returns. On the north sides of the concrete terraces, moss has made itself verdigris, soft to the eye if you do not touch it. On the oldest stone, where a step has not slumped in a season, lichen has mapped itself in pale, continent-shaped patches, growing outward at the speed of weather.

These are the oldest residents of the dent. They do not move, except that the place moves them. When a pan breaches and takes a new tongue downhill, the algae is carried, thick and brown, and settles in the new margin, in the newly covered path. When a gate lifts and a clean white line appears in the mud, the film tears and re-forms along the new route within hours. They are not damaged by the churn. They are made of it. Whatever water leaves behind, they colonize. Whatever the water takes away, they follow.

Under the sheen there is a deeper sheen, and that is alive too, though you will never see it as a shape. The rust is not only rust. It is black-orange and layered a thousand cycles deep because iron-eating bacteria live in it, working in the wet, turning metal to color and color back to metal in the damp air. In the deep central pan, where the water is almost black and holds the sky in a perfect disc, the smell when the pumps pause is faintly sour and mineral — sulphur bacteria at work in the silt, making the blackness blacker, making the rings that spread from no visible cause when something in the depths shifts. You do not see them. You see their effect on the surface, and the surface is their whole world. They ask for exactly what this place offers without pause: cold, wet, iron, slurry, movement too slow to notice and too constant to escape.

The plants that live here live the same way, at the edge of giving up. Sedge has rooted only where a channel has breached and slowed enough to leave a soft margin, a sparse fringe of grey-green blades knees-deep in water that never quite clears. It does not form a bank. It forms a suggestion of a bank, washed out and replanted by the next surge. The stunted birches are more stubborn and more exposed: trunks rising from black slurry, bark already blackened to the height of the usual flood, branches bare low down, leaves small and too far apart, as if the tree were rationing itself against the cold. Their roots do not go down so much as out, feeling along the surface of the pans for a place where the mud is not moving quite so fast. In a season a birch here will grow a few leaves, lose a few to a slump, send a root into a newly slumped bank that is raw and bare for a day, and be covered again. They are not trees in a wood. They are individuals, holding a place that is re-made around them daily.

The animals are rarer, and paler, and they move like the machinery.

In the deep channels, in the brown flow that never stops, there are eels — or something eel-like, for they never surface to be named. You will see a darkening in the water that is not a shadow, a long displacement against the current, a ripple that travels upstream. They live in the relay itself, using the pumps as weather. When slurry is lifted to a higher pan and dropped again, they ride the drop. When a silt dam dissolves from underneath with a muted sigh, they are in the sudden redistribution, finding the new channel before the water has finished finding it. They do not have a home in any one pan. Their home is the route between pans. The central black disc is the centre of their territory, if territory is the right word for a circuit that is never finished.

Smaller lives keep to the joints. A frog — glistening, pale underneath, almost transparent from living in pipe-water — keeps to a pipe joint on the eastern step, where water weeps and then runs and then stops. It is there because the joint is warm enough, damp enough, and undisturbed enough for a few hours at a time. Look directly at it and it vanishes, not by leaping, but by being exactly the color of the wet iron. When the pump behind it surges, it will be elsewhere, in another joint, in the seepage at the foot of the weeping rock, in the standing water inside a roofless intake house. It does not migrate. It relocates by inches, as the water relocates by inches.

Over the one pan that is always a degree warmer — where the electric motors sit in the wet like animals drinking and bleed their faint heat into the slurry — there are dragonflies. Not a swarm. Two, three at a time, patrolling low, their wings catching the flat light for an instant and then losing it. Their nymphs live in the slurry itself, in the mud that fills your bootprint before you lift it, waiting in the cold for the brief warm interval when a motor has been running long enough to make the pan breathe. They are the only residents who truly belong to the air, and even they never rise above the rim. The bowl catches them too. What little leaves, leaves as dampness on the wind that climbs the rim; the dragonflies do not leave.

There are snails on the iron, small and pale, leaving silver trails on the beaded casings that the condensation washes away by afternoon. There are midges over the still pans at the hour when the mist burns off the centre first — so few you could count them, so low you have to bend to see them. There are fungi on the swollen wood of the pump-house door, on the one intact pane of glass sweat and film. None of this makes a population you could list. It makes a film of life over the whole system, thick in some places, absent in others, shifting with the levels.

And then there are the machines, because in this dent the machines are residents too, and the other residents have adapted to them as they would to weather or to predators.

The hydraulic rams in their pits thump softly, like a heart heard through a wall, at intervals that have nothing to do with day or night. The Archimedes screws turn with a slow, grinding patience, half-buried, lifting water and dropping it, lifting and dropping, in the closed loop that is the law of the place. The sluice gates lift by their own pressure and settle. The pumps suck brown slurry from a higher pan to a lower, then on again, and in the cool damp air their breath condenses and is visible, white against grey, for a moment before it is absorbed. If you stay long enough you stop hearing them as noise and start hearing them as presence — a set of slow, tireless bodies that never sleep, never feed except on the water they move, and whose movements are the movements everything else in the bowl must accommodate. The algae brightens when their channels run clear. The frog moves when their joints run. The eels ride their surges. The dragonfly nymphs wait for their heat. The heron stands in their central pan because their central pan is the only still water large enough to stand in.

They are not alive in the way the algae is alive. But they are what the algae is alive *in*. The turbulence here is not a storm. It is settled. It has been churning long enough that the churn itself has become the habitat, and everything that lives here lives by joining the churn rather than resisting it.

That is why there is no chorus, no call, no track that comes out again. The prints that fill with water as you watch them — yours — are the only mammal prints that make sense. The older, narrower set that goes from pan to pan and stops at the pump-house and does not come out: whatever made it, if it was an animal, has learned the place perfectly, and knows that to live here is to be covered and remade, and does not need to be seen leaving. Perhaps it is only the water finding a route underground, then unfinding it, leaving a line that looks like a passage. Perhaps something checks the pumps at night, by pressure, by a shift in the water table, leaving a wet handprint on iron that means a pump has recently been touched. The place does not volunteer an explanation. It only shows its surfaces, and says: read me by these.

By the time you climb back to the rim, the bowl below has changed — the water in different places, the channels taking different shortcuts, a new runnel opened, a new deposit barring a corner — and yet it looks exactly as it did when you arrived. The pans are still stepped. The pipes still cross and recross, low overhead and at ankle height and buried. The central disc still holds the sky, and moves, faintly, in concentric rings with no visible cause.

Whatever lives here does not live *against* that. It lives *as* that: thin, pale, and patient, spread over stone and iron and water, lifted and dropped and lifted again, never reaching an end, and for that reason, never quite gone.
