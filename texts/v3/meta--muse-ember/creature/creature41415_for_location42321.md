At first there seems to be only the grazer, because it is the only one of the lives here that lets itself be seen as a whole.

You can walk a day along an avenue and see no sign of anything breathing. The paving is empty, the doorways are dark and empty, the plazas hold only dust re-laid in thin, silent sheets. Then, far ahead where the avenue runs straight into the grey-copper horizon, there is a mass between two towers that was not, a moment ago, quite a mass. It has the height of a tower and the stillness of one. Only when it moves — a neck unfolding from a low body, one columnar leg lifting and setting down again farther along the avenue — does the shape separate itself from the architecture. Tall, grey, slow, it carries its body low, as if it were trying not to interrupt the skyline, and it travels the avenues the way other animals travel game trails or riverbanks. The city is its route.

Up close it is stranger and less animal than it looks from far away. Its legs are jointed in a way that makes you count them twice: a knee that bends one way, and below it another joint that seems to bend back, each movement locked and unlocked with a patience that hurts to watch. The feet are broad discs, flattened against the dust, and they make no sound at all on the paving. Its body is armoured in plates of the same warm grey as the walls, mottled black and rust-red where the dendritic mineral growth has thickened across its back. Its neck is longer than its body. It is made of segments, not muscle in any familiar sense, each ring able to turn a little, so that the whole neck can describe a slow S in the air, or fold down until the head is at paving level.

The head is a flat shovel. It has no teeth, no lips that open and close. There are slits along the sides of it that may be eyes, or may be something else opening and closing against the light, and they give no impression of looking at you. When it feeds it draws the shovel along the stone, along the pale mineral crusts and the hard-packed dust and the places where the black dendrites have spread thickest, and it takes in grit and the surface growth of the rock. It is the only animal here that could do this. What it takes would poison anything that needed water to digest it. It needs no water. Its dark back warms, barely, under that small pale sun, and somewhere inside the long body there is a chamber lined with smooth stone where the grit turns and turns for years, breaking down the dry mineral growth by friction and time. It does not breathe in any way you can see. Its sides do not heave. In this air there would be no breath to see anyway. It stops in the middle of an avenue and stops completely, and for a while it is a piece of architecture that has decided, briefly, to be an animal.

It comes out in the dim parts of the day, when the shadows are longest and faintest and fall in more than one direction. It is solitary in a way that is not loneliness but spacing. Between one grazer and the next there may be kilometres of empty avenue, empty plaza, empty district. A whole city of room between presences. When two are in the same long view they are not together; they are aligned, perhaps, both travelling north along different avenues that the city has made look the same. You will never see young ones. You will never see a dead one. An old grazer does not collapse. Its feet settle into the dust that has packed around them, its plates soften to the colour of the wall beside it, its neck folds and stays folded, and over a time too long to measure it becomes indistinguishable from the lower courses of a tower. Whether that is death, or sleep, or just the end of its brief decision to be an animal, there is no way to tell, because nothing here rots and nothing leaves a bone.

Under the slabs, everywhere, there are the smaller ones.

You will not see them first. You will hear them, if you hear anything. A tick, low and dry, from a doorway or from beneath a loose paving slab, then another tick far apart. It is the sound the place attributes to cooling stone. Sometimes it is only cooling stone. Sometimes the slab shifts a fraction, and the creature under it shifts with it, segment by segment, and the tick is the locking of its plates.

They are stone-coloured and flat, a handspan to a forearm in length, like a piece of the wall that has learned to move very slowly. Their backs are layered, outer plate over inner plate, the same way the city walls are layered, outer wall and ruined inner wall and later wall in the gap. They live in the mortar lines and under slabs and in the bases of doorways, where the dust gathers thickest on the lee side and where the dendrites spread fastest. They eat what the grazers leave: the scrapings that fall from a mouth drawn along the crust, the grit that works loose from a tower base, the dust pellets that dry out of a grazer and become paving again. They move downhill with the city, a fraction at a time, toward the edge of the plateau where the city is always, imperceptibly, beginning again. A single slab may hold one for a hundred years. A whole district may hold hundreds, and you will cross it and see nothing but rock and ticking.

They are not the young of the grazers, though they live as if they were the grazers' forgotten children — too small, too flat, too wedded to the stone to ever stand up into the avenue. They only ever seem to be moving when they are not. Each tick is a decision. Each stillness is longer than the movement that broke it.

And then there is the hunter, which is known only by the space it makes in things.

You never see it close. Distance here does not behave, and whatever approaches you along an avenue has a habit of not growing nearer, no matter how long you walk toward it. The hunter may not be approaching at all. What you see is a place on a tower wall that was shadow and then is not. The long, faint shadow that fell upward from the wall toward that low sun simply ceases, as if the light has remembered something. And at the same moment, a hundred metres farther along the avenue, a grazer is moving faster than grazers move. Its neck is extended out straight, its body low, the dust ribbon across the paving broken behind it in a line that was not there before. It has not run. It has been displaced. Nothing chased it loudly. Nothing touched it that you can name. The pace between hunter and prey is the same everywhere here: slow, spaced, silent.

No kill is ever witnessed. There is no blood, no carcass, no struggle. The grazer is simply further along than it was, and a little thinner at the edges than it was before, as if something has worn at its outline. You might see it again in an afternoon, in a plaza that appears at the end of three different streets, standing still in precisely the posture it had in the morning. The hunter has not followed it. The avenue has rearranged itself around the hunter's absence, and the grazer has been carried forward in the recurrence.

If the grazers live in the avenues and the small ones live in the walls, the hunter lives in the doubled shadow. It is there when the second, fainter shadow falls downward from a tower, as if light were coming from both sides of the world. It casts no heat. It leaves no print in the dust. It does not need to catch anything, because it does not move through the city the way animals move through a landscape. It moves the way a crack widens, the way a tower slumps a millimetre — by making the thing it wants be somewhere else, and the place it was be only stone.

Everything that lives here is part of the same slow circuit, eating what the stone makes of itself and returning to stone. The mineral dendrites spread a few millimetres a year across the rock, are scraped by a grazer's shovel, are fallen on and eaten by the small ones under the slabs, are ground in a grazer's stone chamber and passed out as dry dust to be re-packed into the avenues the grazer walks. Over time the grazer's own plates thin. The small ones' plates thin and flake into the dust they eat. The hunter — if it is an animal at all and not the thinning itself, given a place to be — thins the grazer until the grazer can slip forward out of its own shape.

Nothing is born that you can see. Nothing dies that you can see. Lives here do not begin and end so much as they wear, very slowly, toward the architecture that surrounds them and toward the silence that surrounds that. A grazer standing still in an avenue is a tower that has not yet admitted it is a tower. A small one ticking under a slab is a wall that has not yet admitted it can walk. A shadow on a wall that disappears is a hunter that has not yet admitted it has a body.

They continue because the place continues: the dust rivers in their dry beds, the dendrites creeping at night and stopping in the light, the grazers migrating between the empty districts, the small ones locking and unlocking their plates in the doorways. The edges of all of it are wearing thin, and the lives, patiently, are wearing thin with them.
