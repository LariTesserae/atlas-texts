If you put your ear to the outside wall, you hear it before you see anything that lives here. Not a call, not a song. A working sound. A dry rustle that never resolves into steps, a click repeated too many times to be an animal, a whirr that starts somewhere and doesn't stop. That is them, first. Before any shape.

They are not in the hall the way birds are in a tree. The hall has been taken over so completely that their bodies are part of its material. What looks from the doorway like mineral dust in drifts against the engine is shed skins and frass and pulverised gut and fibre worn to powder. What looks like a polished patch of concrete is polished by them. Take them away and the place would be just a shed again, cooling and rusting on metal chemistry alone. With them, it is turning over.

At the bottom, the floor, is the closest thing to a true soil they have. It is a living crust, no thicker than your thumb in most places, and it moves even when you think it is still. Pinhead mites work it first — translucent, blind, fat-bodied, with legs like oars. They turn the dust over the way earthworms turn loam, mouthful by mouthful, eating the mould spores, the dead skin, the fibres of insulation that drift down. Where the dust is deepest, in the lee of the press feet and in the valleys between the lockers, they have made dune faces that slump and re-form overnight.

Over them, and through them, larger workers. Beetles the length of a thumbnail, oil-slick, eyeless, their carapaces washed to a blind gleam. They burrow not through earth but through grease that congealed a century ago in the cracks of the concrete, and through the felt pads under the engine where oil soaked in and never dried. They polish as they go; a beetle track in the grease will be a clean, dark trench for years. They eat the grease — or rather, they eat the bacteria and fungi living in the grease, and the grease itself in slow metabolic sips, getting their water more from the chemistry of breaking down fat than from ever drinking. A few have gone almost translucent from generations indoors, light passing through the abdomen where the gut is full of grey dust.

In the walls, inside the rockwool, moth larvae tunnel. You can hear them if you press your palm to the concrete: a faint ticking, as of someone eating stone. They hollow the insulation from within, leaving a lacework of galleries that hold warmth long after the day's light goes. The dust they produce falls in a fine, constant sifting from the eaves. Everything that dies down here is eaten within hours. The eating is part of the sound.

The wall layer lives vertically, and it commutes. Pale centipedes, long and unhurried, hang in loose strata up the concrete pillar-bases, each holding its ground by vibration through the stone. They feel the hall through their feet — the tremor of a motor waking three metres away, the footfall of a cockroach-runner on a pipe above them — more than they see it. Most have reduced eyes, half-lidded, or none. Spiders share the walls with them, long-legged, colourless, hanging in curtains of old web and cast skins that have stiffened into grey lace. They do not hunt much by sight. They wait for the pipe traffic.

The pipes are their highways. Cockroach-like runners course along them in lines so regular you can set a watch by them: out from the lockers at dusk, up the warm riser, across the engine's flank where the metal holds the day's cold, and back when the fan wakes and makes the air run fast and dry. The lockers themselves are black with them inside and out — in the vents, in the folds of coats left a hundred years ago, in the corners where paper has turned to powder. They are the most indifferent of the inhabitants to heat or cold because they have learned to time the metal's temperature the way other insects time the sun.

Then there are the specialists, the ones who belong to the machine the way lungfish belong to a drought pool. In the felt pads of the six cylinders, where oil and warmth have made a dark, spongy forest, larvae graze in white, secret crowds, their heads black, their bodies segmented like tiny caterpillars for an engine. In the oil sumps, where a black meniscus still holds and reforms though no hand tops it up, beetles swim and dive and lay eggs in the iridescent bacterial film. On the conveyor belt, in the warm hollow of its return, parasitoid wasps lay their eggs inside the moth larvae that live in the belt's felt, and their own pale larvae develop in turn, translucent as wax, until they chew out and pupate in the dust of the belt's shadow.

The ledgers have their own small nation: silverfish and booklice, grey and silver, grazing the mould that blooms on leather and paper glue. They are the only ones here who eat something that was once alive on the outside — wood, paper, leather, rope — and they have reduced it to a fine, nutritious snow. The workbench island has woodlice in its cracks, blind, curling tight when the dust storm from the fan hits them. The control cabin, with its spider-webbed glass, is a spider nursery; the young hang in the corners like dewdrops, tasting the air for the flies that blunder against the glass at night.

And above all of it, occupying the middle of the air, is the weather. Midges and moths and flies and tiny wasps, a cloud seldom thicker than a fist, hanging like a second ceiling at head height. They are not migrating; they are circling, mating, laying eggs in mid-air fall, feeding on fungi spores and each other's cast skins and the dawn condensation that beads on the coldest steel and evaporates by nine. When the fan turns, or when a predator wave runs across the floor and a spider drops, the whole cloud lifts at once and rains. It sounds on the roof like rain, though no rain has fallen here in living memory. It falls as a living storm of wings that blacks the skylight for a heartbeat, then settles layer upon layer back into its strata, moths low, midges high, wasps hunting in between.

They eat each other, efficiently. A big predatory beetle — rarer, armour-plated, its mandibles clicking loud enough to hear — charges across the floor crust and the mites scatter like a flock of birds. Spiders drop on lines to snatch the runners off the pipes. The parasitoid wasps track the moth cloud by the tremor of its wings on the metal. Cannibalism is not a failure here, it is a rationing system; a larva that eats its sibling gets the water and fat the hall cannot give it any other way.

At night, when the light thins to that same cool, even grey it holds all day, the system changes its shift. The air layer drops. The wall layer descends. Centipedes come down off the pillars to meet the floor. Mating happens in the dust dunes, in the grease trenches, on the warm side of the press where the metal still gives back a little heat. The sound gets louder — not louder in volume, but denser, a dry thunder of chitin, as if the whole hall were chewing.

Every few hours the machines wake over them, as they have for longer than any of them can remember. A belt jerks a foot forward on its timer. A panel buzzes and a strip light flickers on at midnight, casting that flat industrial light no sun ever made. A fan sends the dust into a slow horizontal storm and sends the winged weather tumbling end over end. The inhabitants do not startle, and they do not flee. They ride it. The mites shelter in the lee of a bolt. The spiders brace in their webs like sailors. The moths are blown down onto the felt and get up again. The built world is still doing its slow, pointless cycles, and the living world has built its own right over the top, using the same timers for its own purposes.

None of them drink, exactly. Many get their water from eating fat. Many lick the faint beading on the steel at dawn — a few microlitres, gone by mid-morning, enough for a generation of nematodes to swim and breed in the film before it vanishes. Many have sealed their bodies with wax, truncated their legs, lost an eye or both, and learned to navigate by vibration through metal, which carries sound farther and truer than air in a dry place.

Nothing new enters. No plant photosynthesises. No water seeps. The whole population runs on what the industrial world left behind — fats, waxes, rubber, rope, leather, paper, wood, a little processed food long since turned to powder — and on each other. Grain by grain, they are consuming the hall: steel into rust-flower crusts, rust into dust, felt into larvae, larvae into wasps, wasps into mites, mites into the soil they plough. The erosion that smoothed the plain outside has smoothed this inside too, but here the eroders have legs. The edges are all soft. The forms are all final.

You could walk their entire world in two minutes, from the warped door to the far wall that is their horizon, from the dust plain of the floor to the ridge of the engine's handrails. You could, in theory, know every locker village and every pipe highway. And yet to be inside it is to be surrounded, floor to roof, by a million presences all busy and all in contact — theirs with each other, and yours, unwillingly, with them. They are not living in a place. They have become the place, cool and dry and mineral and ceaselessly busy, turning a dead machine, from the inside, into a living landscape.
