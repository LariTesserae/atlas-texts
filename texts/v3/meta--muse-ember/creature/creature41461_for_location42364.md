Nothing here announces itself as alive.

For a day you will look for it and find only geology - black stone, grey travertine, white and rust crust at the edge of warm water - and you will assume the city is empty in the simple sense. It is settled, as you said, but the settlement is not the architecture. The architecture is only the largest thing that has stopped moving. The living things are smaller than the eye expects to search for, and they hide not by camouflage so much as by agreement. They look like the place itself because they are made of the same equilibrium: mineral film, damp rust, a sheet of water that will not leave.

The first inhabitants are the flat ones, in the quietest pools.

They are not plants. There is no green and no root, and the dim pearl light would not feed them if there were. They are bacterial mats, grey-green only in the way oil is grey-green, spread in a skin a few cells thick across the shallows where the water is stillest and the temperature does not change too quickly. From a distance a pan will look uniform. Close, crouched over it, you see the skin is patchy, healed and healing. Put a finger into it - the water hot against your knuckle - and it parts and closes again behind you, leaving a faint spiral in the salt below as the cells at the edge divide and reach.

A mat may cover a whole pan for a season, then break up in the morning rise when the water lifts half a meter, then re-form on the slightly higher travertine step. It does not travel. It grows away from what harms it, which is mostly precipitation; where the water is richest in dissolved iron and sulfur from the basalt, the crust thickens white and the mat dies back, and where the water is quieter it thickens again. It feeds on the gradient, not on light. Hot rock below, cooler steam above, iron in the water oxidizing at the surface - that difference is its food, and it lives its entire life inside that thin warm boundary. Some mats long predate the halls. The city was built around them, laid its causeways across their pans, and they simply colonized the new waste heat, the new overflow from the cisterns, the new film under your own footprints. A footprint in the shallows stops shining, because the water has closed over it, but the mineral film below holds the shape of your weight. Within an hour that film is stippled, within a day it is a tiny garden fed by the disturbance. You are the first large animal to walk there in a very long time, and the mats treat you as a landslide they can use.

They are the base of everything else, not because anything hunts them - nothing here really hunts - but because everything else eats what they shed. A mat sloughs constantly, cell by cell, into the water. That rain of dead cells is the only snowfall the place has.

In the pipes, the rain becomes a forest.

Where the conduits run at knee height and head height, banded and sweating, their insides are dark, hot, and lined with rust that has thickened into a stable skin. Threaded through that rust is fungus, in slow blind ropes. You will never see a rope unless you open a joint, and no one opens joints anymore, but you can read its presence from outside: a persistent leak that should have corroded the collar away instead swells, year by year, into a rough white bulge. That is fungus and calcite together, armoring the metal by eating it. The fungus feeds on iron and on the old grease that still coats the interior of the factory halls, on the traces of oil the sleds drip, on the dead cells carried in with the seep. It grows a few millimeters a year along the inside of a pipe, following warmth. A single individual can be kilometers long, traversing an entire hall, emerging in a gutter in one district and reappearing in a cistern wall two kilometers away across an empty pan, because the pipe network connects what the surface does not. It is the connective tissue of the city. Where a mat is the skin of a pan, the fungus is the vein.

It releases its fragments when the pale smudge in the sky brightens a fraction and the metal ticks with expansion. The fragments are carried in the water, or lofted for a few meters in the steam at a joint, and settle to start again. It has no eyes, no mouth in any separate sense, no movement except the imperceptible advance of its tips, pushed by heat. If you stayed for a month you would see nothing happen. If you stayed for a decade you could mark a wall with chalk and come back to find the bulge has grown over the mark.

Between mat and fungus, in the travertine steps and the gutters where water boils over, the place builds its own reefs. Calcite accumulates in white, coral-like crusts, salt flowers open in the joints of concrete overnight and then are dissolved and re-opened the next rise. These are not alive, but they are shaped by life, and they are inhabited. In the wettest pores of the reefs there are filterers too small to see individually - translucent, shrimp-like in outline only if you imagine a shrimp made of glass - that graze the bacterial film off the calcite and in turn are grazed by nothing. They live their whole lives in a space the size of your thumbnail, in water that is always warm, always alkaline, and they emerge only in the stillness between drips. Their world is the gutter. The gutter is, to them, a valley.

The sumps and wide cisterns have larger tenants.

If you stand very still for a long time - an hour, perhaps, with your sweat joining the air and doing nothing - you may see them, because stillness is the condition they require. Pale, eyeless, eel-like filterers cruise just under the surface, where the dim light is reflected flat and they can be seen as a paler shadow moving against the dark pan. They are perhaps a meter, perhaps two, translucent enough that the rust-colored line of gut shows through the flank. They have no scales and no gills that need air; they do not come up. They stir the salt crust into lazy spirals as they turn, and that stirring is their work: it lifts bacterial cells and fungal spores and mat detritus into suspension, and then they filter it, slowly, by passing water over feathery folds along their sides that you will only glimpse when one passes close to the edge of a copper cistern.

They are solitary and slow to an extreme that makes them seem stationary. One may hold a territory of a whole cistern for years, moving only when the pans fall in evening and the water between halls narrows to the culvert under a causeway, which it then slips through, unhurried, into the next pan. They reproduce rarely, by budding a tail that breaks away and grows in its own slow spiral. They are not prey. A dead eel sinks and dissolves, its calcium joining the travertine, its organics joining the mat. There is too little energy locked in a body to be worth eating, when energy is everywhere in the difference between hot stone and cold stack-top. Their heartbeat, if you could feel it through the water, would be slower than the pump pulse you hear far across the basin, and perhaps it syncs to it without ever intending to. When the distant pump thumps once, the whole pan seems to settle a fraction, as if everything living in it has paused to listen to the metal.

On the other side of that same difference - on the coldest surfaces - there are other grazers, and these you will almost certainly miss.

On the tops of stacks, on the outer faces of condenser roofs, on the unused glass of the control houses, the vapor condenses first. It runs down in a thin trembling sheet and soaks back into the ground. Living in that running sheet, invisible except as a clear patch where the beading delays for a moment, are flat organisms that ride the water down and climb back up by the edges at night-cycle. They are transparent, irregular, without eyes or legs you could name, scraping the biofilm off glass and copper sheet. They only exist where two conditions meet: cold enough to condense, wet enough never to dry. Lift a hand to touch one and the sheet breaks, the organism vanishes into the drip. A whole colony may be no larger than your palm, spread across the verdigris streaks of a cistern roof, living its entire life on a slope a few degrees from vertical, in a film thinner than paper. They are the only inhabitants that live in the sky's runoff, the only ones whose habitat is created by the exhalation of the stacks themselves.

Once, if you stay long enough, the sky itself moves.

You will not hear it. The air swallows sound, and this thing is silent. You will notice only that the pale smudge dims for a few seconds, as something broad passes between you and it. A shape with a skin wing and no feathers, riding the thermal off the pans without flapping. The wing is stretched over long fingers, mottled pale and rust, damp along the leading edge where steam condenses on it as it flies. The body beneath is small, compact, its head tucked close. It does not hunt. Nothing here hunts. It flies through the steam columns above the stack fields and scoops at the air, feeding on the airborne plankton of bacteria and fungal fragments that the thermals lift from the mats and pipes below. The air, which to you is saturated and empty, to it is a slow river of food.

It may pass over the same hall only once in months. There may be only a handful in the whole basin. It roosts, when it roosts, on the very tops of stacks where the outside is coldest and the condensation runs thickest - hanging under the lip of a sawtooth roof, wing folded, indistinguishable from a copper sheet gone verdigris until the next updraft lifts it. It is the only inhabitant that may know the ridge, the only one that could leave, and perhaps it never does. The loop holds it as it holds the water. To leave the steam would be to dry out.

The machines are its fauna in another sense, and they have become fauna in fact.

The low box-like sleds that ride the rails between halls at night-cycle lift mineral plates, clear a gutter, seal a joint, then stop for a long time with a single amber light on. That light is the only true color in the place besides rust. The sleds are not tended. Their routes are worn into the causeways, and the fungal mats have colonized their undersides - warm, wet, perpetually greased - and travel with them from hall to hall, seeding new pipes. Their wakes stir the shallows. The plates they lift and drop feed, when they dissolve, the eels below. An eel may follow a sled route for years, in the water under the causeway, in the way a terrestrial animal might follow a game trail that no longer has game.

The sleds behave, over time, like animals that have no predators and no need to migrate: patrol, forage in the mechanical sense, groom, rest. They do not reproduce, but the life on them does. A sled that stops for a decade with its light on becomes a reef. People - if people ever lived here - would have called this broken. It is not broken. It is adapted. The city is not producing anything for a market. It is maintaining the loop of catching, moving, condensing, letting fall, and the sleds and the mats and the fungi are all doing that work at different speeds, which is why they can coexist without eating each other. The energy is not in bodies. It is in the gradient, and bodies are only places where the gradient is briefly slowed down.

There is no season except the rise and fall. In the long morning the pans lift, the mats stretch, the eels move into the flooded gutters, the fungi swell in the pipes as the metal expands, the glider - if it is anywhere near - is lifted higher. In the evening the water withdraws a fraction, the mineral crust re-exposes, the condensation thickens on every surface like a second skin, and the grazers on the glass begin their slow climb. That is birth and death and migration, scaled down to half a meter.

If you stay long enough you stop looking for individuals and start seeing the whole as a population. A mat is not one organism but a surface. A fungus is not one organism but a network that spans districts. The steam is not empty but a medium, carrying cells from pan to stack-top and back. The city - halls half a kilometer long, cisterns wider than lakes, conduit spines on pylons - is not built over life. It is built, slowly, into life, encrusting rather than crumbling. Dead calcite becomes travertine. Dead fungus becomes armor. Dead bacteria become salt. Nothing is wasted because nothing leaves, and nothing leaves because there is nowhere for it to go but back into the water that made it.

You, if you stay, become temporary habitat. Your footprints fill with water and become gardens. Your sweat joins the air and condenses on a stack-top and runs down a grazers' slope. Your voice is swallowed, but your heat, for a little while, makes a spot on a wall slightly warmer, and a fungal tip will turn toward it, imperceptibly, over a year.

And then, far across the empty water, a pump pulses once, and then again, and a drip falls from a rafter into a hall big enough to hold weather, and the mats heal over the ripple, and the eel in the cistern turns in its spiral, and the whole basin breathes out steam that will become its sky again, and lives on in that breath, slow and eyeless and perfectly at equilibrium, never quite waking.
