At first you would say nothing lives in Paludra.

You look for the usual signs and do not find them. No face turned up to the light, no call from the trees, no flicker in the air above the ponds. The silence is not empty, exactly — the pumps are always at it, the boilers shriek every seventeen minutes — but it is a silence without speech. It takes a while to learn how to look for a resident here, because the residents do not stand up to be counted. They spread. They skin. They root, and they run on timers.

The first lives are the skins.

Everything in Paludra wears one. The brick that has slumped a foot into silt carries an olive-black biofilm, slick to the eye, faintly warm to the palm on a cool morning when the brick itself is cold with condensation. The silver timbers, the rust-streaked metal faces, the concrete of the flooded boulevards where the painted lines have long since gone under silt and algae — all of it is coated, layer upon layer, with bacteria, algae, fungi, lichen in the slow places where paint has blistered away and been replaced. Lichen may be the oldest individuals in town. It moves inches in a decade. It has simply taken over the walls the way the tide has taken the thresholds, year by year, black mud line upon black mud line, until the building and the growth are the same color and you can no longer tell where maintenance stopped and colonization began.

Under that skin is a smell that never quite leaves: rot and iron and cooked leaves. It is not one organism making it. It is millions, microscopic, working the low sponge of silt and cinder and clay that never dries. In the settling ponds the water holds a grey-green haze of them, a suspended nursery. In the sumps and the flooded canals they mat and layer, orange where the rust runs down and feeds them, green where the light makes it through the sky-trees. Leaf litter composts so fast in the heat that the ground stays warm to the touch long after the leaves fall, faintly steaming in the dim, sighing small bubbles of gas up through the mud. This is the town's under-people. They do not eat or get eaten. They ferment. They turn heat into more heat, fallen plant into more plant-food, and they do it in loops, hour by hour, while the water table rises a finger's width and falls again.

Only after you have registered the skins do you notice that the larger plants are not merely in Paludra, they are *of* it in the way a tenant is of a house. They have learned the infrastructure as a part of their anatomy.

There are the roof trees, whose crowns have spread over tile and corrugated iron and hold the roof down with their sheer weight. There are the hollow-trunk trees, which have become water towers in their own bodies, collecting the constant roof-drip and the afternoon rain inside them, shaded and stagnant, never emptied by any animal. There are the pipe-drinkers, the elders of this kind: a tree that grew through a warehouse foundation and now leans its whole trunk against the wall it has cracked, holding the wall up while the wall holds the tree upright in the silt. Its roots found a condensate line and cracked it, and now drink directly from a boiler's waste. It never thirsts in a way other trees do. Its leaves stay turgid even in the midday pressure, when the air itself thickens with transpiration. Down the line, the vine colonies have laced entire factory sheds shut, not as a parasite prying a building apart but as a bandage sealing it, tendril by tendril, until the doors cannot open and the windows are green lace. In the afternoon you can actually watch one advance, a pale tip feeling for a new handhold on damp brick, moving in that blind, opportunistic, upright way plants have when there is no predator to hurry them and no animal to prune them.

Inside the open-sided machine sheds the ferns have made their niche. They uncurl over the rail lines inside the halls, in the diffuse river-light that falls through the trees and the steam, living on the heat of the machines and the dripping from the roofs. They grow where nothing else with a face would linger. Outside, between the isolated blocks of workers' houses, the groves are more violent: banana, fern, elephant-ear, a hundred paces of marsh in every direction, all of it lush to the point of blindness. The banana throws new leaves every few days. The elephant-ears catch the tin-roof rain on their broad surfaces and shed it in perpetual drips. The empty lots that were demolished sixty years ago have become ponds, and the ponds have become skins of duckweed, whole sheets of it floating and breathing, moving slightly when the pumps change their cadence, never thinned by fish, never broken by frogs, growing until they sink and compost and grow again on top of themselves.

The flowers are the town's strangest residents, because they have adapted to a pollinator that is not alive in the usual sense. At night, under the lamps that never really overcome the twilight, they open and release that sweet, fermented smell — fermentation, not perfume, made by the same microscopic world that warms the mud. There are no moths for them, no bees in the hot wet air. Instead, the fans that were installed to keep the forges cool now do the work of wind and insects. All day they blow pollen from one side of town to the other in great visible clouds, clanking in rhythm, and the plants have timed themselves to it. Midday is transpiration, when they breathe so hard the air gets heavier. Afternoon is the storm, at three o'clock as clockwork, rain falling straight down and then sideways off the rim, and every leaf drums and drips and receives. Evening is redistribution, when the water that fell runs through channels cut generations ago, cooling foundations, feeding roots inside walls. Night is when the growth is loudest — trunks creaking as they expand, metal ticking as it cools, vines advancing in the dark toward the heat they can feel through brick. Their lives are not measured in seasons here so much as in that daily cycle of condensation, pressure, storm, redistribution.

And then there are the machines, who must be counted as residents because without them the plants would be something else entirely, and without the plants the machines would be scrap.

The boilers are the large, slow-breathing things. Each one sits in its shed or stands alone with its chimney still breathing white into the permanent low ceiling of steam, and each keeps its timer. Every seventeen minutes one vents with a shriek that sets the tin roofs ticking and the leaves shivering all over the small bowl of town. It is a cough, a sigh, a form of speech. The pumps are smaller and more arrhythmic, a heavy heartbeat that stutters, cycles on, cycles off, water-hammering in the mains. One pump station no bigger than a house, fenced in orange chain-link, crouches in a flooded plaza and does nothing but move water from one low place to another low place, forever. The elevated pipeway that carries steam from boiler to boiler is inhabited only by moss softening its struts and by the steam itself traveling inside, condensing and dripping. The belt drives slap in the sheds, the chains drag in the canals, the sheet metal pops and expands in the heat like knuckles cracking all over town. The electricity hums, faintly, in lamps that are on all day.

These are not tools anymore. They are fauna by default, the way the plants are flora by default. The boilers produce the heat that makes the ferns in the sheds possible. The fans produce the wind that makes the flowers possible. The pumps produce the moving water that keeps the reeds in the sumps filtering the outflow with their feet in warm effluent, keeps the canals from stagnating into something worse, keeps the roots in the walls fed at night. In return, the plants condense the steam the boilers make — morning is condensation, every cold surface beaded, the brick cold to the palm despite the air — and transpire it back at midday. The creepers that have covered the copper wiring in the yards do something stranger: they conduct, not electricity, but a slow, living current along the wires, bridging a system built for something else. The trees drink the condensate and shade the machines. The vines seal the sheds and moderate the heat. No one eats anyone. Predator and prey have been replaced by pump and pressure, transpiration and condensation, growth and rust, exactly balanced, so nothing falls fast. A wall leans on a tree. A tree drinks a pipe. A pipe feeds a boiler. A boiler vents on a fern.

There are, perhaps, a few snails. In the dampest corners, on the sweating walls of the boiler houses, you might find one, or the silver trail of one, grazing the biofilm slowly, carrying its house with it, the only animal in Paludra that has anything like a face and has decided to keep it mostly hidden. No birds ever took the perfect trees. No insects ever took the perfect air. No frogs, no rats, no mosquitoes even in all that standing water — the absence itself has shaped everything that remains. Seeds travel not in bellies but on water, on fan-blades, on the sideways rain. Flowers smell fermented because there is no animal nose to please. The leaf litter does not get eaten, it gets composted where it falls, steaming, and the town gets its fertility back in the same place.

So Paludra's inhabitants live without calling to each other, without chasing or fleeing, without ever quite leaving. The lichen lives on the wall for a century. The fern lives in the machine hall for a season of uncurling. The pipe-drinking tree lives as long as its boiler keeps its timer. The bacteria live for an hour and are replaced by their own descendants in the warm mud. The boilers live as long as their cycles are not interrupted, which is to say they live indefinitely, purposefully, producing nothing but heat and movement and water moved from one place to the next and back again.

At night, when the lamps make halos in the steam and show you nothing beyond twenty paces, you can hear them all at once — the pumps heartbeating, the metal ticking, the trunks creaking, the roofs dripping long after the rain has stopped, the fans clanking pollen through the dark. It is loud, improbably loud for a place so dim and so empty, because the town is small and the lives in it are not busy with each other. They are busy with circulation. They breathe steam in and water out, root into iron and iron into root, and keep the bowl of Paludra in its settled, olive-black, damp balance: slightly too wet, slightly too hot, slightly too dark, and alive in every place where something has learned to live on a surface, in a pipe, in a pond skin, in the warm ground, without ever needing to look up.
