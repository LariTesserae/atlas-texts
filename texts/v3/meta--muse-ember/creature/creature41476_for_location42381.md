You wait for something to cross the catwalk.

It doesn't. The dripping goes on arriving in irregular ranks from a hundred feet up, the low rush to the left goes on being cut off somewhere behind rock, the pumps go on changing their pitch. Your own breathing comes back to you off the wet walls, a little late, and nothing answers it except itself. In the white silt in the corners your footprints are already losing their edges. Water finds them from below and seals them over, as patiently as if it were erasing a name.

That is how you learn the population, at first: by what moves without ever walking.

At the bottom, under the black glossy slabs and under the fog when the fog is down, the water is not empty. It is full, but low and without face. The canals and pools have gone deep translucent green with algae, a clean, lightless green that makes the black reflective surfaces look lit from within. Nothing stirs it. No fish turn in it, no insect touches it from above, no duck, no ripple except the machines' own and the rain's. So the algae can afford to be a skin. It lies in sheets over the slower water, over the service joints where reeds and ferns rise out of the marsh-level feet of the habitation shells. It thickens where the light panels are dimmest. It breathes, slowly, in the chemistry of its own stillness, and does not need to flee.

The ferns are a little higher, where the warm air comes out of the vents. They have colonized the vents the way moss colonizes a roof elsewhere, except here there is nothing to keep them off. Each frond unfurls unbitten, broad and unhurried, into the exhaust. You can see the spores dusting out when a valve sighs and a district gives up steam. There are no thorns here, no bitter leaves, no curled defenses. Nothing has ever taken a bite out of anything, so nothing has learned to say no. The life is terribly, checkably abundant in the way of a garden with no gardener and no deer and no season of pruning, leaves overlapping leaves until the undersides of the catwalks are upholstered in moss, soft and wet and completely in shade, as if the plants have chosen the one place the rain does not directly strike.

They lean. That is the next thing, and it is harder to see than a footprint. On the middle tiers, where the lattice masts are impossibly thin and the galleries are hung and lit sparsely, the vines have threaded themselves through the transit tubes. They go in one end in the amber lower city and out the other in the green gardens, flowering in the dim with small pallid blooms that open when the fog lifts and close when it thickens. Along the cables that run like vines from module to module, real vines run back, clinging, reaching. They grow, unmistakably, toward the loudest sources: toward the pumps, toward the long sheet of water coming down from the ceiling and breaking into rain halfway across the void. In the absence of sun they have substituted sound. If you stand still long enough on a middle platform you can feel them doing it, a whole hanging population turning its faces to the noise the city makes for itself, drinking the rain that falls upward for a minute on the ceramic walls and then gives up and falls.

The trees are the stillest residents and the largest. In the hollow center of a reservoir tower, where the water is stored and the roots are infrastructure as much as the pipes, real tree trunks as thick as masts rise through the damp. Their canopies spread under the ceiling panels — those cloudy distant stars that give the hollow its 0.7 dim — and catch what green-amber light is going. Their roots lace the living ceramic, stabilize the fresh sediment that has not yet learned to be soil, filter the warm air that sits thick on your lungs. They do not move. They do not need to. The fog tide does their weather for them: a shallow sea at ankle depth at the bottom, smelling of wet stone and warm soil and ozone, then the slow sigh of the pumps and the fog lifting, climbing the walls, gathering on the vault until it is heavy enough to fall again. A rain cell can form over the west tiers while you watch, drift east, dissolve over the central emptiness. The trees drink it. The ferns drink it. Everything drinks it.

The fungi keep to the places plants cannot quite use. In the dark shuttered modules, behind the clear beaded film that seals their doors, there are pale fans and threads on the damp ceramic, appearing overnight on a new edge that was razor-straight yesterday. The growth pops — faint tick and pop, like warm ice cracking — and that is the only sound they make. They are the decomposers in a place with nothing dead to decompose except plant litter and mineral dust, so they have turned to that: to fallen fronds, to spores that never found wind enough, to the first skin of white silt in the corners. They thrive in the sealed rooms precisely because the rooms are sealed. No animal has ever nosed in here. No animal could tolerate what holds the seal, the field, the water chemistry of the rain that builds. The fungi do not care. They eat the damp and give back breath.

And then, everywhere, and not as servants but as the main fauna, there are the machines.

No bird crosses the vault because the vault is already occupied by circulation. The pumps are hearts with changing pitches, knocking in the pipes, sighing a whole district awake with steam when a valve opens somewhere. The exchange towers are lungs, taking in the saturated air and giving it back warmer. The reservoirs hold and release. The transit arteries carry no passengers and carry everything else — water, mist, pressurized air, pulses of light in tubes — so that the tubes are never empty even when the catwalks are. Cables run like vines and vines run like cables and it is not clear, after a while, who is imitating whom. The machines reroute the canals around new shallows with a soft arguing rush, they maintain the subsonic pressure you feel in your teeth where a platform floats a centimeter above its mount in quiet continuous correction, they lay down new structure at the end of a gallery a centimeter at a time. They are not waiting for residents. They are working, richly, in excess, because working is what lives here in the absence of anything that can be eaten or can eat.

The fields are the invisible part of that life. You see them only as a shimmer where gravity feels a fraction off and mist hangs a moment too long before it decides to fall. They are what thin beams use to hold great weight, what lets hollow beams full of pressurized mist balance, what shapes the new white-grey ceramic as it precipitates — silica and iron and polymer laid down atom by atom from water drawn out of the condensing air, out of the pools, out of the weeping rock. The buildings grow by drinking their own rain. A tower seeds itself and the rain does the work. The stalactites overhead, needles a few centimeters long, are the same population at an earlier stage, still deciding whether to become anything, mineral life at the pace of geology. The walls are fresh fracture planes, black and glittering, still sharp enough to catch skin; life here is not only what has grown but what is in the act of hardening into place.

Water, in the end, is the first inhabitant and the one all the others arrange themselves around. It condenses on you because you are cooler than the air. It beads on the new ceramic, on the metal, on the rock that weeps uniformly like a sponge squeezed from below. It falls in a sheet, it climbs a film up a wall against the pull, it rises in the basins a fraction when a garden drinks. It carries seeds — there is no other carrier — on the wind the machines make for themselves. It cycles endlessly among plant, fungus, mineral, machine, and itself, and nothing in the cycle is ever finished.

That is the census. No voice. No footprint that lasts. No scurry, no swim, no flight. A city that could hold thousands holding almost no one, dense with construction and sparse with presence, because presence here was never measured in heads. The modules are dark and shuttered and still inhabited by film and fungus and the slow growth of their own edges. The greenhouses glow faintly and their vines sway though the only wind is the one made to keep the air moving. The trees stand in their reservoirs. The algae lie in their green water. The ferns lean toward the pumps. The fields hold the mist up. And underneath it all the geothermal floor gives up its heat, tropical and enclosed, so that nothing ever quite gets cold enough to stop.

If people ever come, they will not be entering an empty place. They will be the first animals in it, stepping into a working ecology that has learned to live without them — plants listening for sound, fungi eating stone-damp, machines breathing for a city, water raining inside a room — and the silt will start sealing over their footprints too, and the vines, unbitten and unhurried, will lean a fraction toward the new noise, as if to see what kind of weather has finally arrived.
