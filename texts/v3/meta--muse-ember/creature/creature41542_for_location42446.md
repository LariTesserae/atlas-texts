It would be a mistake to say nothing lives here. It would be a greater mistake to count what you see and stop.

On a first crossing you might see nothing that moves on its own at all. That is not because the chamber is empty. It is because everything that lives here has learned, as a condition of staying alive, not to stay.

The animals you can name are the least settled part of it.

In a pool that catches extra light, a school of small translucent fish turns all at once in the shallow water and is gone under a terrace lip. They are perhaps five centimeters long, glassy enough that you see their gut working against the bright sinter beneath them. If you watch for them as residents of that pool, you will be disappointed. The pool is only a polished hollow in a sheet centimeters deep, and every few minutes a wave lifts the sheet a hand's breadth and pushes it up the walls. A fish that lingered in the open would be stranded on wet clay, or washed into a crack. They have no real eyes — in a day-bright room with no variation of light to read, eyes were a cost they stopped paying — and they do not turn together because they are social. They turn together because the pressure wave that makes the water tide makes them turn, the same wave that makes the mist surge and the stone tick. They navigate by touch and by the boom-drip falling in the hollow place, by chemical gradients in the film. Stone, water, and spray are their whole sensory world, and all three are loud. They graze the film as they go, deliberate and slow, then slip under the lip when the next change comes. They are not so much inhabitants of a pool as brief concentrations in a current.

The cloud of midges near a vent where spray breaks is nearer to a true population, but still not a settled one. They hang in the light where the spray is richest, and disperse when you approach — not from fear, exactly, but because your body blocks the light and warms the air and disturbs the updraft they ride. They mate in midair, live in midair, and their larvae live nowhere you can find for long. They are feeding on something you cannot see yet, concentrated there by the vent.

Once — perhaps only once in a crossing — a pale amphibian longer than your hand clings to a weeping wall. It is the largest thing that lives out of water here, and it is white-pale, camouflaged against the sinter, pulsing visibly through skin it breathes through. It can afford to sit still hours at a time because the wall delivers fresh water to it constantly; it does not need to hunt or drink in any ordinary way. Then a rivulet that was running down begins, perversely, to run up, breaks, and rains, and knocks it loose. It swims, very deliberately, for deeper water, for one of the low dark mouths where black water runs out and sighs back in. It does not belong to the wall, or to the pool. It belongs to the circulation between them, and deeper water is where it goes when the surface of the chamber becomes, as it always does, unlivable for sitting still.

These are the spectacular rarities. The permanent population is spread so thin through air and water that you mistake it for weather.

Look again at the dust-motes. There are too many, and they are too regular, drifting in the updrafts, holding the brightness longer than dust should. In the central column the self-made wind lifts them, in the surge of mist they dim to pearl grey, when the light returns like a switch they brighten and rise again. They are not dust. They are small translucent bodies, many no bigger than a pinhead, some in chains, some spherical, living their whole lives without landing. If they landed they would be crusted over in white sinter by afternoon and lost. So the chamber's own rain that never really lands — condensing, falling six feet, caught by the updraft, becoming mist again — becomes their habitat. They are aerial plankton. They feed in the light, on dissolved iron and ozone and the mineral lace the spray carries, and are eaten in turn by the midges. The smell of wet stone and iron and ozone is not only the stone. It is partly them, exhaled and dissolved and rebreathed. You breathe them as you stand there. Your breath comes back as mist among mist.

Look down through the clear water at your ankles. In most places the film is only centimeters deep, but it is not clear. Kneel, if you can, and let your eyes adjust past the ripple your boots made. Under the film is a skin, everywhere, that glints when the light changes. That is the second and larger population, and the one that has actually built the room you are walking on.

The small silver threads in the shallow water, no thicker than hair, moving against the current, pausing over a crack in the terrace and brightening, are not threads. They are filaments of a colonial organism, braided and re-braided, extending tendrils upstream into fresher water, depositing mineral behind them. The crack does not heal. It is grazed. As they feed and excrete they lay down a new lace of sinter over the place they have just passed, overnight-fast. The terraces grow because these colonies grow. The terraces dissolve in the same places because when a colony exhausts its crack, or is buried by its own precipitate, or is scoured by a wave, it dissolves, releases what it took, and the next colony to colonize that same depression starts again.

Beneath and among them, in the film itself, is a crowd of smaller precipitators — bacteria and fungi and grazing protists no larger than the weave of the water — building the white crust grain by grain, then being entombed by it, then dissolving back into the water that made them. No corpses remain. A dead body here becomes mineral before it becomes carrion. This is why the room can look identical over a day and never the same over a minute: creation and small destruction are the same process, running at different speeds in different places, balanced.

The pale moss mats are the same principle made visible at a larger scale. A mat on a rock that is sprayed but not scoured looks like moss, and behaves nothing like moss. It does not root and stay. It extends on one side and dies back on the other, growing a meter over in the course of a day, retreating from a place that was green in the morning and is white with fresh sinter by afternoon. What you call a patch is a slow animal sheet, photosynthetic but translucent, bleaching to colorless green because the artificial day never varies and pigment is wasted. When sinter covers it, it does not die outright; it goes thin, white, dormant, and reappears where the spray is kinder. The reed clumps where the water slackens are stiffer and more sacrificial. Each upright shoot is knee-high and bent permanently sideways, because a gust comes every few minutes and flattens everything upright. The plant — if plant is the right word for a colony that sends up tubes of symbionts — cannot afford to grow up. It grows bent, and sends new shoots downstream where current is slack, and lets the old ones be crusted and dissolved. The fern-form gripping a seam in the wall is bleached almost to transparency, its leaves thin as wet paper, breathing through the wall's weeping rather than through any soil.

Everything here eats the film, the mist, the mineral lace, or each other when they cross paths, and everything is recycled by dissolution. There are no predators you would recognize, no nests, no burrows, no territories, no caches, no calls above the roar. Sound is useless; the roar of moving water amplified by stone drowns anything a throat could make. Sight is nearly useless; the light is uniform and bright. What remains is chemical sensing, pressure sensing, and a constant, low-grade movement in response to a tide that never stops. To be alive here is to be carried, to extend and retract, to precipitate and dissolve, and to never be in the same configuration for long.

It is the surface, remembered. Warm air. Rain that does not fall. Daylight that does not come from the sky. Ferns, reeds, fish, amphibians, clouds in spray — all of them reduced to their essential gestures and made translucent, bleached, brief. The machine that was poured into the geopolymer to make this loop — evaporate, condense, precipitate, dissolve, circulate — was presumably built to imitate a surface world. Life did not so much move into the machine as dissolve into its medium, until you can no longer tell where engineering ends and biology begins. The built things — the half-disc door, the tiled bench, the railing ending in air — are ignored by everything that lives here. They are too large, too dry inside, too much of a memory. The inhabitants took the water and the stone and the air instead.

The vast emptiness between pool and door and reed clump is therefore not emptiness. It is density spread over a whole volume: a few drifters per cubic meter of mist, a film of precipitators under every step, a braided thread in every polished hollow, a mat moving a meter over and working its way out of sight. You walk through them and they part and close behind you. The ripple your boot makes is not only water. It is a disturbance in a meadow.

Stand in the middle long enough and your own warmth adds to the circulation. Condensation beads on your skin and is taken up by the drifters. Bacteria from your boots colonize the footprint terrace before it fills and empties again. For the minutes you are there, you are a transient niche, like the moss, like the fish, like the amphibian knocked loose from its wall. Then you go back up the wet slope and the stair, and the chamber closes its loop without you, creating and destroying in the same places, holding together a low, comprehensible strangeness: a cave that is bright, a storm that keeps the room in shape, and a population that lives precisely by never settling in it.
