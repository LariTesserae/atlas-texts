If you try to count the Low, you will count it wrong.

Forty-three permanent, the charts say, and a rotating dozen. That is the human count, and it is true in the way a manifest is true. But the Low is not populated the way a surface town is populated, by addition. It is inhabited the way a shell is inhabited, in layers, each layer holding the next one up, and by the time you have lived here a month you stop trying to separate them. The people live inside the plants live inside the machines live inside the warmth, and the warmth lives inside a basalt cup, and everything is leaning, very slowly, inward.

Start with the people, because they are the ones who will introduce you to everything else, and because they are, by biomass, almost the least of it.

They are two generations deep now. The first came down on the lift sixty years ago when the vent field was only a survey mark and a promise of heat that did not need to be made. They stayed because the suit heaters failed on the third day and never quite became necessary again, and because the work turned out to be patient work, the kind that suits people who do not mind being at the bottom of something. They are old now in the way everything here is old, not damaged so much as settled. You can tell them on the street by their suits. The suits have been re-riveted so many times the seams are maps. The knees are white with mineral. Their gloves are always a little too big, handed down.

Their children came down as babies or were born in the dry pods, and have never, most of them, been up through the lid. The lid is not a story to them. It is a ceiling made of yellow-green water and the place where your ears would hurt if you went too fast. They know the surface the way you know a word in a language you do not speak: *sky, rain, June* — June being, in the Low, not a month but the kelp-cutting, and July the thinning of the fan-grass from the pump house. They learned to read before they learned to swim, because swimming is not quite the word. You walk in the Low. You walk slowly, in water that holds you up and holds you back, and you learn the town by your hands first. Third porthole from the bend in Sinter Lane. The pipe that sings when it warms. The place where the street softens under the seagrass.

No one here is in a hurry, and that is not a mannerism. Their bodies have adjusted to a place where everything falls slowly and grows slowly and the smelter cycle is measured in shifts. They sleep in bunks in the dry pods while the water presses gently on the hulls all around them, a pressure you feel in your molars more than your skin. The dry pods are the only true inside: stale air that smells of machine oil and laundry and tea, a luxury they use sparingly, the way other people use shade. That is where they gather to be un-helmeted, to gossip in voices that do not hiss, to eat the algae cakes that are better warm, to argue about the council and the vent. The vent is older than anyone and has a vocabulary of its own — warming, holding, cooling, turning — and to live here is to learn its phases the way farmers learn weather, except the weather is a slow geological breathing to the south, and it will outlast them all.

Their work is what they are, mostly. The pump quarter is deepest, down-slope where the pressure helps, and there are the Listeners. They do the night — there is no night, only the dimmer shift — and they do not so much watch the pumps as inhabit them. The three fan blades at the centre of town, each as wide as a room, turn so slowly you could watch one revolution and miss it, and the Listeners feel them through the soles of their boots and in their teeth, a slow breathing of the floor. When a pipe warms and settles and sings a single low note, someone always knows which pipe it was. The fans are the oldest machines in the Low and they are also, now, partly plants. Fan-grass grows in perfect rosettes through the grates, one frond per slat, in the ducts, in the corners of kitchens. The fans turn a fraction slower each year. Nobody minds. That is an ethic as much as a fact.

The Pruners are a municipal service with a schedule that goes back decades, a crew with shears and a list. They are the gardeners of a flooded town and they treat it with the seriousness of people who know that if they stop, the town will simply grow shut. Iron-weed pours down the factory walls in sheets. Kelp trees — trunked, branching, higher than the houses — stand around the rim and lean east, toward the one true dark beyond the basalt, swaying an inch to the left over the course of an hour. Someone has to keep the kelp off the lift cable every June, thin the fan-grass every July, cut back the sponge from the joints where it wants to fill them, clear the anemones that open, uninvited, in shoeboxes left by a door. The Pruners know individual plants the way the Listeners know individual pipes. They can tell you which rosette has been there longest, which wall of iron-weed will flicker first when a batch is poured.

Ah, the flickering. That is not the people, but the people will show it to you only when they are showing you around, because it is the closest thing the Low has to a wonder that is ordinary. When the smelters pour, a wave of pale blue light runs quick as a blink across the weed on the sheds. The plants are lit by the heat cycle; the whole north wall breathes with the forges. At the dimmer shift, when the lamps drop to half and the amber street-grid goes the color of old tea, the seagrass beds between the slabs of the main street give off a faint green glow of their own, like a field holding its breath. The algae are not eating light, not really. There is almost no light to eat. They are eating warmth and mineral wash and the steady nutrient soup the smelters put out, and in return they make the town legible in the dark. They are, by any honest accounting, the Low's true natives and its true population. The orange bacterial mats banding the vent stacks, soft with sponge, crusted white — they were here first. The town was built in their soup, and they have simply continued, growing over everything the people built, patiently incorporating it. A tool dropped in the street sinks, lands with a soft *tok*, fuzzes orange, then green, then part of the wall within a year. Nothing erodes dramatically. Everything accretes. The oldest conduits are becoming white-boned, mineral a millimetre at a time, and every few years the council has to ream out the Sinter Lane underpass because the passage is growing shut. In twenty years a room in the pump quarter will be too small to enter. The inhabitants are not alarmed. They understand the town is growing inward, the way a shell grows.

This is why the non-human residents are so few and so known. In a place this crowded — houses pressed to factory walls, pipes overhead so low you duck, conduits doing double duty as handrails and laundry lines and trellises, crates and spare fans and bicycles with magnetic wheels filling every corner and then filled again — there is nothing for animals to be wild in. So the animals that remain are all, in effect, neighbors.

There are perhaps a dozen vent crabs, saucer-sized, pale, picking their way over the slag with terrible patience. The Low knows them individually by the pattern of white crust on a claw or the way one favors the south slope of the smelter sheds. They do not scuttle. They arrive. They stay for a season near a warm joint, feeding on film, and then they move, a handspan at a time, to another warm joint. They are the slowest pedestrians in town, slower even than the kelp.

In the old cooling tower live the two long eels. The whole town knows them by names that are affectionate and inaccurate and have been argued over for years — *Knot* and *Unknot*, because they tie themselves in loops in the tower's dark at the dimmer shift and hang there like grey ribbons, motionless, visible if you know where to look through the porthole fuzzed with algae. They come out only then, when the lamps are low, to hang in the open water and be looked at. No one feeds them. No one needs to. They are simply a pair of residents whose habits everyone has learned to time their walks around.

In the archive tank lives a single blind octopus. It is considered, by common consent, a resident — counted, informally, as the forty-fourth permanent, which makes the official number a joke everyone tells newcomers. It lives among the waterproof ledgers and charts and survey tapes, curling its arms around a spine it favors, tasting the paper with a patience the archivist, who goes in every morning to check, finds companionable. It has never been outside the tank. It does not need to be convinced the tank is the world. It has the whole archive to itself, in the dark, in the warmth, and it is quite content to be its librarian.

Beyond that, the animal life is mostly the ones in the market tank. Eleven metres across is the whole market square, and that is considered generous, and in the middle of it someone — no one remembers who first, which means it has been tended for at least a generation — keeps a tank of small bright fish that are not for eating but for looking at. They are bred, somehow, in the soup, generation after generation of orange and blue and quicksilver, pulsing in the amber light. People stop on the way from the pumps to watch them for a minute, helmet to glass, and that is the luxury the Low has chosen for itself. The mackerel that pass at the rim, on the cold side, do not count. They are shadows in the darker, clearer water beyond the basalt, indifferent, and they do not come in. The outside of the rim is the only true dark, and the town keeps a viewing port in the east wall for people who want to look into it. The kelp lean that way too.

And then there are the inhabitants who are neither people nor plants nor animals in any clean sense, but who are the reason the others can stay. The sponge — orange and white — that fills the joints of the pipes and filters the water as it passes. The anemones that open in shoeboxes. The seagrass that has colonized the silt between the concrete slabs and softened the main street underfoot, so your boots never quite clank. The iron-weed. The fan-grass. The kelp trees, tended and cut back and allowed to grow a handspan a year, which is the Low's measure of time. The bacteria that band the stacks. The mineral crust itself, thickening the conduits, narrowing the corridors, turning a dropped wrench into a white bone.

The rotating dozen are the only ones who do not quite belong, and they know it. They come up on the lift with surface news and photographs of a sky no one down here quite believes in, and they stay a month, or a season, learning the labyrinth by touching, counting portholes, learning not to hurry. Some of them go up homesick for cold, for air that does not hiss, for the empty kind of silence. A few of them get down through the lid — that softening, that soupy yellow-green from above, a stain, from inside a ceiling — and decide they cannot bear coming back up through it, and stay, and become permanent, and have their suits re-riveted, and in a year their heaters cut out and stay out, and a bare hand, if they are foolish enough to take a glove off in the street, comes back feeling it has been held.

That is the ecology of the Low, in the end: mostly plant and machine, holding each other up, with forty-three people and a dozen crabs and two eels and one octopus and a tank of bright fish living in the middle of it, pruning the fans, listening for the pumps to breathe, reaming the tunnel, pouring tea through a coil that sits in the smelter waste-line, and growing — the town, the weed, the crust, themselves — slower and smaller and greener, inch by inch, inward.
