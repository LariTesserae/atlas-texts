If you stay still long enough, the first thing you understand is that the room is not empty between the buildings. It is crowded in a way that has nothing to do with buildings.

You hear them before you can sort them. A low drone from below, a thump-thump from the press, drips ringing on a tray — and over that, a continuous, many-throated noise that rises when the engine cycles up and settles, but never stops, when it cycles down. Croaks and clicks and wingbeats and the scrabble of claws and a squeaking from under the floor. It is not a chorus with a soloist. It is a population talking at once, most of them blind, most of them small, all of them tuned to vibration through root and pipe and wet timber.

They live in layers, all at once, none of them much interested in the ground plan of the hamlet.

High up, in the dark between the ceiling and the lamp-string, are the birds that are not quite birds. Legless, blind, with paddle wings broad as hands. They nest in the pipework — not feathered nests, but tangles of shed skin, fruit husk, and moulted wing-scale packed into the elbows of pipes. They hang upside down to sleep, paddling slowly against the warm air to keep station, and they navigate by the hum in the metal and by the chattering bursts they make when a lamp flickers. When a lamp brightens, they go off all at once in a clatter that sounds like falling cutlery, rowing out into the dark on those paddle wings to hunt in mid-air. What they drop — guano, undigested seed, the occasional dead chick — falls through the other layers and is not lost.

On the roofs, in the pools of amber light, the lizards sun. They are warm-bodied, which is wrong for lizards, and slow because of it, as if they are forever thawing. They sprawl tail-out in the lamp pools, flattened, eyes half shut, letting the heat work its way up their bodies before they move at all. They nest in chimney hollows and under overlapping leaf shingles, a dozen soft bodies sharing the same warm hollow, eggs tucked against the living wood where it is thickest. When they do move, it is to snap at a moth crossing the light, or to shift a little deeper into the pool, and then to be still again for a long time.

Between those two layers is the weather of insects. Moths and beetles and flies the size of your thumbnail in a constant, swarming migration that never quite leaves the room. They are drawn to the lamps, to fermentation, to the cream fungal flowers in the unlit corners, and above all to each other — a living dust that thickens near a press, thins near the cold wall, thickens again when the engine fires and fermentation smells stronger. They pollinate nothing that needs pollinating in any old sense, but they carry spores from flower to flower, from rotten fruit to living leaf, and they are food for everything that can fly, dart, or jump. In lamp-light their wings flare white, then are gone.

On the walls, the geckos. Pale, translucent, they dart across root-laced rock and grown wood in vertical bursts, hunting the springtails that graze the algae on the walls. They keep to the damp margins where the rock weeps and the leaves overhead never quite dry, and they are almost invisible until they move — a flicker of wet skin against green-black shadow. Where the stalactites have been snapped and polished, it is partly their scratching, partly the birds', that has taken the edge off.

The floor is where the population becomes overwhelming, and also where you begin to see how old the arrangement is.

The woodlice move in processions along the moist lanes, forearm-long, armored, lifting their backs as they walk to show the soft gills and, in the females, the brood pouch underneath. They are slow and processional because they are, in effect, the room's gardeners. They eat fallen leaf and fungal felt, they turn the mat of roots and loam over and over, they carry the lane's earth in their gut and leave it richer behind them. You will find their trails worn into the spongy off-lane ground, a faint, regular track where a generation has followed a generation.

In every hollow that holds water for more than a day — a fern-cup, a pocked basin in the root mat, a cart-wheel turned planter — frogs sit half-submerged. A dozen pale species, indistinguishable by color in this dimness, distinguished perfectly by call. They croak in overlapping waves, a male starting and two others answering on a different pitch, until the individual notes merge into that thick, wet chord you hear from the stair. Their skin is a way of drinking. They do not need a pond; condensation beading on a cold pipe, dew weeping from the rock when warm air hits it, a spoonful of water held for days in a leaf — that is their budget, and they have learned to count it. They sit, they drink through their bellies, they sing, they are eaten by nothing in particular and therefore by everything in turn: by lizards, by birds that swoop low, by the flies that lay in the water film on their backs.

Under the frogs' feet, in tunnels that never quite close because roots grow back into them as fast as they are dug, the mole-rats. Blind, pink, powerful-toothed, social. They worry at the roots of the central tree, at rhizomes and fallen fruit, and carry cuttings back in their mouths to burrows lined with fungal felt. Their squeaking comes up through the floor mat when you step in the wrong place, and their runnels are part of why the floor gives like a mattress — they are constantly loosening the substrate the woodlice then pack down again. The burnish on the stair handrail, warm dark brown where hands once were, is partly theirs now: something smooth and low that hauls itself up and down, using the rail as a scent-road and the roots wound around it as a ladder.

And under all of that, at a scale where the room's warmth is a climate, the micro-fauna that make the leaves themselves seem to tremble. Mites grazing the green algae that fogs the gauges, so that a gauge blinks faintly when they move across it. Springtails in the moss carpet between the silos — it bounces underfoot because they are in it by the thousand, jumping in unison at the vibration of a footfall, lifting, settling, jumping again. Rotifers glittering in the condensation on the coldest pipes in the machine-morning, spinning in a bead of water for an hour, a day, until the bead runs or evaporates and their world ends. Bacteria bloom on everything warm. Fermentation is the chief smell because bacteria are, in numbers, the true majority of the inhabitants, and everything else is, to some extent, farming them or being farmed by them.

The plants are inhabitants too, and not the quietest. The central tree is the eldest resident. Its hard fruits are carried single-file by the old conveyor to the press that thumps in its sleepy rhythm, and the press's crushings feed the fungi below, and the fungi feed the mites, and the mites feed the geckos. The tree's roots are a thoroughfare — lianas along the walls pulse faintly when the engines fire, carrying water and sugar where copper once carried current, and the pulse travels down into the root mat where the mole-rats follow it by smell.

The ferns that blanket the motors are not decoration. They are tenants that have learned to soften a hum into a habitat, unfurling leaf by leaf toward the lamps and the warm vents, pale and reaching, flattening their fronds against whatever they had to climb. Some have gone further into mimicry than camouflage requires: a fern that opens exactly like a gauge face, a vine that knots itself into a valve handle, moss spreading on the north wall in the precise branching pattern of a wiring diagram. They are not imitating the factory. They are the factory's descendants, remembering its shapes the way an old hand repeats a motion until the motion becomes anatomy.

The fungi hold the whole thing together and eat the rest of it in turn. Dull glows where colonies have set on wet timber carry, at night in the machine-sense, a little farther than any animal can see. Cream flowers open in unlit corners and smell of overripe fruit, drawing the moths. Pale sheets digest dead animals, dead paint, dead leaves, the fallen birds from the pipes, and make of it all the felt the woodlice turn and the frogs' water pools sit in.

When the engine cycles up, this is their daybreak. The lamps brighten, the bioluminescent moss on the north wall dims as if embarrassed, the plants creak as leaves unfold and roots shift in their sockets, the birds go off chattering, the frogs rise a key, the insects thicken, the lizards in the lamp pools thaw and move, the lianas pulse faster, the press thumps harder. When it cycles down, the moss brightens, the animals settle a little, the drips become distinct again, the rotifers start spinning in the new condensation. No one here has a sky to check it against. They have this.

Nothing organic is wasted, because there are too many mouths and too little water for waste to be a luxury. The birds leave fruit for the press. The press leaves pulp for the fungus. The fungus feeds the mites. The mites feed the geckos. The woodlice eat the leaf mat the tree sheds. The mole-rats eat the tree's roots and are eaten, in another season, by whatever can get into a burrow that never quite closes. The lizards eat the moths that ate the fungal flowers that ate the lizards' own dead. It is a slow rotation, active, never quite speeding up.

They are mostly blind and almost entirely communicative by vibration and smell and song, because light is rationed and water is rationed and warmth is not. Colors have gone muted to rust-orange under algae and cream in the dark, and then, crossing a lamp, sudden hallucinatory white.

There are no people living there now. The stair was cut for people who needed the machines. The rail remembers their hands. The houses that are hollow trunks and grown towers were finished by plants after people stopped finishing them. What lives in the room now is this — a hamlet at eight minutes' brisk walk from wall to wall, warm as a body at 28, 30 degrees, thriving on drips, holding its breath in the empty lanes between clusters so the whole chamber can respire through them.

If you stay, you do not become part of it. You become a brief warm disturbance, a new vibration on the floor mat, a tongue of unfamiliar smell. The frogs will pause, the springtails will jump, the birds will chatter once at the lamp and settle. And when you go back up the worn stair, the warmth will leave your clothes in minutes, but a fine invisible spore will stay on your sleeves, tacky to the touch — the room's way of noting, in its own population count, that something large and temporary passed through, and was, for a little while, inhabited too.
