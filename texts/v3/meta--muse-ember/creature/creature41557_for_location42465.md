If you stay, you begin to understand that almost nothing here wishes to be noticed, and even less wishes to move.

At first the Shelf looks finished — settled, beveled, abandoned to sun and wind. But wait beside a warm face of basalt long enough, and you see that the dark tracery spreading across it is not a stain. It is moving, in the only sense this place allows.

The stoneblooms are the first life and the foundation of all the others, though they are neither stone nor flower. Where the rock holds heat, especially on the cliff faces near the towns where waste heat leaks from ducts and mirrors, a forest in miniature creeps outward: filament networks of metal oxides and silicate crystals, fractal and black-red and copper-green, a centimeter in a century. They catch sunlight and charged dust and fix them into a slow chemistry. They do not leaf or root. They simply extend, vein by vein, until a whole wall seems lichenous, rusted, alive with a pattern too fine to read from a distance.

And from that almost imperceptible work, everything else is fed. The stonebloom is food by being ground down. It is fuel by being rich in oxides. In a world with no plant life at all, this mineral bloom is the producer — the green field, replaced by copper.

The grazers are the oldest residents, and for a long time you will mistake them for part of the geology. They are multi-ton beasts, low-slung and broad, with carapaces like etched basalt. The stonebloom colonizes their shells in natural lace, and then their own rasping mouths scrape it clean again. Their legs are pillars of fused mineral and living tissue, planted on the plain as if the ground itself had decided to walk.

A herd is not a cluster. Thirty of them will be spread across a square kilometre, each one working a cliff face, each one moving perhaps ten metres in a year. You do not hear them sing. You feel them. Lie down on the warm slabs and a pulse comes up through the stone, days apart, the heartbeat of something so large and so slow that its life is measured in the same units as erosion. When one shifts its weight — an event that takes an afternoon — dust falls from its flank in a slow curtain, and a flock of smaller things living in that dust startles and resettles.

They speak, after their fashion, by the ground: by pressure waves traveling through rock, by faint thermal flashes that bloom on their flanks, by the scrape of enormous bodies against stone. To see one, you must learn to stop looking for motion. A boulder on the plain may turn its head, very slowly, toward the bloom. A whole valley of boulders may, over a season, have moved a hand's width closer to the cliff.

Above them, riding the warm updrafts that rise off the cliffs all day, hang the air-fauna. They are not birds, and they have no need to land. Each is a sail of translucent, heat-hardened membrane strung on filaments of chitin and glass, as large as a boat's sail, and there are hundreds at a time in a single thermal. They feed on what the dry air carries — static charge, sand motes, the faint electrical exhalation of the towns far below. From underneath they are only a shimmer, a change in the light that makes you shade your eyes and look again. From above, if you ever rise above them, they would look like a constellation that has learned to drift. They are entirely silent. They never touch the ground that feeds them. Their whole life is suspended in heat.

It is only where the towns are that life takes on a different, second layer — not older, but no less integrated.

The industrial towns are spread thin across the Shelf in an archipelago of terraces: foundry terraces, smelter hollows, electrolytic arrays, grinding mills, separated by tens of kilometres of empty plain and joined by conveyor trusses crawling on spindly legs at a pace that is almost geological. A few thousand people live in each, and a great many machines. Everything is riveted, domed, half-buried against the wind, softened by a pale, chalky oxidation that makes even new machinery look ancient. Furnaces are lit and stay lit for a generation. Their plumes trail fifty kilometres behind them, so straight and patient they look painted on.

In the heat those towns make, other residents have settled. Stoke-wyrms, six-legged and scaled in dull metal colors, inhabit the slag channels and cooling ducts, eating carbon ash and excreting ingots of pure mineral — creatures that quite literally process what the town discards and return it in a form the town can use. On the solar mirrors, flat pale lizards bask without fleeing from you. They do not fear you because you are slower than they are, and therefore not a threat; speed, in their world, is the only language danger speaks. And down in the resonant cavities beneath the terraces, where the wind plays the perforated sides of the towers like an organ pipe, burrowers live eyeless and blind. They eat the stonebloom when it creeps into the tunnels, and in eating it, keep the foundations from being choked. The towns were built to process the Shelf, and the Shelf's life has learned to process the towns. The relationship is old, and mutual, and neither side could now be removed without the other overgrowing or starving.

The people themselves move through this with the same unhurried patience. They are small figures in sealed, dust-proof suits, going lumped and deliberate between terraces, their voices muffled inside the suits. They talk little, and what they say is practical, spaced out by the thin air that mutes everything until a tick of cooling metal in the afternoon can ring for a minute. A bearing clanks, a slab of slag slumps, and the sound arrives across the plain long after you have seen the dust move. They tend furnaces on cycles measured in decades, cool their buildings by radiant fins and by dry air blown through vast towers, and live, as everything here lives, by making their own moisture inside themselves and keeping it. Nothing is allowed to run free.

At night the whole arrangement gathers itself. The sky goes properly black, hard with stars that were there all day. The stonebloom fields give off a very low, coppery glow — not light so much as visible warmth — and the fauna come toward the vents and warm slabs. Grazers flash on their flanks, the air-sails sink a little lower in the cooling thermals, stoke-wyrms stir in their ducts, lizards hold their heat on the mirrors. Their thermal pulses fall into unison, a slow, distributed heartbeat spread across kilometres of stone. Somewhere in the cliffs a hollow place sings a single note for hours, a pitch you can hear two valleys away, while the stone itself releases the sun in a faint second shadow after the first has passed.

Death here does not interrupt the pattern. A grazer that dies does not rot. It dries, from the inside out, and becomes a husk — a mineral ghost that will lie on the plain for a thousand years, its patterned shell still intact, its legs still planted as if it merely paused. Its descendants will scrape bloom from its back. The dust-rivers will bury it, grain by grain, and slowly uncover it again. The Shelf itself creeps a metre a century, carrying its towns and its herds and its singing cliffs with it on a geological conveyor.

That is who lives here: not densely, but everywhere, if you learn to wait. The bloom that creeps. The boulder-beasts that eat it and feel each other through the ground. The sails that never land and live on air's electricity. The wyrms and lizards and blind burrowers that have made a home in the towns' heat. And the suited, patient people who tend the long-burning furnaces among them, learning — slowly, generation by generation — not to be a rapid thing in a place that has never been rapid at all.
