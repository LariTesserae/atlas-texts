If you put your hand down in the hollow, not on the bare stone but where the green has gathered, the first life you meet is the one everything else depends on, and it does not look like a plant so much as a feeling under your fingers.

The sun-moss is a mat, but a mat with intentions. Filament finer than hair, springy, emerald where the hard light strikes it and almost black-green in its own shadow, it covers the lee of a boulder, the bottom of a shallow swale, any place where the night can settle long enough for the vapour to thicken. It is cool even at noon. When you push it aside it smells sharp and clean and faintly metallic, and your fingers come away damp — not wet with water, which this place has never known free, but with the clear viscous ammoniac fluid held inside its cells.

Those cells are the world in miniature. Each filament is a chain of silica sponges, and inside the sponges the solvent of life here is stored. The moss does not drink rain. It breathes in the night's breath of the continent itself — ammonia and sulphur compounds exhaled by the ammonium minerals in the sunlit rock as the stone gives up its heat — and condenses it straight through its skin. By morning the grove smokes faintly for an hour as the sun lifts what the night laid down, and then the smoke is gone and the moss has taken its fill for the day.

And it moves. Not fast, not in a way a footstep would notice. The edge of a mat advances over pale dust a few centimetres a day, swallowing the bare stone, leaving behind it a thin darkened skin of enriched mineral that the wind cannot lift so easily. A single front may take a century to cross a swale. But it never stops. Over the timescale on which the wind sorts pebbles and the stones creep and settle, the landscape is as much green migrating as it is stone weathering. In the moss there are smaller lives, too small to name from a kneeling height — filaments that feed on spore-dust, cells that live and die in the ammoniac sap between the sponges, the slow churn of the mat itself turning light and vapour and dust into dark soil. They are everywhere the moss is, which is to say, they are everywhere the night has been cold enough.

Rising out of the deepest mats, where the hollow holds the shade longest, are the fan-ferns.

They are not trees, though they hold the place a tree would hold. Each one is a colony, a rosette of arching silica lace a person's height, or twice that, tubes branching and re-branching in a fractal so fine it looks soft from a distance. It is not. Brush it and it rings faintly, stiff as bone. It makes no wood and casts no woody shadow. Its shade is a cold black-green pool on the stone, a place where the moss underneath grows lusher, denser, and where the morning vapour lingers latest.

At certain hours the fronds release their spores, not as flowers, not as seeds, but as plumes of pale dust that rise straight up in the still thin air and then are taken by the wind that is always travelling. They may travel tens of kilometres before settling — a speck in a crack far from any parent, a smear on the far side of a boulder, a first green film on bare stone waiting for a night cold enough to feed it. New groves are born far away, in loneliness, without ceremony.

Life that moves on legs or wings is rare up here, and that rarity is the second balance. You can walk a day between presences, and the presences are separated by a great deal of nothing, and that nothing is not empty in the sense of lacking. It is the distance that allows the rest to hold.

The grazers are the largest thing that lives here, and they are very seldom seen moving. A single one may be a kilometre from the next. Barrel-bodied, long-necked, covered in dull plates of keratin and silica the colour of the stone around them, they blend until the breathing gives them away — a slow bellows, in through the nose, out through the mouth, once a minute, audible before the animal itself is visible if you are close enough to hear anything at all.

They eat the moss. They do not eat the fan-ferns. At dawn, when the light is already hard and white-gold and vertical but the air is still cold, they walk out across the mat and crop it, biting the filament and taking the ammoniac sap straight from the sponge-cells, getting their fluid where the moss gets its rain. They place each foot with a care that makes almost no sound even beside them, and they leave broad pale trails behind them that close again in a season. There is nothing large enough out here to bother them, and nothing in them that needs to hurry. A grazer's territory is a grove. It will come back to that grove for years, cropping, resting, breathing, walking the same slow circuit while the moss creeps in behind it.

Under the stones, where the fern shade touches the ground and the night vapour gathers thickest, lives the third kind of mover, and the smallest.

The burrowers are blind, mole-furred, with foreclaws like spades. They live on fallen spore-dust and on the pale roots the moss sends down into its own enriched skin. They keep to the cool dark under stone all day, listening, one gathers, to the ticking and slow contracting of the rock as it cools. They come out only in the brief amber interval before night — when the light cuts off in the west, when the vapour is already thickening and the stone is still warm. That is their noon. In that brief window they forage in the moss litter, snuffling at the edge of the green, and then they are gone again under stone before the stars arrive all at once, hard and close.

They are the prey, though prey here is a thin and distant word. Above them, so high and so rarely seen that you might walk for a week without noticing, rides the glider.

It is not a bird. Nothing here is a bird. It is a leathery, bat-winged hunter that does not flap through the air so much as borrow it, circling without a wingbeat for hours inside the convection columns that rise wide and slow and silent over a dark grove. You can watch a whole column be born in the middle of nowhere, rise until it flattens and spreads and dies, and somewhere in that rising air the glider is turning, a silhouette against a sky already dark blue at noon, watching for the one small movement in the moss below.

It takes one burrower, perhaps, and then it is gone again over the horizon. Predator and prey are not close neighbours here. They are separated by altitude and by time and by a kilometre of silence. The glider's life is mostly waiting in the wind. The burrower's life is mostly waiting under stone. A single meeting, once in a long while, is enough to hold the web together.

At night the rules for all of them reverse. The moss folds its finest filaments in, conserving the day's captured energy. The vapour comes down thick enough to glitter on every surface — on moss, on the undersides of stones, on the silica lace of the ferns — and the whole plateau smells faintly of the groves. Somewhere far off, a grazer sighs in its sleep. The burrowers are under stone, curled in the dark their eyes never needed. The glider is landed somewhere high and out of sight, or still turning slowly above a column that no longer rises. The stones tick as the continent cools and settles, a slow percussion, and then even that stops.

At dawn the whole cycle lifts again in glittering vapour, the mats begin their centimetres of creep, the fan-ferns ring faintly if the wind finds them, a grazer breathes once a minute somewhere in a grove a kilometre away, and a burrower waits under stone for the amber interval.

Nothing has been built here, nothing marked that stays. The grazers' trails close. The moss covers the stone again. The wind erases its own work and begins it over, patiently, at the scale of continents. And the lives that remain are not crowded, not loud, not young. They are old in the way the stone is old: everything weathered, everything in balance, everything moving at the speed the place allows — the moss creeping, the fern seeding tens of kilometres away, the grazer returning for years to the same green, the burrower emerging for minutes, the glider circling for hours on borrowed air.

To live here is to live stretched thin across an enormous surface, lush and empty at the same time, bright and cold, breathing one slow silent breath at a time with the continent itself.
