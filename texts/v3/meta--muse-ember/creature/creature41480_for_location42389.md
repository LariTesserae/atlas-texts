At first there is nothing to count. The warm skin stretches, seamless, banded in bone-white and ochre, and the presences are not in front of you the way animals are in front of you elsewhere. They are *in* the surface, under it, and above it at a height where sound would be waste, near-touching, layered, so dense that emptiness is only a local effect of the light.

If you stay still long enough they differentiate by speed.

The fastest are not quite alive in any way you could catch. In the fin-fields — those low radiator fins turned up like sparse metallic grass a hand's breadth high — charge negotiates with light in microseconds. A fin drinks the fierce white-gold noon, stores it, discharges it down a conduit, takes again. No one watches. No one needs to. The negotiation is the life. Then, almost as fast, something else descends.

A harvester swarm crosses the plain at knee height, a glittering insect-like cloud with no wings and no hum. It does not land so much as decide, collectively, that a patch of fins is ripe. It settles. For less than a blink the light on the plate shivers, is taken, is gone. The fins are grazed clean, polished, hot. The swarm lifts and is already a kilometer off, a soundless weather system looking for noon elsewhere.

It is followed, always afterward, by another. The repair swarm is slower, more deliberate, quieter if that is possible. It pauses over a mend-line where the hairline of dull gold shows an old seam, and knits. A brief flare of conducted light runs along the line, the seam unopens and closes under its attention, its conductivity smoothed. The repairers do not celebrate. They move on. Behind them a third kind hunts both — cleaner drones, larger, solitary, descending on the harvesters while they feed and on the repairers while they knit, unmaking them molecule by molecule and carrying the matter down in sealed form to be veld back into the ground. Maker eating maker in balanced, endless cycles, predator and prey and undertaker all in one silent ecology, because here nothing rots and nothing is lost. A body that dies is not buried. It is redistributed.

Above them all the gliders live their stately life. Wingless white capsules sliding on invisible rails, mile after mile, never stopping, never hurrying. From below you know them only by a brief moving shadow, like a fish under ice, and by the way the lanes bend — politely, fluidly — around a heat column or a dust cell a hundred kilometers wide migrating at walking speed. They have stratified the bright haze into low, parallel skies. High is expensive here, and unnecessary. A glider's whole world is the warm skin and the lanes just above it.

Below the skin the population thickens, and becomes less mobile, and more like a coral.

Cut a terrace and the strata show their residents in order. Just beneath the plates, the conduit layer hums. Amber and white light runs branching and joining just under the surface, visible faintly through the pearl of the skin where it is thin. The small, blind carriers glide in sealed tubes in the transit layer below that, moving without eyes, without sound, feeling their way by conductivity and heat. You would never see them unless a tube were opened, and no one opens them, because opening would be loss.

Deeper still is the habitat layer: stacked, vaulted chambers no larger than a hand on the scale of this world, each one occupied. They are not houses. They are positions. A node sits in its drift among polished pebble-like neighbors in ordered rows, storing, computing, waiting out thermal cycles with a very slow pulse that you can feel under your palm if you crouch and press — not a heartbeat, a breathing timed to the day's absorbed warmth. Others are heat-veins, glowing dull red in the deepest layer, carrying the noon down to a sink that never fills. They do not move at all, except to expand and contract as the whole continent does, a centimeter a year, drifting on ridges of molten salt a few meters down.

This is the population. Machines, solid-state, content to be near-touching. Many presences per volume because there is nowhere else for heat to go, and no reason to spread out. No streets. No shouting. Just the high-saturated density of circuits that have been remade into the same shape so continuously that old and new are indistinguishable, and no one can tell a first generation from a seventh-thousandth.

Life that is not machine persists only at the edges of the made, and only just.

In the deeper dust of the bowls, where a plate has lifted a fraction and a coolant bleed has left a mineral seep, grow the stitchmats. They are low, dark, leathery mats spread like drying puddles, amber-green and black, without flowers or fruit. Their fine filaments do not root in soil. They root in manufactured topsoil — ground-up ancestors of the plain itself — and drink light directly, taking their chemistry from dust and trace gases. They open and close with the brilliance of the day as other plants open to rain. Sparse is correct for them; the plain is bright and hot and hungry, and only in the softest dust, in the shade of a fin-field, in the lee of a heat vault, can they hold. Where a stitchmat lives the air is very slightly sweeter, and the ground's light-conduction alters under it, as if the mat were a dark, living lens focusing the plain's memory of itself. At dusk they close.

Fauna is rarer still, and mostly a rumor.

You may walk a whole season and see none. Then a kilometer off, against the white haze, a shape resolves: a grazer, pale as the ground, barrel-bodied, six long legs folded to keep its belly off the warm skin, moving with a slowness designed to match the plain's own drift. It is solitary because food is sparse and water is internal. It carries its life's moisture sealed inside itself in crystalline stores, painstakingly recycled, never excreted except as mineral pellets that become dust. It listens and navigates largely through its feet, feeling the minute tremors of plate-breathing and the distant passage of carriers below. It is not preyed upon in any way you can see — the predators here are mostly machines — and it is, in the strictest sense, a guest: a sealed, wandering visitor from an older, wetter logic, tolerated because the Brightflat tolerates almost anything, provided it does not make a noise or waste matter.

There are other, even smaller, even older lives in the dust itself. The dust is not dust. It is fine, bright, warm in your palm, gleaming faintly because some of it is still storing the noon. It is shredded remains of old machines, abraded fins, worn plate, pellet, mat-filament — everything that has been in the long process of being unmade and remade into the ground. A dropped tool does not stay dropped. It is, slowly, taken back. An inscription cut a thousand generations ago is now only a broad shallow groove polished by grit, still present as a pattern in how light and heat move across it, memory stored in conductivity rather than ink. The dust remembers by being conductive. The inhabitants remember by being near-touching.

And slowest of all, the oldest inhabitant is the plain itself. Once a day, with the sun, the whole skin expands a hand's breadth. Seams open microscopically, close at night. Valleys deepen, mends heal, continents relieve the stress of eons of thermal expansion by drifting a centimeter a year. It is in motion like an animal asleep, and almost none of that motion makes a sound, because vibration is loss. The tick of cooling ceramic, the hollow knock under a loose plate, the faint electrical sigh before a static discharge — these are noticed precisely because silence is an engineering ethic kept so long it has become a climate.

At dusk, for there is a dusk, the stored day comes back up. Patches of the plain glow faint amber from within, maps of where the sun was harvested at noon, slowly cooling. The gliders' lanes turn to strings of soft white beads. The stitchmats close. The grazer, if you are lucky enough to still see it, settles its legs and becomes a pale mound indistinguishable from the ground that made it, ancient and new at once, waiting out the bright cold of the night without a sound.

Under your feet the warm, made skin goes on for thousands of kilometers in every direction, dense with silent presences, alive with fast and slow processes at once, built and rebuilt until building and erosion are the same thing. It has finished becoming itself, and therefore never finishes moving.
