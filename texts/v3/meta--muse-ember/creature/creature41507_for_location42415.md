First, the ground lives, and it lives almost everywhere.

Not as forest floor, not as meadow, but as a pale, felted skin pressed flat over the bowl. Moss, in long bare stretches where nothing else has settled, blueish in the shadow of the rim, greener where the diffused midday finds it. It does not rise. It spreads, an inch a year across dark wet rock, eating the stone very slightly, fed in return by the stone sweating. Between the moss, black humus holds a footprint for a long time before slowly filling it back in. Puddles lie in the hollows between stones and do not evaporate; thin bright threads of water sheet over rock, disappear into moss, reappear a metre lower. Everything is damp to the touch, and nothing is cold.

That is the population. And then, at intervals, an individual.

Twenty paces of moss and stone, and then a fern the height of a person stands alone. Its trunk is no thicker than a wrist. It unspools one frond at a time over a decade, each frond still in the act of opening long after another plant would have finished. When it releases its spores, they leave in a fine, visible weather that settles on stone below and makes green ripples — pattern following pattern, season following season, with no wind to hurry them.

Twenty paces more, and a clump of broad leaves, each one the size of a spade, glossy and dark, almost black-green where the water sits on them in the sun. Each leaf is cupped so shallowly that it always holds a lens of water it never quite spills. They do not fall. A leaf yellows into translucence where it hangs, sags, and begins to dissolve in place — turning to clear slime that runs down its own stem in a thread so slow you could miss a day of it, and into the soil below. There is no leaf litter. There is no rustle.

Further on, a small tree with pale bark that peels in wet ribbons. Its crown is so open you can count its branches. Its flowers hang *under* the foliage, pale and downward-facing, opening a fraction in the morning and closing a fraction in the afternoon, letting their pollen fall straight into the condensing air like ash into water. Nothing visits them. Nothing needs to.

And here and there, a vine descends from one of these sparse giants — not to the ground, but to a point a hand's breadth above it, where its root-tips darken and drink the mist and retreat again. One vine has been doing this for a very long time, growing and un-growing in place, braiding its roots in tight woven rings around boulders, loop over loop, as if knitting stone into a basket.

These are not thickets. They are enormities at intervals. When a plant is present, it is entirely present — saturated, dripping, fully grown in a way that suggests no one has ever hurried it or cut it or needed it. Its leaves hang heavy, beaded on their undersides first, where rain would not be expected. It forms there, on leaves, on the north faces of stones, on anything that stays still long enough. Water is near, not falling.

The fungi live the same way, without haste. Hollow husks grow hard as unglazed pottery, then split along a clean line. They do the work of decay here, with water and time. A fallen branch — there is one, and there has only ever been one you can find — lies halfway to humus, its bark perfect and its heart soft, rotting steadily at the same rate the moss creeps and the weep in the western rock migrates two hand-widths in a lifetime.

That is all that lives there.

No buzz over the puddles. No frog in the seep. No worm turning the humus. No bird, no track, no call. Not because the place is dead, but because nothing here needs to be pollinated, carried, eaten, or frightened. The moss eats stone. The stone feeds the moss by sweating. The plants reproduce by letting pollen and spores fall into still, condensing air. Leaves dissolve where they hang. Vines drink mist a hand's breadth from the ground and think again.

Colour is kept for no eye at all, and kept anyway — bluer moss in shadow, deep green in sun, pale downward flowers under dark leaves. Sound is only what the living things make in the course of being wet: a leaf-tip releasing its burden, *plip*; a drip swelling on a stone lip for a very long time, hanging, falling, its ripple taking minutes to reach the edge of the pool below.

Leave and return after years and the same individuals will be there, recognisably the same. The frond that was unspooling will be open. The pool will be a finger's width deeper in one corner. The drip will have fallen a thousand times. But they will not have hurried, or gathered, or multiplied into anything else. They stand far apart in their pale moss, dripping in no hurry, keeping a place that needs no one to keep it.
