If you stand still long enough after the cage lift lets you off, you stop being the only thing in Chamber Three that is moving.

That is the first way to meet its residents. Not by seeing them — the light is too rationed for that, sodium orange and standby and mostly off — but by hearing which of them is awake, and in what order.

The oldest voice is the turbine. It does not roar. It turns, one revolution, a breath, another, at a pace you could count if you wanted to waste time in the heat. It has been turning like that since long before the people left, and it will keep turning after the inspection tour you are on, because nothing here can tell it to stop. It is not loud, exactly. It is the frame the other sounds hang from: the tick and tiny ping of a pipe expanding somewhere in the dark, the low hum behind a panel that you feel more in your teeth than in your ears, the drip that takes four minutes to gather at the tip of a stalactite and then falls and echoes once off a polished slab.

The furnace is the second. Its mouth never quite closes, a dull red eye in the south wall. It breathes, slowly, unevenly, and throws amber onto whatever happens to be near it — hot metal, the underside of a duct, the turn of a leaf. It is not burning much of anything anymore. It is simply not cold. That is enough. Heat, in this place, is a resource that is generated and then carefully reused, and the furnace is one of the sources, the turbine another, the rock itself a third. Everything that lives here has learned to live on that warmth before it learns anything else.

The green learned it first, because it had to. Or because it was made to.

Under every pipe joint that weeps, under every valve, under the condensation line that runs the length of the north wall, the plants are thick to a point that would look like jungle if jungle grew in a machine hall. It is not one kind of green. It is several colonies, each with its own habits, each adapted to a particular leak.

The north wall ferns are the most obvious, and the most at home. Fronds three feet long, uncurling from the basalt and from the cable trunk and from the base of the pipes where the cold side sweats in a clear sheet. They do not grow toward light, because there is barely any to grow toward. They grow toward damp and toward the nearest warm joint, turning their faces to heat the way other plants turn to sun. Their spores drift in the still, hot air in a constant, invisible snow, settle on damp metal, on a sensor arm, on the rim of a tank, and begin again. No pollinator is needed. No animal is needed. They are engineered, originally, to hold the spoil heaps and to eat waste gases, and in their engineered way they still do. You can tell by the thick, waxy leaf above, dark green, and the startling pallor below, almost translucent, with veins that light orange when a frond crosses in front of a heating duct. They are thorough. A fern will grow *into* a vent, *around* a gasket, not to destroy the machine but to occupy the slack, the way ivy occupies a fence, only slower and with more patience.

The broad-leaved ones near the southern steam crack are different. They are less elegant and more deliberate. They were made for another purpose and retain it in their shape: big, flat, waxy pads that catch condensation and hold it, then release it in slow drips. They look wet even when the air is dry, because they are. Their roots are not in soil — there is almost no soil here, only drifts of ash and grit and fallen leaf — but in mineral film on rock and in the scale inside a pipe that is now a millimeter thicker than last season. They grow in the heat of the turbine's exhaust and in the sulphur tang from the slow leaks, and they seem to prefer it. Carbon dioxide that would choke a lung is, to them, simply weather.

Then there are the ones that have learned to hang. From the underside of the walkway, from the underside of pipes, from the truss that holds the tired sodium tubes, fronds and broad leaves dangle upside down where the condensation is richest. They curl not up, but out and slightly in, toward warmth. They rub, very slowly, against sensor arms and railings worn bent by a fallen crate or a pushing frond years ago. In a few places a hanging colony has grown so heavy it has braided itself into the cable trunk, making a soft, green loom over the industrial one.

The moss is quieter than all of them, and perhaps the most widespread resident of all. It creeps on the cold side of pipes, on the concrete of the pump house, on the waterlines of the tank, on the filmed window of the control hut. It does not look alive until you notice that it has moved — a centimeter over a season, a new darkening at the edge of an instrument panel, a blinded sensor that cannot be read anymore because moss has grown over its eye. It lives on steam that rises from the crack in the southern floor and condenses on everything cooler than itself. It lives on the mechanical rain a pipe releases every few minutes, a fine burst that darkens a circle of basalt and is gone. It keeps the rhythm of those sighs, even when we cannot.

For the machines are residents too, and they have their own small, unmusical lives. To call them alive would be inaccurate in a biological sense, and unkind to the green, but to call them dead would be inaccurate in every other sense.

The pump house door stands open. Inside, in the dark, a single panel blinks: green, green, green. Not a heartbeat. A habit. It has blinked that way on standby for years, rationing its own light, waiting for an operator who will not sit in the chair in the control hut facing the instruments that no longer need facing. Rust has begun at the bolts. Biofilm has begun at the waterline. The paint is still legible on a stenciled number: 3.

The conveyor belt in the center is the busiest of the slow things. Rusted but not dead, it creeps forward a centimeter an hour, carrying a mat of fallen leaves — fern fronds that have finished, broad leaves that have yellowed at the edge — toward a hopper. The hopper turns them, almost imperceptibly, into compost for the next patch. Over a season, the pile compacts. Over several seasons, it becomes soil where there was none. It is the closest thing this room has to a food chain, and it moves so slowly that you would swear it was still, until you look away and look back and the leaves are an inch closer to becoming earth.

The pruner arm wakes once a day. It extends from its housing in the east wall, reaches for nothing much — a frond that is already out of its way, a stem that has grown into a vent — and retracts. It does its programmed cut on air and green, and goes back to sleep. The fan in the side alcove does something similar in its sleep, turning over and sighing, moving the hot, damp air a little so that the spores can drift a little further.

The gantry crane hangs over nothing, hook empty, arm grown into the ceiling shadow. The cylindrical tank stands half-full of black water, its gauge frozen not at zero but at a productive-looking middle, its surface perfectly still and warm to the fingertip and reflecting nothing but a brief orange glint from the furnace. The walkway zigzags across the shallow pit, grating cold under a boot that is already sweating, railings bent where generations of plants have pushed.

None of them struggle with each other. That is what stays with you. The green does not overrun the machines and the machines do not burn the green away. They have reached a finished, slow symbiosis: the machines leak heat and water and gases, the plants take them in and make leaves and compost, the leaves feed the hopper, the hopper feeds the next patch of plants, the plants shade the pipes and slow their cooling and keep the condensation where it can be used. The rock, newly exposed and still sharp as broken crockery, is still learning the rules, its first thin stalactites reaching down a finger's length toward a warm pool.

When you leave — when the cage lift rattles and the concrete collar and the ladder take you up out of the thickened air — they do not notice. The turbine turns. The panel blinks green, green, green. A pipe sighs somewhere, a different place from the last time, and a fine mechanical rain darkens a circle of basalt. Under the north wall, a fern uncurls another imperceptible notch toward the heat.
