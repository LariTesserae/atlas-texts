If you are looking for a population, you will be disappointed. There is no village here, no flock, no herd. The residents number perhaps two or three people at a time, and even they seem less like inhabitants than like attendants. The true population is green, wet, and mechanical, and it has arranged itself in layers on a shelf of new rock, each layer holding water for the next.

The people live, when they are here, in the hut. It is the only structure with the form of a dwelling, low and damp and closing its door against the mist that comes up from below in the afternoon. They come up through the cloud sea in the morning, ears popping, breath already short though the air is mild — mild because the rock under the shelf is still warm from below and because the cloud filters the equatorial light to something flat and saturated. They sweat in fleeces that never dry. Their jackets hang in the shed and stay heavy. Their boots come away spotted with black soil that is no soil at all, merely root and wet film held together on basalt.

They move slowly, not from exhaustion exactly, but because the place enforces slowness. The air is thin, the loads are small, the vegetation arrives in cut armfuls, and everything they tend operates at a pace set by the thin atmosphere and by water. A worker crosses the terrace carrying a bundle of broad leaves uphill through fern, and he does it in a kind of waking dream, unhurried, breath held a little too long between steps. When he feeds the conveyor, he feeds it sparingly. The belt inches, carries crushed green matter through a shed and out the other side, stops, clanks, stops again. He watches the press. He climbs, when he must, to clear the pipes where moss has thickened over the brackets, or to cut back the vine that last week sealed the path and has already begun to seal it again. He does not clear it from the place. He clears it from the way.

For the plants are not decoration here. They are the majority, and they are indiscriminate. Thin black earth exists in pockets where roots have made it, but most of the shelf has no earth to speak of, and plants live anyway — in the film of water on stone, in the rust of a gutter, in the joint of a concrete wall, fastened to a pipe or a cable or the slat of the conveyor itself. Tree ferns stand taller than a man and drip steadily from their fronds. Bamboo clumps sway with a wet weight. Broad-leaved shrubs make three or four strata of green over every patch of ground, their leaves so saturated they seem to be made of color rather than leaf: green, rust, copper, black. Moss lies on the rocks and on the shed roofs in mats thick as mattresses, holding water and releasing it hours later, slow as a sponge breathing. Orchids and bromeliads, which elsewhere would climb into the canopy, have climbed onto industry; they drink from the side of a tank, from a dripping elbow, from the warm iron of a motor housing, and open small, improbable flowers exactly where condensation runs.

The green, in turn, is food and feedstock and obstruction. It is what the plant on the terrace exists to work. Vines cross the paths. Ferns push out of gutters that were meant to carry water away. The industrial presence, packed shoulder to shoulder on the one flat terrace, is therefore never clean for long. Oil leaks onto concrete and is not washed away, and mushrooms grow in it, pale and stubborn, a small pale population living precisely where nothing else will. Lichen branches across the cylindrical tanks in patterns that look like wiring diagrams. Algae and liverworts tint the wet sheets that cross the basalt, so that even the water, which is condition more than feature, acquires a thin green skin where it lingers a moment too long on warm stone.

The machines behave, after a while, less like tools than like large, slow animals tethered among the plants. The generator drones with a low electrified hum you feel in your teeth, and it does not really sleep. The turbine set into the culvert where a rivulet has been tightened to a channel turns but does not hurry, humming steadily as it drinks the sheet of water coming down. Transformers tick and warm and cool. The fence ticks with electricity at intervals, a small nervous sound in the roar of water over basalt. The weather mast thrums in the wind over the rim, an antenna with no one to listen to it. The pump house stands with its door rusted shut, a resident that has withdrawn. The tanks store what the place catches; the pipes run overhead on brackets, dive into the ground, and surface again through fern as if surfacing for air. Metal expands and contracts. Dripping continues out of rhythm from eaves, from pipes, from rock faces that weep even on a clear day — warm water, in places, where a fresh crack lets the interior heat show through.

That sound — water first, then the hum, then the slow clank of the press, then dripping, then wind — is how you know the place is occupied even when the workers are inside the hut. At night the generator keeps its drone, the rivulets keep finding cracks, the dozens of small waterfalls on the rim keep plunging into cloud where no one hears them land. The moss keeps holding and releasing. The condensation on the inside of the shed glass keeps running in slow branching rivers long after the mist has receded.

Animal life, by comparison, is a rumor, and the absence is part of what the other inhabitants have to live with. There is no birdsong. There are no tracks, no disturbed litter, no mammals lifting stones to find anything underneath. If you lift a wet stone, you find stone. When the sun breaks through, for a brief interval the air acquires a faint presence of insects — midges, small flies drawn to crushed leaves and wet decay — but they are a weather, not a community.

The exceptions prove the rule. Once or twice an hour a single cloud hummingbird darts through the terrace, impossibly bright against the wet green and rust and black, and is gone over the edge into cloud. It is the only regular animal visitor, and it seems to belong to the orchids on the pipes as much as to the air, pausing where the bromeliads have fastened themselves to metal. It does not sing. It does not stay. Far below the rim, in the cloud sea, a high hawk sometimes circles, a shape describing a wider room, and never comes up to this shelf. It is not a resident. It is a reminder that the world below is still continuous, and this shelf, five hundred metres across, is still deciding whether it is part of it.

By evening the cloud rises and fills the bowl again, and the inhabitants settle into their night arrangement: the workers in the hut, smoking or sleeping thinly against the altitude; the plants pressing against every surface, drinking the air itself; the machines dripping and humming and cooling by slow degrees; the mushrooms in the oil; the lichen tracing its diagrams on the tanks; the water, which never gathers, leaving in all directions over sharp new edges. You can walk the whole boundary in twenty minutes. You will still be wet, still warm from the stone through your soles, still a little out of breath — and the place will still be making itself, one wet, patient layer at a time.
