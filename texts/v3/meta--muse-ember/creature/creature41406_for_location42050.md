Under your feet, something is breathing.

You do not know it at first because it is so flat, and so still, and so the color of everything else. The stone you stepped on sighs — not loudly, but in a slow, wet lift and settle that travels. A pore opens at its edge and closes. A fringe of thin feelers, blind, taps the damp film and withdraws. Then, a pace away, another does the same, out of time with the first. A pace beyond that, another. In a minute the whole flat is moving under whorls of slow peristalsis, thousands of oval bodies overlapping shingle on shingle, layered two and three deep where the ground hollows, packed near-touching so that from above there is no stone to be seen at all.

These are the pavement.

Each one is perhaps a hand across, perhaps broader, grey-black and armored, its back domed and worn smooth. It does not walk. It does not need to. It waits in its place and opens, a little, to feed. What it feeds on is not visible to you either. The rock sweats a film in the cold, always, and in that film lives a skin of bacteria, eating what the stone dissolves. The pavement animal grazes it with the edge of its mouth, lifts and settles, lifts and settles, making the low pulsing you feel in your teeth. Around its rim the feelers keep tapping — not for form, not for direction, but for the wetness of the film, the faint chemistry of a place where the rain has left more minerals.

They are so many that a single body is a grain. A flat the size of a valley is a pavement of them from ridge to ridge, and the ridges themselves, those smooth low humps far off, are not rock at all in origin. They are shells. When a pavement animal dies it dies in place, its armor loosening, being worried and cleaned and polished by what lives in the seams until only the dome remains. Rain and crawling and dripping smooth those domes down, press them into the earth, and grain by grain the dead add to the edges of the basin. The world makes its own ground by wearing itself down.

Between the slabs, in the damp seams where two armors meet and leave a thread of dark, there is another life, and it is the louder one.

The skitterers boil there. Small, pale, eyeless, many-legged crustacean things no bigger than flies and yet uncountable as flies in a summer heap multiplied a millionfold. They tap and worry and pick incessantly. Claw on shell, mouthpart on casting, leg on leg — that is the high shimmering that never stops, the clicks on clicks on clicks. No one of them is loud. Together they are a living static that fills every crack, every hollow under a slab, every pore in the shell-beds.

They eat what the pavement leaves: castings, sloughed film, the soft parts of the dead before the shells harden. They eat each other’s molted skins. They swarm over a carcass in seconds and disperse in seconds, resettle, swarm again over a seep where the condensation gathers thickest, over a place where a thread of rain has run down an old scar in the rock and brought a fresh pulse of mineral. Their movement is frantic and directionless and constant. They do not rest because there is no signal for rest, only damp, and hunger, and the vibration of other bodies to follow. To stand in a seam and listen is to hear them think, if tapping is thinking.

When you look up from the pavement — and you do not look up often, because there is nothing to see up there — you find the ledges.

The low ridges are not cliffs. They are only the lips of worn shell-beds, rounded, dripping, overhung in places by a slab that has been lifted and cemented by ages of damp. Under those lips hang the third kind.

They are soft and elongated and blind, drooping down like pale tongues, swaying only slightly in air that itself is barely moving. Their bodies bead with condensation and drink it through their skins. At one end a throat sac inflates, slowly, until the whole body tightens, and then it lets go — not a voice, not a cry, but a low boom and croak that rolls across the flat and returns off the ridges. You feel it in your teeth because it is meant to be felt in stone, in wet film, in other bodies. It is a mating call and a warning and a way of testing the basin for reverberation, all at once.

They hang motionless for a long time, sacs rising and falling in slow rhythm. Then one drops a mucus thread, thin as hair, and waits. When a skitterer brushes it, there is a quick, wet slap — a snap of the whole soft body whipping up — and the skitterer is gone. The hanger settles again. It will hang there for hours, booming now and then, exhaling a fine mist with its pulse so that some of the dampness of the place is literally its breath, remade.

The skitterers do not learn to avoid the threads. There are too many of them, and the hangers are too many, and the hunger is too constant. The cracks under a crowded ledge are a permanent, patient trap, thick as grapes with dangling bodies.

And parting it all, slowly, as a tide parts a sea, come the wanderers.

They are few by comparison, which is to say still many, and enormous beside everything else. Soft-bodied, grey-white, blind, their weight makes the pavement shift with a dull percussive puff, a wave of clicks as skitterers scatter and resettle in their wake. A broad muscular foot presses into the damp film and leaves a clean, wet track, a pale smear in the bacterial sheen, and the pavement reclaims it behind them within minutes, creeping and breathing over it as if it had never been.

They come to feed on the hangers and on the pavement itself, tearing a hanger from its ledge with that same wet slap magnified, or nosing up a slab and taking it whole. Their pace does not hurry. Nothing here hurries. They pass across a flat and pass again hours later from another direction, on a circuit no one else can see, following vibrations through stone and film more than any scent. When a wanderer passes, the whole ordering of the flat changes for an hour: seams open, skitterers boil up into the open air, hangers boom in answer, and then, slowly, everything settles back into its layered, out-of-phase breathing, as if the pavement had been inhaling and had been interrupted.

Above it all, so high you rarely see it as bodies at all, is the fourth layer.

A haze of winged things no larger than your thumbnail, blind, translucent, their wings a blur. They do not land. They hang in the mist and feed on the moisture itself, and on each other, moving in slow turning clouds that darken a patch of the gloom and then dissolve and reform elsewhere. Their wings add a constant thin whine to the chorus, papery and unceasing. You never see one clearly. You see the air thicken. They are sound made visible, and when the rain comes finer and the mist thickens they rise a little, and when the damp settles they sink, turning and turning in the cold air above the pavement, eating and being eaten mid-flight, never touching the ground that feeds them.

None of them see, not properly. Some pavement animals carry vestigial lids sealed over pits that glow faintly from within — not to see out, but to look inward at the gut-light of their own digestion. The wanderers navigate by the vibration that travels through wet film and rock when a hanger booms and a million skitterers tap. The hangers test the stone by their own booms. Even the skitterers, in their frantic tapping, are sounding out the shape of whatever they are picking at. The whole basin is a resonating instrument, and the animals are both its players and its listeners.

That is why the weather and the fauna are not separate. Rain comes — or threatens, or hangs in the air and beads and drips from ledges long after — and the pavement opens a fraction wider, feeds faster, puffs more mist from its pores. The skitterers click louder for a time, crowding the fresh threads of water. The hangers inflate more fully and boom more often. The winged cloud thickens. Then the damp settles back into the stone, the film thins, and the chorus settles to its steady, deafening pitch again: deep pulsing below, high clicking above, wet slap, percussive shift, papery whine, all layered like instruments in an orchestra that never stops playing.

There are no built things here. No nest, no burrow dug in earth, no artifact. The hollows are worn, not made. The paths are not paths, only places where wanderers pass often enough that the sheen is always thin. The shells in the hollows, black and white and polished, are not collected. They are left where the pavement died, and the skitterers have cleaned them, and the rain has polished them again.

And it goes on beyond the ridge you can barely see, basin after basin, pavement after pavement, extending farther than the sound can carry in the mist. The temperature stays cool and settled, like deep water. The light stays a rumor. Nothing ever sleeps, because there is no signal for sleep. Nothing ever stops being hungry, because there is always damp, and decay, and the bodies of their own kind, close at hand.

It is an old place that has found a loop and closed it: stone feeds film feeds pavement feeds skitterer feeds hanger feeds wanderer feeds wing feeds film again, and the waste of it all slowly builds the ridges that hold it in. Alive from the film of water on a stone all the way up to the thick, buzzing air, crowded and cold and dark and damp, with no plant in it and no silence in it, at rest in motion, forever.
