The first inhabitant is the one you are standing on, and for a while you do not recognize it as an inhabitant at all.

At ankle depth the surface is a felt of mosses, liverworts, small rosette plants, algae, and root hairs knotted together into a sponge. It is continuous. It does not break into shore and shallows and dry land. It gives under your weight like a wet mattress and then springs back, pushing clear cold water up around your boots. Put a hand down and it is fibrous and cold and full of slow motion — a movement that goes nowhere and therefore never finishes. Under that felt is more of the same, thickened layer upon layer of tuber, peat-making stem, and decaying leaf that never quite become soil, because the whole mass is being eaten from below and grown from above at exactly the same rate. Only far down, on the rare step where the mat has worn thin, does a stone show through, rounded and dark and entirely upholstered in green.

So the ground is not a place where things live. It is the first thing that lives, and everything else lives in, on, or through it.

Work starts in the channels. They are broad and braided and worn into gentle terraces, each tread soft with plants, each step down carrying the same shallow sheet of grey-blue water. Along them algae bloom in long straight rows, unit after identical unit, processing the pale ordinary light. Through the root felt fungi run like conveyor lines, carrying sugar one way and minerals the other, so that what is loosened by decay in one place is taken up again a hand's breadth away. In the deeper pools the roots cross and cross in the dark and weld nutrients together with a small bioelectric discharge. You can feel it as a faint tingling if you put your hand in. Every so often the whole thickness of the mat pulses — a low thump you feel through your soles, through the water, through the plant — and after it the air carries a metallic taste.

It is industrial, in the way a forest can be industrial if it is left to run long enough. No factory, no engine, no artifact. Only bodies, scaled up until a continent of them hums as one system.

The vertical signs of that system are spaced far apart. A solitary column rises out of the flood — not a tree, but a hollow, ribbed stalk the height of a house, banded, venting a slow stream of oxygen bubbles from its crown. It drips, chiming, and it pulses with the mat beneath it. Where the mat is thickest, one grows, and it stays. Another, and another, a mile apart, markers across an emptiness that, on the surface, looks almost total. For a hundred paces at a time there is only the waterlit green, a few emergent reeds holding the same note in the wind, the reflection of a sun that does not seem to move, and the roar of water falling off the plateau edge into the cloud sea below. Your own splash is taken up by that roar and lost.

And then, far off, the emptiness moves.

Long, flat-backed grazers wade in the open sheet water, shoulder-high, their bodies furred with the same moss as the mat so that at a distance they are not so much animals on the plateau as patches of it that have decided to travel. They move with the slow deliberation of things that have nowhere to hurry to, cropping the mat in wide swaths. Behind them the swaths heal. A track fills with water and the plants close over it within the minute, at the same rate they opened. They do not push the water level up or down. They do not alter their ground enough to leave a mark.

In the channels, closer and harder to see, eel-like swimmers loop in the current. Silver and blindish, they filter as they go, turning with the sheet-flow rather than against it, their bodies making the same gentle curve over and over. On the surface, rafts of floating bloom drift on the still water, and on those rafts small bright creatures walk — hard-shelled, round as seeds, stepping carefully over their own bright platform, carrying themselves and their raft nowhere in particular.

Above all of this, sparse flocks of glider birds circle the vent-stalks. Their wings are more membrane than feather. They call in a chorus that does not rise or fall, at the same volume for as long as you can listen, and the sound does not begin or end. You can pick them out a mile away against the pale cream of the cloud sea, and then nothing again for another mile.

None of this crowd is a crowd. Abundance here does not mean many bodies in one place. It means that if you walk far enough you will keep entering a different niche being used, and that under any given square meter the use is stacked instead of spread. Under the mat, invisible except for the bubbles they send up, are the filterers and the root-eaters who make the machinery run — dozens of kinds, hundreds of kinds, each with its patch, none overlapping in a way that ever gets crowded. They nibble the tuber and stem and leaf from below while the grazers crop from above and the algae process light in their rows and the fungi carry the result sideways and the vent-stalks breathe it out at the crown.

What lives here does not build. There is no path, no cairn, no shelter, no nest of sticks that is not just the mat folded over. What lives here does not leave bones arranged or tools dropped or scars freshly cut. Even death is worn smooth before it is absorbed. A living thing is not left to rot into a new shape. It is unmade at the rate it is made, its substance returning to the sponge without a ripple.

That is why an individual is hard to hold onto. A grazer is a cropping moving across the mat, a swimmer is a filtering loop in a channel, a stalk is a breathing column where the mat is thickest, a bird is a circle in the air around the stalk, a seed-creature is a step on a raft of bloom. Each continues, and each is replaced exactly. The mat they crop is always cropped to the same height. The water flows but the level never rises or falls. The rain falls in its fixed veil over one end of the plateau but the pools never deepen. The sun gives its cool white light but neither warms the water nor dries the air.

You could mark a waterline with a cut in a reed, leave for a year, and return to find the cut exactly as you left it. You could watch a vent-stalk breathe and notice that after each breath the air is exactly as it was before. You could listen for an hour to the grazers splashing, the swimmers looping, the bubbles rising through the mat, the dripping from the stalks, the root pumps beating at the scale of a landscape, the birds calling, the water falling unbroken along the whole rim — and hear no sound begin or end.

High, cold, flooded, green, loud, and working, the inhabitants go on making themselves over and over so precisely that they never need to become anything else.
