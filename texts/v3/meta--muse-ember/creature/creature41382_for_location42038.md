You learn to tell them apart not by shape, but by tempo.

All the domes look alike at first — broad, low, squat humps of overlapping plate, iron-dark and rusted, wet even where it is not raining. But stand long enough and the stillness sorts itself. One breathes deep, slow, fifty cycles down in your teeth. Another breathes shallower, higher. Another ticks and clanks constantly, like a drawer of loose change being shaken in a dryer, though nothing on its surface seems to move. Another does not breathe at all in the way you understand it, but pulses, a rim of faint electric blue running around its edge, over and over.

They are packed so tightly there is no between. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, plate tucked under plate so water runs off. Where the basin is deeper they stack, upper ones perched on the backs of lower ones to keep a slit above the slush. Where it is shallow they lie just under, and you know them only by the heave of their backs and the rank, sooty streams of bubbles.

Up close, each one is a machine made without a machinist. The plates are not laid on. They grow, overlapping like boiler plate, each edge under the next. Between them tendons pull like cables. Along the flank, in regular rows, are knobs — bone or grown metal, catching the dim light. They are not rivets. They swell and dull with heat. Perhaps they feel. Underneath, hidden in shadow, a seam glows dull furnace-orange, in time with a slow intake of black water through a mouth that is really a filter.

They take in water, and gas seeping up from below — sour, sulfur-and-ozone on the cold air — and what they give off is heat, sound, and waste. Heat sheets off them in patches so the water around your legs goes lukewarm and the stone beneath it is warm to the touch, even as the air bites just below freezing and your breath fogs and hangs. Waste sheets off them too: a thin oily runoff in rainbow films, a fine metallic dust sneezed into the fog, salt and iron crystals that precipitate from their exhaust in the cold and fall back onto their own plates.

For hours a section will idle, the hum deepening in their chests. Then the ripple comes. You feel it through the soles of your feet before you see it — a peristaltic band crossing the whole basin like a wave over a field, taking days to cross what looks like a morning's walk. Each animal in the band lifts a fraction, clanks against its neighbor, shifts a hand-span forward, and settles, exhaling steam that fogs and instantly freezes in a clear skin on the windward sides of things. A hand-span at a time, over centuries, is a migration.

They do it again. And again.

They reproduce the way a production line would if it had learned to eat. A flank splits — the plates separate with a sound like tearing rivets — and a smaller dome pushes out, soft and steaming, its plates still unhardened, and inflates with water until it hardens into its place in the packing. Count the plates if you like: seventeen domes, then the same seventeen, in exactly the same tilt, with the same scar on the central one, and then again. That is not coincidence. That is family. A lineage holds its position in the weave the way a cell holds its place in a tissue, cloning its tilt, its scar, its tempo, wave after wave.

Old ones molt. The emptied shells do not rot. They do not fall away. They remain in the mass for a long while, still warm, still ticking as they cool, and the cooling is a kind of death the others understand. Nothing here rots, because nothing here can spare the heat. A shell is warmth to be spent, then a house.

That is where the small ones live.

No bigger than your fist, all legs and rattling joints, they scuttle in the seams between the big ones, where it is warmest and the light never reaches. They make most of the high noise. They run, unhurriedly, from shell to shell, from living back to dead back, cleaning. They pick at breathing slits, scrape oily film from plates, eat the fine metallic dust before it can foul a filter. If you watch — if you can watch them between the clanks — you will see a kind of tending. A large one will idle, its seam dimming, and a dozen small ones will gather along its flank, ticking faster, until the seam brightens again. They are not parasites. A production line that has learned to eat needs hands to keep its mouths clear.

Above the paving, the water itself has a life that is not water.

Overlapping discs, black and glossy, raft across the surface in mats so dense you cannot see water between them. Each pulses blue at the rim, like a running light. They swarm the warm patches where the big ones exhale, break apart and rejoin without ever leaving a gap, and occasionally one screams — a high, metallic note cut off as if the water took it. They are harvesting the difference between the warm animal below and the cold air above. They eat heat. And they shed.

Because the air above that is also full.

In the low fog hangs a haze of micro-fauna so fine it looks like industrial dust, or snow that will not fall. Tiny winged things with engine-bright thoraxes, hovering on the updrafts from the warm backs below. They bow and dart in the steam, they dust the plates with something that glitters, they follow your breath and try to colonize its fog. There is no empty space here at any scale you test. Ground, seam, surface, air — each is packed. Stand still for a few minutes and condensation beads on you. Stand still longer and the nearest domes will begin, unhurriedly, to grow over your feet.

Where there is an animal that eats heat, there are animals that eat what it sheds, and the loop closes below you in a place you cannot step. The thin grey film where the water is slowest — mineral, fungal-smelling, the only thing here that resembles a plant without being one — is a stomach. It breaks down crystals and oily waste and metallic dust into something the filter-mouths can use. There is no green here at all. No leaf, no stem, no root holding mud, because mud was ground away long ago between animal and rock, grain by grain, animal by animal. Life here took another road long before plants were an option, and it stayed on it.

It is an end-state. Whatever mountains once fed this basin have been worn to these low weeping lumps. Every edge is rounded. Every line softened. The place has settled into its final form and is now only polishing itself.

And the rules breaking out here are not breaking to them. You hear the clank a second before the animal moves, sometimes the other way around. You step forward and the foam forms, then the foot lands in it. A molted shell hatches before it splits; a small one pushes out while the seam is still closed, and after it has scuttled clear the seam opens on nothing. Water runs uphill up the warm sides of domes to bead and freeze at the top, although the stone is warm.

They do not notice. Or rather, from inside the packing, this has always been the way of movement: sound travels before motion can catch it, so you listen for the clank to know where a neighbor intends to be. Growth travels before splitting, so a seam is a promise long before it is an opening. Distance is measured in hums, not in sight — the fog makes sight useless anyway — and a hum that has been heard once will be heard again a little to the left, because the family pattern repeats.

Only something standing outside the system, trying to keep its footing on a floor that is alive and working and old and new at once, calls it apart.

By the end of what might be a day, under a light that never changes enough to tell, you understand that you have stopped looking for an edge, because there is none. There is water and stone and animal and fog, packed and weeping and humming, extending past the immediate in every direction. But you also understand that the four kinds were never four.

The domes are the skin and the lungs. The small jointed ones are the hands. The discs are the surface of the blood that rises to meet the cold. The haze is the breath between them, carrying dust from one colony to another the way a factory carries heat from one floor to the next. And underneath it all, the deep heat rising through everything, the gas seeping up, the wave that crosses the basin over days — that is the gut, the heart, beating somewhere below the bedrock, so slow that a whole life is lived inside a single pulse.

It is one animal. It only looks like many because it is large enough to have weather inside it: sleet and thaw and condense and freeze cycling in minutes, breeding, molting, hot seams brightening and dimming with the cold.

You walk because standing still means being covered. The wave comes, you feel it through your feet, and you shift your weight with it, piston-like, without deciding to.
