At first, standing at the bottom of the descent, there seems to be nothing living here at all.

Nothing moves quickly enough to register as movement. Nothing calls, or scurries, or lifts off. The heat comes up through the grates in a slow, wet breath, the turbines sigh through their bearings, something far off clanks and settles, a drip falls, and falls again. You could, for a long moment, believe this is only machinery — old, stained, abandoned machinery — slowing down toward its final stop.

Then the green separates itself from the stain.

It is everywhere, but not as a surface. It is in layers, in thicknesses, in places where green has no business being. Under the turbines. Across the electrical trunking. Rooted in the condensation on the ceiling ribs and growing downward. Braided around a steam line so tightly the metal is only a rumor inside. Growing out of a pool of oil and water. Growing, very deliberately, in the exact branching pattern of the pipes above it.

It is difficult, at first, to tell one plant from another, because they are all touched by the same conditions — hot, damp, dim, and unvisited — and they have all grown toward those conditions until they resemble each other. Then, if you stay, the layers begin to sort themselves out.

The floor belongs to the ferns.

They crowd between turbine bases and spool under transformers in a near-touching mat, root to root, frond to frond. From a distance they look soft. Up close they are not. Each frond is thick and waxy, blue-green rather than green, hot to the touch, and divided along its length into hard little segments like the fins of a radiator. They did not grow that way to catch light — there was never much light to catch — they grew that way to shed heat. A fern here is as much a cooling surface as a leaf.

They unfurl with a slowness that makes the word unfurl feel too quick. A fiddlehead that is a tight green cinch today will be a spread fan wide enough to shade a locker in a year, in three years, in longer than anyone has watched. Once open, a frond does not stop because it has reached a proper size. Nothing prunes it. Nothing chews it. Its margins stay immaculate — no insect will ever take a bite here, no animal will ever brush it down — and so it simply keeps being itself, getting larger by fractions, adding a segment here, thickening there, for as long as the warmth below holds.

Under them the roots are worse, or better. They are a feltwork, probing the failed grating, the packed earth, the cracks in concrete, the grease in the sumps. Around one turbine shaft the roots have packed themselves so densely that they have become a living grease for the bearing, and the great slow shaft sighs through them, minute by minute, without seizing. The fern is not merely growing beside the machine. It is keeping the machine alive as a condition of its own life.

Above the ferns, climbing, are the broad-leafed ones. Their leaves overlap like shingles up the pipes and the paneled walls, each leaf cupping a little damp against the next. They are not climbing for light, either. They are climbing for warmth, and for the steady runoff. What they trap they spill back down in slow threads, drop by drop, so that the whole wall is gently, unevenly raining inside the chamber. If you brush one, it answers with a tiny crackle and a faint smell of ozone, as if the leaf had been holding a charge in reserve.

That is not a trick of one species. Many of them do it.

The vines are the connectors. They have insulated the steam lines in a living lagging, braided tight around the metal, generation upon generation until a line that once hissed bare is now a hand’s thickness around. Elsewhere they have found the electrical cables and fused with them — not climbed over them, fused — so that you can trace a green vein running along a black cable and see it pulse, very faintly, in time with the room. With the transformer’s hum. With the slow blink of the control panels. It is not a heartbeat. It is draw and release, draw and release, as current passes and the plant lets it pass.

And then, farthest from the floor, hanging from the stained concrete ribs, are the upside-down plants. They root in the thin film of condensation on the ceiling and unfurl toward the warmth below, leaves opening away from the rock and toward the furnaces. They will never know a sky. They have no need of one. The drip that feeds them arrives from above, as vapor condensed on the coolest surface, and the warmth they grow toward is the warm, amber-lit air rising off the grates. They sway, if they sway at all, in the same patient current that carries the spores down.

Spores are the real population here, in a way. Because there are no insects to carry pollen, no birds, no bats, no webs, no footprints in the damp dust — pollination, if you can call it that, happens by proximity and by current. The hot, still air is full of drifting spores, settling down onto everything and taking hold wherever there is a skin of moisture to hold them. A moss will grow in an oil sump under a black skin of separated oil and water, greening the stagnant pocket from below. A fern sporeling will root in grease. A vine will start from a crack that is only damp. Some flowers open only when a machine cycles, gaping toward a transformer as if to drink its field, and close again when the cycle ebbs. What is fertilized, what is not, matters less here than what can endure the heat and the dimness long enough to not die.

The youngest leaves tell you when they have been watered. Along their veins they carry a faint phosphor, greenish, that brightens right after a drip falls on them. In the furnished dimness — the amber slits in the floor back-lighting the fronds from below, the two tired sodium lamps bruising the wet metal orange — you learn to see by that response. A drop from a pipe joint, seventeen seconds apart for longer than anyone has recorded, carves its groove in concrete, and each time it lands, for a moment, something lights up.

Not everything here contents itself with being a plant.

Near the far wall, a stand of what looks like broad grass is in fact a bundle of insulated cables that have sprouted leaves along their length. Each leaf is a thin copper-green plate. When current passes through the cable, the plate warms; when it does not, it cools. It is photosynthesis translated into another medium — the plant taking the machine’s energy the way a surface plant takes the sun’s, and doing the same slow, unhurried work with it.

Some of the ferns have gone further into imitation. Their branching copies the piping above them, leaf for pipe, division for division, in a fractal exactness too deliberate to be accident. It is as if, having grown for so long in the presence of the plumbing, they have learned its shape and begun to answer it.

In the hottest corner, where the floor grates glow almost red, there are pale stalks that never open at all. Flowerless, almost bloodless, they only lean — minute by minute, away from the heat, and then back again — following the slow breathing of the furnace under the floor. They are the most animal-like thing in the place, and they are not animal at all. They are simply plants that have learned to tell time by heat.

And under everything: the dead.

Because nothing eats here, nothing carries anything away, what falls stays. Dead fronds drop straight down and become a spongy black peat under the machines, sweet and mineral-smelling, deepening by a millimeter at a time. The peat breaks down in place and becomes soil in place, and new roots grow down into it. The drip feeds the peat, the peat insulates the pipes, the pipes warm the roots, the roots transpire moisture that condenses on the cool upper pipes and falls again. It is a closed weather system, a closed life cycle, with no outside and no waste.

So the whole chamber, over time, stops looking like many plants and starts looking like one long, slow negotiation: the radiator ferns shedding the machines’ waste heat, the shingle-leaves returning that heat as damp, the vines lagging the lines and pulsing with their current, the ceiling plants catching what rises, the cable-grass warming and cooling with the load, the pale stalkers bending with the furnace. Each one is separate and has its own unfurling, its own rhythm of drip and spark and glow. Together they have made a place that keeps itself.

They do not hurry. They do not collapse. A frond unfurls for years. A vine thickens by a fraction. A pressed bundle on the half-buried conveyor creeps forward a leaf’s width an hour toward a press that will sigh shut every three days or so, and take it in. Nothing is new here and nothing is sharp, and nothing — among the things that live — ever leaves.
