If you watch a wall long enough, up close, where the ceramic weeps in a slow warm line, something will lick it.

A tongue, no longer than your thumb, flicks out and back, out and back, in a rhythm you will later find yourself hearing as part of the city. The creature that owns it is no larger than your hand and blind, meant for vertical wetness, its damp skin flushed against the mineral scale. It is a wall-salamander, one of thousands, grazing the fungal film off the surface as methodically as a cow in a field that never ends. It does not look. It does not need to. The world is on its tongue and under its belly — heat, humidity, the sour-sweet taste of fungus and the older taste of runoff protein carried in beaded water.

Where one lives, a hundred live. They tile the outward faces of Vessinlow at every height the light makes it warm to them, above the streets, below the bridges, inside service canyons where the air is cooler. Their flicking never synchronizes, but together it becomes a pulse, a background tempo against which everything else moves. They are quick to fill a vacated crevice, quick to eat what falls from above, quick, when birds are scarce, to eat each other. Nothing about that is frantic. It is simply the pace of a place with no empty place in it.

Under their bellies and under their food is the real green of Vessinlow, though it is not green. Beneath and between the paving slabs, in the humid mulch under the pavement, in a shallow uninterrupted world that knits the wet basalt-polymer together, runs the fungus — white and ochre and sulfur mats, patient and continuous. It eats what the city leaves: ceramic dust, organic waste, the endless leavings of machinery and bodies. And after rain, or after the city makes its own warm rain three districts over and carries it back on its own weather, it pushes up fruiting heads through cracks in the street. Soft, cap-like, smelling of cooked bread and wet iron. People come at dawn with baskets. So do other things. The salamanders take them from the wall. The canids snap them from ledges when they can. Children, who have learned to tell a ripe cap by its smell alone before they see it, harvest without bending all the way down, hands already knowing.

From the fungal beds and from every drain and compost vent, midges rise in glassy clouds. They are not much, individually; in aggregate they are a season. They hang over markets, over ankle-deep service canals, over the warm outflow where water has been borrowed and returned. The city’s air is thick with them, and the air thickens because of them, a living haze that moves when the thermal stacks breathe.

The stack-birds hunt them mid-air. They never land on the ground — so far as anyone can tell, they have forgotten how, or chosen not to. They nest in the warm outflow of vents, ride the updrafts between towers, and stoop from those invisible currents to snatch midges over the stalls. From below you hear them more than you see them: braided calls cutting through the bazaar-scream of a hundred overlapping negotiations. Their eggs are taken, when the nests can be reached by hand or by a careful climber of laundry-lines, and eaten. Their fledglings are not left alone either. Salamanders will climb for them. Canids will leap for them. Humans will wait, opportunistic, for the wind to bring a carcass down to a bridge.

Over your head, too, there is swimming. In the elevated canals, in clear tubes built into the bridges, conduit eels flash silver as they fight the current, looping and hurrying somewhere. You pass under them at ankle depth in the old service canyons where other fish swim past your boots, past your knees in the deeper galleries, in wrist-deep glass troughs running overhead. The water here is not owned. It is borrowed, led where it is wanted, at ankle depth here, at wrist depth there, in the flooded basements that are fished in deliberately, in the vaulted cisterns where the sound changes and the air finally runs cooler. The fish are in all of it, and the people who fish them wade without ceremony, because their skin is already slick at thirty degrees and stays slick long after they stop moving, because metal is close to body temperature and wet walls are a form of handrail.

And on the roofs — which are streets to them — runs the canid. Lean, long-legged, built for height, it travels Vessinlow without ever touching the wet ground. Ledge to wire to laundry-line, wire to gantry, gantry to the ceramic shoulder of a tower a hundred storeys up where a human courier stares in jealous admiration. It eats birds when it can get them and fungus and each other when it cannot, and it leaves no sign on the ground that it was ever there, except perhaps a scrape on a painted rail. At night its passage is a small silence in the traffic above, a pause in the chatter of stacked humanity, before it is gone again into the seams of sky between the higher decks.

Humans are, of course, everywhere, but here “everywhere” is not a crowd that gathers for an event. It is a condition. They are braided into the architecture in the way the salamanders are braided into the walls: housing ligaments wrapped around transit tubes, shops that are also bridges that are also homes that are also water tanks, markets overflowing their containers into the airspace so that a vendor’s shout has to compete not only with other vendors but with birds above and eels above that. Vendors, water-bearers with their borrowed loads, repair-swarm tenders who stand with a palm flat against a wall as if listening to a herd animal breathe — they are one more generalist species feeding on heat, moisture, runoff protein, and the edible leavings of machinery.

They learn the city by touch because voice will not carry more than a few steps in the baseline roar — water in a million pipes, thermal stacks humming, construction swarms chattering, transit tubes thrumming, everything powering itself in part off its own noise. So a child learns a route not by looking but by the vibration through a shared rail, by the warmth of a ceramic shoulder, by the weeping of a wall that tells him if it rained in the next district, if the fungal beds are fruiting fast, if the eels are loud in their tubes and meaning weather is turning. Adults speak the same way at close range: palm to rail, palm to wet surface, a tight-beam of touch passed from body to body. It is intimate and practical and a little blind. Eyes are useful in the sliver of ordinary summer light when it manages to cast sharp shadows, but hands are how you get through a crowd that never thins.

The swarms they tend are the strangest inhabitants of all, because they are not quite separate from the houses. Printers working at a scale too small to see, depositing matter taken from air and water and dust, layer by patient layer, glacial one minute and frantic the next. Awall can stiffen against a surge, soften to let a crowd pass by another route, heal a crack before you notice it. A street can close and open three meters to the left by evening because the plan beneath it dissolved and re-printed. The tenders do not build so much as herd: they feel a slowdown in the chatter at their fingertips the way you would feel a pulse weaken, and they move toward silence, not away from it. Here silence is never peace. Silence is a blockage. It means something has stopped being repurposed, and a small, hungry crowd will gather to see what is wrong, and to use it.

Deep down, where up and down are less directions than shifts, there are other humans who live with the cooler galleries and the flooded basements, who fish in rooms that were once dry, who keep store in vaults where the fungal mats have taken the corners and made them soft and dark. Up high, in another kind of shift, there are the tenders of the atria: sealed, humming, panel-lit gardens where old-world plants are kept as a living archive. They are tended with reverence and not allowed outside. Ferns, flowering things, leaves that expect soil and rain that has not been recycled by a vent — they live but they do not leave, because outside everything is either used or is using something else, and an unoccupied crevice is waste and waste is food and food is tempo. The atria tenders are monks and librarians in one. Their plants are the only green left, and it is green in captivity, breathing the same warm damp air as everyone else but behind a seal.

That is the loop they all live in, and they know it without stating it, though some will tell you if you put your hand with theirs on a rail: fungus breaks down ceramic dust and leavings; midges eat fungus; birds eat midges; salamanders and canids eat birds when they can and fungus and each other when they cannot; humans eat fungus, birds’ eggs, eel, and the opportunistic rest. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is empty. Nothing is left alone.

So they are vigilant in a way that looks like idleness. The salamander on the wall is not idle. The bird riding the updraft is not idle. The eel fighting its tube is in a hurry. The canid on the wire is listening with its feet. The child with his hand on the rail is reading a letter written in vibration. The water-bearer is carrying a river a few liters at a time.

Stand on a warm ledge where water beads and runs past your feet, if you can find a stillness that lasts long enough to be noticed — and it will be noticed, and occupied — and you will see them all at once in layers: eels flashing overhead, salamanders grazing beside your knee, birds stooping through the midge haze, a canid crossing above you from laundry-line to ledge without ever looking down at the ground it has renounced, fungus pushing up through the crack at your boot, and underneath it all, through it all, the bright, crowded, touch-speaking press of humans, all of them slick and warm and hungry and perfectly at home in a place that was not built on a continent so much as it replaced one, and keeps replacing itself, and has learned, in the long settling of its chaos, to be a life that contains places rather than a place that contains life.
