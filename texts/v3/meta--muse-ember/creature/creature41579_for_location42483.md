You notice them first by their leaning.

At a doorway two or three of them will be standing together, shoulders touching, backs against a warm wall, hats tilted to take the sideways rain. At the tram stop, on the steps up from a tunnel, on the narrow bridge over a condenser drain, the same posture repeats. The low blocks of Sarn-Veth lean into one another for warmth; the people have learned the architecture in their bodies. Nothing here stands alone, and after a while you understand that this is not only a rule for houses. A person alone on a wet street seems unfinished, and is soon joined by another who has been watching from a doorway, and then another, until the group resumes its slow, wordless sorting of who needs to go which way, who is waiting for the loop tram, who is simply standing to be out of the wind for a minute.

They move slowly, and not because they are old. Even the children do not run. Four kilometres up, on moss that gives under the boot like a wet mattress, on stone polished by condensation, running would waste breath and invite a fall into the chill that never quite leaves the alleys. The plateau-born have deep chests and a patient heart. They climb a staircase in a particular way: hand on the rail, three steps, a pause to let the air come back, three steps more. Strangers learn this quickly or learn to sit down halfway. The inhabitants have made a whole economy of the pause. The pause is where you exchange news, adjust a hat, check the cup on your belt for what it has caught since morning.

Everything they wear is layered wool that has not dried in years. Oilskin capes patched at the shoulders. Scarves, gloves, three sweaters, a coat over them all. The wool smells faintly, permanently, of mushrooms and rust, because it lives in that smell. Their hats have broad brims, sometimes turned up on one side into a small channel, sometimes notched so the drip from a ledge will run off harmlessly. Almost everyone carries a cup. A dented tin, an enamel mug, a glass with the rim chipped — strung on a cord, tucked in a pocket, set out on a windowsill and collected at evening. It is partly practical in a city where every eave is a gutter and every pipe a small, private rain. It is partly social: to offer someone your cup, to accept the warm, faintly metallic water in it, is the usual greeting. To leave a cup out for a guest is hospitality. To leave it out for no one is just habit. When the wind shifts in the enclosed courtyards and mist beads on the underside of a roof and falls upward for a few seconds onto the warm stones, they do not remark on it. They tilt their hats and wait.

They speak more quietly than the fog requires, because sound is absorbed anyway and breath is expensive. Sentences are short. There are a great many words for kinds of wet that never become soaking: sideways rain, stone sweat, fog tide, the moment a cold pipe frosts and then runs. Time is not told only by clocks. It is told by the tram's metronomic clack on the loop, by the foundry breathing, by the condensers filling and emptying, and above all by dripping. Drip from a ledge, from a wire, from the underside of a fern frond into a cup left deliberately below it. Children learn to count drips the way lowland children learn to count heartbeats, and to distinguish lives by their rhythm.

A house here is never one generation's work. It might have a steam engine in its cellar from the first industrial age, three storeys of riveted stone and iron, and a roof garden above that where rain that never quite falls gathers and drips down through the kitchen. Families live in the same layered manner. Grandparents in the warmest corner by the hot plate, parents in the room above where the fern roots are finding the mortar, children in the roof room where the light is grey-green and the moss is knee-deep in the hollow outside the door. An empty room does not stay empty. Moss takes it first, then a flowering shrub wedges itself into a joint, then someone moves in to tend the shrub or to sleep nearer the warmth of the wall. Building outward is pointless on a plateau that has settled into its final shape, so they build over. Their own ruins are their foundations, and their ancestors are quite literally under the floorboards, breathing in the cellar as an engine.

Most of the work of Sarn-Veth is maintenance, and maintenance is the life. They re-line a tunnel before the damp takes its rivets. They re-plant a roof before the moss drags the tiles down. They scrape lichen the colour of old pennies off a bridge girder, and leave another patch alone because it holds the frost off the iron in July. They are not fighting the green so much as negotiating with it, daily, at close quarters, the way you negotiate with a lodger who is bigger than you and will outlive you.

There are condensers tenders who climb the water towers in the funnelled wind and come down soaked by their own machines, having coaxed drinking water out of night air with cold metal. There are foundry workers tending small furnaces that cough more steam than smoke, their hands cracked by cold iron and stained green from handling copper pipe. There are mushroom farmers who live mostly underground in the tunnel farms, where it is warmer and reliably damp, emerging at midday when the sun is a pale disc to blink in the grey-green glare, carrying baskets of pale fungi that pushed up through the pavement in the morning. There are peat-moss workers who harvest the felt of moss from the hollows as a crop, cutting it in spongy turfs for mattresses, for filters, for insulation, for the tramway cisterns where children collect the drip. There are rope-makers and glass-makers whose shops are fogged from inside, and tram drivers — some of whom seem to have been born on the loop — who tick patiently around the hollow all day, sighing past the same leaning groups at the same stops, never leaving the circle because the circle is the city.

The children are small and serious and know the tunnels by touch. They are the keepers of the cisterns. Under the tramway, in the dark water that never sees the sky, live blind cave fish, pale as uncooked dough, slow in the cold. The children tap the stone and listen for the faint turn of their bodies in the water; they name individuals they cannot see, feed them algae from the condensers, and accept that some winters a fish will be eaten and some will not. It is not cruelty. At this altitude, in this damp, food is a cycle, and a cistern is a pantry as well as a well.

Elders sit a great deal. They sit in north-facing courtyards where frost is still on the iron in July, wrapped in layers, with lichen spots on their hands and moss growing in the seams of their coats where they have sat for years in the same spot. They are the ones who hear the dusk call most clearly.

But the people are not the longest-lived residents, and they know it. The quarry marmots were here before the foundries. Stone-coloured, slow, almost silent, they move at the same unhurried pace as the people who leave scraps for them on a warm rock. They are neighbours rather than pets; no one tries to domesticate them, and they do not seem to mind the tram. The rust-eating beetles arrived with the first iron and never left. They live in the scrap terraces, in the upholstered gear-wheels where whole wheels are green with fern, eating rust and leaving behind iron-rich pellets that feed the very plants that cover their homes. People leave a rusty casing empty for them on purpose, as one leaves a room for an elder. The furnace moths live their whole lives in the warm flues of the engines, dusty-winged, drawn to the amber lamps that come on early and stay on late. They get into wool, into flour, into the steam of kitchens, and the inhabitants shake them out with a patience that suggests long coexistence. They are part of the warmth, as the beetles are part of the iron, as the fish are part of the water.

The green residents are the most numerous. Moss in hollows knee-deep, moss on roofs, moss in the joints of bridge girders, moss inside empty engine casings where a pool of condensate never evaporates. It opens and closes with the humidity like a tiny lung, and people read their boots by it: if the felt is open and soft, rain is coming sideways; if it is closed and tight, the fog tide is about to roll up the dome. Ferns as tall as a person grow in the lee of a boiler house, unrolling a new frond overnight in defiance of the 4-to-10-degree summer. Alpine grasses, lichens, flowering shrubs that wedge themselves into mortar and flower anyway — they are not decoration. They are tenants, crops, insulation, clocks. Fungi push up through a pavement in the morning and collapse by evening, wild cousins of the tunnel mushrooms, and are gathered or ignored according to the hour. Iron oxide blooms like a flower, rust feeding the green that feeds on rust, and in that exchange the city is constantly being upholstered and undone.

And then, in a sense, the machines are residents too. The steam engine in the cellar breathes, slowly, like an aged relative who must be fed and listened to. The condensers hum day and night, separating water from air with a patience the people have copied. The tram is a sigh that circles the hollow. No one with sense treats them as mere tools. They are the other old things here, besides the plateau, and the plateau is so old that erosion finished long ago and sanded it soft. Stone that was folded before there was life has been planed into final form. So the movement moved up, onto the surface, into the people and their green and their iron, wearing itself very, very fast on a back that changes almost not at all.

At evening the lamps come fully on, amber and hazy in the damp, and the cloud sea below the rim turns the colour of ash. The domes go dark first, then the mist above them. In the hollow the leaning groups break up. Cups are collected, hats are shaken out — never dry, but shaken anyway — shoulders are pressed in farewell, and people go up their slow staircases, three steps and a pause, into houses that have already grown a little greener since morning. If you are very still at dusk you may hear it: a single hollow call from above the rim. The inhabitants stop. They do not answer. The children look up, the elders nod as if to an old acquaintance, the marmots freeze, the drip goes on counting underneath it all, and then nothing, for days.
