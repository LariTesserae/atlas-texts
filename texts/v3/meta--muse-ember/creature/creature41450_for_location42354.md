You hear them first, but they are not the people.

The thump comes up from the hollow before anything else. Seven seconds. Seven seconds. A clank, a whirr, a conveyor sighing every few minutes, a forklift beeping in a slow arc. And under it, answering it, the other residents — much louder, and much more at home.

Start with the water, because most of them start there. The rain does not run off here, it waits. It collects in the ruts of yards and stays for days, skinning with pollen, denting when a crow steps in. Along the foot of the grey scarp the springs bead out of fresh stone into shallow muddy pools. In every one of those pools there are frogs, and in the culverts and seep-hollows and slow leaking places too, and they have already laid. Their chorus rises and falls with the presses as if the two were in conversation. A single frog will croak on the off-beat of the press and the whole puddle will follow. They do not flinch at the machines. They time themselves to them.

Above the pools, the next population is air. At dusk the rafters of the open shed fill, not with dozens but with thousands of starlings at roost, a sound like tearing silk that goes on and on until the shed seems to be the thing making the noise, not the birds. Pigeons coo in the eaves of the plain houses. Crows sit in the tops of the loose, alone-standing trees and shout and wait — a crow here will hold its scream until the saw has finished its cut, as if politeness were a rule of the place. Swallows nest on the sharp new ledges of the scarp, where nothing has yet softened the angle, and sally out over the meadow in the flat grey-white light like stitches, back and forth, back and forth. In the day they use the warm pipes to dry the dew out of their feathers. The pipes, the motors, the generator shed — warmth is a resource here, and everyone who can climb or creep or squeeze has found a way to live on it.

At night the shift changes but the residents do not go away. Bats come out in a thin, fast stream from under the bridge of the conveyor, where they sleep through the day in a line they have chosen for themselves. Moths crowd the yard lights in a slow, damp snow. Hedgehogs snuffle through the wet grass of the empty lots. Rabbits graze out in the open between factories, unhurried, because nothing here hurries at them. A fox trots the grid of roads at the same time every evening, unafraid, and the roads, which were laid for forklifts and carts, seem at that hour to be laid for him. In the warm wall cavities of the generator shed live rats, happily, unseen except for the scratching. On the motors themselves, when they are warm and idle, sleep the cats. They are not exactly the sheds' cats and not exactly the houses' cats. They have simply decided that this is the warmest place to be in a cool place, and the people who work there have decided to let them.

And then, in from the edges, come the visitors who behave exactly like residents. Sheep from the smallholding beyond the scarp sometimes get into the orchard, and then out of the orchard, and stand damp and placid between the houses while people walk past them carrying shopping. They do not move for the shopping, and the shopping does not move for them. For a few hours at a time a small flock is just part of the streetscape, another kind of inhabitant, checking whether the grass in the fallow lots is still wet.

They are not the only ones who do not live in a house. The plants are perhaps the largest population by far, and they are the ones who have filled the places the people left empty. This was built like a town, with a grid, with power lines sagging between poles, with more lots than houses on them, but it never filled in. A press shop, then a hundred meters of wet meadow. Three houses, then a timber stack going soft at the edges. The emptiness has been colonised, impatiently, lushly. Ferns grow in the culverts. Moss thickens down the north side of every brick wall and up the legs of the forklifts. Blackberries and nettles have taken the empty plots between factories. In the fallow the wet grass grows and flops and grows again without ever being cut. Cow parsley, meadowsweet, clover in a field by itself around a generator shed — the understory is thick everywhere the buildings are not.

The trees are residents of another kind. Sycamore, ash, lime, tall and mature, but spaced so far apart that their canopies never touch, as if each one were planted to shade a single roof. They stand in a row with the houses in that sense: one tree, one roof, one household of shade underneath it. The orchard trees are left unpruned, and live accordingly, fruiting where they like and dropping where they like for the sheep. Inside the sheds, where condensation is always beading and running in slow lines down cold steel and under corrugated roofs long after the sky outside has cleared, mushrooms have moved in on the inner faces of the timber. Inside it smells of forest as well as hot oil, because a fungal population is living quite comfortably on the walls weeping.

It is only after all of that — frogs and starlings and bats and cats and moss and mushrooms — that the human residents come into focus, and when they do they are fewer than you expected. Smaller than the noise suggested. A hamlet built like a town, but with whole hundred-meter gaps of meadow between the press shop and the next house, between the sawmill and the warehouse. If you counted by noise, or by weight of bodies at night, the people would not be first. There are not thousands of them. There are not even thousands of starlings at a time to match.

They live in the row of plain houses, and in the low spread-out buildings themselves — the sawmill with its log deck, the metal shop that makes hinges and brackets and buckets for farms, the power house with its two generators throwing a little heat into the damp air, the warehouses with their roller doors, the small station house, the hall with its noticeboard. A forklift driver. A sawyer. Someone who tends the generators and does not mind the rats in the walls. Someone who keeps the noticeboard, someone who will be at the station house when a train is not. A smallholder just beyond the scarp, whose sheep are the ones in the orchard.

Their habits are shaped by exactly the same patience. It is cool even in summer. They keep a jacket over the back of a chair in June, and the iron is cold to the touch at noon. In the morning everything metal is beaded. In the evening it is beaded again. They do their close work in the sheds under electric lights, and otherwise live in that plain, even illumination that makes the green very green and the rust very orange and the concrete very pale. They hang their washing on a line — the thin white line you can see from the scarp — and walk to the shops past the sheep, and they have lived with the thump so long that no one with a sense of place finds it strange anymore that the frogs answer it and the crows wait for it. They, too, wait for the next seven seconds. They let the grass grow. They let the orchard go unpruned. They let the drip under the valve keep dripping, drip, drip, drip, the clock of the place, and the little pool of rust and mineral under it has grown maybe a finger's width in a year, and nobody has thought to stop it.

Newer buildings still wear paint glossy. Older ones are only beginning to rust at the seams. The people are a little like that. Not new, not weathered away. Seasoned by the damp. Spring lingers here and autumn lingers, the air stays cool, and over years they have slowed down to the tempo of everything else that lives here: the rust creeping, the pools waiting in their pockets, the plants waiting in the empty lots, the animals waiting on the warm metal, the machines turning slowly.

So who lives there? Almost everything that has found a niche, and found it without asking. Those who live in the standing water, those who live in the rafters, those who live under the conveyor bridge, those who live in the warm pipes and the wall cavities and the culverts and the north faces of walls, those who live alone in a tree's shade for a single roof, and the few people in the plain houses between the meadows, walking past the sheep with the shopping, listening to the frogs time their calls to the press. From the scarp at the end, looking back, you can take the whole population in at a glance — scattered roofs, wires, trees standing alone, fields, puddles catching the normal light — and it does hold together, because none of them, human or otherwise, has hurried to finish becoming itself.
