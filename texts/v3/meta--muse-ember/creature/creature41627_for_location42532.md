At first you will think nothing lives on Vhar's Crown.

The stone is too new, the heat too bare, the light too ordinary and too fierce. There is no soil to root in, no crack with a weed, nothing green to catch the eye. The city is stacked too tight to see between the walls, and above it the sky is empty except for the slow exhale of the stacks. Life here does not announce itself. It does not grow up in the open. It keeps to the damp pockets, and it keeps its distance, and it moves so slowly — or so thinly, or so quietly — that you have to stop breathing for a while before you notice it at all.

It begins under the great white tanks, and in the shadow of a north-facing duct, where the rock stays damp long after the sun has beaten the rest of the terrace dry. Cool to the palm in a hot place, dark with a rust-bloom, that is where the first living thing settles. Not a plant. A mat.

Copper-coloured fungal wefts creep in translucent sheets over the condensation, weaving themselves across black glass and sintered ash. Over them, finer than hair, run silicate threads, and in places white mineral whiskers stand up like frost — though the place is hot, and the frost never melts. People know these mats by where they spread. A weft maps a drip better than any gauge: it only lives where a tank sighs, where a pipe sweats, where night dew has gathered and not yet run off. It grows at the pace of the furnaces, a season and more for a hand's breadth, fixing rust and copper out of the water that visits. No one harvests it. To scrape it away would be to lose the sign of a pocket that will hold.

Inside the pipes, where the warmth never quite leaves, the mats give way to microbial films. Warm condensation beads and runs inside the metal, and the film lives on it, a slime of dull turquoise and ochre that clogs the flow by degrees. It is the only thing here that can truly close a passage. The Crown must be cleaned not of dust, but of this quiet interior life, and that work belongs to the crawlers.

They are the Crown's ground animals, though they were never born. Half a meter long, legged, blind, made of ceramic and iron in the same palette as everything else, a maintenance unit creeps along the ducts feeling its way into the sinter with its forelegs, scraping, pausing, scraping again. You meet them on the terraces at the unlikeliest hours, wandering with the unhurried logic of animals — a stop, a turn, a long slow traverse of a wall no one thought a path — and everyone steps around them. A terrace has its crawler the way a house has a hearth. When it tires it wedges itself into a duct-shadow and ticks as it cools. When it breaks, someone crouches and mends it in silence. It does not beg. It does not hurry. At noon, when the glass floor is too hot to stand on and the city flashes white, the crawlers are often the only movement on the benches besides the heat-shimmer.

Higher still, where a glass wall has been set double, heat on one side and damp on the other, lives the kiln-lizard.

It is flat and translucent as shed skin, pressed between the panes like something that has been in the wall forever and only remembers how to be an animal at dawn. You can see the shadow of its gut through it move when it has drunk. Its legs splay wide to hold itself in the narrow gap, claws silent on glass. All day it is invisible. At first light, when every roof and pane is beaded with dew from the sharp night cold, it emerges — slowly, so slowly that you think it is only the condensation shifting — and drinks bead by bead. Head tilted, tongue out, bead gone. Another bead. Another. It works its way along a roof edge, sharing the dawn with the people who have come out beneath their wing-like condensers to catch the same dew in jars for the day. They do not interfere with each other. The lizard never climbs for a jar, the person never lifts a pane. By mid-morning the glass warms past bearing and the lizard slips back between the panes and is a rumour in the wall again, until tomorrow's beading.

Above all of this, in the blue that goes black at the zenith, are the thermals.

They are solitary raptors, dark against the sky, and they do not so much fly as ascend. A kiln breathes out its season-long heat, the updraft stacks on itself, and a thermal finds it and circles for an hour without a wingbeat, riding a column the Crown makes without meaning to. It never lands on the Crown itself — not on the glass, not on the terraces, not on the cold top of a tank. Its feet are for another country. It belongs to the air between crowns, to the long cable spans over the cloud-sea, and the Crown is only a tower it uses to climb. People watch it the way they watch a gantry move: they have coffee during it, they let a full circuit mark a task. Its shadow, when the sun is straight, falls for a moment on the rippled floor, the only shadow here that moves that fast. Then it leaves, to find another kiln's breath on another crown.

And then, everywhere, pressed arm's-reach from your wall and your heat and your pipe, are the people — ten thousand presences in a city that is somehow silent enough to hear your own breathing over all of them.

They live in the newest stone, because the Crown grows upward and the oldest levels below have cooled and mineralised into cliff. That is the measure of a life here: when your terrace was the top, when a new course of slag-brick went down per decade, when the furnace wall settled a centimeter in a generation. A household is a bench clamped to the rock and to the furnace behind it, sharing its warmth through the wall, a duct running over the doorway, a stair bolted outside because inside was not room enough. Privacy is a matter of not looking. Distance is a matter of quiet.

They are dressed against the noon in reflective ceramic, pale plates and veils that throw the fierce light back and make a person look like a piece of white sinter that has learned to move. They move slowly — the air is thin and the lungs work too hard, the heat comes back up through the boots, the benches are narrow — and slowness is not a performance but a conservation. Water is only ever visited, so they spend it as they spend breath. They speak rarely, and when they do it is close, low, meant for one ear in the hush that sound falls into at this height. Much is said by a tap on a pipe, by a hand on a wall, by simply not being in the way.

At dawn they are out with their jars, listening for the drip from their condensers to count itself into the day. All the quiet noises of the Crown are a kind of conversation: a fan turning over once, metal ticking as it contracts, steam deciding to leave, a crawler's leg on a duct, a lizard's tongue on a bead of dew. At evening, when the glass gives its heat back slowly and condensation threads down the black rock as if the mountain were sweating in its sleep, the terraces light one by one, and the people go in against the furnace walls to share the warmth that will be gone by a different kind of cold at night.

What lives on the Sinter Crown is not many-hued or many-voiced. It is a copper mat under a tank, a film in a pipe, a translucent animal in a wall, a blind machine on a duct, a dark bird that will not land, and a pale, slow, quiet people in ceramic, all of them patient as cooling metal, all of them keeping to the margins where heat and a little damp cross, all of them emerging, layer by slow layer, into a place that is not yet finished becoming itself.
