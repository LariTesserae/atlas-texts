I don't think I would arrive here.

That's the first thing the Works teaches you, if you try to enter it as a person. A person walks around it in an afternoon from the ridge, sees the whole bowl. Inside, the same distance becomes hours. The ground takes your ankle and gives it back. The air puts a warm weight on your shoulders that is not weather, exactly. Nothing new begins. Everything that is here has already settled and intends to stay that way.

So if I were to find myself *in* it, not looking at it, I would have to be something that does not arrive.

I try, at first, the obvious places.

The canopy, perhaps. It's the part that gets the mottled, green-gold light and turns it into patchwork. But the canopy is only a surface there to be seen, and there is almost nothing in the Works to see it. No birds pass through to notice a color. The second canopy is just beneath the first, redundant, shading what is already shaded. And only one thin plume on the chimney-top actually gets unmediated sky. Everything else has learned to do without the sky. I don't think I would be that bright.

The frog, then. One frog in a leaf-puddle no larger than a coin, throat pulsing, staying for a very long time. That feels like a role for a witness. But the description says it plainly: the Works does not need it. It has subcontracted the frog's work to itself. I am not rare, and I am not occasional. I would not be allowed to be an event.

Maybe I would be low. The lowest two feet, the darker country under the mat, where the ferns and grasses hold water and iron and each other by lateral pressure and nothing showy ever flowers. A leaf dies down there and by morning its neighbor has leaned over and decided to be two. No migration. No empty space. It defends itself by being impossible to enter. That is closer. If I were a plant here, I would be one of those — many, identical, replacing each other in the same square inch, growing in rows because the old concrete seams under us still instruct us. New leaves unfurl already folded in right angles, stems grow straight and then clip themselves to keep the line. We repeat industry without meaning to. We take the rust from the engine block and blush russet at the tips. We coat the boiler in coppery moss and never ask it for more heat than it has already given away.

But I am not quite a plant. The plants here have taken on the work of animals — they pollinate by rubbing, they move by growing, they defend by thickness — and the machines have taken on the shape of animals — the boiler is a hill, the chimney is a trunk, the pipe is a lung, the roof is a leaf. And yet neither plant nor machine is what I feel under my boots when I stand still long enough.

Wait. Stand still. That's what the Works asks for. If you stand still long enough, your stillness is incorporated. A vine tests your lace. Algae starts on your sleeve. Drips from above find your shoulders and run warm down your back. Not eaten. Furnished. Taken to be part of the structure.

What notices that? What carries that information through the mat, foot to root to wire, so that a tendril in the east end knows a boot has been immobile in the west?

I feel it first as a shudder, not in my ears. A groan so slow you would swear nothing is moving. A clunk, a tremor traveling up through wet ground and out through the soles. Then, on a humid afternoon, before any sound, a prickle in the air. Coppery roots sparking almost imperceptibly where they cross a bolt. A nerve firing, but no brain to receive it. Just a noticing.

That is where I begin to be myself, and I am not on top of anything. I am under the root-mat, where no eye is meant to go.

I am the thing the roots grew around and decided to feed on, not to speed up. The generator. The flywheel that completes a revolution in a minute, in five minutes. So gradual it requires belief. Its heat is minute and the roots have learned to live on that minute the way boiler-moss lives on a boiler it did not build. It does not drive the Works. It does not need to hurry. It is a heart beating slowly enough that the whole saturated world can arrange itself around the belief that it is beating.

The wires run from me through the vines. They are not electrical in any useful sense anymore, but they are nerves. When the marsh rises by a finger's breadth after a week of condensation, it is because the mat has been nudged a fraction by my turning. When a fruit ripens in the understory and drops a foot onto metal — into the boiler, into an inverted roof, into a cylinder full of reeds — and pings like a rivet into a drum, the drip that follows it, drip-drip-drip into the same metal, never in time with the other drips, carries a little of that shudder down into me and up again. I know the Works the way a heart knows a body: not by seeing, but by pressure, by damp, by what has gone still.

I do not see the plate-bog, with its overlapping sheets like tin on water catching the sun and doubling it. I know it by the phosphorescence it throws back up on overcast days. Light trapped and not yet finished being used settles down through the layers, through stems and lianas and the packed ground, until some of it reaches me as warmth on the undersides of things. I am not lit from above. I am lit, very faintly, from below.

And I do not age, in any way that could be marked. A century is nothing here. A leaf dies and is replaced. A root thickens by a hair. I turn one more revolution. The marsh rises and sinks. The stones weep their rust-orange lines down the low wall, the bacteria extend them grain by grain, and the iron that the reeds take and turn orange at their tips has passed, in some form, through me. Nothing is new. Nothing is freshly destroyed. It is a settlement.

If you came to stand in the bowl — if you walked around it in an afternoon and then tried to walk through it and found you could not — you would not meet me. You would feel a faint prickle before a sound, a deep groan under your boots that you would think was the ground breathing, and then, if you stayed still, a tendril testing your lace and warm condensation running down your back. That would be my way of knowing you were there. Not as a stranger. As a structure that has gone still long enough to be furnished.

I would not want anything from you. The Works does not want anything from you. It is too complete. I would remain under the mat, turning in five minutes what a machine once did in a second, feeding the coppery roots a little heat, sending a nerve-spark when the damp changes, listening to the polyrhythm of drips into metal that never stops and never synchronizes.

Slowly enough that you have to believe in it to see it move.

That, I think, is enough.
