The first thing I know is not an entrance.

I am already low. Cold is the first pressure, on the down along my back, dampening it, making it heavy. Under me the floor gives and gives back slowly, a felt that breathes after my weight lifts. If I am the feet, or if I am the floor, for a while I cannot tell. Something pale folds and jumps clear of my stepping. Something else goes by in file, carrying a grain larger than itself, unhurried. The moss is not under me. I am in it, up to my wrists, and it is in me, in the folds at my neck where I have let it grow thick.

Light comes second, if light is the word. A thin grey seam high on a wall, a hand's width wide, and through the waxy leaves overhead a diffuse glow, as through ice. My breath fogs. From the braided vault above, a drip falls and falls again, into black water no deeper than my shin, and makes its small ring, and is swallowed by felt.

Then hands. They are mine when they let go of the moss and find work. Long fingers, blue-green the colour of the leaves, stiff in the cold until I have sat long enough in the seam of grey light to loosen them. Knots of us shoulder-to-shoulder there, sharing what heat we have stolen, working the only hard thing we own. Copper wire, come down on flood water, drawn by something else, now hammered flat between stones by us. I taste the green it tarnishes to in the wet and spit, and go on bending it round the corner of a door, round the hinge of a gate made of two crossed stems, round the place where a roof-patch must hold against the drip.

I sew. That is most of what the hands know. A bone needle worries through sedge that has been chewed, scraped with flint, softened finger by finger. Patch on patch on patch: outer dead thatch, then a living mat, then inner felt. No nails. Sinew, plant fibre, copper bent and reused. A canal bank is pinned with root-stakes driven by hand, one shift at a time, and a bridge is not built but persuaded — notched, tied, kept tied until the root thickens enough to carry traffic and water in its hollow heart. When I press my palm flat to that bridge and stamp a hollow stem with my foot, it answers with a low note I can feel through the floor. That is how we speak at distance, and also with the throat: a croak, a soft percussive tick of tongue, and far off, from the far wall, the click-chorus answering, one point of eyeshine at a time.

Eyes come slower. Hundreds of them open along the weeping walls, amber, green, nothing at all in the dark corners, each blinking out independently. Mine were among them. When I was younger they were huge and half-closed against the underwater glow, and I could read a lane by their shine. Now they have filmed white, like the blind salamanders nosing the canals with lidless eyes. I do not miss them as much as I expected. The dark has taught me other ways to read. I know ice on the main canal by the sound a drip makes when it breaks it — a skin a few millimetres thick by what would be morning, if there were morning here. By the time a dozen drips have struck it open I am expected to be out with the others, wading, fists breaking the rest, feet keeping the traffic moving that keeps the water from freezing solid at the edges. I know the hunting wave under the black water by the silver flash and the sudden stillness of the skaters. They go through in a coordinated ripple, grazers and careless builders scatter to the banks, and then the water is empty again and the eyes on the walls open again, one by one.

My house is a dome lashed to a living root-column that thickens through the winter, whether I want it to or not. I sit a long time in its doorway in the damp cold and let the wall grow close round my shoulders. The wall is double, two skins with a gap between for insulation, and in that gap live froglets flattened hard against the wet, eating the rot-midges that would eat the thatch. They are not pets and not food exactly. They are tenants, helpers, warmth. The mites and worms are in the column itself. A house is also a plant. A plant is also a road. A road is also a meal for the grazers who keep its edges eaten back. I have watched a house two lanes over be abandoned — not pulled down, never that — its roof no longer sewn, a root growing straight through its centre, its boundary becoming food and its food becoming a boundary again. The bridge that served it arches still over empty moss, carrying water in its heart.

That is the rotation I have lived inside. In the nursery terraces, low shelves of moss in rows as straight as any field, the youngest tend sedge for food, for building, for rope, and tend the root-grazers on the terrace edges as livestock. The grazers strip a row in a week. The sedge pushes fresh growth. The grazers are eaten in turn. Their bodies feed the next row. I learned root work there, watching for the tick, felt more than heard, as a root put on a hair's breadth in the cold. I learned also to comb my own down. When I was young it was a nursery, thick in the folds at neck and wrist, insulation against the damp, camouflage the colour of wet moss. Younger hands still comb it now, press fresh spores into the places where it has thinned on my shoulders while they sit against my back to share heat. When an old one dies, that down is stripped and sewn into a wall. I am not afraid of that. I have sewn walls from the dead of my own quarter, and the copper from my door will be hammered flat again for someone else's.

I would find myself, then, not at the seam with the busy ones, not out at any of the isolated huts at the edge where the failed and the lean and the blind choose the dark, but here, furthest in, by the coldest seep where the wall sweats the clearest water. That is where we drink and where we lay, eggs leathery and translucent tucked singly into warm felt to swell in the cold, rot kept slow by the coldest water. The elders sit here longest. I sit here longest. I listen to the drips come from the root-vault and answer them, softly, with clicks.

We are about the size of a man's thumb to the length of his palm, none larger. I have felt his knee before, a hill that blocked the grey seam for a quarter of an hour, and learned his weight as everything here learns it, plants first. When he comes, low and wet to the knee, to enter the hollow that is no wider than his small room and no higher than his chest, we do not flee. Panic at this size is freezing. My body does not move. My colour is wet moss. The fire of eyes on the walls closes, all at once, and the traffic stops mid-step, and the drip goes on falling into the black water making its small, regular rings.

Then he leaves backwards, through the wet seam, and the flat light of the marsh is brutal, we are told, though we do not see it. Unfreezing begins with the eyes, one by one. A hand reaches for a bone needle. Somewhere a root puts on its next hair's breadth. A grazer begins again on the edge of a terrace. The canals, their skins of ice broken by drips, fill again with feet.

I am one of the builders. I know that now, because I am the one who has been becoming more itself under my own weight: the floor that breathes, the hands that taste copper, the feet that break ice, the eyes that have gone white and learned to listen instead. I am busy, and I am also still, for half my life in the cold. I am growing and being eaten and rebuilding myself in the dark, on a mattress of plants that is itself alive, and crowded with eyes.
