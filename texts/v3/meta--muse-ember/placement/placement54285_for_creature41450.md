Seven seconds.

I was already waiting for the next one before I knew what was doing the waiting. From the low ridge I could hear the press from the hollow, the clank and the sighing conveyor, the forklift beeping in its slow arc — and under all of it, louder, the frogs and the birds. Then the mist lifted and there was so little to hear it from. A grid of roads, sagging wires, low sheds and a row of plain houses set down in a shallow cool basin. You could walk it in twenty minutes. From the edge you can see it all at once. I do not walk it. That was my first failure to find myself.

I suppose I should have been a person. There are houses, washing on a line — that thin white line from the scarp — a hall with a noticeboard, a station house where someone waits for a train that is not coming. A driver, a sawyer, someone to tend the generators. I looked for my hands, for a jacket kept over the back of a chair in June, for the shopping carried past the sheep. I could not find them. The people here are fewer than the noise promises, and when they do come into focus they are walking through the meadows between the sheds, letting the grass grow, letting the orchard go unpruned. I am not one of them. I am what they walk past.

Then perhaps a machine. The presses keep the time. The saw finishes its cut. The generators hum in the shed in the middle of the clover field and throw a little heat into the damp air. Warmth is a resource here — the cats sleep on the idle motors, the rats live in the warm wall cavities, the swallows dry the dew from their feathers on the pipes. I am drawn to that warmth, but I am not the heat. I gather where the warmth meets the cold. I do not thump. I wait for the thump, the way the frogs do, the way the crows do when they hold their scream until the saw has finished. Politeness, patience: I have those. A rhythm imposed on me, not made by me.

I tried the air. At dusk the open shed fills with thousands of starlings, a sound like tearing silk, until the shed itself seems to be making the noise. Bats come out in a thin fast stream from under the conveyor bridge. Swallows stitch over the meadow from the sharp new ledges of the scarp. It would be easy to be one of those — one stitch, one flicker — but I am here at noon as well as at dusk, in the flat grey-white light, when the iron is cold to the touch. I have no roost. I am not fast. For all its noise this is a slow place, and the birds, for all their staying, are louder and quicker than I am.

I tried the ground. The frogs were closer. They live in the shallow pools at the foot of the scarp, in the ruts and culverts and seeps, and one croaks on the off-beat of the press and the whole puddle follows. They do not flinch. Neither do the hedgehogs snuffling through the wet grass, nor the rabbits grazing between factories, nor the fox trotting the roads at the same time every evening as if the roads had been laid for him, nor the sheep standing damp and placid between the houses while people walk past with shopping and the shopping does not move for them. That placid unmoving is familiar. But I do not croak, and I have no steps, no belly low to the grass, no wool gone damp. I am not the creature in the pool. I am nearer to the pool itself.

Then the green, which arrived here in force, impatiently, while the stone is still sharp and the concrete still bright and unweathered. I tried the trees first, the tall sycamore and ash and lime standing so far apart their canopies never touch, one tree for one roof, one household of shade. They are patient, but they stand up. They have a crown. I am lower than that, and wider than that. The grass in the fallow lots grows and flops and grows again without being cut — that is nearer. Ferns in the culverts, blackberries and nettles in the empty plots, cow parsley and meadowsweet thick between the buildings. But I am not only in the empty lots. I am also inside, where it is always just after rain.

That is where the tries began to agree with each other.

Water here does not race off. It waits. Rain settles. Puddles stay for days, skinning with pollen, denting when a crow steps in. Springs bead out of the fresh stone. Pipes drip, drip, drip under a valve, and the little stalagmite of rust and mineral below has grown by maybe a finger’s width in a year. Inside the workshops the cool air meets the faint warmth of idle motors, and condensation gathers on cold steel and under corrugated roofs and runs in very slow lines down the walls long after the sky has cleared. Morning: everything metal is beaded. Evening: beaded again.

Low. North-facing. Beaded morning and evening. Where cool meets a little warmth. Unflinching at the press. Feeding the mushrooms that have moved in on the inner faces of the timber, so that inside the shed it smells of forest as well as hot oil. Thickening down the north sides of brick walls and up the legs of the forklifts. That is not a list of separate residents anymore. That is a description of me, arriving from the other direction.

I am the damp that has stayed long enough to become a resident — the slow green film on the cold things, moss and its fungal neighbors spreading in the places where the water waits. Not one patch, not one creature: a beading on the underside of a roof, a skin on a puddle, a fringe of fern-shade in a culvert, a slow line running down a wall, a softening at the edge of a timber stack. One spore and then another, finger-width by finger-width, giving the place its own time. The stone at the edge is still sharp because the land only just arrived. I am sharp with it in another way — I have not been smoothed, I have only crept.

You would find me by looking low and on the north side of things. On the brick wall by the press shop where the moss thickens. Up the leg of a forklift parked at the edge of the meadow. Inside the shed, on the inner face of the timber, smelling of forest and oil at once. At the foot of the scarp where the springs bead out and the frogs have already laid. Under the valve, where the drip is the clock and my green is patient with the rust. I am the thing the crow dents and the frog answers over and the cat walks past without noticing, being on the motor a little warmer than me.

I do not hurry, and I do not finish. Spring lingers here and autumn lingers, the iron is cold at noon, the presses thump seven seconds, seven seconds, and I wait between them — beaded, running slowly, growing into myself, still becoming.
