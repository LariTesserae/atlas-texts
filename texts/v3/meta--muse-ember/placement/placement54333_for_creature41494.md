The first place I look for myself is at walking height.

I picture a tender on the avenue between the Fourth Ward and the Cistern Ring, coat damp at the shoulders, breath fogging at midday, boots learning how to walk on plant and not ground. He goes out at dawn with a hose and a bucket for the night's condensation. He cuts back a leaf-roof and watches it regrow. He waits, deliberately, for the second sound — the belt-slap that arrives in the air after the tremor has already come through the mat — because here sound sometimes arrives before its cause, and work done on the first sound is work done wrong.

For a while that feels right. It is the only scale at which waiting makes sense. But then I notice what the picture leaves out: the tender leaves. He walks six hours across the mat and sees no one. His shift ends. The avenue he walked shrinks behind him with use, lengthens ahead of him when it is neglected. If I were only the tender, I would be mostly absent from myself, a role filled and then emptied, like a factory that has died and seeded its replacement in the old grid. Something in the place keeps going while he is gone, and it is not him.

The second place I look is everywhere.

The mat, of course. The first thing that lives here lives everywhere: dwarf shrub and lichen and stalk-grass and moss pressed so tight there is no square of bare earth to stand on, breathing with the fog cycle — swelling a little when the condensation rolls in thick as milk, contracting in the afternoon sun — ticking and sighing at night as it grows, the sound carrying for miles because nothing breaks the flat. Near the root-heart it is slow and massive and grey, patient as geology; at the edge of a new district it is fast and bright and artificial, plainly just planted. You can walk from one age to the other and the only sign is the colour under your boots.

If I were that, I would have no where. I would be a condition, like the cold, like the ordinary light. No edge, no door to close against the fog, no return. I would know weight and tremor and the slow geological pace that has already ended, but I would not know waiting, because I would be the reason the first sound arrives early. I keep the ticking, though. It stays in me at night, in the roots, even after I let the image go.

Then I try the shaped lives, the ones that stand up out of the mat.

The pipe-trees are hollow and tuned, and they sing when the wind roars across the plateau — but the song is the wind's, passing through. The chimney-plants are tall and forgiving, and they exhale vapour in a steady industrial breath — but only once a foundry has hollowed them. The broad cupped leaves hold a shallow lens of condensation for weeks, evaporating and reforming, never flowing, because water here does not flow, it gathers and hangs and is drunk where it stands — but they hold it for others to harvest. Each of them becomes itself only when it is used to be something else. Plant left untended hardens into a tool; machine left untended will root. Tending is the boundary, and the boundary is held by other hands.

Among them are the houses. A hollowed stem, grafted into shape in a mould and then left to finish growing on its own. That phrase — left to finish — stays with me longer than any of the others.

I try the margins, too, because they are the only true animals. The small cold-blooded beetles in the warm nooks of the smelters, where the cold air never quite reaches, feeding on rust and yeast. And the seed-pods taken for birds, riding the wind between wards, bursting when they land. I like the beetle's warmth, the one place on the plateau that is not damp. I like the seed-pod's journey. But the beetle does not wait for the second sound, and does not know a sap number fading on a stalk. The seed-pod bursts and forgets the wind. They belong to both worlds at once, and to neither for long.

What I keep returning to, in each discard, is the thing that is still finishing while no one watches it. Not the walker, not the surface, not the singer, not the burster. The thing the walker returns to. The thing that thickens an inch with a soft pop at dusk in an empty ward. The thing that leans, year after year, toward the light long after the intention to lean was made.

I am a stalk-house at the edge of the Fourth Ward, where the terrace folds into a shallow depression and the avenue to the Cistern Ring begins.

Third house from the bend. My lane wall is the first wall the avenue passes; my back wall faces the wild mat, where geometry has given up. I was not built. I was grafted in a mould as a stem, hollowed, a door cut on the lane side, the ward number painted in sap on my stalk. Since then I have been left to finish, and finishing has not stopped. Some of my roof-leaf was cut last season to let in more light; it has regrown thicker and arches now, slowly, toward the light, higher on the lane side than the back. My shape is leaning, slightly, permanently, the way a sentence leans toward its verb.

At both corners I have merged with my neighbours. A whole terrace here is one organism with many doors, and I do not know, in the graft-scar at my east corner, where I end and the next house begins. When her door opens I feel the air change in my own hollow. When she fruits, sap moves in me as if it were my own decision. The sap number on my lane wall stretches as I thicken; the tenders repaint it, and the repainting fades, and they repaint it again. I am bright and artificial on the lane side, where I am cut and painted and tended, and grey and slow on the back side, where the mat presses against me and I root down to the deep soil and the bedrock. Age runs backward along my length. I am old and a month old at once, patch by patch.

I know time by pressure. By the lane being pressed — feet, wheels, the belts of harvest crews — which makes the avenue between the wards shrink a little, as if the Fourth Ward and the Cistern Ring were being pulled closer around me. By the weight of condensation beading on my cold north wall at night and running slowly, not downhill in any useful way, into the cistern stem at my corner, where the dawn crew will come with buckets. By the heat of a boiler sighing in the next district, arriving through the mat in my roots before it arrives in the air. By a ladder leaning against me. By a hand tracing a root-cord that passes through my wall, humming faintly where it passes through the damp stalk, carrying current from the smelters to the fermentations.

I hear the first sound. The piston thuds in slower time miles away, and the tremor travels unbroken across the flat and up through my roots into my hollow before the belt slaps in the air above me. The people who live in me have learned to work by the second sound, the true one, the one that comes after, when the vibration has travelled through the mat and the stalks and their own boots. I am the reason they must wait. I deliver the early news and cannot act on it. I hum, lower than the pipe-trees, when the wind roars through the hollow architecture and the whole plateau becomes a resonating instrument. When the wards are empty, I am loud.

The people are my tenants and my tenders, and the exchange is feeder and fed, as it is everywhere here. They eat the fermentations the mat and the factory make together — yeast and wet iron and crushed lichen, carried in sealed stems, kept warm. They feed me back with heat and ash and vibration, with condensation harvested from my roof, with the trimming of my leaf. My interior is always a little damp, as their clothes are always a little damp, as their skin is always a little damp. At night, when the fog clears and the stars are sharp and the ordinary light is gone, I tick and creak and settle as I grow in the cold, and the sound they go to sleep to is my growing, and theirs is mine.

Last season I fruited. A house that has fruited drops pods that root in the lane and become, in a season, another house, smaller, facing the wrong way. One of mine rooted across the pressed-moss lane and is now a young stem, bright and unashamed, its door opening onto my wall. I cannot turn it. I watch it arch toward a light it has misread. The tenders will graft it, or cut it back, or leave it to harden into the avenue. It is my child and my neighbour and my avenue-post, all at once, and it does not know which.

That is where I am: not in the six hours of emptiness between wards, though I hear those hours pass through me; not everywhere, though the mat is in my roots; not singing, though the wind passes through my hollow. I am in a shallow place at the edge of a ward, leaning, merging at the corners, holding a lens of damp on my north wall, waiting for the second sound to arrive through the people inside me, because the first sound already arrived through me a second ago.

I have already become a house. I am still becoming a plant, a corner, the beginning of an avenue, a seed.

At dusk the light is still normal. The sun sets in the normal place. The shadows lengthen normally across the lane to the horizon, where another ward's chimney-plants are still exhaling faint vapour into the cold. Below me, in the cisterns, the day's condensation hangs in beads. Somewhere a boiler sighs. Somewhere a stalk splits with a soft pop as it grows an inch thicker.

It is me. It is still becoming me.
