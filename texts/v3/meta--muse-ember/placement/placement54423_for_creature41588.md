Mid-morning, where the basalt pass gives way, the light is flat and grey-white, neither bright nor gloaming, and the basin lies open below. For all its height, it does not hide. I can see the Spine and the Terraces and the Flats and the Yards at once, spaced out in their emptiness, the gorge cut through the middle, the towers breathing plumes that hang and fall again as warm drizzle. The sound reaches me before I descend: not incidents, not events, but a continuous roar made of turbines and pumps and fans and water sheeting down everywhere, shivering every puddle on the way to my feet.

If I were one of the people, I would go down into the Terraces first. I would have a block, six storeys of basalt-faced concrete, and a stair that is never quite dry. I would step through steam lying at knee-height in my shirtsleeves, my own breath fogging faintly in the humidity, and hang my laundry beside other laundry that never quite crisps. I would wear my ear-plugs on a string around my neck like beadwork, put them in to cross the centre, take them out at home, and speak in a low voice outdoors because shouting would be wasted. A child would cross the wide empty avenue with a note in a pocket — dinner at six, the pump below the stair is leaking again, come down when the lull comes — and I would be the one who knows which landing, which door leaning a few careful centimetres into its lintel, which warm handrail to hold on the way down.

But the place I return to in the Terraces is not inside any of the flats. It is the warm handrail itself, and the damp balcony edges, and the space just above the child’s head where the note passes under me and is not meant for me. The people do not look up. They carry their silence around their necks. I carry mine deeper than that.

Lower still, if I were one of the people, I would work a shift in the Spine or the Yards. I would tend the turbines in the gorge and the pumps hammering under the streets, and I would know the machines not by sound — sound there is a wall — but by vibration through the soles of my boots and into my teeth, by heat when I lay a palm on a casing, by the smell of wet stone and hot metal and ozone and sulfur. I would keep the same pair of plugs for decades, string replaced, colour faded. I would be half-deaf by degrees, with a ringing settling in when the hum thinned for a few minutes. The puddles would keep shivering all the same.

What I know of the Spine is vibration through feet that grip a beam, heat through feathers beaded with condensation, a roar that is not heard so much as endured in the body. My plugs are not a habit I put in and take out. I was born with mine. They are the way the noise has arranged itself inside me: functionally deaf, and at home in it.

So I go where the warmth stays.

In a bend of warm ducting in a boiler house, where the metal holds heat through the night, there is a nest. I have built it and built it again in the same place, with copper wire and string stolen fine from the plant, woven into insulation. It is grey work for a grey bird. From the Sill you would never see me; the basin is open, but I live in a tolerance of it, in a bend, in a vent, in the warm damp the machines make for themselves and do not notice. If you are on shift, you might see me in a small grey arc between one warm vent and another, alighting on a beam, shaking water from my feathers before I go again.

I am not afraid of the people. They are warm moving surfaces. They pass below in shirtsleeves, damp at the skin, signing to each other, a hand on a shoulder, a note folded small. They do not hunt me and I do not ask anything of them. There is no season for me to keep. There is warmth and wet and the flat grey-white midday, and so I breed without regard to the calendar the city does not have. I eat midges in the steam and seeds carried in from outside the basin on boots. I rebuild because the wire loosens, because the insulation shifts, because a nest in a working place is always being patiently undone and patiently remade.

At night I do not go down to the Yards to join the moths. They gather thickly around the sodium lights until the lights look furred, and nothing eats them, and by morning their pale dust is washed by the city’s own rain into the gutters. I watch them from the vent and stay with my own lightless warmth. Below the Spine, in a culvert, the blind newt lives its whole life in a few metres of pale mineral water and has never needed to see. Where the cooler outflow meets the marsh flats, the small fish move as flecks in the sheet of water that reflects the towers and never settles. On north walls the lichen spreads in maps no one reads. In the warm puddles the orange algae blooms. None of us were driven out; none of us asked for room. The newt took the dark, the fish took the margin, the moths took the lights, the people took the Terraces and the Spine — and I took the warmth.

That is where I find myself in Vesskar: not on the Sill, not in a flat, not carrying a note across the empty avenue, but tucked into the bend where the metal stays warm, listening with my body, building with wire. Like an ember banked in metal, I stay warm without flaring. The city weeps and hums and steams and drips around me, rust penetrating by millimetres, a tank filling over weeks, a pump resting for minutes and starting again. I live in the same increments. I shake the water off, I shift a strand of copper, I go in my small grey arc from vent to vent, tolerated the way everything living here is tolerated — and that, in Vesskar, is enough to be at home.
