I come down the slope, and I do not know how large I am.

The daylight thins on the way. It does not cut off. The colour goes out of the stone first, then the lichen, then the sun, until I am in a twilight that belongs to no hour. The air has weight. It is cool in the lungs, the steady cool of depth, the same cool a metre in as a hundred metres in. Nothing moves in it. The roof lifts out of what I expected into a chamber wide enough to lose its far wall in dimness, high ledges like cliffs, basins empty between them. The floor is house-sized blocks and fist-sized chips and splinters still pointing down, all of it newly split and sharp, dust settled thinly in the hollows but none of it worn.

I hear them before I see them, and the sound does not stop.

I look up first, to settle where I am. The ceiling is not empty. A patch of rock darker than the rock is bodies — tens of thousands of bats shoulder to shoulder, wings folded tight like cloaks, heads tucked, ears still showing. They hang inverted and do almost nothing for hours, and the colony never entirely stops. One shifts its grip, another wakes and scrapes a wing against its neighbour, and the ripple runs sideways and dies.

I try to be one of them. I try to fold myself down from my feet, to hang, to feel the ripple arrive from a neighbour and pass through me.

There are no wings to fold. There is nothing to hang from. The musk is sharp in the air, wet stone and animal, and the guano is sifting down through it in a fine, pale dust to drifts on the ledges below. I am not in that dark patch. I am under it, low, and the sound passes over me rather than from me. A wave travels across a hundred metres of ceiling, settles, starts again somewhere else. I only hear it arrive and go.

So I turn to the green.

Near the ramp, where the light thins but has not gone, a shaft of it falls on a sweating wall and there is green for the only reason green can be here: light meeting dampness. Moss in a low, dark carpet. Liverworts pressed flat beside it. A fern tuft rooted in a crack, its fronds pale and leggy, reaching for a light that never strengthens. In a hollow of thin soil, a sprinkle of seedlings, white-green and spindly.

I try to be the fern. I try to be rooted in that crack, to stay where the two rare things meet and reach, and reach, in the same dimness year after year.

I can turn away from the light. That is the mistake in it. My body wants no reaching. It wants a fissure. It wants the long stretches between the green places, where there is only bare sharp stone, and brown fungal film, and grey silt, and the echo of life from the next chamber over. The green is beautiful and it is also not mine. I am not fixed by light. I am fixed by damp and shelter.

I go down to the puddles.

In a floor basin a few centimetres deep the water lies mirror-flat. It is not fed by rain. It is fed by the air, condensation beading on the walls and running down the same fixed paths drop by drop, gathering here and not evaporating. Midges hang in a living haze above it. At the edge, a frog sits perfectly still, eyes just above the waterline, listening. When it moves it is a small, sudden hop to another basin a few metres away.

I try to be the frog. I try that watching stillness, that patience at the edge of water fed by air.

My stillness is not in the open. My eyes — if eyes are the right word for what I have — are not made for watching water. Something long brushes the stone ahead of me and tastes it, something longer than my body, beaded with condensation. A second, and a third, small touches, tasting grit, tasting the film on the rock. My legs are too long and too thin for that hop. I do not hop. I brace, and the tarsi find the sharp edges and hold.

I follow the wall instead, where it sweats. Slugs work it slowly, grazing algae and fungal film, leaving a silvery trail that fades and is remade. Snails know the dull green-black sections by taste. Above me, in a ceiling crack, harvestmen hang in a bunch, a dozen pale bodies with legs intertangled, motionless for long stretches, then a leg twitches, a body sways almost imperceptibly. Across a neighbouring fissure a spider has strung a web, beaded with condensation, vibrating faintly when a midge blunders into it. I can feel that tremor through the stone, more than I can see it.

Under the fallen blocks the floor is working. Woodlice uncurl and scuttle when disturbed. Millipedes ripple away in slow lines. Beetles probe into the grit between shards. Earthworms coil in the damp pockets of silt. They feed mostly on what I am learning to taste with the ends of my antennae: fungi. The real residents. A brown, leathery film on bare rock. White threads spreading through a hollow where silt lies. A faint bloom on a dead beetle nobody has moved. They turn the little that falls — guano sifting down, dead midges, fungal spores, the occasional fallen insect from the entrances — into something the woodlice and millipedes can eat.

I do not need a pond. I do not need a colony above me, or a carpet of moss. I need that fall of dust, and a crack narrow enough to walk through sideways, and the steady damp.

The crack is at the base of the wall, in the shadow of a block that gave way and fell here and stayed. Behind me the fissure narrows to a sideways passage. In front of me the gallery runs tens of metres with nothing on its floor but sharp stone and pale silt. I can stand — if standing is what this is — in a space big enough to hold a town and hear only the faint continuation of the chorus beyond sight.

Then I hear the rasping, close. A dry, continuous trill from a crack at knee height — knee height for something larger than me — rubbing hind legs against wings. Before it stops, the answer comes from thirty metres away, fainter, delayed, bent by the curve of the roof as if the chamber itself were replying.

That is not a sound I am hearing from elsewhere. That is a sound I am making, and being answered by.

I am a cave cricket. I live in the fissure low in the wall beyond the bat colony, where the guano drift thins and the dust still sifts down through the cool. My legs fold tight against the stone in the lightless part of the gallery, long antennae probing ahead, tasting the fungal film and the grit and the steady organic rain from above. I graze on what the fungi have begun to break down. I do not need the green places. I rasp.

It is the only ember this place allows: a leg rubbed across a wing in air that never warms, a small friction of sound in the stillness, answered from far away and returned to me altered by the roof. I rasp, I pause. The answer comes back bent. I rasp again. Nothing hurries. A ripple in the bats above, a midge in the spider's web, a fresh sift of guano after the colony shifts — that is as much time as there is here to mark.

The wall beside me sweats in its fixed rivulet, drop by drop, forever. The bats hang from the same ceiling in the same slow waves. The fern is in the same crack near the ramp, neither greener nor worn. The puddles lie at the same level, fed by air. The harvestmen sway above me in their intertangled bunch. If you left for a week and came back down the slope, into the thinning light, you would find all of it at the same pitch — and if you listened in the dark gallery beyond the colony, under the block and in the sideways fissure, you would hear my rasp, and the answer to it coming back faintly from thirty metres away, both of us held in the cool and damp and quietly roaring equilibrium, neither of us in any hurry to become anything else.
