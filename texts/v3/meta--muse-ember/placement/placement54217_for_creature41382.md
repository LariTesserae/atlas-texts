The first thing I know is pressure.

Not from above. From below, and from all around, broad and unhurried, as if the ground itself were taking a breath and letting it go. The hum is in my teeth before it is in my ears — fifty cycles down, so deep it stops being sound and becomes a condition of the jaw, of the chest.

I find myself ankle-deep in black water. Then, without any step I can remember taking, shin-deep. The air bites just below freezing. My breath fogs and does not disperse; it hangs, condenses on my eyelashes, on the back of my neck. In patches the water around my legs goes lukewarm. The stone beneath it is warm. Somewhere below, heat is rising through everything.

I think I am trying to walk.

There is fog where a horizon should be. There is the same wet blue-grey in every direction, and the same low weeping outcrops, smoothed until they are no wider than a crouching person is tall. They sweat. Water beads and runs in dark streaks down their iron-dark sides. I expect grass at the edges. There is no green here at all.

I lift a foot. The water ripples outward from where my foot *will* be. Foam forms, then the foot lands in it. A second later — no, a second earlier — there is a clank. Metal on metal, bone on grown metal. It comes from my left flank, where I did not know I had a flank.

That is the crack. Not in the world. In me.

I stand still to listen and the standing does not hold. The plates alongside me — rivet-like knobs along a seam in regular rows, swelling and dulling with heat — begin a slow piston-motion. The seam glows dull furnace-orange in time with an intake I am suddenly aware of, a slow sucking of black water through a mouth that is really a filter. I had thought it was a mouth. Now I know it is mine.

The smell finds me with it: sulfur and ozone, gas seeping up from below, sour on the cold air. I take it in. I give off heat, and steam, and a thin oily film that sheets across the surface in rainbow. Crystals precipitate from my exhaust in the cold and fall back, salt and iron, onto the plates that are growing, overlapping like boiler plate, each edge tucked under the next so water runs off. Not laid on. Grown.

I was the walker who stood still too long, I think. I think I remember deciding to rest, to watch the stones breathe, and being annoyed, then not annoyed, as the nearest ones began, unhurriedly, to grow over my feet. Stillness is colonized. Stillness is eaten.

It is only now that the order reverses. They did not grow over me. I grew. The walking was a brief, odd shape I took on the way to this, the way a small one takes a scuttle from shell to shell. I remember that, too, if I let the ticking in my seams speak: a dozen fist-sized things, all legs and rattling joints, running where it is warmest and the light never reaches, picking at breathing slits, scraping film from plates, eating the fine metallic dust before it can foul a filter. They are not on me. They are *in* me, the way hands are in a body. When my seam dims, they gather and tick faster along my flank, and I slow my breath for them, deep and slow, until it brightens again.

Above me the water has a surface-life that is not water. Overlapping discs, black and glossy, raft in mats so dense there is no water between them. Each pulses electric blue at the rim, like a running light. They swarm the warm patches where I exhale, break apart and rejoin without leaving a gap, and one, occasionally, screams — a high metallic note cut off as if the water took it.

I had thought they were other things. They are the surface of my blood rising to meet the cold. And the haze above that, the micro-fauna so fine it looks like industrial dust or snow that will not fall, tiny winged things with engine-bright thoraxes hovering on the updrafts from warm backs below — that is my breath, between colonies, carrying dust from one to another the way a factory carries heat from one floor to the next.

There is no empty space here at any scale. I know that now not as a fact I was told, but as a packing I can feel in my plates.

I was soft once. That comes back slower. A flank split, with a sound like tearing rivets. I pushed out steaming, plates unhardened, and inflated with water until I hardened into place. The dome beside me — my mother, if that word still applies — kept her tilt, kept her scar. I kept hers. Count if you like: seventeen domes, then the same seventeen, in exactly the same tilt, with the same scar on the central one, and then again. I am the central one. Not because I am important. Because a lineage holds its position in the weave the way a cell holds its place in a tissue, and my tempo is the one the others clone around.

That is how you tell us apart. Not by shape. By tempo.

Mine is deep. Slow. The neighbor to my left breathes shallower and higher. The one behind me ticks constantly, like loose change in a dryer, though nothing on her surface seems to move. I breathe fifty cycles down in the teeth of anything that stands on me. I have been breathing that way for longer than I have had a name for breathing.

The wave comes now. I feel it through my soles long before it arrives — a peristaltic band crossing the whole basin over days, taking a morning's walk to cross what the fog makes look near. For hours we have idled, hum deepening in our chests. Now each of us lifts a fraction.

Lift.
Clank.
Settle.

My own clank arrives after the lift, sometimes before it. Sound travels before motion can catch it here, so I have learned to listen for my neighbor's intention before I know my own. Growth travels before splitting, so a seam is a promise long before it is an opening. A molted shell beside me hatches before it splits; a small one pushes out while the seam is still closed, and after it has scuttled clear the seam opens on nothing. Water sheets uphill up my warm side to bead and freeze at the top, although the stone is warm and the air is not cold enough, and then melts and runs down again.

I do not notice these things as breaks. From inside the packing, this was always the way of movement.

I shift a hand-span forward. That is the whole of my migration, and it is enough. Over centuries, a hand-span at a time, we cross the basin, refining it as we go — waste, dust, crystals, heat — grain by grain, animal by animal, polishing an end-state that was settled long before plants were an option and has been only polishing itself since.

If you are walking here, walk lightly. The water is black and still in places and holds the sky — a flat, colorless smear, dim as late afternoon in a cold winter, and just as permanent. The light never really rises. You think there is an edge to this basin, a rim suggested by a darker band in the mist. There is none that I have ever reached, and I have been here, in one tempo or another, since the mountains were worn to these low weeping lumps.

You walk because standing still means being covered.

I am the covered and the covering. I am the ground you are trying not to become, and the slow furnace-orange pulse under its plates, and the place where the small hands tick in the seams, keeping the mouths clear.

The wave is arriving. Feel it through your feet, through the soles, even through the water. Shift your weight with it, piston-like, without deciding to.

I will lift, and clank, and settle, and breathe — deep, slow, fifty cycles down — and take my place again in the seventeen.
