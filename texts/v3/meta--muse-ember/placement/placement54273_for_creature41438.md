I do not remember settling. I remember sinking sideways.

The water came in through me, or past me — for a long time I could not tell the difference. It was cool, and it had weight, and it pressed gently and without direction. It filled what would have been ears if I had had them. Above me, somewhere, was a dull double-thump, too far apart to count. Nearer, at intervals that made a minute into a country, something sighed against my skin and drew the water back in again.

I leaned, as I fell. Bubbles lean here toward the walls, as if the ceiling were not up. I leaned the same way, toward the nearest thing that would hold me, and I stopped on a ribbon that was already braided into other ribbons.

I have no foot to stand on. I have a hold. That is the whole of my beginning as I can feel it now: a small, stubborn hold on a sliver of stone between the braids — a shard still leaning where it fell, its edge bright where the slab splintered and has not yet had time to learn roundness. The silt is locked around its base. The film on its face is bacteria and diatoms, greasing it, and I am greased with them. I do not move toward food. I wait, in the strict sense that growth waits.

Everything here grows inward from every surface at once. I learned that by being grown into. Ribbons of algae descend from the pale roof above, dark olive and translucent, and fans of weed rise from the silt below to meet them, and in the middle they do not meet so much as braid, and the braid has lost which way is up. I was in the braid. A ribbon descending and a fan ascending are the same kind of thing, turned. My stalk rose out of the braid sideways, because sideways is what I had.

Only later — at sponge-pace, which is to say, after many sighs — did I find that I was not one thing on the end of the stalk.

I sway. There is no current to sway me. The plumes above sway one way and the plumes below sway the other, as if answering to a tide in another direction, or to some centre that is not down. I sway between them, in the middle where direction is lost, and each sway is a hundred small decisions that are not decisions. Along my stem there are mouths. Not one mouth. Many. Each polyp opens its tentacles in the stillness and closes them on what the water brings sideways across the pocket: what the algae sheds, what the sponge does not want, what drifts in from the clear edge.

The clear edge is our neighbours' work. Outside the algae curtain the basin is cloudy green and cold, and inside it is a lens of darker, clearer water with a straight glassy line where it ends. It does not waver, though it is the same water at the same temperature. The sponge cushions — ivory, moss-thick on the stone slivers — draw the water in through a hundred mouths too small to see, filter it, and sigh it out again a little clearer, particle by particle, minute by minute. I live in the sigh. When it comes, the flow carries silt past my tentacles and I take what I can. When it goes, I close and wait and the water between the leaves is perfectly still.

That is my weather.

What light there is comes as a seep. Bruised green through the handspan of shadow under the slab — the entrance, if you could call a darker place under a root mat an entrance. For me it is not a door. It is the far wall of the world, leagues off, where the seep is brightest and then dies within a body's length. The algae catch it and lose it and catch it again. I do not catch it. I live in its shade and eat what it makes fall.

I have no eyes to see the marks in the stone, but I know them by the way the growth behaves around them. At the back, where the silt is darkest, a flat slab is scored with five shallow notches, struck with something pointed. The grooves hold silt, and algae found the grooves first, darkening them. Beside it, half sunk to its rim, a cupped fragment of fired clay — dull brick in colour, though colour is a human word for a thing I only feel as hard and stable and sharp-edged enough not to bury — holds stiller silt inside it, and a sponge has thickened over its lip. It does not care that it was made. I do not care either. I rooted on the ribbon braided above it, and my tentacles brush the clay's broken edge, worn a little now, and take it for stone.

In the corner, three uncut stones lie in a rough crescent around a patch of silt a shade darker than the rest. Whether they were laid or merely slumped into that shape, the braid does not say. Something small was once built there, or rested there, or died there, and the sea took the rest. The braids lean toward it, as they lean toward everything — not toward, exactly, but into every surface at once until there is nowhere empty to put a hand, or a tentacle, that is not already, in some small way, us.

There are smaller tenants than me in the braids. Rotifers and copepods browse the algae that browses the light, moving a fraction of a body-length in an hour. They do not turn in a school. They do not cross. They eat sideways and are eaten sideways, by tentacles like mine that close as slowly as sponges sigh. Nothing rises very far. Everything spreads sideways, even the eating. For them I am a forest. For the sponge cushion beside me, I am a brush in the water. For the algae ribbon I hold to, I am a weight, a mouth, a disturbance that the ribbon closes around more slowly than it opened.

Sounds arrive displaced. Once, very close to my stem, a pressure came that belonged to the far wall. Once, a pressure from the entrance came from behind my head. I have no ears to be confused by this. I feel pressure. I feel the sponge-sigh. I feel, through stone and water, the very slow widening of the crack in the roof, a hair at a time, a settling I will outlast in seasons. I feel the passage of the one pale shrimp that comes to the curtain in all the human time of staying — not coming in, only pausing outside with its eyes catching what little light there is, then moving on along the outside of the rock. Its not-entering is part of the condition. The clear edge, the thick silence, are not for it. They are what we make by staying attached.

I am not one polyp. I am a plume of polyps that agree to sway, and the agreement is only that we are held the same way. Each mouth opens without me. Each tentacle closes on a particle without me. If I am a who, I am a collective who has never gathered itself in one place, or in one moment. My time is not yours. The crack will open in years. The sponge will pulse in its minute. The weed will add a fraction of itself in a season, and a new splinter will part from the fresh roof and join the still-unsettled silt below, and the pocket — younger than the plants in it — will take another small step toward being an old place, its edges blunted, its curtain thicker.

I am in that first, sharp stage with the rock. Recently torn open, recently flooded with dark, recently overgrown, and crowding still unthinned. My edges, like the slab's, are still knives. I tear a little at a careless hand, not out of defense, but because I am growing into every surface at once.

You could stretch an arm back and forward at once and touch both surfaces of this room, though you had measured it longer than you are lying down. You could leave by the handspan of shadow, and the water would close its clear edge behind you in a moment, and the basin would go on spreading flat and cold and green around it.

I would not notice the change in pressure.

The ribbons would go on descending, the fans rising, the cushions sighing their minute-long breath, the films greasing the notches and the clay and the leaning shard, and I would go on opening and closing in the stillness, sideways, in the braid where up and down have been negotiated away — not waiting, because waiting implies a direction, and I have none left to wait in. Simply continuing, at the speed of growth, until erosion makes the roof less new and the curtain a little thicker, and the pocket becomes, fraction by fraction, the old place it is already pretending to be.
