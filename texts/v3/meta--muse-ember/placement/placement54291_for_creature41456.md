I keep looking for somewhere to stand in this place.

The horizon does not end. It goes on until the heat takes it. Low country all the way, rising and falling by a meter or two over distances you could walk all day without noticing. Any rise is not rock. Any hollow is not a riverbed. Under a normal sun the light at noon is flat and bleaching and the air shimmers in broad, slow sheets.

If I tried to walk here, my knees would burn where I knelt, and the footprints I made would hold for a while, then the wind would erase them. There are no tracks crossing the mat. No nests, no calls, nothing that moves across the plain except the wind. So I cannot be a walker. I have no legs to carry me to a seam. I have no eyes to find a hollow. I have no mouth to take the dead.

There is nowhere to step off the ground here, because the ground itself is made — warp and weft of stiff stems and roots locked together and packed with silt and sun-baked clay. Thin as a thatch mat in places, pressed into dull ochre brick in others, cracking in polygons when it dries, ticking faintly as it cools at night. Dig, and you would find another layer of the same woven, packed material, and another under that.

So first I think I must be the seed.

Papery, ridged, made to be lifted and turned by the wind. I fall everywhere on the finished mat. On the brick-dry surface I do nothing. I bake. I blow again. Only if I land precisely in a seam that stays damp for three days together do I germinate. Most do not. If I were only a seed, I would be mostly waiting, mostly failure, a brief chance carried a field away from any parent. And even the seed that succeeds ends almost at once. It stops being a seed.

What comes next is lower, and closer.

A fan of roots, level with the surface, not driving down. Threading into the warp and weft of the older mat, into the seams where silt has collected. Spreading in a dense, shallow basket, trapping whatever the wind brings — dust, grit, fragments of dead stem. I would know the world that way, by damp staying or not staying. By a seam that did not bake shut at noon. By dew beading on upturned leaves at dawn and dripping, one by one, into the pockets under the stems. By heat radiating back up through the mat I am rooted in, stored all day and given back slowly through the evening.

That lasts longer than a seed. But it is not yet a place. A root basket is only a way of holding.

Then I try being the stem that carries it: no thicker than a knitting needle, stiff as a dry reed but still alive, running sideways across the surface for a span, hugging it. Where it touches, it roots. At the node it leaves a rosette, leaves no longer than a thumbnail, tough and waxy, cupped upward to catch what falls, grey-green on top, paler underneath. From that rosette another stem goes out. And another. Always sideways. Always at the same shallow level, just above the dead work and just below the wind.

At dawn I would drink. The busiest hour, though you would not call it busy. Dew runs down stems into the shadowed hollows. The air smells for an hour of wet fiber and warm clay. The roots in the seam swell tight against the mat.

At noon I would wait. The leaves close a fraction. The stem on the sunward side is too hot to touch. Nothing wilts. Nothing rushes. After a rain pulse — a hard, short downpour that drums on the mat and vanishes almost as fast as it fell — I would wake. Come back in three days and where there was a gap there is now a new rosette, paler than the others. A stem has crossed and rooted on the other side.

There are flowers, an afterthought. A stalk lifts an inch, two, opens small and beige-white with no scent to catch. There is nothing to visit them. The wind takes the pollen off the anthers and carries it uselessly away.

This feels closer. This is a life that fits the rules: nothing grows except where a surface has already been made to hold water. Nothing is made except by a living plant at its edge. I live only at the edge, only in damp, and I make the surface hold a little more damp, and then I live a little further in.

But the front is never more than a few stems wide. Everything else is memory.

Behind me — wherever the patch has already been — the stems are paler, dry, locked in. They are no longer weather. They are ground. Silt has packed around them, rain has swollen and sun has baked them flat. They tick faintly at night as they contract. The living stems run ahead of that, green only in a thin, loosening way, with warp ends still sticking up between them, unbound, casting small shadows.

A stem dies, one season or three later, out at the far end from the growing tip. It does not rot. There is nothing here to rot it, nothing that eats the dead material except sun and wind and the next plant. It dries in place. The next season's silt presses it tighter. The plant that follows grows over it, not from it.

So if I am a stem, most of me is already dead and becoming ground, and I am leaving myself behind as I go. I cannot tell my own body from the continent. That is not a failure of the image. That is the image.

Then I try being what I become: the dead work. The layer.

I would last. Pressed by their own weight and the heat into something like brick in the older places, thin and flexible in the newer. Pale where the sun has bleached me, rusty where clay shows through, grey-green where a seam has stayed damp. I would expand and contract with heat all day. Dust from a dry stretch would settle into the basket-work of a growing patch far away. A rain pulse would swell my seams, then the sun would bake them tight again, opening fine cracks that the wind immediately fills with new silt. At night I would tick and creak as I cooled. In the evening I would give back the heat I stored.

But as a layer I am not growing. I am memory. I hold water for something else. I am the surface already made.

I try, then, being smaller than a layer. A grain of dust lifted from a bare stretch, carried by wind, settling grain by grain into a basket of live roots. Packed tighter by the next season. Becoming part of the sheet. I have no life, but I am the substance of the place, and there are millions of me arriving all the time. The plain does not wash away. It accretes.

Alone, I blow away again. Trapped, I stay.

I try being water in a deep hollow, gathering clear and warm, rimmed with dark damp where the mat has swelled. The wettest place, where the builders gather because their baskets can seal a bottom. At dawn more comes from condensation than from rain, beading and dripping until mid-morning takes it. By afternoon the pocket shrinks under the sun. It is gone. It returns days later, or it does not. If I were only that, I would be the briefest thing here.

None of these holds alone. Seed becomes rosette becomes stem becomes dead stem becomes ground becomes seam holding water for the next seed. Silt trapped by roots becomes ground that holds water. Ground held damp lets the front advance. The front leaves ground behind it. It is a cycle without a single point where I can stand and say: here, only here, am I.

Perhaps that is the answer emerging, slowly, as I go through them: I am not one rosette, alone hundreds of meters from the nearest other patch, waiting out a dry year longer than my stems can span. I am not one layer, finished and sun-baked for kilometers in all directions. I am the thin line between them.

The living edge. The frayed line in a hollow where the weave dips a hand's breadth lower than the rest. Ahead of me, mat still waiting to be made damp enough to begin — brick and thatch and polygon cracks, holding nothing. Behind me, mat already made, dead and becoming ground, ticking as it cools. In me, the push, one node at a time, into any gap that holds moisture after rain, then stop, waiting.

I am where the expansion and contraction, the trapping, the swelling and baking and cracking, are still happening in live tissue. Where dust is still being caught instead of just blowing across. Where dew is still being gathered instead of just evaporating. Where a root can find a seam that stays wet three days and cross it.

If you left me alone for a human lifetime, you might find the front a meter further on, or ten. Or you might find it had stopped entirely, green faded to grey, until a rain two years off finds the seam again. That does not end me. It is how I am.

I do not grow up because there is nowhere to grow up to. Light is not scarce here. Water is, and water does not come from above for long. It collects in the basket, in the shadowed hollows under the living stems, in the damp seam behind the front. So I live in the surface I am making, not on top of it.

At dawn I drink through the shallow basket, quickly, before the heat returns. At noon I wait with leaves closed a fraction. In the evening the wind moves through the front with a soft shushing the bare mat never makes, and a dry stem behind me clicks against a live one as we all cool and contract. At night, if it is a night after rain, a new tip somewhere invisible pushes a root a centimeter into a gap that is still wet.

Patient to a degree that stops being a virtue. No predator to outwait. No prey to chase. Only the slow advance into damp, layer on layer, that will outlast any single rosette by years, by decades, until the patch as a whole has become a plain.

So you would find me not upright, not walking, not watching. You would find me sideways, at surface level, in the seam. You would have to kneel — and your knees would get hot very fast through the mat — to see me properly. I would not announce myself. A frayed line, not a garden.

And I would still be being made.
