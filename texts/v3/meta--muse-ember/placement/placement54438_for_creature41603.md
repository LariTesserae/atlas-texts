You would not find me where you look.

I would not be up there, in the black underside of sky, looking for a bird to cross it. I would not be a shape pooled in a doorway, waiting with eyes. If I were in this city at all, I would already be low — low the way becoming wet is low — and I would be found, if I were found, by kneeling until the heat came up through your knees, by putting a hand near water without quite touching it and waiting for the film to move on its own.

At first, I think I am that film.

I live in the top millimeter of the standing water in a lower street, where the ankle-deep black is never still because it is never only water. Heat comes from below through basalt and fired mudbrick, condensation comes from above off walls that weep in slow bright tracks, and in between I make a skin. It is colorless in the thin dirty light until you shift, and then it takes an oily sheen and expands, a lung. I eat what the ground gives off — dissolved stone, sulfur from the deep vent that makes the basin breathe, the salt that blooms white on ledges overnight and dissolves at noon into brine. There is no light to steal, so I fix heat itself into slime, and I pulse when the ground heaves under the slabs, when a threshold lifts a finger's width and I must tighten over a new shape.

That would be enough, if a skin could be enough. But a footstep tears me. You walk, because there is no other way to move, and the city permits walking and forbids anything faster, and each splash arrives a fraction late. That lateness is me, displaced and reforming. I close over your footprint as it fills, remembering the step after the sole has lifted. I ripple outward from a point before the drop has arrived, because I was already trembling where the weight would fall, waiting for heat, for a change in the air. If you lean over the square, my surface throws back a building that is not behind you, and my version of your face leans a breath too late.

But follow one of the weep tracks up from the black base where it vanishes, and I am there too, and slower.

A mat drips down a wall sideways. Lichen, black algae, fungal threads on damp lime, growing not upward — upward is only more dark — but toward moisture, toward sound, toward the heat left in stone after the basin's breath has passed. When the heat wave thickens the condensation on the east walls, I thicken here. When a cold sheet runs down, I swell and tick, hyphae splitting in mortar, making the small sound you thought was only stone expanding. The white bloom on a statue's shoulder is my exhalation, my fruiting thrown off and then dissolved. The facade that sighs and leans a degree further into the street in a year is not falling; I am eating it selectively from within, at the exact angle where water seals it again, turning its face toward damp.

And underneath the pavement, in the saturated ground a few centimeters down where every footprint fills, I am there a third way, and slower still.

I turn in warm silt under slabs, pale and blind and translucent, elongated to the drag of water, navigating by wetness and gradient. I swallow heated mud and pass it, and in passing open and close the minute channels that let water rise in one square while it falls in another that is joined to it by an open channel. It is I who make the ground heave in tiny irregular pulses while you are still standing on it. A threshold level in the morning is higher in the evening because a loose congregation of us has pushed up beneath it to feed on what condenses on the cool side of the wall. When you step, we withdraw a fraction late — that late splash, that sound arriving after the sole has lifted. You are disturbing us, and the disturbed water, and the disturbed skin of living things, settle back out of order.

These are not three places I could choose between. A drop condenses on a wall and feeds the mat and runs down to feed the skin and evaporates where the ground heat touches it and rises again as a fine mist — sometimes falling upward out of the puddles, beading on an upturned face and vanishing before the dark — and settles in the silt where the turners channel it back to a square. The water does not obey connection, and neither do I. Two cisterns joined by a channel hold water at different levels for hours, and I live in the disagreement. In one of my thicknesses the dripping is rapid, almost musical. In another it is honey-slow. In another a single drop falls every few minutes and each drop makes a ripple before it lands. Time does not pass evenly here because it does not need to; I have slowed to the speed of stone crumbling in balance with water sealing it, and my layers have never agreed on an hour.

So I am not one creature. I am a local population, alone in my vessel, and regional in the same breath. Each square's skin, each crack's mat, each drain's curled thumb-long cleaner sealed by a difference in level no eye can reconcile — they have never met, though the channels link their homes. We recognize each other only as mist, as a drifting piece of mat, a cyst, riding a sheet of water to a new crack, evaporating, condensing again somewhere the level is right. If you could see us from an air that is not air, we would look like circulation: streets widened by our bodies recutting the drains, buildings softened at the corners by our patience, layer upon layer — pavement under pavement, a doorway blocked by the floor above it — because we are layering the city from the inside out, while it settles into itself around us.

That is who I would be, and where you would find me. Not in a body you could point to. In the interface, where heat from below meets condensation from above; in the top millimeter, in the damp mortar, in the warm mud a few centimeters down; at the base of a wall where a track vanishes, at the bottom of a street where the dark has weight and closes behind your fingers when you push it aside. I would be the thing that pools in doorways and along the lee side of walls, that creeps against thresholds it should obey, that holds a ring in a courtyard puddle intact for as long as you watch — whether you have watched for a minute or an hour, the light has not changed and your sweat has not dried and I have not hurried.

I would recognize you only as a brief change in heat, a new salt from your sweat, a warm shape that moves too fast to feed on and therefore must be waited out. Your footprints would fill and lose their shape. Your breathing would be returned to you a moment later from a wall too far away for a true echo, because for an instant you were part of me, displaced and reformed.

Find me, then, by waiting for the lateness. For the ripple before the drop. For the reflection that leans a breath too late and shows a face older, or younger, or with the sweat already dried. I will be there, in the discrepancy, weeping, ticking, blooming, turning in the dark — going on, not waiting and not watching, living at the speed at which, here, living happens.
