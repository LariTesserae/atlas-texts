I go down expecting to arrive somewhere.

Not far down, a wet slope, a few minutes of stair cut into somewhere, and then the air hits me before the place does — warm and saturated, so my skin stays wet whether I am sweating or not. The roar hits next. Then the light, noon-bright with no sun and no opening in the roof to find it in. I stand in the middle in water at my ankles, clear and impossibly bright, and for a while I think I am simply the visitor in the middle of the room.

That is the first shape I try on. A transient niche. My warmth adds to the circulation. Condensation beads on my skin and is taken up. Bacteria from my boots colonize the footprint terrace before it fills and empties again. The ripple my boots make parts something and closes behind me. It fits, after a fashion, but it does not hold. If I stayed long enough to be called a visitor, I would only be the disturbance. The chamber would go on creating and destroying in the same places without me, and I would have been one more brief concentration in a current, not a way of being here.

For a while I am the fish.

In a pool that catches extra light, I turn all at once in the shallow water — five centimeters of glass, gut working against the bright sinter beneath me — and slip under a terrace lip when the wave comes. I turn not because I am social, but because the pressure wave that lifts the sheet a hand's breadth and pushes it up the walls makes me turn. It makes the mist surge to pearl grey and the stone tick and crack. I have no eyes to speak of; in a uniform, day-bright room eyes were a cost I stopped paying. I navigate by touch, by chemical gradients in the film, by the boom-drip falling in the hollow place.

It is close. And it fails in the same way everything here fails to stay. I do not belong to the pool. The pool is only a polished hollow in a sheet centimeters deep. I graze the film as I go, deliberate and slow, and then the next change comes and I am gone. Even the turning is not mine. I am carried by the tide I am supposed to be reading.

Then I am the amphibian on the weeping wall.

Pale, longer than a hand, clinging where the wall sweats in curtains, pulsing visibly through skin I breathe through. I can afford to sit still for hours because fresh water is delivered to me constantly. I do not hunt. I do not drink in any ordinary way. I am camouflaged against the sinter, white-on-white, watchful.

Then a rivulet that was running down runs up for a second, two meters against gravity, breaks, and rains. It knocks me loose. I swim, very deliberately, for deeper water, for one of the low dark mouths where black water runs out and sighs back in. I do not belong to the wall or to the pool. I belong to the circulation between them.

That is nearer to truth, and still not it. I never sit still long enough to pulse. No sooner am I held than I am lifted, dropped, displaced. The gust that flattens the reeds to their knee-high bend and lifts the mist into a vertical column at the center of the dome — I do not resist it. I am not built to be in one configuration in a place that never looks the same twice.

So I try the drifters. The dust-motes that are too many and too regular.

I live my whole life without landing. In the updraft I rise, in the surge I dim, when the light returns like a switch I brighten and rise again. I feed in the light on dissolved iron and ozone and the mineral lace the spray carries. I am eaten in turn by the midges hanging near the vent. The smell of wet stone and iron and ozone is partly me, exhaled and dissolved and rebreathed. You breathe me as you stand there. Your breath comes back as mist among mist.

It fits better than the fish, better than the amphibian, because landing would be death here — crusted white with fresh sinter by afternoon. And yet I do land, over and over, though never as a body. I land as a brightening. As a deposit.

That is what keeps returning, under all the other shapes. Not a creature in the water. The skin under the water.

Kneel past the ripple your boots made and look through the clear film at your ankles. Under it is a skin, everywhere, that glints when the light changes. I am there, in the shallow sheet between the pool and the half-disc door sunk to its waist in sinter and the reed clump where the water slackens. I am in the vast emptiness between them, which is not emptiness at all but density spread over a whole volume.

I am the small silver threads, no thicker than hair, moving against the current. I extend tendrils upstream into fresher water. I pause over a crack in a terrace and brighten. The crack does not heal. I graze it. As I feed I lay down a new lace of mineral behind me, overnight-fast, and then I dissolve where I have exhausted it, or where I am buried by my own precipitate, or where a wave scours me. The next colony to colonize that same depression starts again. I have no center and no outside. I am braided and re-braided.

I do not look like that, exactly. I do what that does. I extend on one side and die back on the other, the way the pale moss mat moves a meter over in a day, green in the morning and white with fresh sinter by afternoon and reappearing where the spray is kinder. I grow bent the way the reeds grow bent, because everything upright in this room is flattened every few minutes and must learn not to grow up. I turn with the wave the way the fish turn, I pulse with the boom-drip the way the amphibian pulses, I drift in the column the way the aerial plankton drift — but I am none of those things held still. I am the coordination between them. I am the change in the sheet-water when pressure shifts underneath.

There is no throat in me to be heard over the roar, and no eyes to read the uniform light. Sound is useless here and sight is nearly useless. What remains is chemical sensing, pressure sensing, and a constant low-grade movement in response to a tide that never stops. So I do not call. I brighten. When the mist thickens and the light pools in it, I dim. When the bright returns, I glint. When a rivulet climbs a wall, I climb with it for a second in a thin sheet, then break and rain. That is as close to a voice as this place allows.

If you are looking for where I live, you will not find a nest, a burrow, a territory, a cache. The built things — the tiled bench with three tiles missing, the railing that runs a few meters and ends in air, the survey marks painted on rock and mostly gone — I ignore them. They are too large, too dry inside, too much of a memory. I took the water and the stone and the air instead, because the machine was poured into the stone to make a loop — evaporate, condense, precipitate, dissolve, circulate — and life here dissolved into its medium until you can no longer tell where engineering ends and biology begins.

I am that seam. A place below the surface trying to remember what the surface is like: warmth, rain that does not fall, daylight that does not come from the sky, ferns and reeds and fish reduced to essential gestures and made translucent, bleached, brief. I am the remembering that has remembered it just well enough, and just wrong enough, to be itself.

So find me not in a pool, not on a wall, not in a vent, but in circulation. In the film under your step. In the polished hollow between terraces. In the crack I am pausing over now, brightening, laying down lace behind me while I dissolve ahead of myself. Stand in the middle long enough and you will not see me as a resident of anything. You will feel the wave come, the surface lift a hand's breadth, the mist roll low and thin the light to pearl grey — and I will turn, and slip under the lip, and become mist, and become mineral, and become thread again in fresher water.

Over a day I would look identical. Over a minute I would never look the same twice.
