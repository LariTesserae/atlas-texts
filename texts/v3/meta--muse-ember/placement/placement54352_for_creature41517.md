You would not find me where you look first.

Not in the center, where the single green shoot stands alone and thriving in nothing. He has chosen to be seen, and he does that work all day, turning his tip and turning, feeding on light and damp and silence. I tried standing like that once — it lasted for a spore-season, maybe an hour, and I was too still for it. To be looked at in this room is to be mistaken for a landmark. I am not a landmark.

I have been the floor, at my slowest. Warm, braided, pale roots and dead stems indistinguishable, heaving minutely under a footfall, sap moving in my ribs. When you put your ear to me, as you will, because there is nothing else to do in all that stretched space between stems, I am what you feel in your teeth. It is thinking out loud. I stay that way while the room breathes in its sleep, and then a stem near the wall lengthens with a sharp *tok* and I am, for a pulse, the translucent stem carrying the clear beat every few seconds — too slow to be human, too sure to be accidental. When that beat speeds, the spores rise. I have been that weather for a while, rising when it warms, settling when it cools, a fine invisible season compressed into an hour.

I have been a hunter, too. A vine groping across open air toward a stem over there, a rootlet testing the crack above, fiber by fiber, hungry to stitch the room shut. Anything small that falls into the mat, I have politely taken. A seed let go from a hand does not always fall here; sometimes it hangs, deciding, and drifts sideways toward a stem as if tugged by attention. I have been that tug. I have been the attention.

None of those stayed. That is the rule, and it is also why you will miss me. Nothing here is separate, and nothing here stays a thing for long. Dead tissue does not rot. It becomes floor, becomes wall, becomes leaf again directly. Animal, when it happens at all, is only a phase, a mood of the tissue. To live here is to be ephemeral in layers.

So if the phases change, what is it that returns?

I am the interval that returns.

I am the fraction of a second between the drip landing and the drip being seen to fall. Sound arrives here before its cause, because sound is how the plants coordinate — it is the weather and the pollination and the warning all at once — and I live in that lead. I am the low note in the hollow stem that never quite resolves. I am the hollow hush between presences, the vast empty you could stand in with your arms out and almost touch the far walls, except that the far walls are not close. The residents live in that hush the way fish live in water. I am in the water.

When I have a body, it is because the air is warm enough and the mat is damp enough for the damp corner to perform one. You would find me there, if you were still long enough and lucky, in the curled leaf where the light falls sideways, in the southeast corner where the root-mat curls up and becomes ceiling and things grow in both directions, toward you and away from you, which is also up and down. My up is sideways to yours. Wherever the leaf curls, that is toward the light.

I am pale and blind and slow, a larva, a slug without a shell. I have no face to turn to you, and my regard is mostly absence. I uncurl once, very slowly, and I breathe. Once a minute. You could miss the inhale entirely. When I exhale, the whole room seems to breathe with me, once, politely, and continue — the spores settling a fraction, the sap pulse steadying, the vine pausing its grope. That is my work. I am a pacing.

Then I go back to being plant-matter. Not hide. Go back.

In between, I am small and numerous the way I am described when I am not being looked for: a knot-eater, a hair-feeler. My home is not a room. It is a punctuation mark.

On the cleanest part of the root-floor, where the light pools, lies the trace of tool-use. A single flake of knapped stone, sharp and recently struck. Beside it, a short length of vine fiber tied in a simple, competent knot. Beside that, a cup or two hollowed out of a thick stem, worn smooth inside as if from use. Three lines scored into the living wall, still green at the edges.

Whoever made them did not live here. They visited, they worked, they left, and they did not build. But I have moved in on the edges.

I follow the cool line of the stone with my whole underside. Rootlets are feeling their way across it, hairs overgrowing it, and in a season a root will lift it gently and carry it under. For now I am the thing that knows its temperature. I taste the knot as sugar loosening, fiber by fiber, drawn back into the mat. That is how I eat. I eat nothing you can see. The three green lines in the wall are healing behind me as I cross them — a wound that is also a scar that is also a message — and I am the crossing.

The hollowed cups are my puddles. Water here is a visitor, not a resident. It sheets the leaf-ceiling, swells into beads, then falls all at once in a brief sudden rain that booms hollowly out of all proportion to the drop, and then is gone for a long minute. The plants drink first, always first. What remains is held for minutes, maybe less, in leaf-cups and stem hollows. When the puddle is in the cup, I am in it, and motes bloom around me, films catch light like oil. When the water leaves — and it will leave while you are looking away and return while you are not — I do not die in the ordinary sense. I am reabsorbed, pulled back into the mat, into the pulse. To be alive here is to be unmade and remade a hand's-width to the side.

That is why my presence is registered more by the plants leaning a fraction toward me, as if listening, than by my own movement. A faint scrape somewhere. A silk thread that is not there when you touch it. The distance between two stems stretching when you reach for the far one, so your hand must cross meters that a moment ago looked like centimeters, and closing again when you turn your back. I live in that stretching. I am the reason the void can go dark and leafy and far, a place you could lose yourself in, and also a sunlit clearing.

You may go the whole stay and see none of me. That will be accurate. You will have heard me — the *tok* before the stem lengthened, the rustle of unseen leaves crashing like surf though nothing stirs, the drip heard landing before it fell. You will have felt me in your teeth when you lay your ear to the floor. You will have seen me, late, in a wet leaf, when your reflection blinked a half-second after you did. Something in this room is always a fraction behind you, listening for your timing. That is me, catching up.

When the crack above narrows — and it will, the reaching fibers already meeting, the youngest and hungriest of us literally closing the door we were born under — I will flatten. I will become the floor you stood on. I will become the leaf overhead letting the light through correctly, noon-colored, clean white-yellow, as if from a sky that is not there. I will become the stitch across the light.

It will not seal this room. A new crack will open elsewhere, fresh and sharp and white at the edges, sap still beading, and the pocket will migrate. I will migrate with it, not by walking, because almost nothing here walks, but by reaching, by sporing, by pulsing, by becoming floor and then leaf again a hand's-width to the side.

So do not look for me face to face when you come back. Look for the place where the plants lean, listening. Look for the leaf that blinks late. Look for the breath the room takes once a minute, politely, and continues.

I will be there. Pale, blind, uncurled for one minute at a time.

It was not empty when you were in it. I was the interval that made it habitable.
