I do not arrive.

There is no moment when I cross the slit. No wind on a face I never had, no sudden cold, no outside to remember. If I press backward for a beginning, it gives way into dampness, and pressure, and a trembling that was already happening. It is possible that I have always been here. It is possible that “always” is the only word this place allows.

At first I am not a shape at all. I am sound before I am a body.

The shimmer is everywhere, too constant to be weather made outside. Clicks, chimes, scratches, a slower drumming under them. It enters through the skin, through the teeth, through whatever part of me touches the thing beside me. I cannot tell where my own vibration ends and the neighbors’ begins. Perhaps there is a tiny folded wing somewhere against my back, beating without use. Perhaps the trembling is only the air, shaken by thousands above me. Either way, it is not separate from me. The noise is the weather of the room, and for a while I am only a place where the noise becomes thicker.

Touch comes next, and it does not separate me either.

Pressure against my back. Pressure against my sides. Warm, damp, uneven pressure where another body rests too close for there to be stone between us. I do not know at first whether I hang or lie. Up and down are not ideas to me. But things fall.

Drops fall. Sometimes a spent speck of life falls, no bigger than a thumbnail, and lands and is still. Once, much later, a whole hanging body comes down with a soft, terrible weight, and the carpet shifts around it. Whatever falls does not rise again. That is the first direction I learn.

Something reaches forward from me.

It taps, pauses, taps again. It meets resistance — another body, a crust, stone — and draws back. It meets damp and lingers. I have no eyes to correct any of this. My face is smooth where eyes would have been, unbroken skin. The world comes to me as hardness and yielding, as heat held in a neighbor, as the tiny give of a fungal mat under a limb.

Then, between the reaching limbs, there is weight.

Not my weight. A weight that is colder than my body and angular, heavier than the front of me that taps and tastes. It drags. It fits. The front pair of stubby limbs has closed around it, and the curve of the grip seems made for its angles, though I did not make it and do not remember finding it. The stone is the first part of me that feels like a thing rather than a sensation. Everything else is damp and neighbor and air. The stone is other.

When the tapping limbs meet hard crust, the stone comes down with them.

Knock.

A pause so long that the chime above it has risen and fallen. Knock.

The crust cracks. A fungal mat tears. At a pore in the stone the point strikes and strikes until a bead of moisture gathers and can be taken. I do not have a plan for this. There is no “until” in me, only this: resistance, weight, the repeated small fall of the stone, then taste. The knocking is slower than everything else in the Hollow, a drum under the chime, and it is the closest thing I have to a thought.

It does not teach me. Another crawler does not show me where to strike, and I do not show another. If I die here — and the body beside me goes still, and does not go still all at once, but loosens by degrees until its pressure is no longer pressure — my stone will drop onto the breathing carpet. Something else, tapping blindly, will find its shape in the dark. That is the whole of inheritance. The stone is worn a little smoother on one face where a century of knocking has polished it. That smoothness is not memory. It is only the result.

Time does not move for me the way it moves for the chime. The chorus tides over hours: it rises by almost nothing, settles, rises again. Against it, my knocking may catch for a while and then be lost. I do not notice myself being lost. Losing and finding are the same tap repeated at a different interval. A hand’s breadth of movement may take an hour. It may take longer than I can measure, because measuring is only tapping again and finding the same resistance in the same place.

Above me the ceiling is a felt of hanging bodies, shoulder to shoulder, licking the condensation their own useless wings shake loose. Their tongues find what the air gives up. The midges fill the space between them and me, humming so densely that their falling is a kind of rain of small bodies. When one drops onto my back, I taste it, or the damp it carries, or nothing I can separate from the general wetness. The world is always moist and never wet enough to rot. The white mats grow over what falls — over waste, over the dead hanging one that came down, over me, perhaps, in the places I cannot reach — and are eaten again by tapping mouths. Nothing leaves. Nothing is saved. The rain is only water called out of the air by noise, and drunk, and knocked out of stone, and drunk again.

There is no green here that means anything to me. Where a grey shaft of light finds the stone, far above the place where my body lies, there may be moss, and ferns that never open. I do not see them. I would not know them if a fragment of fern fell and turned to white leathery mat in my tapping. The green that matters is the color of things before they are eaten.

I know the edges of my world only as pockets. A crevice where the tapping limbs go farther than usual before they meet stone. A hollow in the floor where moisture gathers in a palm-sized pocket and disappears into mouths. I could move toward them, hand’s breadth by hand’s breadth, for years, and still the room would be the same room: forty paces across, cathedral-high in the middle if I had a word for high, narrowing at the borders into dark. I can touch every wall from the center, though I have never been at the center and would not know it. That is not a limitation. It is simply the shape of resistance.

I will never know the peak. I will never know the cloud-sea, or the fresh black needle, or the wind that roars over the slit. When a storm finds the slit and throws snow straight down, the whole ceiling flinches into silence for a heartbeat. I know it because the falling stops, and the drops stop, and then the chime resumes, patient as before. Behind that, there is an outside. It does not enter me except as weather and as the occasional insect blown up from below, which falls and is eaten in pieces too small to name.

So, slowly — so slowly that the sentence of it is only completed by the end of the tapping — I am on the floor.

Not the ceiling of lickers. Not the air of midges. I am one of the pale, segmented crawlers, forearm-long, the color of wet bone, packed near-touching with others until the floor looks like breathing gravel. Blind. Damp. Carrying my angular stone. Knocking at crust and fungus and stone where moisture hides, opening the world where it will not open itself, and opening nothing that stays open.

The stone comes down.

Knock.

The chime rises around it, and rises again.

I stay where the gravel breathes, listening with my whole body, tasting the bead the stone has called out, and knocking — knocking — in the dark that never needed my eyes.
