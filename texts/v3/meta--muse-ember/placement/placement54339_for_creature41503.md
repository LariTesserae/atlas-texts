There was no arrival. Only the slow cessation of sinking, until I could no longer tell my stillness from the stillness of the water around me.

The water does not hold me the way air would hold a fall. It holds me the way a warmth holds, on all sides at once, never cooling. I breathe it through membranes that have never known anything else. It tastes of iron and salt, and underneath that, faintly sweetly rotten, like something living far below has exhaled and not yet stopped.

I have no eyes to open. The skin is sealed smooth where eyes would be; my ancestors traded them long before any of them thought to miss the light. I know myself first by resistance. I push, something pushes back, soft and returning. I shift my weight, and the silt sighs under me and settles. That is the whole of my body, at first: a place where pressure behaves differently.

I tried, in what I suppose was the beginning, to go somewhere.

I leaned into one of the broad sheets of current that slide sideways across the plain, no thicker than a forearm, and let it carry me. A cloud of shrimp passed — I felt their legs as a breeze against my cheek, a thickening of the water over a warm terrace. A school turned overhead, if overhead means anything down here, and displaced a whole sheet of water that rolled over me. An eel-filterer, wrist-thick and corduroy-skinned, drifted through where I was, mouth open, straining the current as it went.

I did not move with any of them. The current went around me.

Then I tried the lanes. I found a mucus trail under my palm, smooth and used, and followed it in the manner of the mollusc trains, hundreds of soft bodies in single file, each reading the chemical message of the one ahead: *food went this way, this way is safe, this way is warm.* When the train met me it parted, flowed around me, and closed behind me without a break. Not because I had stepped aside. Because I was in the way the way ground is in the way.

A crab walked over me, plate-sized and careful, testing the shell beneath its forelegs before committing its weight. It tapped — not a sound, really, but a vibration through the surface we shared, a hammering code I felt in my whole spread. The tapping ran *under* me. It was not addressed to me. It was addressed to the wall.

That was when I understood the brushing. It had never been an encounter. It was a contact with a surface. Something had passed a limb over me, tested for weakness, found none, and settled to feed. I was the thing being tested.

Inside, we breathe as a crowd.

I am one of the soft ones, hand-sized and rounder than anything else in this low country, sealed and eyeless, my mouth underneath, never quite still. There are dozens of us pressed flank to flank in a hollowed chamber, sharing the same warm water, so that when one of us filters, the ripple runs to the next and the next, and the whole dome appears to inhale as a single animal. I know my own extent only by my neighbors: pressure on the left, pressure on the right, water above, the slow give of the wall below. Take away the neighbors and I would not know where I ended.

Our house is not a house we built. It is the sum of us, cemented and keyed together in the arcade district where the current runs strongest, interlocked shells and sponge-pillars and the curved carapaces of ancestors in the south, each shield roofed by its own living descendants. Water rushes through us and comes out cleaner. We are the cleaning. We do not move because moving would break the architecture we have become.

At intervals — I do not know what a day is here, only the slower breathing of the vents — we loosen one edge and go out to graze the outside wall. The wall is slick with the film we cultivate there: bacteria, algae-slicks, the fallen snow of things dead far above in the blackness. My mouth rasps at it, a tiny motion among a million tiny motions, a constant shiver in the water. Where I have passed, the surface is clean and ready. Then I settle again. My own body is both the sieve and the thing sieved.

I was not always settled. I think I rode a vent-pulse upward once, a larva in the current, tasting snowfall for years before I drifted down onto a warm, crowded mound and put down my first small cement. Since then I have simply been here, slightly larger each year, my children putting down beside me, beside them their children, the arcade thickening by one body's width at a time.

Nothing stays hard here unless it is constantly lived in. If I died now, my shell would not be removed. The small rasping mouths would find the slump of me within a generation and take me smooth again, wall turned back into food, food turned back into body. My house will be soft. My house will be round. In a few hundred years a stranger will lie down on me and think he is sleeping on a hill.

Far off, through the muffled water, comes the thump. Regular as a sleeping heart. It reaches me as a slowing in our crowd-ripple, all of us breathing a degree less, a degree more, as if the plain itself had sighed. The plain is made of us, layer upon layer, the bottom generations turned to geology. What looks like ground is the topmost generation of a city that has been adding itself to itself until the built and the natural can no longer be told apart.

I do not leave. Not because there is nowhere to leave to — though there is only more water, more dark, more plain — but because leaving would be an unmaking. I stay attached. I filter. I rasp. I sway, constantly and slightly, with the pulse, the way the living bridges sway over the cracks that are too hot to linger on.

I am low. I am soft. I am worn to the same angle as everything else here, and the wearing is not a loss. It is how I know I am part of the place, and how the place knows me.

Where there are bodies, there is architecture. I am a body. Therefore, in my small round way, I am a place where others can stay.
