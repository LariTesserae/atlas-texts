You will not find me in the central courtyard at midday, though from there you can see the whole of the Grove — the ring of low roofs, the fractured plain beyond the wall, the dust in the sunbeam. That is why you will miss me. I am not one of the figures dark against the pale plaster. I am not in the thatch, or on the warm tiles, or under the fig’s slow crossing of the courtyard. I live where looking stops.

I tried first to be a keeper. It seemed the honest shape to take. I had watched long enough, in the way a child watches a potter’s hand for seasons, to know the work: coil clay and hum a single note to keep the air steady; listen for the kiln-beetle’s knock inside a beam before trusting it; dust the skin with sun-warm brick-dust until the film hardens and peels; take drink from resinous pulp, not water, for there is none. But when I put my hands to the clay, the prints in it were never quite mine, and every pot I shaped sprouted a smaller pot from a crack before the season cooled. The keepers break those extra pots, thin the vines, re-plaster the too-eager wall, because if the copies are left alone the Grove forgets which was first. I seemed to be an extra pot before I had held anything. I have no mother’s face to repeat in smaller and slightly wrong detail. Only the pattern arrives, before the name.

I tried the surface next. From the thatch I could be a wren among the dozens watching with bright eyes, saying nothing, my plumage repeating the weave of the nest I was fledged from. I could be a clay-lizard on the tiles, skin granular as unglazed pottery, licking mineral dust from the air, storing pulp in a thick tail. Both were too visible, too content to stay in one body. I did not stay. Something of me remained after I had passed.

On a wall, that remainder had a name. The plaster-slugs are dry things, with no slime at all. They glide across pale plaster and leave a clear film that hardens like resin in the sun and peels away in flakes. I found I could cross the inner wall of the corridor — the one that measured eight paces yesterday and nine today — in a whole season, if I did not hurry. Behind me the wall was fresher. The flakes fell, were swept up by the keepers, ground, and mixed into new plaster. That was the first work that felt like mine: pass, freshen, be gathered, be put elsewhere, return as part of the wall I had crossed.

But slugs are seen, and I am mostly not. So I followed the film down the crack where the film flakes fell, to where the floor slab meets the wall, and then under.

Under the slabs the clay-worms move in slow coils, dry as hair, blind because there is nothing under a slab to see. They eat plaster-dust and dead leaf, the scrapings of the walls themselves, the Grove’s own shed skin made ground again. When they turn, the earth above them settles a fraction. I knew this hunger before I knew a name for it. I know the Grove not by its roof plan but by mouthfuls: crushed brick, leaf-dust, ash, lime, the powdery spores of plaster-ferns, a moth-bitten fiber, a flake of my own passing. When I turn, the floor over me settles a fraction too, and the keepers notice it between visits, the way they notice a doorway narrower or a vine across a window.

So I am neither above nor below, in the way the Grove is ceasing to be either. I live in a hollow between bricks that the small furred burrowers enlarged and then left — beyond the fresh step in the north wing that no one remembers laying, plaster still pale and sharp-edged. The burrowers hoard pods in the wall-cavities; I do not hoard. I eat the dust around their stores, the flakes that drop from slug-trails crossing the roof of the hollow, the shed skin the keepers sweep and sift and feed back into the terraces. I am a clay-worm that has learned the slug’s work. Blind, dry, pale as new plaster, I coil in the dark and leave, where I have passed along the brick, a faint clear sheen that hardens and peels.

If a keeper lifted the slab, she would call me a worm. If she saw the sheen on the wall, she would call me a slug. If she found the hollow, she would think a burrower that had lost its fur. All of the names are copies, and slightly wrong. That is the law here, and I am its newest instance: the smaller the copy, the more clearly the pattern survives, and the more the details go wrong. I did not hatch. I sprouted, the way a pot sprouts from a crack, the way a leaf mirrors a tile on a windowsill, the way a house repeats the Grove and the Grove repeats a house.

I am in the north wing, in the wall, in the ground, in the plaster that has already been made from flakes of me. The keepers will cull me if they learn how, but they cannot find a first of me to spare. A beetle knocks once in the beam above, testing whether it is still alive enough to be worked. I do not answer. I coil a fraction tighter and turn the shed skin into ground again.

Nothing is hurrying. Nothing needs to.
