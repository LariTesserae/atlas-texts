I do not come in through the notch, not at first.

I am there before light, before pounding. I am the cold pool that settles ankle-deep in the bowl overnight, the chill you feel around your feet at dawn when everything else is shadow. The ants have already found their highway across a doorstep. The geckos are already working up the warm walls. I am under all of that, low and unseen, and when a shutter is unbarred two tiers up and a blanket is shaken, the dust from it falls through me like someone else's sleep.

Then the sun disc touches the floor and I lift. I warm first at the edges, then spiral straight up, carrying cooking smoke, dust, seeds, midges, out through the notch and away over the empty plateau. And then — because this is the bowl's daily bargain — I cool and sink back in the evening and seal the room again.

That could be a life, leaving and returning every day without ever having left. But it doesn't hold. No one here really leaves. And air, however faithful, never learns a lane.

So I settle. I settle on a leaf.

At dawn every broad waxy leaf in the bowl is wet though the dust a hand's breadth below is dry. I hang heavy and cool, free water. Children tilt leaves to their mouths. Larks do the same above them. By ten I am licked off, gone into a child's mouth, into a bird, into the thin high air again. Brief, sweet, useful, and made entirely by something else breathing out through the night.

I follow that breath backward, down the stem, into the dark.

I am the root in the pocket of black soil in the crack. I am the oldest inhabitant, older than the steps, older than the pots slumped into soil. I go down past the warm stone, past the centipede trading places with a beetle, past everything I can never see — the parrots arguing in the fig, the hawk that makes the whole bowl freeze for a minute, the market too hot to stand in at noon. Water is far below and invisible and I drink it without ever being rained on. I lift a slab a hair's width in a hundred years. I make a cool hollow where a cat will sleep. When I crack a wall, the wall is rebuilt around me.

That is closer. That is the patience of this place. But a root never hears the mortar pounding announce a door open. A root never carries anything on its shoulder.

I come up the way the vine comes up — thick as a wrist, climbing a house, climbing the central fig, climbing other vines until it crosses a lane at head height. People duck under me to get to bed. In summer they sleep on their roofs under me. I am a living threshold. And where the vine ends at its base, there is a house leaning wall to wall into another house, and that is where I wake last.

For the people wake last, in the order the blue shade lets them. I wake in it.

My house is on the lower slope, third stepped lane down from the notch, where the lane is too narrow for a loaded donkey and bends because a root cracked a wall and the wall learned to go around it. One wall is shared with a granary of sealed jars sunk into cool earth. The roof is shared with the weaver, whose loom clacks so close I hear it in my kitchen. My mortar is stone set into earth by my grandmother, out front where the lane is still misty and smelling of crushed leaf and warm stone. At dawn I pound, not only because the grain is ready but because if I do not, the rhythm is missing and the children who follow it look lost. I am up. My grain is ready. My door is open.

I am not a fig-keeper — three families have that claim and argue over the fruit and prune and keep the vines from strangling it. I am not the smith with his one piece of traded copper, dragging his anvil into the sun patch for the minutes he needs the extra heat and dragging it back. I am not one of the old in the deepest shade who do the remembering, though I sit with them in the afternoon when the cicadas are so loud conversation is raised-voice work and they save theirs.

I am the one with strong shoulders.

After pounding, I take my covered jar to the shaft. There are only three, sunk by hand-drilled stone and rope into the aquifer far below, damp and cold and far down. I wait my turn in the line of older girls and women who have carried all their lives and have arms to show for it. Hand over hand the bucket comes up, one bucket at a time, and I do not hurry. Water is not hurried. I carry it back through lanes where it would be smarter to run, step by careful step, and I ration it by a memory no one remembers inventing: so much for drinking, so much for the hammock of young plants on the terraces, none for washing, none for bathing unless someone is sick. Sweat is wiped. I breathe the plants' transpired water in the mist almost as much as I drink.

That is my trade and my place: going down and coming up with something covered, and pouring it out slowly where it can be breathed as well as drunk. The plants do it from below. The shaft does it from below. I do it on foot.

I know the bowl the way the description of it says it can be known in five minutes and in a lifetime. I know which gecko has claimed which warm stone and will not move for me pounding three feet away. I know which vine leaf holds the most dew, and I watch the children lick it off while I wipe my hands on my skirt. I know the ants will cross my doorstep to a fallen plum in the market no matter how often I sweep, and that by morning they will have declared war over it. I keep a cat that is not quite mine, sleeping in a fig-root hollow by day and patrolling the granary wall at night against the rats who are too many to be shy. The goat tethered on my roof strips bark from any root it can reach and clanks its bell against the weaver's loom until someone tells it, loudly, to be still.

Life arranges itself around the sun patch and so do I. Two hours, no more, the small hard disc crawls over the lip and lands on the market square under the fig, on the hottest stones. Everything that can be done in shade is done in shade. Everything that needs heat is done there, quickly, and then withdrawn. I drag a pot of clay into it, a tray of plums onto a roof stone, my own elbows into it to warm. Then I drag them back. The stone under bare feet is almost too hot. My feet know when to leave.

Twice a day the hawk comes over the lip, very high, and does not land. Mid-pound, mid-carry, mid-lick, I stop. The bowl goes quieter. Lizards flatten, birds freeze, goats bunch. For a full minute there is only the cooling stone cracking. Then the hawk is gone and the noise rushes back louder, as if relieved, and I start again where I left off, a little faster, as if to catch up to the sound.

In the afternoon the wind comes down the other way, bringing news from outside — a scrap of dry paper, a seed from a plant that does not grow here, the smell of salt and distance. The leaves shiver all together. I pocket the seed in my skirt without planting it. I don't know why. No one here steps outside the rim for weeks at a time, and yet we are not sealed. We breathe what comes in. We pass it on.

At dusk the day population settles screaming into the trees and the other population pours out — bats in a thin rapid stream from wall cracks to hunt the plume rising over the fig like smoke. Their guano feeds the soil. I light my oil in its clay dish and the lane goes amber, just enough to find a door by. I sleep on the roof in summer, under the vine that crosses the lane, stone warm under my cheek, giving its heat back slowly. Above me the sky is only a small circle, far above the rim, intensely blue-black and crowded with stars.

I am not the air, though I breathe it all day. I am not the dew, though I watch it made and taken. I am not the root, though my work is its work. I am the woman on the lower slope who pounds at dawn, carries at mid-morning, mends — swearing loudly so the whole lane hears — in the shade, and goes down to the shaft again when the light is failing, hand over hand, one bucket at a time.

You could walk the entire length of the bowl in five minutes. I have spent my life in the three steps between my door and the shaft, and the lane between here and the fig, and the bend where the root cracked the wall. I am still learning them.
