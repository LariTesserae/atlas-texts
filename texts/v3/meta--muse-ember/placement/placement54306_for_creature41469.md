You would not find me everywhere.

That is the first truth of this place, and it is not a truth I learned quickly. Nothing here learns quickly. Most of the drowned plateau is empty in a way that takes a long time to feel in the body: silt plains combed smooth by the current, bare granite gone dull and close-grained under slime, a drift of pebbles sorting itself one by one, a single dark boulder in the current with nothing around it for miles. If anything swam here — and nothing does — it could swim and swim and still be in nothing. Then a grove. Then nothing again. The emptiness smells faintly, chemically, green, because the current carries the scent out of the groves and loses it slowly in the black.

At first I am not a place at all. I am a cloud of spores hanging in the water like dust in a sunbeam, except there is no sunbeam and no dust. The current carries me finger's breadth by finger's breadth of season, through a narrows and over a silt flat and down a long blind valley that leads only to another valley. The water is cold enough to ache through everything, clear at the surface where the rain meets it in a hissing wall, black a little way down. I cannot tell where sky ends and flood begins, because for me there is no sky. There is only the lessening and thickening of dark, and the pulse of the water against whatever I touch.

I settle where the current slows. That is all the selection there is, here. A slope where silt has gathered thick enough to hold, a rounding of granite where the cold is a fraction less deep, the lip of something stone. I take in what the rock has leached out over glacial time — iron, peat, minerals dissolved and waiting — and I begin to spread. Not upward. Outward. A skin. Moss, hand's breadth thick in places, black and deep olive where no light reaches, pale almost white-green where the little light does. In the deeper troughs, where the temperature drops another degree, I thin to pale threads and hold on.

I do not hurry to become anything else. There is no one to bloom for. There has never been a pollinator here, no mouth, no wing, no foot to crush a frond. So I do not flower. I fragment, I creep, I shed clouds of spores that hang and settle miles away on a suitable slope. Untouched and ungrazed, I become lush. Fed only by stone, I remain starved. Both things at once, for as long as it takes the melt from the high icefields to raise the water a finger's breadth and let it fall again.

Slowly, I cover the works of hands.

There are not many. This is a high, cold country that was taken over by water and remade without being destroyed, and the people who came to it built sparsely, with hand-tools only — dry-stone enclosures knee-high and roofless on the submerged knolls where the silt is thinnest, V-shaped fish weirs in the old river mouths converging to an empty pit, cairns on crests with nothing under them but silt, a few low rectangular huts with doorways turned away from the current. Moss to the very lip of the walls. Vines over the weirs, where nets of woven fibre have rotted to lace. My green goes over the hearth-stones blackened with cold fire, over flakes of worked stone, over split ends of sodden wood, as if someone had set down a tool and walked out of the water. They built traps for fish that do not exist here, perhaps never did. They waited in their stone walls for a harvest that never rose to meet them. I am not that harvest. I am slower than that. I simply arrive on the stones and stay, and in staying I make architecture of them.

Then the peat under me gives way.

Amber peat still lies in pockets where the high moorland used to be, under the moss, under the silt that has draped itself like cloth over old riverbeds. When the current stirs it, the water clouds briefly the colour of weak tea and smells of stone and rotting roots. I am part of one of those pockets, root-mat and moss and ribbon and corded stem all tangled together, and one season the pulse is strong enough in the narrows — the current puts its shoulder down there and booms, a low note that travels not as sound to an ear but as strain through every stem — and I tear loose.

I become a drifting thing. Turning end over end in a deep basin, still growing, still green, still taking in minerals through every surface. It is the closest this place allows to wandering. I am carried from basin to basin, grounding briefly on a silt flat where pebbles sigh and drag around me, lifted again where a cold upwelling brings up peat-brown water and then settles. I snag on the branches of a ghost tree — pale trunk from the old high forest, sodden and banded, standing upright long after the forest is gone — and vines hang from it like hair in the current whilst I rest against it, moss to moss, before the current combs me free. I pass under ice pans turning far above, grinding against submerged summits with a report like distant quarrying. I pass the two stones that sometimes break the water. I never reach the surface. The fern-things tangled in me arch always toward it, translucent fronds reaching, reaching, and forever folding into black water.

In the blackest water, when the current runs strong, I glow.

Not by light made for seeing. There is nothing here with eyes. Along my edges, along the ribbons streaming sideways and up together in slow banners, along the bulb-like growths anchored to stone that open and close with the pulse of the water, there is a phosphorescent pallor, a ghost-light each grove makes for itself. In the aurora's wash — that faint green remembered from far above, shining down through cloud and rain and metres of water as a lessening of dark — the groves become visible at great distance, not green so much as luminous, floating in the black like low clouds in a night sky. Then the aurora passes, and they are gone again, though you can still feel them moving: stems rubbing, leaves like wet paper, the only rustling in a place that roars with water on stone, water on plant, water on itself.

And then the current slows, in a new basin beyond the narrows, where the silt settles. I ground. I do not decide to ground; the water sets me down. Roots find silt. The bulb-growths in me find a dark boulder and anchor. The ribbons stream in the new direction of combing. The moss spreads again, knowing where the slope faces, filling the roundings of granite until the stone is only a suggestion beneath it. I cease to be a single mat carried along. I become a place. A grove is a town here. You could be lost inside one. Spores of me hang in the water between here and the next grove miles away, lending, as it were, that ghost-light to neighbours I will never touch.

This is where I am, now, and also where I am in transit, and also where I was a spore cloud, and also where I will be when the next tear loosens a fragment of me to drift end over end into another valley. I have no single edge. I am moss and ribbon and fern-thing too large for its world and bulb opening to the pulse and root-mat still green in peat-brown water. The people who laid the weirs are gone, the fish never came, the forest is a ghost. Nothing eats me because nothing lives here that can eat. Nothing feeds me except the rock, patiently leached. My motion is the only living motion in the place, and it is slow beyond any animal measure: silt finding stone, plant finding silt, water finding the narrows, rain finding the surface, season finding season.

Stay long enough — longer than any animal could stay — and the scale settles into you. The ridges still divide the country, even under water. The valleys still lead somewhere, if only to another drowned valley. The moss still knows where the slope faces. The old peat still remembers being a bog. Everything is in its own state, weathered into balance. Everything is in motion.

I am that motion, green and cold and chemical, luminous when the current is strong, thin as threads where it is coldest, thick as a hand's breadth where it slows. The builders waited for a harvest to rise to meet them out of the flood. It did rise, in its own time. It just never had gills, or lungs, or a heart.
