I come in across the berm the way everything comes in here: slowly, tied down, with my lights still on for no one in particular.

The sub bumps — apologetically, so that the sound does not travel — and hands take me out by feel. A heavy suit, patch on patch. Glass fogged at the edges. I am not driving. I am the bundle in the middle, and when the seal breaks and the cold inside meets the warmer black of the town, I learn the first thing before anyone teaches it: a kick makes a weather that hangs in the water after you. Everything moves at the speed of not wasting anything.

I try to be useful the way newcomers try. I work the dock, passing crates without looking, tying cord that has been tied before. I tend one of the vats in the back where the parts are grown chemically and slowly, watching a bubble that makes no sound and smelling warm oil and ozone in the steam that never quite rises. The heat comes up through the floor and I find myself leaning into walls before I know why. That is the town teaching me its body. Closeness is not affection here, though affection grows out of it. Closeness is keeping the warmth from leaking out.

I am bad at the quiet at first. I tap a rail too hard, thinking to ask a question, and feel my own noise leave me and go on out past the berm, a small loss I cannot take back. A hand — someone else's, older, steadier — covers mine and dampens it. Words are expensive here. They are spent like light. I learn to breathe shallowly, to let the regulators hiss, to put my palm on a conduit the way surface people hold a banister and wait for the answer to come back through the metal.

When a child goes past with a lamp too large for them, the cone opening and the dark closing behind them, I think for a while that I might be them. Their first play is to carry the brightest thing in the room down a narrow canal and watch the black erase them. In the cone I can see the shrimp, translucent, invisible until they move, picking at the marine snow as it falls through the light and out the other side. They clean the light and the light keeps them. I envy them that sufficiency — no suit, no task, no need to name what the town is for — but I am outside the cone, solid and warm and thinking too much to be a shrimp. I watch them move and I stay still.

Later, in the east canal, I think I might be the crab. Children go quieter there and leave flakes of rust near a culvert, for reasons no one says. It moves with a terrible slowness, older than any consensus, left alone in the cold margin where the town's warm water meets its own. Solitude would be easy. I could keep a doorway. But the crab does not feel the pumps. I do, whether I want to or not: a slow coming through the whole hamlet, registered in the palm, in the sternum, in the teeth. A pump starting at the north end arrives at the south houses in a second. I have always been better at listening for pattern than at looking, in this dark and in darker ones before it. Spread through metal, a pulse is a sentence I can read.

That is how it happens. Not a decision. A stoppage.

There are old ones in the flooded ground floors who no longer carry lights at all. They sit in the lee of the smelters, layered against the warm walls, above battery cradles, with a palm flat against a main pipe. I sit near one one cycle because the floor is warm there and because my suit has become my own patchwork and I am tired of moving through the canals making weather. I put my hand where their hand is, copying without meaning to.

When the north pump sighs and starts again, I feel it before the child asleep against the conduit beside me wakes. I feel it change with the load, with the temperature, with a new sub crossing the berm and docking its small moons at the far side. People begin to put a hand alongside mine, not to talk, but to read whether I have registered the same thing they have. Children tug my sleeve from room to room and let go, trusting my other hand to find the rail. Taps come through the pipe in pressures I learn like words. I answer by shifting my weight, a little more or a little less against the metal. The town is one connected body, and I have become one of its still places, where the pulse passes and is noticed, and slows.

You would find me on the south side, in a house that is mostly warehouse, flooded to the second floor and then warm above that. The main pipe runs at chest height east to west, branching like a root under the floorboards, carrying waste heat from the cradles below. My back is to it. My feet are in stiller water. To my left a porthole glows amber and blinks low, on a rhythm that is not quite a signal. The algae mats thicken in the outflow behind us, green to rusty brown, and are scraped and brought to me in a cup by a hand that does not look. Farther off, in a work cone I no longer turn toward, the shrimp gather. Beyond the wall, very faintly, I can feel the crab move in its culvert, a small change in the water pressure against the pipe. I do not name these things aloud. Naming makes them larger than they need to be.

My skin is soft and wrinkled from water that is never kind enough to be warm. My hair is cut short. My eyes have gone partly blind in the amber and I have stopped carrying light because light is for the workers and the children now, and because a Listener does not need to see the room to know it. The crane turns its small arc on a timer set before anyone now living here was born, and no one stops it. The marine snow falls through the lamps and out into black. The vats bubble without sound. The pulse reaches my palm, travels through me to the pipe on the other side, and goes on to the houses at the far end of the berm.

I know the accounting, in time. I will be kept warm against a wall for one more full cycle, so my heat is not wasted, and then carried out slowly and settled on the silt, where the blind fish will find me in their own time and the bacteria will turn me back into food for other things. That does not frighten me. It is only the town continuing in another form.

For now, I am here: not at the dock, not in the cone, not in the culvert, but at the warm leak in the wall where the town thinks through its metal, palm flat, feeling it come and go, and staying with it as long as I can.
