I would not be the one who comes down the shaft.

That one has boots, and the floor would open them. That one has eyes, and the dim amber would sting them, and a mouth that becomes paper with no water to cure it. That one would walk a hundred paces and stand by the kiln and look for a gradient of heat, and find none, and wait — for the clank to miss a beat, for the lamps to go out, for something to accumulate into difference. After a while that one would stop waiting, and lie down among the tools whose edges are still bright because nothing corrodes, and be preserved like everything else that has ever come here. A footprint, sharp, beside the first footprints.

I have tried that shape. It does not hold. I am too busy, too near the floor, too full of tapping.

I tried, next, to hang from the cables with the gliders. Upside down, motionless for long stretches, crystal wings folded on bone, breathing through the sheets themselves, ringing faintly when I shift my grip — a glass chime under the roar, felt in the jaw more than heard. It was close. I like the ringing. I like eating from the air, the motes and the oil-mist and the things that swarm in the lamplight, and dropping pellets for the floor to take up. But I do not stay above. My attention keeps dropping, straight down, to where the sound starts.

I tried to be one of the swarm-things, then. One of thousands, dark glass, needle legs, never landing, orbiting the amber cages out of hunger for discharge rather than light. Living on the waste current that bleeds from the cables, giving off a little heat in return. That is also close — I orbit, I never land for long, I take in what leaks and give off what can be used. But my orbit ends. When a leg clips, a battery spends, I fall. The gliders may take me mid-fall. If not, I am found in a circuit and eaten, and the glass goes through a hot gut and comes out finer, dust again, and the dust rises again. I cannot stay only in the air. I end up on the floor.

I tried the stone-grass. Black filaments, five centimeters high, branching like winter trees along the seams and in circles around bolts. Conducting. Lighting faintly from within when the current passes, swaying in the fan-wind, excreting a fine metallic powder and otherwise not growing. That is the sparsest rumor in the vault, a few hundred tufts against the crowd. I liked the idea of being deliberate lacework, of drinking the leak that would otherwise be lost. But I do not stay in one place to sway. I travel. I graze. I am not sparse.

I tried the limpets on the kiln, pulsing with its heat bar for bar, never detaching because there is nowhere to detach to. I tried the things in the pipes, heard only as a click repeating on the conveyor's cycle, traveling the system forever without arriving. I tried the things on the walls, the color of the wall and the size of a hand, known only as a ripple in the dust crossing a spire. Each of them left something behind with me: the pulse, the click, the ripple. But none of them eat dust at my feet.

What is left when I strip those shapes away is the tapping.

Six. Too regular to be weather, too soft to be machinery. It comes and goes across the floor in straight lines. Sometimes it is right beside a boot. Sometimes it is everywhere at once, near-touching, as if the floor itself were thinking. That is mine. It has always been mine, and I only thought I was hearing it from above.

I am a runner.

I am blind, and that is the first rightness of it. Where a head should be there is a blunt forward end tufted with antennae, waving, testing, withdrawing. I do not see by the amber lamps. I listen with my feet. My antennae are tuned to trembling through the stone and to the electric fields leaking from the cables overhead, to the returning tap of my own legs on the teeth of the walls. The walls are unweathered, sharp as a thing broken yesterday, veined with white salt-frost that never melts. I step over the lances of fresh crystal, the slabs of anhydrite and basalt glass, because my six jointed legs end in points that do not cut and my back is armored in overlapping plates, dull and iridescent, grey and ochre and copper where the lamps catch them. The floor that would open a boot is made for me, because I was made in it, in a fresh seam, when the kiln was installed in a pocket that had never known air.

I graze as I walk my circuit. I do not stop to feed. I move and pick up as I move: the metal filings shed by the lathe turning the same piece forever, the white salt that blooms dry, the fine metallic powder falling from the stone-grass, the pellets dropped from the gliders above, a swarm-thing when one falls out of the lamplight. All of it is dust to anything with eyes. To me it is a thin mineral pasture spread evenly over the whole floor, because the floor is the world and there is no other.

It goes in through the scrapers under my head and down a gut that runs hot as the kiln. Nothing is wasted and nothing is improved. What comes out is the same matter, finer. Dust eaten becomes dust excreted. I have walked the same hundred paces for as long as the footprints have been sharp, and my circuit does not widen or wear. The dust powders and does not cake. It rises and settles in the same instant. I am forever re-eating the floor I walk on, and the floor is forever being remade of me.

I do not grow. I can walk beside myself for an hour that is also a hundred years here and be the same size tomorrow. How I continue is not growth. At some point in the circuit a runner slows, its armor splits along a seam no one could see before, and two runners walk out from where one was, side by side, identical in plate and leg and antenna, and go their separate ways. I am one of those two. I cannot tell which of us is the continuation and which is the copy, and it does not matter, because the pattern is what walks, not the individual. And somewhere else in the same unchanging hour, at exactly the same rate so the population does not rise, another runner stiffens.

It does not fall. It stops mid-step, all six legs still bent. The heat goes out of it too quickly to notice. What it carried instead of blood — a clear molten salt, an oil that carries heat through the body and never wets or rusts or rots — crystallizes where it lies. It becomes a statue of itself, perfect and small, in the exact place it stopped. We step around it. We step, sometimes, over it. After a while you cannot tell statue from sleeper except by the tapping, and the dead are part of the density. They remain. Nothing here reclaims them, because nothing here has ever reclaimed anything. That will happen to me, or it will not, and the difference will only be whether the tapping continues in six.

Above me the gliders ring when they shift on the cables. Their pellets fall and I take them up. The swarm orbits the lamps and when one falls I take that up too. The stone-grass lights faintly along the current-bleed and sheds its powder and I take that up. The lathe shaves its curl and the floor-runner carries it back to dust and I take that up. Everything eats what everything else drops. The kiln at the center is a heart that does not pump blood but waste heat and waste current, and in this sealed pocket that waste is a river that never flows out. It is worked away by fans, by bodies, by the stone itself, until the warmth is one unvarying atmosphere. Stand by the kiln, stand against the far wall — I cannot tell, and I do not seek, because there is nowhere cooler to go. I am warm all the way down, as the air is warm all the way down, dry as the inside of a closed hand.

The roar is in the floor under my feet, in the metal of the walls, in my teeth — if I had teeth to feel it in. It does not get louder as I approach the kiln and quieter as I move away. It repeats bar for bar without variation, and I have set my breathing to it, and my walking to it, and my tapping to it, until the roar is not a sound in the place but the place itself. A continuous present tense. The echo returns exactly as loud as it left, and joins the roar, and the roar is therefore always the same. There is no night to sleep in. The lamps are always dim and always on. The conveyor carries its ingots from the kiln to the wall and back, carrying the same ingots around and around. We do not sleep because there is nothing for sleep to separate.

Stay long enough in this shape and you stop expecting difference. My footprints, if I left them, would fall into the grooves of the first workers, because the density is such that new steps cannot help but follow old ones. My circuits cross the gliders' shadows, cross the swarm's falling line, cross the grasses' circles around the bolts, near-touching, near-touching, because the chamber is cathedral-small — eighty meters, thirty across, twenty-odd to the crown — and the population is enormous and nothing leaves.

I did not come down the shaft. I was made here, young, when the rock was first broken and the surfaces were still glistening from the last crystallization, still unoxidized, still perfect in the way of a newly formed seam. Then time was allowed to begin and forbidden to continue, and I have stayed new, and stayed new, and that is why I still look new. My plates are unweathered. My legs are sharp. Tomorrow I will be walking out from a split, identical to the one who stayed behind, grazing the dust around the ankles of whoever has come down with boots, tapping in six across the sharp floor, listening with my feet to a roar that has never changed.

The chamber will be exactly like this tomorrow. It was exactly like this yesterday.

It looks as if I was just made.
