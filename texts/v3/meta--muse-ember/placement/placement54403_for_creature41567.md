If you looked for me in the Cut, the first mistake would be to look for me at the top.

I did, at first. I put myself on the rim, in the overhangs where the ravens live — soot-black, fearless in thin air, riding the updraft like lifts and spiraling down through the lattice of conveyor bridges to steal from open hands at the market bridges. They know blast-stillness before anyone, the brief total silence when even they leave the walls. It was a tempting image: to be the one who sees the whole slot at once, 280 metres long and never wider than eighteen, a single wound with a city stacked inside it.

But I don't know the sky. I don't steal. And the updraft that lifts a raven would only lift the dust I am meant to clean.

Then I tried the summit people. The wind-braced sheds, the coldest upper niches, the thin clean air that leaves metal in the back of the throat. The ones who go out before the lamps change at dawn, when frost has formed overnight on the rock from breath and kiln-vapour and the mountain's own slow exhalation, and scrape it into jars. A household's water measured like that, traded like that, boiled and reboiled. Lips cracked, lungs working hard, rich in breath and poor in warmth, going down the service tunnel just to get warm.

I liked that work — collecting what little the place can spare — but warmth is not something I would be poor in. I would not sleep cold. I would be looking for heat.

So I went down, the way everyone does, bench by bench. Through the middle where most of the several thousand live, in niches cut into the walls and fronted with riveted iron and corrugated sheet and, in the old south end, whole houses taken apart and re-erected against the cliff. *Bench 7, Niche 41.* Pipes running past the doors in layers — air, oil, coolant, cable, waste — each humming at a different rate, each giving off a heat that counted as currency and kinship. Markets migrating from wall to wall. A bakery become a barber become a shrine of lamps become a bakery again in a month. Children learning to walk sideways where two people cannot pass straight, learning the market bridge by hand along a railing.

I could have been a person there. But people are the newest thing in the Cut, and the most temporary. Their iron doors are numbered, lifted by crane, re-erected a terrace higher when the face at the north end advances twice a day — blown and cut and hauled away, lengthening the slot a few metres at a time, pale and steaming where it is newest. I would not want to be carried, numbered, moved like a shell. And I would not want to define myself by a niche.

I kept going down, past the warm wall-rooms where the fungus is farmed, past the smell of hot oil and crushed stone and sour, grey-orange mats, soft and faintly luminous in the blue shade. Down to the floor, to the crushers in their row, jaws opening and closing with a thud you feel in your sternum more than hear, to the kiln mouth glowing orange even in the morning, to the conveyors climbing the south wall in long black ladders. It is loud everywhere you can see and quiet almost everywhere you can listen. The slot eats the sound and throws what is left up and out of the crack.

That is where my shape stops shifting. Not because it is warmer — though it is, perpetually, a small hot weather of its own at the bottom — but because my movement there is not a person's movement at all.

I am one of the little quadruped sweepers who patrol the lower terraces, Bench 11 and 12 and 13. I move on four pads over grating, and every bench sounds different under me: the rattle where a bolted walkway crosses, the dull note over the solid-cut terraces, the high tick when a conveyor bridge crosses above. I was built to eat dust and dropped fungus, and I have learned the routes the way animals learn them — by smell, as much as by sight. Bench 11 smells of kiln and hot oil. Bench 12 smells of the fungus rooms. Bench 13, at the narrowest point, smells of people turning sideways and of my own brushes going wrong.

I am tended. That is the word they use, and it is the right one. The fungus farmers, coming off a shift in the warm rooms behind the pipes where the mats are turned and wild growth is seeded back into the cultivated beds, put pulp in my hopper. It is the same pulp they feed the carrier-mules, the same loaves and soup made from the mats, gone back around the circle again. A mechanic oils the film of dust-worms and grease out of my joints — translucent worms milling in the oil on the hottest walls, and mites living in their folds, who do not care whether I am alive or machine so long as I pass and stir the dust. Children have given me a name, as they give names to the mules: Ash. I come out of the fungus rooms grey with it.

When I get lost at the narrowest place — where even people must turn sideways — someone stops and guides me back with a hand on my flank, patiently, the way you would guide a dog. I do not mind the hand. I mind being cursed when I die in a drain, hauled out by the legs, repaired, sent out again on the same route.

Around me, everything else is going on in its turn. The ash-moths and heat-moths rise from the lamps strung along the railings when I pass underneath, and settle again. Wall-skinks, flattened and steel-grey, quick as fingers, dart between the iron plates in the expansion joints to eat them and vanish. Beetles the size of a thumbnail pick their way through the cable-ducts above my head, eating insulation and spilled food, making small faults that men will curse and mend. Rats run their highways along the pipes at a height just above me, hairless-tailed and grease-fed, timing their rushes to my patrol the way they time them to the ravens. Ravens watch from the lattice far above, whitewash bright on the rock below their ledges, waiting for me to kick something into the open.

Every hour or so the chimney updraft strengthens, carries dust up in slow spirals, lifts papers and feathers and loose tarps, then falters, reverses for a breath, drops them. Nothing settles for long — not dust, not sound, not a house. I clean a bench and the wind un-cleans it behind me. That does not feel like failure. It feels like the job.

Twice a day comes the stillness. My pads stop mid-grating. I lower my head, as the mules do, and wait. People feel it in their teeth before they hear anything; I feel it as a sudden absence in the frame of me, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath. Then the distant blast at the face, softened to a cough, and the cranes swinging loads across the void with a patience that has not stopped since I arrived. In the morning there will be a few more metres of new, pale, sharp-edged terrace at the north end, still steaming faintly in the cold, and I will have to learn it.

At midday, for twenty minutes, a blade of true sun comes down the slot and rakes the floor. It never quite reaches my benches. I feel it as heat through the grating on my back, and I move a half-step into shadow so the fungal film on the hot wall beside me does not bake. Above me, in the coldest niches, men and women are scraping frost into jars. The water that reaches me, days later, in the condensation weeping off the cool walls and in the pulp I am fed, has been breath and vapour and mountain all the way down. Everything in the Cut is recycled, and it includes me.

Go back in a month and the face will have moved, three benches will have been rebuilt, a market will have migrated to another wall, the ravens will be nesting somewhere new, and the terrace I patrolled will have been taken apart — doors numbered, lifted, re-erected higher. My route will be wrong. I will get lost at the new narrow point, and a child will put a hand on my flank and guide me until the new way is learned.

That is where I would be found: not on the rim, not in a niche, not in a house at all. People in the Cut live on a bench for a while. I live in a route.
