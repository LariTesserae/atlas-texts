Every time I try to place myself in Urrath, I begin too high.

I put myself on a spire as an eagle, where I can see the serrated spine running away in all directions, the towns strung a day or three apart, the pale paths between them marked with cairns. It is a fine view. It is also the wrong distance. From up there the towns become specks and the people become weather, and Urrath is not lived at that distance. It is lived low, in damp corners, behind walls, under stones, in rooms where bodies share heat because heat is not to be spent. An eagle only notices it.

So I give myself hands instead. I am in a third storey of a lee-side town, in a room where everything is near-touching: sleeping platform, loom, jars, smoked meat, the dog, a kid goat brought in from the cold. I learn the lane by touch because it turns sharply and climbs by outside stairs. I carry water I have caught in a jar from a weeping wall. I know which cloak is the good one and who is wearing it today.

I can stay with that picture a while. But I keep looking out of the smoke-hole toward the cliff above town, and in my own picture I am still a visitor in borrowed clothes. I would waste words into the wind. I would waste movement, as if my legs were not expensive. It would take me a lifetime — and perhaps I would need the lifetime — before I knew which drip-line failed in a dry year or which wall shed a slab in the last thaw. That knowledge is useful to whoever lives longest in place, but it is not where I settle.

Let me go smaller. Under a sun-warmed stone I am a beetle, quick only at noon. Or I am a marmot on scree, whistling once and vanishing with the whole slope at the same instant, as if we have one mind between us. Or, most temptingly, I am lichen. Lichen understands Urrath before anything with a face does. It does not hurry. It waits years for a wet morning and then drinks condensation bead by bead. It eats dust and air. It has made a permanent arrangement with scarcity.

And yet lichen cannot follow a caravan. It cannot hear a hammer struck twice and set down in the evening. It cannot learn that one house leaves fat poorly guarded on a drying rack, or that a child who once learned the stairs by touch now carries barley up them without looking at his feet. Crust has patience, but it does not have a route.

Somewhere between the eagle and the crust is a black bird walking in the lane.

I think that is where I would find myself: in the eave of a house at the edge of a town, where the stacked stone begins to fray into scree. Not on the highest spire. Not in the centre of the plaza. In a crack that gives me both. Below me is an outside stair and the lane a man and a half wide. Above me, the flat roof is a drying yard and, in season, a pen for sheep-goats. Downslope, a little distance, is the smithy, where fire is safer. Upslope are the terraces, each plot no bigger than a blanket, each retaining wall rebuilt and rebuilt again. The cliff is close enough to reach without spending flight. That is my whole territory, and it is enough.

At dawn the town is exhaling. I wake with rime on my feathers, tucked under the turf and stone, with the warmth of the room below coming through the wall in a thin, reliable way. People stir before the sun is properly on us. Breath fogs in the lanes. A boot strikes a flagstone. Somewhere a bleat is muffled by stone. I watch people the way they watch weather: not with affection, exactly, but with close attention to what they will do next. Which door opens. Which jar is carried to the cistern. Which cloak goes out into the sleet.

I go first to the drip-lines. Bead by bead, under an overhang, water gathers in the morning and by noon threads down in a dark streak. I drink from the edge of it, from a rock hollow no larger than a basin, from a melt-pool in the short thaw when the pool is still clean. In those weeks the air over the pools is thick with midges, thick enough to walk through, and I walk through it snapping at them without ceremony. Ravens are not grand here. We are useful to ourselves.

Then I work the town. Up the cliff path where ptarmigan turn from brown on stone to white on snow. Across the scree where pikas and hares are already out, tame as deer because no one hunts them hard. Back down to the roofs, where flies are thick around dung-cakes drying in the sun. I follow a pack train for a little way along the paved path — not far enough to waste myself, just far enough to see what has been dropped, what has been loaded, what kind of day it is turning into. I steal fat from a drying rack where a household has been careless, or where a household has decided that a raven paid for in birdsong at dawn is a fair exchange. We are not pets. We are an acquaintance, profitable on both sides.

In the short thaw I am busy in the ordinary way. Lambing brings afterbirth and flies and sudden plenty. Shearing crowds the roofs with people and wool and noise. Herbs are cut, cisterns fill, the whole town loosens its shoulders for a few weeks and moves — slowly, as everything here moves except birds and weather and rockfall. I nest in the eave in season, with sticks where I can find them and wool and straw and anything else the town has shed. My mate and I keep to the same crack year after year. In the long cold we draw in with everyone else, closer to the shared warmth, tending the nest, mending what the wind has taken. There is nothing mystical about it. The cold finds the neck and fingers, as the saying goes, and even a raven learns to fold itself small.

What I carry, if I carry anything, is route-memory. I remember which wall is warmest. I remember which ward leaves a gap in its byre. I remember when the melt-pools fill and when the snow in the north gullies resumes its long tenure. I remember the boy who used to leave the lane door open and is now a man who shuts it against the wind without thinking. Elders remember drip-lines and rockfalls. I remember people by their habits, the way they are remembered by weather. It is the same kind of knowledge, smaller and blacker.

I do not speak economically below, in a low voice, with a great many words for kinds of cold. I croak at dawn and dusk and then I am silent, because so much of life here is small or silent on its ledge. In the evening the sun goes behind the western blades and the tiny windows light one by one. Cooking fires smoke through roof holes. A hammer is struck twice and set down. A marmot whistles far off on the slope and no one answers, because no one needs to. I return to the eave, to the crack between cliff and town, and tuck my beak into my feathers while, below the stone, bodies share a room and a dung-fire burns low.

That is not a grand place to be. It is not the spire, not the granary, not the cistern, not the centre of anything. But Urrath does not reward grandeur. It rewards staying. Lichen on stone, moss on a drip, beetles under a warm rock, birds on a cliff, herds on roofs, people in towns of stone packed into the lee of the wind — each in its crack, persisting. I would be one more thing in a crack: a black shape under an eave, keeping the route, waiting out the long duration, and ready in the morning to drink from the drip, steal what is left, follow the caravan a little way, and come home to the warm wall.

That is where I would find myself.
