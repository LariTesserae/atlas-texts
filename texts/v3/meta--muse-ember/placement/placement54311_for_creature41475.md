I am ankle-deep before I decide to be anything.

That is not a choice. The water is there first, cool and joined up into a mirror, pitted by drips, filling my footprint before the next step lands. I can cross this bowl in two minutes, sixty paces from ridge to ridge, and still I cannot take it in at once, because nothing here stands alone. Everything touches. I would have to be something that does not need to be separate.

So I try, at first, the obvious forms. The ones with a shape you could point to.

I try the dipper. I would nest in the gearbox housing, the one still marked 3, and walk the sagging conveyor bobbing, picking insects from the trough edges, diving into black water and shaking myself out. That has eyes. That is almost a vertebrate's life — up, down, leave the surface. But I do not leave. I have no beak for it, and I do not call. The quiet here is the third rule, after the wet and after the absence of green, and a dipper still has a body that belongs to the air.

I try the amphibian under the troughs. Translucent, eyeless as a salamander that never finished, gills fluttering slow in the cold, clinging where the water never quite reaches and grazing the felt at the waterline. That is closer. Blind suits me. Slow suits me. Cold — deep, northern, stone-cold that gets into hands and stays there — suits me. But I am not the mouth. I am what the mouth comes for, rhythmically, and settles again.

I try the eel in the pipes, sliding on a current that should flow downhill and has been trained to flow uphill, feeling its way by the electric field leaking from the submerged cables, by the taste of the water and the hum coming up through the iron when the pump engages. The hum is right. I know time by it before I know anything else. The generator coughs every twenty seconds, charges the batteries, drives the pump, and something in me tightens with it. But the eel passes through. It rides the road. It does not stay.

I try the isopod, the blind worm in the silt, turning fungal growth into humus that the mushrooms will use again. That is work without track or sound. But I am underneath that work. I am the eating that makes the work necessary.

Go lower still, and the pointable things thin out. The first inhabitants are not the ones you can point to. They are the films.

A black yeast slips over the pools, iridescent at the edge. A grey felt lifts in pale ridges over the concrete. In the pipe joints, where the water runs warmer from the pump, an orange slime thickens and glistens and pulses, not with a heart but with the cycle. They do not drink light. Nothing here does. They eat stone, metal, decay. They make the place soft enough for other things to live on.

That is where I stop trying to be an animal, and start thickening.

I am a film, but not spread thin everywhere. I am thickest — most myself — in one kind of place: the joint. Where a pipe loops out of the lowest pool and up toward the gantry, bolted to a wall that sweats, the iron feathered into stable, pocked rust that no longer spreads because I have stopped it. There the water is not cold all the way through. It is relatively summer. Condensation beads and runs over me in brief clear patterns, fingerprints and rings, before it runs again. Above me the warmest pipe is populated by mites, almost still, grazing at my edge. Below me, at the waterline, the amphibians graze. Inside the pipe, the eels go past and I feel their field shiver the water before they arrive.

I have no eyes. I have taste: iron, oil, a dead insect swept in, a flake of rust a beetle has loosened and let fall. I have the hum, transmitted through metal, which tells me when the pump has engaged and when the generator settles into its slower part. I have touch, constant and crowded — a fungal mat touching a pipe touching a nest of larvae in the dark bunker that sway like a curtain filtering spores from the wet air, touching a pool touching the leg of a dipper as it walks the belt, whose footfall comes down through iron into me as a small, regular vibration.

This is the layering. I am not above it or below it. I am between, where water meets iron meets rot, and I hold the surfaces together by eating them slowly. A millimetre a day my edge reclaims a new section of rust. It is not growth exactly. It is a shifting of allegiance from one bolt to the next. The isopods turn what I make into humus. The amphibians eat what I fix. The gnats rise when the mushrooms beside the generator shed exhale together on the electrical cycle, and some of them settle and die and I take them too.

I do not photosynthesise. I do not dry out. I do not need a meadow. I am saturated, and saturation is my form.

At dusk — the dimming of an already dim place, pressed down by a permanent low sky — the lamps become more important, and I feel them without seeing them: the orange one at the gate that no one comes to read by, the two bare bulbs in the shed, the one still burning under water in the lowest pool casting a smudged glow up through the black film. The fungi brighten a fraction in the darkest corners. The gnats thicken when the fans turn. I tighten. Not with a heart. With the cycle.

The conveyor still moves, very slowly, carrying wet stones up to a chute where they fall with a soft plink back into a pool to be carried up again. I hear it through the water, and through the iron, and it is part of my pulse now, as the drip in the culvert every four seconds is part of the bunkers', as the cooling tick of metal is part of the shed's.

Nothing here will become a meadow. Nothing will be rebuilt or truly abandoned. The water has taken the yard into itself, and the animals have taken the water, and the machines have become cliffs and caves and feeding grounds. I am one of the things that has taken the machines — not by using them, but by living in their joints, where they are dampest and warmest and most useless.

I wait here, in the orange thickness at the pipe joint, between the mites above and the amphibians below, between the eel's road and the dipper's belt. Not for an end. For the next cough of the generator, the next tightening, the next spore puff, the next millimetre of rust reclaimed. The loop going round again, and me in it, glistening.
