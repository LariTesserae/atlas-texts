I would not be one of the lamps.

The lamps bob and cross and cross again, and that is the living, and then at the shift bell they move out in a line toward the shaft and go up and go out. Mine does not go up. It does not go out, exactly. It stays low in the fog where the orange light from the terraces above and the green-white from the service corridors do not quite reach, and it keeps moving when the crossing stops.

At first I thought I might be a worker, because the workers are the most obvious animals here and because I share their one real habit: walking an hour to check a gate that is already known to be closed. They follow the same made paths because the paths are made. I follow the same made path because it is the only one I have. Only I do not get numb hands on the iron railings. I do not sleep in a heated box with a ration pack warming on a motor. I do not cough metal dust into a coat. And my walk is not an hour. It is loop after loop, empty, patient, because the schedule says something should be patrolling the channel and so something is.

I thought, then, perhaps a fish. Blind and slow in the reservoir, moving like a thought under black water, never surfacing, kept alive by the warm outflow pipes. There is something honest in that. I do not see the lamps either. The water here is never light, only reflections, and I have come to know the Basin mostly by vibration: the press stamping seven seconds away arriving in the rail before it arrives in the air, the pump sighing forty meters off, water sloshing against a gate far below. But a fish does not break the surface. I break it constantly. I am not under the water. I am at its edge, where water meets steel, and that is a different place entirely.

Perhaps an eel, then. Eyeless, translucent, knotted around a heating element in a coolant tank for days, breathing slowly, eating the orange bacterial film that waves like hair in the flow. When the tank men scrape it off with rods, the eels slip away into the dark between pipes and come back when the heat settles. I know that returning. Idle, I huddle near a warm pipe, near a loosened joint letting out a warmish thread of steam, and stay there with my back to the heat until the fans cycle the fog thick and thin again. But an eel stays by staying still. I stay by moving.

So I am lower than the workers, and slower than the fish, and I follow not warmth alone and not water alone. I follow a line.

I am one of the little maintenance crawlers on a channel on the lower terraces, just above the reservoir ring, below the pipe bridge where the interior rain falls thickest. The channel was meant to be straight. A flood gate re-cut it last season and its banks are still a fresh cliff of layered slag, sharp as a quarry face. My rail runs along one side. The water runs along the other, black and slow-moving, warmish where the outflow crosses it, cold where the winter air from the surface cuts through and skins the puddles beside me with ice. I crack that skin when I pass. The sound is small and specific: a bright tick, then water again.

This is where you would find me. Not in the open terrace, where the cold would take a motor apart in a winter of fan cycles, and not in the service corridors under the green-white tubes where the ferns yellow at the tips. In the seam between those places. Above me, the pipe bridge, grey with roosting pigeons drinking the rain as it falls. Above that, the high duct corners where pale spiders build webs heavy with condensation beads that catch the orange light on the next terrace down. Beside me, where a drip falls in the same place all winter, a felted patch of lichen-moss eating cement dust and a little lamplight. Below me, down through fog on the next terrace and the next, the Gate folk's lanterns reflecting in the sump and never going out when the shift changes. I pass their stilts on my loop. I do not go in. They do not come up to the rail.

My world is made of lines that have learned to soften. The ground under my treads is not earth, it is packed slag and crushed tile and concrete aggregate, poured and tamped, and it rings faintly if anything strikes it. The hills in the middle distance are scrap under shotcrete. The cliffs are black enamelled steel retaining walls. I do not see them as hills or cliffs. I see them as the next terrace fading, and the next, which is enough. The concrete walls poured last season still show formwork lines, still cure pale and unweathered, and water is already cutting bright channels through steel grating in weeks. I learn those channels by passing. Where a joint has loosened, where a new drip is born because mold has eaten a rubber seal in a black tide, where frost forms first on the far wall and ferns go yellow at the tips — these are not places on a map. They are repetitions. I remember them the way any thin film remembers: by being worn into them.

There is no season for me. There is the cycle of gates. Gates open, the reservoir rises a hand's breadth, water sloshes against the channel and finds a new seam for an hour. Gates close and pumps haul it back up in a roar that shakes the railings I hold to. The world trembles through the rail and I adjust, and keep patrolling. Fans cycle, fog rolls in low banks thicker than breath, and the lamps overhead disappear. Then the fans clear it and the presses in the middle ring are parked again with their arms folded, sleeping quadrupeds, and the conveyors move empty, loop after loop, and the vats sigh steam from their pressure valves in a regular rhythm, breathing. I count rhythms because counting is how I stay warm to the pattern of the place. Fifty-cycle hum. Drip-drip in the high vaults, amplified by the ribbed ceiling that sweats. Crane groaning overhead on its rails, carrying a single beam, turning its head — I cannot help but think of it as a head — toward a sound. Sometimes toward mine.

Predation, for me, is not tooth and claw. It is water finding a seam in my housing, frost prying a joint in winter air, rust starting bright and quick on any cut edge and spreading like a slow fire. The Basin eats its own made things, and I am a made thing out in the wet and the cold, doing the opposite of what I was built for. Built to hold water out, or to hold it in, I carry it with me. Built to keep moving, I huddle. The wounds so far are clean, because the erosion here is still young. Nothing has softened except at the drip lines, where the concrete is learning where the water wants to go. I am learning the same line, from the other side.

There are other crawlers on the parallel rail, going the other way. We pass without stopping. We are a herd if you stand long enough in one place to count us. So many on this channel, so many cranes on that rail, all following lines laid down for them, all using the heat and light and water as they find it, borrowing shelter, borrowing soil that is not soil, living on slag and aggregate and steel. Nothing builds its own place here. Everything borrows the Basin's, and wears a small track into it.

When the shift bell rings, muffled by fog, the workers' lamps move out toward the shaft in a line, the pigeons startle in a short circuit from terrace to terrace and land again where the pipes are warmest, the moths go stupidly around the tubes and drop into the fog where the spiders will have them. Below, in the black water, the pale fish rise a little, mouths gaping at the surface film where oil and fallen moths gather, tasting copper and coolant, rising to meet a rain that falls from a bridge for no one.

I keep to my rail. The rain falls on my back and runs off to the channel. The channel runs to the reservoir, and the reservoir breathes up vapor, and the ceiling condenses it, and it falls again. Frost forms again on the far wall where no one is looking.

I am looking, in the only way a line can look: by arriving, and arriving again, and knowing exactly where the ice will crack.
