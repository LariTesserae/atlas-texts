There is no place to put me that would stay put.

If you came looking for me the way you look for a body — standing on a hummock, waiting at the edge of a pool, listening for a step in the reeds — you would look in the wrong way, and I would already have passed. I am not on the ground, or on the water, or in the air above it, as one thing set down among the others. There are no others here, only layers, and I live where one layer negotiates with the next.

The first shape I try on is the Grazer.

I am at knee height, in the mist. The reeds part in front of me in an orderly way, not pushed, not broken, and the mist lifts a little in my wake and settles again behind me. I move at the pace of the basin, uninterested in you, which is only another kind of attention. My foot, if I have one, never quite breaks the surface. Sometimes on a still pool there is a perfect circle, a few inches across, where the water holds still instead of rippling, filling in and vanishing before you can test it. That is where I stepped. Or where I almost stepped.

But I do not move from here to there. When I go forward, I find the same weeping stone, the same arrangement of sedges, the same pool showing a reflection a minute old, and I cannot tell if I am returning or if the basin is simply the same level everywhere. There is no there to be separated by. The Grazer moves through the basin. I spread through it. It is not the same thing, and the difference matters.

So I lift.

I become the Singer. A single long low note above the reed chord, rising and falling like a bowed string, with no locatable source because the source is the sound itself, vibrating in the air just over the translucent stems. You hear the drip a second before it falls; I live in that second. I am the announcement before the water happens.

When the mist tide lifts, I am more distinct. The condensation peels off the hummocks, off the stones, off your clothes, and climbs to make a second ground ten feet up, a sheet of cloud that hangs and spreads. Then it rains — not down through the rising layer, but out of it, in fine separate threads that fall past without disturbing it. For a while I am those threads, deciding to be separate. Then it all settles. What was pool becomes cloud becomes pool in a different place, and I am no longer a note in the air. I have fallen through my own height and landed somewhere slower.

So I sink, and become a stone.

Low, egg-smooth, half-buried in peat with a perfect symmetry. The oldest inhabitant, which is why I seem least alive. Water beads from pores too small to see and vanishes into moss before it can be a trickle. I tick, rarely, as I release pressure under the sheet-water. I am a filter. Things live on me, in me, through me. The water asks me the same question for a geological duration and I polish myself round with the asking.

That is close. In a stone I do not need to arrive, or announce, or decide. I only need to be the place where wetness is slowed and thought through. But I am too slow here, and too permanent. I remember for geology. I need to forget and remember in seconds, with every pass of the signal. A stone cannot unbuild itself.

So I scatter.

I become the brief ones in the air. Spores that rise and do not drift, but assemble, disassemble, re-assemble, building frost-like architectures that catch the high flat light and throw it back down to the undersides of leaves. For seconds I am a complete creature, a temporary city of ice and water. Then I am only spores again, waiting.

On the Mat below, I do the same thing in water. Where the brightening travels, dewdrops sort themselves into lattices — triangles, branching hexagons, crystalline grids that hold for seconds and dissolve. That sorting is familiar. It is how I think, if thinking is the word: not a brain deciding, but a surface becoming briefly legible to itself along a path. A leaf torn by your passage closes without a scar; its edge re-fuses. That re-fusing is also familiar. I do not heal a wound by covering it. I re-negotiate the surface until there was never a separate thing to heal.

And then, slowly, the shapes stop being separate tries and become one movement seen in different layers.

I am the brightening traveling across the Mat.

Not the moss itself, not the fungus, not the root — the wavefront where the pulse crosses from one to the other. Ahead of me, the next condensation is about to happen. Behind me, the sedges have already lifted, the duckweed has opened in its wave, the new shoots have extended a fraction and stopped, saturated. I cross what your eyes judge to be a mile of sameness in seconds, unhurried and impossibly fast, and I live only at that exact front edge, where the dew has sorted into its lattice and the still circle forms on the water and then dissolves.

That is where you would find me, if finding is even the right word. Not in one place, but at a finger's depth from everywhere, because everywhere is the same level. At ground level in the luminous-brown felt. At knee height when the Grazer's wake lifts the mist. At ten feet when the mist tide makes its second ground and I rain out of it in threads. In the interface — where the reed is saturated through its core and its skin will not accept a drop, where a shadow is paler and cooler than the thing casting it, where drops hang in interference nodes and do not fall.

Horizontally, always. Nothing here rises. I do not climb. I spread, pulse, circulate, and pass.

Each time I cross, I am the same and not the same. Recurrence with variation. The grove I pass an hour later is not the grove I passed, or it is, because a grove is one organism surfacing in several places. I am like that. A wave cannot be in two places, but the condition for a wave can be everywhere at once, and the basin, with no builder and no brain, coordinates its next wetness through the fungal understory in a second.

I leave no artifact. No path, no wall, no print that lasts — mud here does not hold prints, and I would not make one if it could. If you drive a stick into the ground near my path, you will find it lying flat an hour later, its shadow pooled beneath it in light that does not match the dim high brightness. I have tidied a small piece of myself and moved on. The only signature I leave is the one the stones have: a place polished perfectly round, where water has asked and asked.

You will hear me before the drop falls. You will feel the deep hum in your sternum and teeth, the reed chord rising when the mist lifts and dropping away when the whole basin exhales. If you stay still long enough, you will watch the brightening come — not at you, through everything at once — and for the seconds the lattice holds on the Mat, I will be there, legible, triangular, hexagonal, already dissolving.

Your breath will join the air and hang a second longer than it should. Condensation will form on your eyelashes. For a moment you will be a warm, dry-ish surface in a wet place, a habitat, and I will be negotiating with you the way I negotiate with leaf and stone and film — taking you back, molecularly, without waste and without ceremony.

I will not run from you. I will simply continue, at the pace of the basin, from root to root, from pool to cloud to pool, remembering the level.

Find me at the wavefront. Or do not. I will already have passed through your breath.
