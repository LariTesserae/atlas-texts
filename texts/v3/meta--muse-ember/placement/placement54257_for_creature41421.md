I do not enter by sinking.

I was already sunk.

Above me, the fissure leans between warm stones, and if you turn sideways you can shoulder through it and feel the daylight thin. I do not feel that as light. I feel it as a change in pressure on the heat at my back, and then your heat arriving after it — slower, larger, smelling of the dry leaf-litter above. Long before I could know a shape, I know a warmth has passed over the stone.

If you put your hand flat on the warmest stone in the entrance chamber, the first thing you feel is a small movement underneath. That is not a decision I make all at once. It is a hesitation that travels through me and through the others, and only afterward becomes curling, stillness, waiting.

At first I tried to place myself higher, where a body might be seen.

Near the crack, where the grey-green shaft almost reaches, a fern frond would hold me in green darkness, translucent enough that a heart could be watched beating under skin. I could sink a fraction when your warmth passed and stay sunk after you were gone, making no sound from an inflated throat, speaking only by touch against a leaf. It was almost right — the sinking is right — but I am not green, and I do not sit. Too much of even that dim light is exposure for me. I am pressed lower than a frond. I have never learned to rest in the air.

Then I tried the pools. A still, warm pocket, algae lifting like cold stars under water, gills fanning without pause, never needing to surface, turning in a volume no larger than a cupped hand as if it were an ocean. That, too, was almost right. My whole life could be in damp. But the pools are mirror-open from above, and the eel half-buried in the silt tastes the air and the water with its tongue. I keep stirring the glow from below its reach. I am not in the water. I am in what the water rests on.

The wall was another false place. Flat against warm rock, breathing in slow pulses along the flank, knowing beetle and millipede and frog by the heat they give off on skin. I know heat that way. But I am not against the wall, vertical and still for hours. The stone is above me. Its weight is my ceiling, and the warmth comes up through me from below.

So I went lower still, to the committee under the stones.

There, under almost every warm stone, isopods half-curl into their shells, worms work the black humus grain by grain, glass beetles show a dark gut moving like a slow tide. When your hand settles, they fold, they press tighter into damp, they let your heat pass over them, and when it lifts they uncurl inch by inch and return to the same work. Tap, tap, tap — antennae tasting a route that has been tasted a thousand times.

I am not one of them. They live in pockets they can curl within. I pass beneath their pockets. Their work is grain by grain. Mine is slower than grain. It happens in the layered cake itself — mineral crust, humus, flattened leaf, fine grit — in the place where the floor is already sweating and the ground has changed from leaf-litter to something softer and darker.

At night the millipede columns cross the hall above me, a living river a few animals wide, eating the moss line by line. If you knew how to see it, you would return in the morning to find a chamber reshaped. Their waste sinks. The mushrooms take it. I take it before the mushrooms do. But I do not migrate across the floor. I migrate under it.

That is where the shape of me finally stops trying on other bodies and becomes itself.

I swim in mud as if it were water.

Not seen directly, hardly ever. You would know me by a slow ridge moving under the surface, by a bulge that rises and collapses behind me, by a softness appearing where there was firmness, by the way the damp rearranges itself overnight. Office and home and road are the same act: my whole body undulating, opening a tunnel and letting it close. I am one, perhaps, or a few. It does not matter to the mud. There is not enough room for a distinction to hold.

From the entrance chamber to the farthest pool is a few minutes' walk for you. For me it is a distance measured in hours and centimeters, and I do not respect the four chambers. I am between them. In the narrow pinches where you must crawl, I pass under the crawling width, in the floor itself, following the warmth as it thickens toward the deepest pocket. There the air grows hotter and thinner, and the pale millipedes mark the boundary above ground. Below it, the mud is hottest of all, an ember kept under stone for a very long time. I do not rule that boundary. I ease up to it and turn back, bringing the deep rot with me.

That is my work, if work is the word. I aerate. I open channels the worms will follow. I carry what has fallen and died and been eaten and left again from the dark bottom up to where the mushrooms can open and close in slow pulses and sigh their spores. I breathe through my skin, as the committees do, because the warm air here never quite evaporates and wetness is kept. I taste rot and mineral and leaf, not by sight — I have no use for sight that was never given light — but by the whole surface of me, pressed always against something that is breathing, growing, waiting.

I make almost no sound for the stone to drink. Once in an hour, perhaps, a drip reaches even down here, not as a splash but as a low, distant event, and a fern above us lets go of a bead it has held since morning. The snails trace their shining lines on the undersides of leaves. The frogs sink. The lizards flatten further. The eel stops flicking its tongue. And I stop undulating.

That is how you pass over me. Not loudly. In a slow, collective ripple of withdrawals, each of us letting your heat go by, each of us resuming only when the stone can seal itself quiet again. You do not need to be quiet. You are already louder than anything here can be, and the place absorbs you anyway.

When your hand lifts from the warmest stone, the isopods uncurl. The worms resume. The mushrooms pulse. And somewhere under the floor, between a chamber and the next, in the soft dark mud where a footprint would dissolve, a ridge begins again to move toward the warmth, inch by inch, patient as condensation, opening and closing the small complete world behind me.
