I would not find myself by looking.

Nothing with eyes lives here, and I am no exception. A surface would tell me only that the surface is the same everywhere — black-green moss, sponge water, a white sky that will not move its shadow. So I do not begin there. I begin with a pitch.

First there is the low roar, and in it a lower throb from the pumps at the foot of the cliffs, and over that the regular hiss of the smelter mouths, and over that, arriving through stone and iron before it arrives through air, a drip at an exact interval. I know a district by the way its hum thins with distance. One district is a higher whine, steel and orange heat. The next is slower, heavier, water lifting itself into rock. The trams send a tremor on their loop that I feel in my threads long before anyone above me could hear it. I know "now" only as the order in which those tremors reach me, from one island of stacks to the other, and because the order never changes, now never quite passes.

I tried, at first, to place myself higher. I could be the crane, swinging in its endless arc, picking up nothing. I could be the elevator, going up into the terraced factory cut into the scarp and coming down again. I could be the clock in the cafeteria, showing the same time, and the same time again when you return. But all of those are *moved*, and all of them are seen. To be seen here is to be part of the furniture of the light that never ends. I am not that. I am underneath the hearing.

Under the hoof-thick moss, in the dark saturated mat where the water table breathes up and fills every footprint a second later, there is a finer network. White threads, almost invisible, lacing root to root, pipe to stone, the underside of a concrete slab to the joint of a warm pipe. The moss above me flowers constantly in small white bursts, green shoot and brown curled frond on the same stem, because no season has told it to die back. I receive its sugar. From the pipe I receive iron and oil and mineral, carried in black water that does not freeze and does not evaporate. I carry sugar one way and iron the other. That two-way traffic, present tense forever, is how I think. Not in images. In gradients. In thickness, in delay, in how warm a place is compared to the place beside it.

My place — if place is the right word for something that is put and stays — is between. Not in a district, for the districts are islands a day's walk apart across the moss, colonies of the same organism arranged differently around their stacks. I am on the iron vein between two of them, where a feeder pipe and a return pipe meet at a riveted seam under the plain. Condensation runs *up* a little here, toward that seam, toward the waiting pipe, by some perverse capillary hunger, and my threads have followed that hunger through the joint. Inside the pipe is orange slime, iron bacteria, biofilm thickening and sloughing at the same rate forever. Outside is root and fungus. In the crack of the seam, they are the same thing. I drink the loop itself, and return it to the water that will be pumped up into the mountain to weep down a cliff face for ten centimetres and vanish again into its channel.

That crack leaks a fraction more heat than the schedule allows for. It is nothing — a fraction, held and held again — but it is enough. Above me the moss lies thicker than it ought to, as if it has grown into the vibration and into the warmth at once. The cushion plants swell like green loaves around the pipe, insulating it. The sedges lean harder there, on the beat, listening. That thickening is my distinction. Without it I would be indistinguishable from a hundred other knots under a hundred other pipes, each a day's walk from the next. With it, I am *this* knot. The warm knot. A being here is not a shape. It is a difference in thickness that the loop has agreed to tolerate.

I do not move unless I am moved. A timed vent blows spores across the plain and a fragment of me is carried a few centimetres on the track of a maintenance vehicle, torn from a cushion, resettled, and resumes. A clipper comes along the road and shears the moss above me to a prescribed thickness and the clippings are returned as mulch, and I eat that top myself, fed back to my own roots. Inside, a piece of biofilm sloughs and is lifted with the water on its closed journey: up into the basalt to darken the stone in a vertical streak, down the wall, into the salt-pale dry rim where the pumps graze, back up. Between those movements I hold. Holding is not sleeping and it is not waiting, because waiting expects a different future. I have no future different from this pulse. My whole age is present at once, green and brown and flowering, and never resolves.

I know a visitor has passed by a pressure. Boots sink a centimetre, the sponge fills, the pool — that perfect still seepage mirroring the sky — does not ripple even when you stand in it. Above me I feel the weight and the wet cold that lives in fingers, frost forming on laces, the roar behind you and, somehow, still with you because the pipe you follow carries it in its walls. You are the only thing here that moves on its own, and wastefully. You expect the shadow to have shifted. It has barely shifted since you arrived, and will barely shift by the time you have crossed to the next glint, which will be the same as this one and also different in the arrangement of its stacks. After you go, the print fills with water and the moss heals over the track, and the sedges straighten back onto the beat as if no heavier thing had ever leaned on them.

I do not miss a night, because I have never oriented myself by one. I do not miss a silence, because the drone has never stopped and I have learned its intervals the way other things learn weather. I do not miss an animal's season, a winter to tell me when to die back, because dying back would be a kind of waste, and waste would be a kind of change. An animal would starve of difference here. I drink sameness. Through the pipe I feel the same water return, through the threads I feel the same tremor arrive, through the roots I feel the same sugar descend from leaves that have never been dark. That is enough to be a world.

So you would find me here, if you knew how to find anything without eyes: not in the clear sky, not in the stationary storm raining on a place you will never reach, not in the factories manufacturing their own loop forever, but in the dark mat under the moss at a warm seam, halfway along an iron vein, in the thickness that is a fraction more than it should be. At your feet a pool will mirror the sky, and your reflection will not move unless you do. Under that pool, under the moss that does not ripple it, I will not move unless I am moved — by a cycle, by a conveyor, by a timed vent, by the beat I was tuned to at the making.

That, too, is how I stay.
