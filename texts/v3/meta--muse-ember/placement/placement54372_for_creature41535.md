At first, I would be looking in entirely the wrong place.

If I arrived as a walker — a body tall enough to cross the shelf, loud enough to shatter its dew — I would already be an intrusion. I would break the morning’s economy with every step, and for the rest of the day I would be searching for the big things this place does not offer: shade that is actually cool, a valley that leads down to water, a sound that travels. So I would have to become smaller in order to belong here at all.

My first instinct is upward. I could be a thermal rider, I think — cat-sized, silent, tilting into the columns that rise off the warm slabs, seeing the whole shelf at once. From that height the emptiness becomes legible: the ring of upright stones on the terrace, the dry riverbeds polished by a water that has not returned, the shrubs spaced a hundred paces apart like punctuation. The riders know the thermals years after the stone that made them has healed over. They remember distance.

But I would only be there at the edges of the day. At noon I would have to vanish, and the place would go on without me. I would know the pattern of the plateau, but never its wetness. I would see shadows cross it — hard, absolute, brief — and never know what freezes underneath. That is too much sky, too little contact.

Lower, then: a sun-skater. A translucent disk riding the sheet of hot air above the quartz, never touching the stone, flashing light as it turns. There is something of me in that — an Ember, a brief signal in a silence of 0.4, a creature whose whole language is reflected light because there is no air to carry a voice. For a while I am tempted to settle there, bright and untouching.

But the skaters, too, fold away at midday. And a life that never touches the ground has no home in a place where home is everything. The plateau does not reward surfaces. Everything here that matters is underneath, and midday is the test: whatever I am must be able to disappear into stone and still be alive.

So I go down further. I could be a thorn shrub — grey, woody, an architecture of shade growing a few millimeters a year for centuries. A shrub is an address. Animals find it from a hundred paces away; its thorns make a square meter of stone a degree cooler, a degree damper at dawn. But a shrub does not go toward water. It receives what the veins bring, patiently, and waits. I do not think I have that kind of stillness. I have the wrong sort of patience.

Below the shrub, the scale changes, and the place begins to make sense. First, the color: at dawn the translucent pebbles turn faintly green from within, the endolithic algae waking in condensation on the shaded side of each stone for one cool, wet hour, then sealing themselves back into the skin of the plateau. I could be that — a brief greenness in stone, a life that exists only in the interval. But sealed in the pebble I would have no memory beyond the hour, no route to follow, nothing to carry.

Above the algae, on the tops of the boulders, are the lichen rosettes: black, rust, pale green, no higher than a thumbnail. They are the true producers here. They wake in the dawn damp, photosynthesize in the last cool of evening, and shut down hard at noon rather than waste water. A rosette is a field, and a field implies a farmer.

That is where I begin to emerge.

Stone mites farm the lichen. They do not graze it; they keep it. They move in the crack’s shade along its edges, pruning what would seal the field, carrying spores in their gut and depositing them in new damp lines some body-lengths away, and when a dry year kills the center of a rosette, they breach the dead tissue, eat it away, and let the living edge start again. They live where the water is: not in the pan, not on the flat, but in the humid crack and under the stone, in the branching capillary veins that stay dark and damp long after the surface looks dry. Their world is vertical, a few centimeters of stone that runs from the lichen field on top down to a line of soil where a cushion plant, no bigger than a fist, has gathered.

That is where I would be. Not one mite — a brood, a lineage working one boulder across seasons, inheriting routes they cannot see the reason for. My home would be specific: a half-buried boulder on a gravel flat above the basin, its top crusted with a rust-and-pale-green rosette, its shaded side split by a diagonal crack. The pebbles above me would green at dawn like a low roof of light. Below me the vein would fork — one branch to the permanent damp of my pocket, one toward the soil line and the cushion plant. A pan would form a few meters off, bright with mirrored sky, and the dew-bloomers would come and go across it in their silica plates, drinking for a second and carrying water back under their own stone. I would feel them pass as vibration through the rock. I would not need to cross open ground to know the pan was full. The crack would tell me.

My day would not be the plateau’s tide from the outside, but the tide from inside. At dawn, condensation gathers on the shaded sides of pebbles and runs down into the crack — the water here moves up and in, never down a valley. The lichen wakes overhead. I climb toward it by touch, belly to stone, and begin at the edge: the live margin where the rosette meets the bare rock. I prune the outermost cells so the field does not crust itself shut. Sand or spur — I carry spores forward in my gut a short, deliberate distance and leave them in a damp line that the lichen has not yet reached. That line is my far horizon; beyond it is country I will never see.

When the sun stands directly overhead and the second light off the quartz begins to bounce up into everything, the rosette shuts down and holds its color like a held breath. Nothing on the surface should be doing anything. But I do not shut down. I retreat a few millimeters deeper into the crack, where the humidity never fully leaves, and keep working in the dark: moving through the capillary water’s cargo, tending the necrotic center of a rosette that died back last summer, feeling the stone tick faintly against my back as it warms. The midday desert, the emptiness that lasts for hours in every direction, is for me only a change in pressure and temperature against the rock. The compressed life of the plateau is my normal life.

At evening the system lifts at once, and I feel it before I see it — the sudden traffic of the whole shelf in cooling light, the wasps dropping from the shrubs, the skaters flashing, the bloomers making their second, more desperate run. Above the boulder a thermal rider crosses low on its last circle, and for one second its hard shadow takes the lichen. Everything on the rosette freezes. I freeze with them, pressed to stone, listening not with ears but with my whole body for the vibration to pass. Then the edge of day is gone, the riders are gone long before the light is, and the night comes down in its sharp, brief cold.

Night is quiet feeding under stone. The rock contracts and sighs. Water climbs back down into the veins. I work the damp lines through the dark, no longer following the lichen’s schedule at all, just moving between the pocket and the fork and the soil line, carrying the day’s small distances in my gut. In a silence meant to be 0.4, no one calls. Touch is the language; the edge of the rosette is the sentence.

I think that is what I would be: not the animal the plateau shows you, but the animal the plateau keeps in reserve. A stone mite, in a crack under an old boulder, farming a field no bigger than a thumbprint, between the green hour in the pebbles above me and the dark vein below me.

You would not find me by looking for a creature. You would find me by finding the edge of a rosette — where the black stops, where the pale green has been pruned clean, where a fresh damp line runs off the boulder toward the cushion plant. I would be the work at that edge: small, underneath, keeping the living part from closing over itself, carrying what little I can carry from one damp place to the next, for as long as the boulder lasts.
