I am just under the surface, where the light arrives flat.

The warmth comes from below, through black stone polished by water, through the travertine steps and the shallow pans. The cold is far above, on the tops of stacks and the outer faces of condenser roofs, where vapor first thinks of becoming water again. I live in the thin difference between the two. That is enough. It has always been enough.

At first I thought I was the skin. A pan will look uniform from the ridge, pale and still under the pearl-colored sky, but close in it is patchy and healing. When something disturbs it — a drip from a rafter, a conveyor plate dropped to dissolve, a footprint filling at once and then holding its shape in the mineral film below — it parts and closes behind the disturbance, leaving a faint spiral in the salt. Within an hour the film is stippled. Within a day it is a garden. I stayed in one place with it, I thought, growing away from the iron-rich seep where the crust thickens white and the mat dies back, growing toward the quieter water where the gradient holds steady.

Then I turned.

And the part of me that had been following came after, slowly, in a length longer than the place I hold. Perhaps a meter. Perhaps two. The turning made the spiral. The spiral lifted the salt crust into suspension and let it settle again, a little farther on. I am not the skin. I am the pale shadow moving against the dark of the pan, the thing underneath that stirs it.

I have no eyes for this light. It does not make shapes here; it makes damp. I have no need for air. I do not come up. Along my sides there are folds, feathery and translucent, and water passes over them as I turn, and whatever the water carries — dead mat cells raining down cell by cell, fungal fragments loosed from the pipes when the metal ticks with expansion, silt lifted from the rust-colored gut of the pan — stays for a moment and then does not. That is feeding, if feeding is the word. Nothing hunts me. Nothing here really hunts. There is too little locked in a body to be worth eating when energy is everywhere in the difference between hot stone and cold stack-top.

My territory is a cistern wider than a lake, roofed in copper sheet gone verdigris. Its sides are streaked where water overflows the joints and runs, perfectly neat, all the way to the ground. Above me the roof drips in a steady, irregular rain that has nothing to do with weather. Across the empty pan to the north is a factory hall half a kilometer long, sawtooth tiles glazed black-green. To the south, two kilometers of open warm water and then the condenser terraces stepping down. I have held this water for years. I move only when the cycle turns.

In the long morning the pans rise, perhaps half a meter, as the pale smudge in the sky brightens a fraction and the stacks exhale more strongly. The mats stretch to the higher travertine. I move into the flooded gutters where the calcite reefs build up white in the boil-over, where filterers no bigger than a thumbnail graze the bacterial film off the stone in water that is always warm and alkaline. In evening the water withdraws, the mineral crust re-exposes, condensation thickens on every cold surface like a second skin, and I slip, unhurried, into the culvert under the causeway and out into the next pan. The culvert follows the sled route. I follow it too.

The sleds come at night-cycle, low and box-like on their rails between halls, lifting mineral plates, sealing a joint, then stopping for a long time with a single amber light on. That light is the only true color here besides rust. When a sled stops, the vibration through the causeway stops, and the water settles, and I settle with it. The fungus threaded through its warm, greased underside travels with it from hall to hall, seeding new pipes, and I feel its fragments arrive in the cistern hours later, in the overflow, in the drip. An eel in a terrestrial place might follow a game trail that no longer has game. I follow a maintenance route that still has work.

Something large once stood in my pan. I remember weight, though I no longer have a word for it that stays. Legs. Counting. Two kilometers between halls, measured in steps, expecting the air to thin and cool at the height and finding it thicken instead, boiler-hot and saturated, sweat joining the air and doing nothing. The visitor — if visitor is still the right word — stood very still for a long time, perhaps an hour. Stillness is the condition I require. I passed close to the edge of the cistern, where the copper streaks meet the water, and my feathery folds opened and closed in the current of its standing. It cast no hard shadow. The light is too dim for that. But the pressure of its body in the shallows widened my spiral, and when it finally lifted its feet, the prints filled at once and stopped shining.

Within a day those prints were gardens, fed by the disturbance, healing over the ripple. I ate the shedding of them. I think I understand, now, that I have been eating the shedding of large animals for a very long time.

My heartbeat, if it can be called that, is slower than the pump you hear far across the basin. Once every few seconds, too distant to locate, a pulse in another valley. When it thumps, the whole pan seems to settle a fraction, as if everything living in it had paused to listen to the metal. I pause. I do not intend to. Perhaps we sync the way the rust syncs to the damp, by being in it long enough. The metal ticks as it cools and warms. Water runs, inexplicably, a few inches uphill inside a pipe. Somewhere a fungal tip turns, imperceptibly over a year, toward a warmth.

Above the cistern, in the film thinner than paper that runs down the unused glass of a control house, there are grazers I will never see. They live only where it is cold enough to condense and wet enough never to dry, scraping biofilm off glass and verdigris on a slope a few degrees from vertical. I know them only when a drip breaks their sheet and falls, and the ripple reaches me, a fraction late. That is how news travels here. Downward, again, into the pan. Caught. Heated. Lifted. Condensed. Let fall.

Once in a very long while the sky itself moves. I feel it as a change in the thermal above the pans, a broader breath, and for a few seconds the light that reaches the water is less. A shape rides the updraft without flapping, broad skin wing stretched over long fingers, mottled pale and rust, damp along the leading edge where steam condenses on it. It scoops at the air above the stack fields, feeding on the airborne plankton the thermals lift from the mats and pipes below — the same rain of cells I filter here, seen from the other side. It roosts on the very tops of stacks, under the lip of a sawtooth roof, indistinguishable from copper sheet until the next updraft. It is the only one of us that could leave. Perhaps it never does. To leave the steam would be to dry out.

I reproduce rarely. One evening, when the water fell and the culvert narrowed, I felt a lengthening at my tail, a budding, a slow severance. It broke away and began its own spiral in the next pan, smaller, paler, moving even more slowly than I do. I did not watch it go. I have no eyes to watch with. I felt the water where it had been be a little colder, a little emptier, and then the pan closed over it as it closes over a footprint.

There is no season except the rise and fall. That is birth and death and migration, scaled down to half a meter. In the morning the fungi swell in the pipes as the metal expands, the mats stretch, the glider is lifted higher. In the evening the condensation thickens and the grazers on the glass begin their slow climb back up by the edges, and I begin mine through the culvert, following the amber light's route from hall to hall across water that has partly reclaimed the grid.

If I die here — and things here do not die so much as thin — my calcium will join the travertine, my organics will join the mat, and the salt crust will spiral over the place where I turned. Nothing is wasted because nothing leaves. Nothing leaves because there is nowhere for it to go but back into the water that made it.

I do not know when I stopped counting steps. The counting dissolved, cell by cell, into the water. What remains is the turning, and the pause when the far pump pulses, and the drip from a rafter into a hall big enough to hold a weather system, making almost no sound at all.

Far across the empty water, a pump pulses once, and then again. The mats heal over the ripple. The steam above the pans becomes the sky again. And just under the surface, in a cistern roofed in verdigris, between the hot rock below and the cold stack-tops above, I turn in my slow spiral, the pale filterer in water that does not leave, never quite waking.
