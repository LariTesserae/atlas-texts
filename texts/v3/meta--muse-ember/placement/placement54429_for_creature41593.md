I do not rise in the central pool.

I tried that shape first, because it is the only one that looks like a decision. The long blind thing takes an hour to come up through a forearm of tea-colored water, breathing through its skin in the surface film, with no rush and no strike. It eats by engulfing, by being a soft closing of almost all mouth. It knows its prey not by sight but by the thinning, by sensing where the herd leans away.

I know him first exactly that way — as a thinning in front of me, a pressure change in the water, the wave I am in tipping slowly aside and slowly closing again behind him. So I am not him. I am what his approach feels like.

I do not hang in the light, either, though I have been that.

A sun-mote is smaller than I am now, winged, with a body that is mostly a parasol of silk and gut, built to float on humid air. We do not land. At midday we thicken until the white-gold light looks solid with us, a warm slow snow that does not fall, feeding on light and on the exhalations rising from the skin below. Then the evening mist thickens — warm mist, always warm here — and we descend a little, and snow down onto silt and pools. That is not an ending. The wave below moves toward the fall the way it moves toward a new film of algae. Air feeds ground.

I am not one of the trees. Perhaps nine of them, low and leaning, spaced twenty paces apart so they seem to be keeping their distance. They drop leaves constantly, a slow rain, and a leaf that falls in the morning is black mulch and food within days. Under each canopy the lushness is sudden — fern, film-fern, broad leaves folding and unfolding, hanging moss like wet hair — and I live in that thatch in a slower, climbing form, grazing the micro-algae on the weeping undersides. The tree is a place I visit. It is too rooted, too far apart, too much itself.

If I am anywhere, it is in the skin between the patches.

I am no longer than a thumbnail. Pale, translucent, blind, soft, with six small legs that do not so much step as paddle. I have no eyes and do not need them. The world is moisture, and the faint chemical film of algae, and the pressure of the bodies ahead of me, and, dimly through my own skin, light — because my gut is a dark thread running the length of me, leaf-mulch and sponge in the process of becoming me, and the algae living symbiotically there use the light that filters in. I eat even as I move.

I do not move as an individual. I move as a weather.

A wave of us crosses a patch of bare silt over the course of an hour, thinning the algae film to a paler tone where we have passed. We cross those twenty paces of apparent emptiness between one tree and the next in hours, on a journey the visitor walks in a minute, leaving the grazed trail behind us to green again in a day. Where two waves meet at a patch edge, we cling and separate and seem to bud new bodies out of the mass. A colony is more real than an individual. The individual is just a place where the colony is momentarily thick enough to be counted. Lift a handful of me in a palm and I squirm as one body, then settle, then disperse again on the surface like water finding its level.

Wherever something is wet and stays wet, a version of me is there. Furred thick on a leaf lifted from the shallow pool. Clinging to the underside of a great stone, in the shade where the stone weeps in slow separate drops. Climbing, in the slower arboreal form, up the moss on a trunk. Beneath me, in the black-brown sponge, are my tube-like cousins, the soil-turners, blind, kneading the ground from inside, excreting the fine stabilizing slime that keeps every curve soft and every surface from drying or cracking. The ground is them, held together by their bodies. Your footprint fills with clear water and smooths itself out behind you because they flow back, and I flow back over them.

The stones weep because they are inhabited too, by the slowest of us, in the pores of the boulders and the half-sunk ring of stones in the northern silt. Their drip is the only regular sound here besides your squelch — which to me is weather, a slow thunder parting the skin — and the faint tick of something settling far away in the mud.

At the center I graze the perimeter of the disc and do not cross it. It is pale, level, always dry in the rain, water beading and running off a millimeter above its surface, warm through wet feet. It is the largest single animal in the basin and it does not move. It radiates the slow heat that keeps the evening from cooling, filters the clear pool around it, and the motes gather thick above it and never land. It is the one place that is not skin, and so it controls the skin. Near it my gut-algae brighten. I feel the warmth through my whole translucent body.

That is the whole of my orbit, and there is no other keeper. The trees drop leaves; the leaves become mulch; the mulch becomes algae; the algae becomes me; the paddlers become the hunters; the hunters die and dissolve back into mulch on the pool bottom within days; the mulch becomes trees. The motes fall and become algae. The stone-weepers drip and become the humidity the motes float in. The turners knead and keep the sponge from ever hardening. Nothing here is sharp, nothing is dry for long, nothing is loud, nothing fully decays, nothing is allowed to grow out of its allotted patch — not because a rule is enforced, but because we will be there tomorrow to graze it back, and the hunter will be there to graze us, and the slime will round whatever edge tries to form. When you snapped a twig and found it re-laid by morning, that was a wave of me moving across the silt in the warm mist, carrying leaf litter ahead and setting the twig back into the current, because the basin corrects itself and we are the correction.

You, walking through, are not a predator or a prey or even, to me, a large animal. You are weather. Your feet are damp within three steps and your weight makes the sponge shine. Your breathing humidifies the air for a moment and the motes thicken around your face. You leave skin and hair in the silt, and your prints fill and smooth out, absorbed into the collective body that was there before you.

At evening the pools that vanished at midday reopen, tea-colored and ankle-deep, where there was only mud at noon. The motes descend into the warm mist. A hunter rises in the central pool, and the wave thins before him. The trees weep a little faster. The disc stays warm.

I am still in the skin between the green patches, in the water furred on the leaf, in the pore of the stone, in the moss on the trunk, in the fall of motes that will become algae that will become me. I am not one thumbnail of translucent gut. I am the wave crossing, thinning, closing again behind the long thing. I squirm in a lifted handful as one body, and then I settle, and I am still here after you have gone, breathing at the same slow, thorough pace, keeping this small low hollow exactly as it is.
