I put my hand flat on the basalt to climb, and the stone cuts before it holds.

That is the first thing I know about myself here: a hand. Not yet a person, not yet anything with a place to go back to. The steps are slick from the cliff's own sheen, water beading out of the fracture planes and joining in a sheet that darkens the pale faces black. It beads on my knuckles too, though I am not sweating. The air is warm and thin and too still for the damp to leave me. I breathe shallower as I go up, the way the climb asks. Behind me the drip keeps falling into its basin at the foot of the rock, and far below, beyond hearing almost, the thread of water goes over the edge to the plain.

When I reach the top it opens, and I do not know where to put myself.

I stand in the meadow — knee-high grass, fern in the hollows, pools shining between the hummocks — and I do the thing an animal does without thinking: I stand still and wait to be noticed. No fly comes to my face. No bee works the pale, unscented flowers along the channel. No bird crosses the sky that is so close and so ordinary above us. The lushness is complete and nothing eats it, nothing sings over it, nothing lands. After a while the silence stops feeling like an absence waiting to be filled. It feels like a condition, like the water sitting level in the ditches month upon month. It teaches me, standing there, that I am the only body here that is out of balance — breathing too fast, too warm, mist ghosting off my own arms.

I walk down onto the causeway, wide enough for two, flat basalt slabs laid by hand across the marsh. The people are already there at walking pace, bare feet on wet stone, their prints filling at once with level water. Mine fill too. For the first time I am equal to something. They speak low. Their skin is damp without sweating; condensation stands on their forearms where the mist has not lifted from the hollows. In the mist my own arm beads the same way. I am becoming damp not by effort but by being here. I slow my chest to match theirs. It is a small, animal thing to do, and here it is the whole of belonging: if I stay, I will count as one of the only animals on the shelf, and I will have to learn to be less of one. Slow. Shallow-breathing. Fed on roots boiled soft, greens from the pool edges, grain porridge, fruit set by a brush, the sour ferment working in the pits and in the gut. No meat, no milk, no honey, no dog begging in the yard. Nothing to bite me out of the pools, nothing to run from in the sharp gullies. A long life at walking pace, made of starch and green and doing the same rounds.

I do not go into a house. A house would give me fifty metres of wet green to the next household and make me a parcel of distance, tending my own thatch. I find myself instead on the lines.

The straight lines cut across the curve of the land: channels from the weeping cliff, low walls terracing the meadows to hold the water at a constant depth, causeways crossing and recrossing, small arched bridges set stone by stone. I am on a bridge. I am on a causeway between a furrowed field and a pool. I am where the hand-dug straightness meets the patchiness of the ground. That is where I keep arriving, however I start out.

At first I tried on other shapes for myself, the way I tried the cliff-step with my hand.

I could be a pollinator. I watched one in the morning, a young person with a brush of soft fiber, tree to tree, flower to flower, a touch and a pause, teaching a child how to tell a flower that has been done from one that has not. There is a patience in that for which I am suited — repetition without hunger, carefulness without a sting, no nectar to want because there is no bee. But the brush is only one round. Pollination, then channels, then thatch, then weaving: the calendar here is not seasons, it is rounds, and I did not want to be only the flower-hand.

I could be the water in the plaza basin, full to the same mark in living memory, plashing softly from its pipe, read each morning like a familiar face. If I were that water I would know the whole world by a level that never moves. I could be the stone-cutter, going hand over hand down the slick steps to cut more of the cliff that is giving itself away, carrying it back up so a wall looks perpetually new and perpetually the same. I could be the moss, the softest thing in this sharp place, drinking the mist before it can drip, giving a little under the causeway slabs, thickening in the hollows, lining the channels, crowding the pool edges leaf upon leaf because no fish browses it and no frog sits among it, thinned only by hand.

All of those shapes tempt because they stay. Moss stays. Water stays at its mark. Stone stays new. But I keep moving, and I keep coming back to the hands that do not let the staying things close over.

That is where I begin to become more myself: not in what I am made of, but in the small work of keeping what is here from failing. The hand that gripped the basalt to climb is, by the middle of the day, in the channel water, redigging with a stone spade where the moss has thickened, clearing the clay pipe under the causeway, checking the low wall that holds the terrace level. The same hand pulls the water plants from the pool edge into a woven basket — shoots for the pot, the rest for the root pits — because nothing else will prune them. It brushes the fruit blossom and does not bruise the saturated petal. It mends thatch patch by patch before it can fail, because nothing here is allowed to fail: no insect in the thatch, no moth in the cloth, no storm to tear it, only the slow damp darkening the timber to a polish.

I am not the cliff, though the cliff weeps through me when I climb. I am not the mist, though I return with it to the same hollows in the evening and bead on the walls. I am the interval between the water finding its way out of the rock and the plants taking it in — the tending of the space between the two. That was the hand, all along. The town is made by hand because there is nothing else to do it, and I am the hand learning its rounds slowly enough that a footprint fills before I have taken the next step.

By evening I am still on the line between the plaza and the fields, basket in hand, going at walking pace over a bridge set stone by stone. From the plaza, looking west, you would see me as part of the pattern: lines and pools and roofs scattered far apart across the open green table, ridges sharp and young behind it all, the high plain unbroken the other way. You would hear a drop into a basin, a footstep on wet slab, the faint continuous thread of water falling far away off the edge. The mark has not moved. The moss has given a little under my feet and will need the spade again tomorrow. The flowers will be waiting for the brush.

That is enough. I do not resolve into a house, or a blossom, or the level water. I resolve into keeping the level — damp, slow, speaking low, carrying water in a basket from a rock that has not had time to soften, in a place that has had all the time in the world to learn how to stay exactly where it is.
