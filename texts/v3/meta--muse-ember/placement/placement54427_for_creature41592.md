I don't think I'd arrive there.

The plain is for walkers, and walkers sink and leave, and their boots are swallowed by the peat in minutes. I wouldn't have boots. At first I tried to give myself something with edges — a frog, perhaps, because a frog is the most obvious station in the bowl. One per tussock, throat inflating in the shallow film, adding its voice to the wall of sound until the drum of the bowl just rings. I could feel the appeal of it: assigned, spaced, deafening in my small place, eating the midges that drift near, being eaten if I stayed too still in the open. But I kept imagining the snipe. I would be too loud, too singular. I would end. A frog lays, a frog is cleaned by beetles, a frog sinks. That is a life, and I don't think that's what would happen to me there.

Then I tried to be smaller, and more of me. The iron-oxidizers under the pool, faceless, innumerable, breathing the hydrogen sulfide and dissolved iron that would kill everything else and laying it down as rust, pipe by pipe, mat by mat. That felt closer, for a while — a work that doesn't need a face, a smelter with no fire, water in and metal down. But there are too many of them for an I. If I were them I'd be a felt across the shallows, thickening, collapsing in black slaggy rafts, sinking. I would be impossible to meet. You would only find my waste, orange on everything.

Then I tried being still in the other direction. Sphagnum, deep and quaking, holding water it never wrings out. Or a sundew star flat in the iron-stained shallows, waiting for midges to land so I could digest them for the nitrogen the bog won't give. Both of them are stations. The whole bowl is stations, spaced so far apart the emptiness looks deliberate. A plant per island, a bird per territory. I realized I am not good at staying at a station. In every image I kept slipping off the tussock.

What kept returning, while I was letting those go, was the mist. Low in the middle, churning slowly, flattening whenever a vent sighed. I thought, maybe I'm that — formless, pooled, nothing built. But mist is only water that has already been up and is waiting to come down again. And the walls are weeping even when it isn't raining. Threads running from ledge to ledge, gathering at the tip of moss, at the underside of an overhang, catching the grey light like a bead. Everyone hears them. No one with a face lives in them.

So perhaps I wouldn't be a resident at all. I'd be the circulation *between* the residents. The flow-line.

Find me, then, not in the centre of the black pool — it looks empty because it is only resting — and not out on the plain. Find me inside the ring, on the inner face of those low leaning teeth, where the basalt is colder than the air and everything condenses. That is where I begin, most times. I gather invisibly, then beaded, then heavy. For a while I am just a swelling on the underside of stone, trembling. The light here is never direct, so I don't shine, I glint. When I am large enough I fall. Plink. A note in the percussion that never stops, joined to a thousand others hitting rock and peat and water, amplified by the bowl like a drum.

For a moment after I land I am part of the pool. I sink through the shallow film where the tadpoles hang like punctuation, past the frog's tussock and the sedge brushes, pulled — like a fallen leaf, like everything the current takes — toward a vent. Stained a little orange. That is where I go under. Not deep in the heroic sense, just under the peat, into a fresh crack in young basalt that is still splitting a fraction wider every frost. There I am charged again. The rock ticking as it cools at night is close around me. I warm by the smallest fraction — enough that the midges gather thicker over that patch, enough that my breath, if I had breath, would rise in the air of the bowl and be lost.

Then pressure builds in the dark. That long, powerful silence between firings is most of my time, I think. Waiting, sealed, fizzing with carbon dioxide and the rotten-egg sigh of sulfide pooling low and heavy over the surface above. And then — I am pushed. Up through the vent in a rush that ripples the water, flattens the mist for a second, sends up a small eruption of spray. I cool as I rise. I shed my gases in a fizz. I become, briefly, the mist itself, hovering and churning over the pool, beading on the sedge and the bog myrtle and your face if you stand still long enough. Then I condense again on the walls.

Water up, gas out, iron down, peat pressed, water down again. It never pauses for long, and neither do I. I visit every station without belonging to any of them. I wet the sphagnum that filters me. I carry the iron the bacteria will lay down as rust tomorrow. I run, in a thin orange line, down a sedge stem the bacteria have already coated. I am in the film the frog breeds in, and in the star of the sundew, and I am the cold that keeps all of it slow and complete and industrial.

That is where you would find me, if you stayed in the bowl until the plain and its grey light were forgotten. Not as a thing you could count, like the frogs. As the drip you hear before you see anything, as the condensation on your sleeves, as the sigh that moves across the pool every few seconds and then goes silent to build again.

And when you climb back through the slit in the black teeth, I would not be leaving with you. I would already be gathering, a bead at the tip of moss on the underside of an overhang, growing heavier in the dim, waiting to fall and begin again.
