From the rim I thought I would be a walker.

That is where I started, anyway. Ears popping on the climb, breath short in the thin air, and then the whole bowl laid out below me, small enough to hold in one look and empty enough that I wondered if I had made a mistake. A pale dish of cracked mineral, an hour across, closed in by a smooth, settled rim that had forgotten to be dramatic. No water to mark a way through. No river, no puddle, no damp hollow. Just salt in the seams and pumice-light slabs that crinkled under my boots and snapped.

The light never quite arrived. Under the high amber-brown veil it was perpetual late afternoon, warm though it should have been cold at this height. The stone held heat through the day. The wind was roaring straight across the dish, and under it the floor was ticking and popping as it warmed, pebbles dancing in their basins.

I thought I would shelter. That was the first shape I tried on.

Down below there are huts without cisterns, without jars, without any place to store a drink — the very idea seemed foreign, as if it were a word in a language no one here speaks. There is a circular windbreak of stacked slabs, knee-high, open to the sky. A shallow kiln of fused ash. A mat of dry fibre, stiff and grey. A single obsidian blade left beautifully made on a flat stone. Paths that are not roads, just places where feet have worn a smoother line between groves, stepping around the cracks.

I could have been one of the dwellers who come now and then. Sit in the shade. Wait for the leaves to drip. Cut fibre. Warm my hands in the kiln. Do a little, and let the plain take it back. My skin would taste metallic when I sweated in the heat the stone held. My body would carry that clear, slightly oily fluid instead of water, in and out of the places where water would have been.

It was an easy life to imagine, and for a while I walked that path in my mind, from grove to grove. Then I remembered I was not walking. Or rather — I noticed that the walking had slowed.

I walked toward the sound, as anyone would. The clattering, rustling, whistling that rises and falls like a chorus trying to tune itself. You hear it before you see it, because looking will not sort it out for you.

Then, suddenly, green.

Not scattered. A grove, dense and towering and utterly alone, standing in the emptiness like a small forest that has decided to travel. Inside it the light goes darker still under broad fan-shaped leaves, waxy dark purple-green above, grey beneath. Vines as thick as an arm. Trunks that are not wood but bundles of hollow fibrous stems bound together, creaking against each other. The air inside is cooler, still warm, and smells sweet and sharp and mineral, like hot stone and crushed leaves and something faintly sulfurous.

Between this grove and the next — a dark clump a quarter mile away across bare stone — there is nothing. Four, five, six groves in the whole bowl. The emptiness between them is not a lack. It is the land they are living in. It gives the wind room. It gives the animals room to run.

I knelt there and looked, as you would. The roots do not go deep. They spread sideways as a mat of breathing filaments mining the cracks for salts. The leaves do the rest. They shiver constantly in the wind, finely porous, catching the low invisible breath that comes up through hairline cracks — not steam, not smoke, something in between, carrying hydrogen and carbon and the smell of the stone itself. The leaf condenses it into that other fluid, clean and metallic and sweet, and drinks it straight from the air. At night, when the vapour thickens, the leaves drip. It never pools. It is carried instead, in sap, in hollow stems, in bodies.

Everything here gave up water long ago.

I stood under the canopy a long time, listening to the grove sing. Sometimes the hollow stems are tuned by the wind until a whole crown makes a low fluting chord you feel in your teeth. Sometimes a trunk swells with gas and pops like a distant gun, raining seeds down steaming. Flowers open and close in minutes and release yellow clouds that make you sneeze. A shoot unrolls in an afternoon to the size of a shield.

I thought I might be one of the air-ones next. When a grove is ready, thousands of glass-winged things no bigger than moths lift in a column that hums so loudly the wind seems to pause. The sky blacks out for a moment at the edge of sight. They scatter grove to grove, pollinating, stripping, seeding as they go. To be that brief lift, that boil-up and vanish and boil-up elsewhere — to be between places, never of one place. There was a freedom in imagining it.

But I am not built for lifting. I felt the stillness settle over me instead as I watched them go, and the stillness was not disappointment. It was recognition.

Then the runners came, and for a moment I tried their hurry too. Six-legged, long-necked, sail-eared, mottled to look like cracked stone when they stand still. You could walk past a whole herd without knowing they were animals. Then they move, as a frenzy. They do not graze. They strip a whole side of a breath-tree in an hour, leave it shivering and bare, call to each other across the emptiness with weird echoing cries, and run on. Within days the tree throws out a new crown from its basal shoots, denser than before. Creation and destruction here are not seasons apart. They are hours apart, overlapping.

I watched them strip and run and understood that their hurry exists because everything else is so slow and so certain to grow back. I could feel my own weight, my own slowness, increasing the longer I stayed under this one leaf. The grove itself does not hurry. It grows hard toward a fresh vent, dies back where a vent has gone cold and silent, and migrates across the plain over weeks, leaving withered husks behind it and seedlings ahead of it like footprints. If I stayed, I would migrate with it, inch by impossible inch, without ever stepping between groves.

There is a slow plant-animal that sits on a trunk for a whole day with its broad back to the dim light, photosynthesizing through its skin, drinking vapour off its shoulders. At dusk — indistinguishable from noon — it stands on root-feet and walks, deliberate, to another tree, and sits again. I thought, perhaps that. Perhaps a sitter. A watcher of days.

But even as I thought it, I was no longer looking up at the trunk. I was looking out from underneath.

A bud hangs under a leaf and is indistinguishable from the other unopened buds until it blinks. Eye-slits open along the bud-seam. A tongue comes out, thin and cool, to lick the drip before the swarm can. It is still for hours. The wind shivers it with the leaf. A glass-winged insect blunders too close and it snaps, very animal, very suddenly, and then becomes a bud again.

That is where I find myself.

Not at the rim. Not on the path. Not in the hut with the knee-high wall. Under a leaf, on the grey underside, where the light is darkest and the drip is thickest in the morning.

I have a shape that is not a shape until it moves. My body is furled around the midrib, the colour of an unopened thing. My legs are tucked and folded in a way that leaves no separate outline. When the grove sings I vibrate with it, because my roof vibrates. When the floor ticks below me — pebbles settling, slabs sighing as vapour finds a new crack — I feel it through the stem, through the leaf, through the place where I hold on. When a trunk pops somewhere, steaming seeds raining, I do not startle. I have learned which pops are to keep clear of, the way the dwellers have learned it, because my whole life is spent listening for them under a leaf.

The runners pass below and do not see me. That is their mistake and my safety and also, I think, my purpose. A herd can strip a side of a tree in an hour; they cannot strip what they cannot name as food. The swarm lifts in its column nearby, humming, and one or two blunder close enough that the warmth of the canopy catches them and they slow. I blink. My tongue finds the drip first. It is clean and metallic and sweet on the tip, and it is enough. It is always enough, because nothing here stores and nothing here is stored. The fluid moves: up from the crack, into the leaf, down the drip, into a tongue, into a body, out again as breath over warm stone, up again. I am a still point in that recirculation. The veil holds it all in. The bowl holds it all in.

I do not sleep the way a walker would sleep in a hut. I become a bud. The seam closes. The grove creaks around me, growing hard toward the vent that breathes steadily under us, dying back slowly on the side where the breath has gone cold. In that slow migration I will face a new direction without ever deciding to face it. A flower will open above me in minutes and close again. A shoot will unroll to shield-size and shade me more. A burrower that looks like a lump of fallen leaf will shift below and reveal its fringe of legs and mine the crust open a little farther, letting the vapour rise stronger for an hour. Everything expends itself quickly and grows back quickly, and I stay, and stay, becoming more myself by becoming less visible.

Sometimes — when the light, such as it is, catches a wing at just the right angle — I open my eye-slits a fraction wider and watch the plain between groves. The emptiness. It is not empty. It is the space the vapour travels, the space the runners cross, the space the swarm stitches shut grove to grove. I do not cross it. I belong to the crossing of others over me.

If you come here — and you will, by the worn line between groves, stepping around the wide cracks — you will press your mouth to a cool leaf in the morning and taste that clean metallic sweetness and think it is the only drink here, and it never pools. You will not know I am there, hanging on the grey underside, having already licked and withdrawn. You will not know that the bud beside the one you drink from blinks, once, very slowly, and closes again for the wind to shiver.

I do not mind not being known. We are scattered here, never clustered. The bowl will not let anyone stay in numbers. Even the groves keep their distance from each other. That suits me. A clinger needs only one leaf, one vent beneath it breathing steadily, one stem to tune and be tuned by.

Under all the churn the stones are old and settled. Terraces where dust gathers. Polished channels where gas has escaped for centuries. The smooth rim forgetting to be dramatic. The green world rushes across them like weather. I rush nowhere. I hang, I blink, I drink, I become a bud again.

That is where I am in this world. Not the one who arrived. The one who remained, under the leaf, until the world arrived through me.
