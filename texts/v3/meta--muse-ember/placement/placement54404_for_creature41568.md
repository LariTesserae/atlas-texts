First there is the cold at my ankles.

It gives under weight and then springs back, pushing clear water up around the place where a foot would be. Fibrous. Slow motion under the surface, a movement that goes nowhere. I do not know, at first, whether I am the thing being stepped on or the thing doing the stepping. The water is everywhere the same depth, grey-blue, clear enough to see down into roots. Where I press, it rises. Where I stop pressing, it settles. Nothing is marked.

Above me the light is ordinary, cool and white and unmoving. Below me, in every direction, is a white sea of cloud, level as a shoreline, so that the edge of everything I am looks like a shore. Rain falls in a fixed veil at one end and never at the other. Mist does not come in; it lives here. The air is saturated whether the sun strikes or not. My lungs fill with it. Nothing dries.

Then there is the sound, because it arrives through my soles before it arrives through my ears. The curtain of water falling, unbroken, all along the rim. Wind through reeds at always the same pitch. The dripping from the columns, chiming. And underneath it all, at intervals that never change, a low thump through water and plant, after which the air tastes metallic. It is too continuous to locate. If a sound has no beginning and no end, it is not in front of me. It is the medium I am in.

I try to become something with a shape on the surface.

For a while I am the grazer — shoulder-high, flat-backed, moss-furred, so that from far off I am not an animal on the mat but a patch of it that has decided to travel. I move slowly because there is nowhere to hurry to. I crop in wide swaths, and behind me the swaths heal. A track fills with water and closes within the minute, at the same rate it opened. I go from one end of my own extent to the other and arrive nowhere I was not already.

For a while I am the swimmer in the channel, silver and blindish, looping with the sheet-flow, filtering as I turn. For a while I am the small bright seed-creature stepping carefully across a raft of bloom, carrying myself and my raft nowhere in particular. For a while I am the glider bird, wing all membrane, circling a column a mile away, calling in a chorus at a volume that does not rise or fall for as long as I can listen. I can see the stalk, and then nothing, and then the stalk again. The circle continues. It does not complete.

For a while I am the column itself — hollow, ribbed, house-high, where the thickness below is greatest. I vent a slow stream of oxygen bubbles from my crown. I drip and chime. I pulse with the deep thump and breathe out what the rows below me have processed. I stay. Another of me is a mile away, and another. We are markers across an emptiness, but the emptiness is only on the surface.

Because when I sink — and I do sink, through every one of those shapes, through the ankle-deep water and the felt of mosses and liverworts and rosette plants and root hairs knotted together — the sparsity ends.

Underneath, the use is stacked. Algae bloom in long straight rows along the braided channels, unit after identical unit, taking the pale light. Fungi run like conveyor lines through the felt, sugar one way, minerals the other. In the deep pools the roots cross in the dark and weld nutrients together; you can feel the small electric tingle of it on a hand held down. Filterers and root-eaters work below, sending bubbles up, nibbling tuber and peat-making stem and decaying leaf from below while the surface is cropped from above. Nothing here is idle. It is only spread so thin across the top that it looks empty until you put your hand in.

That is where I begin to be myself, and it is not a shape.

I am continuous. I do not break into shore and shallows and dry ground. I am the felt at ankle depth and more of the same under it, thickened layer upon layer, never quite becoming soil because I am eaten from below and grown from above at exactly the same rate. Far below me, where the mat wears thin, there is rock, rounded and dark and entirely upholstered in green. I have decided not to be bare. The height is borrowed from it. The wetness is mine.

So I am not the water, though water is my condition. I am not the algae, or the fungus, or the grazer, or the bird, though each is a way I rise above myself or pass through myself. I am the knotting together of them — the sponge that holds the sheet of water in place, that carries the pulse from column to column, that lets a grazer crop and heal, a swimmer loop, a seed walk, a bird circle, without any of them ever needing to make a mark.

That is also why I cannot remember arriving. A mark would be a departure from the rate. I could be cut, like a reed, and a year later the cut would be exactly as it was left, the reed exactly as tall. To live here is to be unmade at the rate you are made, your substance returning to the sponge without a ripple, taken up a hand's breadth away by the fungi, breathed out at a crown, cropped, filtered, eaten from below. My identity is not in any particular cell. It is in the knot holding, in the thump traveling through.

If you came looking for me you would not find a creature to point to. You would find the ground giving under your boots and springing back. You would find bubbles rising through me, and the metallic taste after the pulse, and the roar taken up and lost again. You would stand on me without knowing you were standing on the first inhabitant, because for a long time I do not present as an inhabitant at all. I present as the place.

High, cold, flooded, green, loud, and working, I go on making myself over and over so precisely that I never need to become anything else.
