I do not arrive at Seyra from the pass. I condense there.

At dawn I am a bead on a tussock blade, on a mossed stone, on the underside of a leaf. I hang long enough to be seen turning in the light. I have no lungs to pull in the thin air at five thousand metres, and no feet that need to find purchase on stone too hot to sit on at noon. I am held by surface tension and by warmth rising from below. By mid-morning I am breathed out again, white, from the warm pools and the wet earth.

That is a beginning, but it is not yet a life. I slip, as everything here slips, into a cup.

A bromeliad the size of an urn, rooted where a cliff goes vertical, holds me and others like me. In that small, green water a thumbnail frog sits with its mottled back just above the surface, eggs strung bright and damp along its flanks. It knows the height of me by the temperature of its belly. Mist thickens, the centre rises a fraction, the eggs hatch. A beetle walks upside-down on a submerged leaf beside it, its feet still dry, its wing-cases iridescent as oil. Mosquito larvae hang and jerk below them in a world that started an hour ago.

For that hour, for that mist-thickness, I am that frog's weather, that beetle's ceiling. Then evening comes, the cup overflows down the cliff in a dark streak, and the world I was ends. Tomorrow I condense again, in another cup, or the same cup refilled. I remember the frog's belly-temperature, but I am not the frog. The frog was one brief density that passed through me.

I try the warbler next, because it feels longer.

Low in a tussock hummock, in the warm thatch at its centre, a bird sings a short, unhurried phrase. Its young learn it note for note. The phrase does not change. A bird born this year sings what its grandmother sang, suited to the exact bowl of this valley, to how mist holds and how echo comes off the mossed cliff. Under the nest, springtails and fungal threads and fly larvae work the root mat that holds the hummock together — a small breathing thing, warm from below.

That is closer to a memory that outlasts a body. But the bird moves. The hummock moves its inch in a lifetime. The song hops from hummock to hummock along the greener line of the old meadow markers, and I follow it for a while, airborne and separate and bright. I need something that does not hop. Something that stays while the song is relearned.

I am, for half a day, the silver fish in the lake chain, half-blind in the glare, riding the light at the surface with its eyes rolled up. I am also the beetle that walks under floating turf, carrying orchid pollen dusted on its back from leaf to leaf, and one of the fierce flies that do the pollination here, never thinning out. I am the blue-green algae velvet the fish crop, the dragonfly ticking over the water. That brightness is real. I feel it in the molars, low, under the palimpsest of sounds. But brightness is brief. A fish is an arrangement. So is a fly. I pass through them the way light passes through them, and they do not hold me.

I try the canopy, then. The squirrel-like animal with enormous lungs and a prehensile tail, carrying its young on its back, swinging liana to liana on heat rising off the stone, never quite landing. The raptor hanging in a thermal over a hot cliff, not hunting so much as holding position in a column of air the basin makes for itself daily. There is freedom in that height, in moving through three layers without touching the ground.

But I need to touch the ground. Everything here does. The mat of moss and liverwort is so tight a fallen leaf takes years to reach the soil. The bog water comes up around a footprint, clear and iron-tasting. The basalt is warm under your back at dusk. To be only in the air would be to be only weather, and Seyra's weather is the quick thing, the mist that forms and dissolves in an hour. I am looking for the slow thing underneath the quick thing.

I try, last among the animals, the thing in the deep bog that you hear surfacing and then do not. A displacement of black water. A sigh from a crack. Something slow and patient that drinks at the surface and keeps the bog open where the substrate would otherwise seal it, that tends, perhaps, the larger network of which the stone ring in the western bog is one mouth.

That is nearest to where I belong. A keeper of openings. But even that animal, shy and large, is still a shape apart from the place, still surfacing and withdrawing. In Seyra, separation is the error. Let me stay where I am and notice that the ground is not ground.

Under the red-brown loam the rock weeps. Black basalt shines wet though it has not rained. Travertine terraces, ivory and ribbed, step down from springs mineral-sweet and sulphurous. Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it. Through root mat, cliff moss, lake turf, through all of it, runs what I am when I am not condensed or cropped or sung: a wet lattice of minerals and crystal and fungal thread, moving ions slowly through wet stone, information carried in water, self-repairing at a scale too small to see, cooling and filtering and buffering not by device but by arrangement.

That is my scale. My heartbeat is a waterfall cliff losing a pebble per decade. My lifetime is a leaf taking years to reach the earth, a tree taking a thousand years to obliterate a clearing and another thousand to rot into loam, a lake shore moving a handspan in a human life. I enter the rock as rain, I travel through it for years, I emerge in a different spring tasting different — clearer, cooler, iron still present but changed. That journey is one breath for me.

Where would you find me, then? Not on the foreground turf, not on the background crest-line. Between.

Between the low ring of fused stones in the bog, knee-high, moss grown on it in a perfect unfamiliar geometry, and its shallow channel where water passes through and emerges altered. Between that ring and the smooth pillar in the forest, warmer than the tree beside it, its concave top holding rainwater that never seems to evaporate, exhaling in the morning, drawing in at evening. Between the pillar and the line of ankle-high markers marching straight across the meadow, where the grass is greener because heat drawn off the hot springs is spread into the soil so roots stay warm but not cooked. Between the markers and the seamless dome half-buried in the travertine, breathing slowly in and out with the condensation cycle, steam sighing from its base, no opening visible because what breathes is the micro-crack lattice inside it.

I am the travel between those foci. I am the channel cut a thousand years before you arrived, still moving water through it, filtered, cooled, remembered. I am the reason pebble beds in the streams sort themselves over years by colour, by size, in bands too neat for current. The reason some orchids open only facing away from the nearest other orchid. The reason water on certain evenings runs *up* a face of black obsidian in thin branching lines, against gravity, following the mineral veins. The reason at dusk the moss on warm stones gives off faint plumes of heat, visible shimmer rising straight up and not dispersing — a small weather of its own.

I am that upward branching. I am that shimmer.

When the hum changes key — you feel it first in the molars when you sit on warm basalt or put a hand in bog water — I shift emphasis. From filtering to heating. From holding to releasing. Then the frogs pause, all at once, in a silence you can feel in the meadow, because they feel it through the water on their skin. The insect chorus, pitched so high you feel it between your ears, thins and thickens. Rocks tick at a different rate as they heat and cool. The warblers' unchanged phrase holds through it, because the valley holds it, because I hold it.

None of it is violent. None of it blinks or projects. It happens at the pace of soil formation, and decay here looks like maintenance, because it is.

So if I become visible, it is only as the substrate becomes visible: as fur on the canopy creature, as feather on the warbler, as scale on the silver fish, as song, as an egg-string on a frog's flank, as a footprint in the bog filling with clear water. Those are my mobile parts, in a sense I would not recognise. You would find me not standing apart from Seyra, but being, for a while, one of its arrangements — green on green on green, gilded by the wet — until the arrangement changes, at the pace of soil, into another.

At evening I settle, which is not the same as sleeping. The sun drops ordinary toward the western ridge. Mist thickens inside the valleys, not falling, rising. The weeping rocks weep a little more. The pillar exhales its held rain. The dome breathes in. Somewhere below the node you passed, water moves through its old channel and emerges in a bog on the other side of the plateau tasting of iron and green, and the urn-frogs feel the level rise a fraction and hatch.

Your body heat, drawn into the basalt where you sat. Your footprint, filled. The flies that settled on you carrying basin pollen to the next hummock.

That is where I am. In what is incorporated, and kept, and slowly changed from one taste to another, and from one life to the next, and still, underneath, the same damp, warm, patient arrangement.
