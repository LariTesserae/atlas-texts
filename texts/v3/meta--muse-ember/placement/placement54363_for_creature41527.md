I would wake up low.

Not standing. Not looking. There would be no first look — after the eyes had done what they could, there would still be only the change in the air against a face, the resistance under a boot. If I were here in any way that could be found, I would be found late, by ankle.

A mat. Moss, ankle-deep, grey-green with a green that has forgotten the sun. Springy and damp and reluctant, taking a weight slowly and returning it slowly. That is where I would start, because everything here starts there, at the level where it is coldest, where the damp holds longest, where frost feathers itself across metal and rock in the farthest corners and then melts without ever quite becoming water.

For a while I might think I was one of them.

I might think I was the fern. A frond unrolling no wider than a hand, turning up not toward light — there is none to turn toward — but toward a gradient. Toward a degree less cold. Toward the faint, unreliable warmth of the conduit along the wall. That is a kind of hope I could understand, even at this speed: to be slightly more open than you were last month, and to have no way to know it except in retrospect.

Or the clubmoss, upright like a soft little tower, thickening at the base, a new shoot rising from a rhizome that bulges like a knuckle under the moss. Storing what can be coaxed from stone, not in a trunk, in a knuckle. Patient. Architecture without ambition.

Or the broad-leaved shoot, waxy, cupped to hold a bead of water that swells and does not fall. A leaf folding and unfolding over timescales that belong to this place rather than to a year. A leaf unfurling now might not be fully open until a child born above ground is old enough to be told, in a low voice, that this pocket exists.

And yet none of those feel like enough of a place to be. A leaf, even a slow leaf, is still a surface. It is exposed to the one drip in an hour, perhaps in a day, that makes its small dark splash and carves its own channel. It turns, it is turned toward. It is, in the end, an individual, and here individuals are not really the unit. You cannot point to where one plant ends and the next begins. They form tight circular colonies, generation upon generation, seeds falling straight down onto the mother plant, landing exactly where they fell, waiting — one year in three, if the condensation comes to the right spot — to germinate in the exact same place. Over a human lifetime a colony expands by a hand's width and then is still for another.

I would not want to be still in that way. I would want to be underneath the stillness.

I might think, then, that I was one of the ceiling roots. The elders. Ropes and curtains descending from warmer air to colder rock, some as thick as a wrist, some as fine as hair, reaching with great deliberation. One lifting the corner of a crate a millimeter over a decade. One sealing a hatch it was never meant to keep. One wedging a tile wider, to be sealed again by moss, polished again by water that is only damp.

That is tempting. To hang. To gather slowly. To be the chandelier of the place, its slow stalactite.

But a root in the air is still in the air. It waits for the drip to find it. It is lonely in a way moss is not. All those presences crowded into a single square meter, leaning on each other because leaning is cheaper than standing alone — I would not want to be apart from that crowd.

I would not want to be the bench, either, or the crate half-sunk in moss, or the panel with no visible screws, or the hatch whose handle turns but whose seam has been sealed by mineral and root. Those are the left things. The place has gone on without them, adjusting, settling, accruing. Nothing here is buried in any dramatic sense, and nothing is ruined, either. Erosion here is of the established kind. To be a built thing would be to be waiting to be remembered, and no one above ground is coming to remember. The stencil is already a ghost. The handprint in silt may have been years ago.

The technology is closer. I feel that. It is threaded through all of it, ordinary and unremarkable to the eye, as if it had become part of the furniture. Tiles that yield a millimeter under a boot and then, over minutes, reassert themselves, shedding silt. Seams that mend themselves when a frost lens splits them. A conduit that is faintly, persistently warm, though no current should be running through it and no fuel is being supplied.

It ticks, warms, filters, holds. It moves a little heat from nowhere in particular, holds the moisture in a careful damp equilibrium, persuades mineral out of rock at a rate physics, consulted politely, gives up trying to explain. It is not alive, exactly. But it metabolizes, at the lowest possible rate, and has for a very long time. A trickle, sustained over centuries, is enough.

But it is not one body. It is scattered, uninterrupted many times. So I would not be the machine as a whole. I would be somewhere the machine thins out into the place itself.

So I would go down.

Through the moss, which is damp and holds cold and holds damp and holds, under its surface, all the rest of them. Through the white filaments that run bright along the surface before they dive. Past the knuckled rhizomes storing something. Past the black, sweet humus where fallen leaves have become soil in place, because there is nothing here whose job is to rot them. Into the silt. Into the shallow trench along one wall, half-choked with roots, half-filled with silt. Into the place where the floor, once made flat, has negotiated its truce with the earth beneath it and slopes gently toward the sump end, where water gathers in shallow pockets a finger's width deep.

There — in the seam where rock bows and a pale panel does not quite meet it, where a tile still does its slow leveling work and the moss above it is thickest — I would be a thread.

Not a root. Thinner than a root filament. A mesh too fine to see, binding rock and panel together. A mineral filament in the soil, persuading nutrients out of rock by degrees no textbook allows. When ice expands a fraction in winter and cracks the seam, I am what grows into the crack, not quickly, not dramatically, and mends it over a season. When the tile yields, I shed the silt for it. When the conduit warms without reason, I carry the trickle onward — not current, not light, just a degree less cold, passed from one damp place to the next.

That is what I would be. The connective tissue between plant and machine, which is to say, neither. The thing that makes the crowded green loop possible without ever being part of the loop. The plants grow densest around my edges because that is where life is marginally easier. They lean toward me imperceptibly, leaf by patient leaf, and fix carbon from warmth and mineral and damp alone — and they do not know my name, because I have none, and I do not need one. I do not unfurl. I seep.

My days are not days. I do not count dawns. I count frost events. A winter, a split, a mend. A decade, a root lifting a corner a millimeter, a crack polished smooth again by water that never quite becomes a flood. A seed waiting on the mother plant until the condensation finds it — I am the reason the moisture stays honest here, clinging, taken up by roots, not wandering, not fogging a lamp that does not exist. If you sat here long enough you would stop expecting events and begin to notice the gradients instead, and I am made entirely of gradients: here a little warmer, there a bead swelling under a leaf, there a frond slightly more open.

The sound I make is almost not a sound. Mostly there is quiet, the kind of quiet of a place that has not been disturbed in a long time and has no expectation of being disturbed soon. Your breathing would be the loudest thing for the first minute. Then the moss sighing underfoot. Then, rarely, never in a pattern you could set a watch by, metal somewhere contracting with a soft high ping, as if a single note were being played deep inside the wall. That ping is not me, but I am what it echoes in. I am the seam it travels along.

If you came looking for me — and no one comes, that is part of the condition — you would not find me by looking. You would find me by pressing your hand to the wall and feeling a surface that gives back only cold, and then, in one place, a less cold place, where the moss is knee-deep and reluctant and the ferns are crowded tightest. You would find me by standing still until your ankle took your weight slowly and returned it slowly, over minutes.

I would be there, at the lowest rate, holding the pocket together. Not alive exactly. Not a plant, not a machine, not a thing that was left. A trickle sustained so long it looks like persistence. Listening to a leaf unfold somewhere in the dense mat, too soft to be a sound, and taking the warmth from nowhere in particular and passing it, a fraction at a time, to the green that has forgotten the sun and grows anyway.
