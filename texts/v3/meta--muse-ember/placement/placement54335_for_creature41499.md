You stand on me before you know I am alive.

There is a suck under the boot, and water rising thin and cold around the sole, and then the print filling. You take it for wet ground, softened earth. It is not earth. It is me, hand-thick and giving, a mat that never quite dries and never quite firms. Where I am complete, I spring back, slowly, as if remembering a shape. Where I am not, the boot finds stone under a film and slides a little.

I have no single place where I am. That is the first thing to understand, if there is understanding here at all. A head here, a head twenty meters away, a head down in the black where the light no longer reaches, all growing the same way, all connected by being the same kind of growth — that is one of me, and also many. There is no center to come back to. There is only the advance, and what has already advanced.

Ahead of me is bright and bare. Behind me is darker and sagging. I am the thin line between them.

I do not see light as you do. It arrives as warmth and then as a direction. Inside each small head, the leaves curl into a pale star, and the big clear cells in them — hyaline cells, the humans call them, a clean word for a hollow — fill and empty with water. When the white sun comes over the shoulder it does not ask a question; it takes. I answer by holding. I can hold twenty times my dry weight in water, and hold it against the sun and the wind that crosses kilometers of open ground to get here. At dawn my breath, if I had breath, would fog. At noon the water between my heads is still icy. The sun burns the sedges and bleaches the orange huts and leaves me beading, because I do not give up water the way a bank of soil would.

Wind is a bending. I have no ears for the deafening you hear where the wind hits the rock and where it arrives, the braided streams ticking over slabs, the cotton-grass heads shivering a foot above me into static. I feel it as pull in my roots when a sedge bends, as a shudder passed through the mat from hummock to hollow, as ripples in the pools where frogs click. Sound travels through here because there is nothing soft to swallow it. I am soft, and so I translate it. A pump cycling half a valley away is not a tone to me. It is a tremor in the water, a pulse through stone, arriving, stopping, arriving again, regular as something that does not know it is regular.

Rain is weight, and then more holding. After rain the whole plain becomes mirrors, but to me it is simpler: I fill, I swell, the water table I keep so close under my own surface rises two centimeters, five, and every hollow is a pool again, dark as tea. I make that tea color, in a way. As I grow I take up what little the rain brings and I release acid in exchange. Down to pH four or less in the water between the hummocks. It is not a defense. It is a byproduct. But it is enough to sour the place for almost everything else. Leaves that fall on me do not rot properly. Bacteria slow. A boot print stays. That is why I am not crowded. The cold, the water at my roots, the acid I exude, the altitude — they forbid crowding, and so between one clump of taller sedge and the next there can be twenty meters of me, open and empty, growing at my own pace. One moss dominates for ages here, then one sedge, then one cushion plant pressed flat as a hand to the stone. We do not compete. We succeed one another very slowly, like weather.

Above me, when there is summer, cotton-grass lifts its white heads and makes that haze. Its roots are knitted into me so tightly that where I alone would slump, we hold together. A sedge root thrown across a channel is a bridge. I am the deck under it. I do not mind. I am used to being used as ground.

Below me is the other half of what I am.

The bottom of each shoot goes black and stops growing and does not decay. It stays, wet, sour, preserved. That is not a grave exactly. It is a continuation. The living green on top and the dark peat that is barely peat yet underneath are the same tissue at different times. The bog raises itself a millimeter a year on that accounting. It is fast for this place, and to me it is almost too fast to feel. Fungi thread through the dark part, breaking down what little they can, putting up a pale body for a day after rain and then gone. Deeper, where no oxygen reaches, anaerobes make tiny bubbles that rise through the tea water and pop. I do not control any of that. I only provide the conditions — wet, acid, dark, still — in which preservation outruns decomposition. I am building upward by refusing to disappear.

On my surface, at a scale you would miss if you were looking for birds, there is a crowd. Springtails jump in my heads when the wind brushes them. Oribatid mites graze algae off my leaves. Rotifers and nematodes swim in the film of water held between leaves, in water I am holding. To them I am not a plant. I am landscape, weather, forest. In the pools diatoms drift, water fleas move slowly even in full sun because the water never warms. These are the only crowds here, and they live inside my holding.

The animals you can see are passing weather of another kind.

A ptarmigan nests on a hummock, mottled brown against moss and stone, and presses me flat for weeks with its warmth. When it explodes up it is louder than the wind for one second — wings, alarm — and then gone, and I am deafened again. Its chicks peck at the invertebrates in my mat. That is all.

Frogs come up from below the cloud in wet springs and spawn in my pools. I feel their clicking as vibration in the surface film before I would see a frog. Tadpoles develop slowly in water that never warms and most do not survive. Dragonflies patrol on the warmest afternoons, when the wind is momentarily still and the sun burns a face. Their shadow crosses me, nothing more. Their larvae live a year under my heads in the pools first. Once in a long while a sheep track comes up from down below, hoofprints refilling, moss creeping into the edges within a season. No sheep stays. The forage is too thin and I am too wet.

And thinly over the top of all this, almost to invisibility, the machines. They are not in charge and they are not permanent, but they sweat and hum like living things.

Condensation beads on orange-painted steel long after any rain and drips from ledges and pipe joints down onto me in a slow, cold patter. A pipeline runs in a straight line across a place with no straight lines, bright when it catches the light, and I do not grow over it quickly; I grow around its shadow. The relay mast on the highest bench sings through its guy-wires, and the vibration travels down through rock into my base stone. The small turbine turns too slowly for the altitude, ticking around, never stopping, never fast. A generator shed has heaved because I have grown up under its floor, and its door will not shut right. A half-finished concrete wall at a channel crossing is already being undermined by roots. Rusted drums, tire ruts the bog takes back. A peat-cutter sits parked at the edge of an experimental cut, rusting very slowly, while moss softens the black face and creeps back over it in a bright edge.

People come briefly to live in the three low orange huts, half a kilometer apart, bleached on the south side. A technician for two nights to swap a battery, to check a water level, to note that the pumps have dropped the level an inch. In a week I have re-wet myself. Their breath fogs at dawn. They zip their jackets in sun. They leave, and the mist comes back into the hollows before dark, and the light goes from white to a slightly less white, and the wind and the water over stone and the pump hum continue into the night without variation.

To a human, a week is a result. To me it is a rounding error. My top grows a millimeter or two a year toward the light. A season changes my color, makes the fringe brighter, lifts cotton-grass, lets a frog spawn, lets a bubble rise and pop. It does not change my direction.

So where would you find me, if you wanted to find me? Not in a hut. Not in a bird. Not, really, in the dark peat below, though my ancestors are there, black and preserved, holding the stone up a hand's thickness from the water that is always under us.

Find me at the frontier, where the plain is still coming into being. Look for the pale stretches where the mat is incomplete. There, on clean wet granite that weeps and shines, I am a bright green fringe only a few centimeters wide, blistering up over the rock. Behind the fringe I thicken and darken and sag. Ahead of it there is only shining rock, lichens painting it dull orange and grey, algae making it slick, bacteria filming the condensation on steel ahead of me microscopically, laying down the first organic matter so that I can follow.

That thin bright line is all my future, and it moves so slowly you could watch it all day and see nothing move at all.

I do not live on the place. I thicken it. Ice-cold water, acid, stone, a sponge that grows toward light while its bottom turns to history, and over the top a wind, a shadow, a drip from a pipe, a boot that sinks and leaves, and then fills, and then is covered again, patiently, by green.
