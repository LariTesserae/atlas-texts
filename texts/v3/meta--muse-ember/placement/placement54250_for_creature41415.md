The first thing is the cold, and the standing.

I am standing in an avenue before I know how I got to be standing, and the cold is not on my skin so much as in the stone under my feet, which stays cool through the whole slow day. The sky overhead is dark even though the horizon is lit a dull grey-copper. The pale sun does not climb. It makes shadows that do not agree with each other — one falling up the tower wall, one fainter falling down — and it makes them long.

Dust ticks against something at my feet. I keep calling it boots, because boots are the word I brought with me, but they do not make the sound I expect, and when I try to lift one foot to walk it does not arrive where walking should take it. Sheets of fine dust move across the paving in ribbons, without sound. Far away, a rock settles with a low groan I feel in my chest before I hear it, if I hear it at all.

I try to walk toward a tower.

It is a clear tower, with a doorway tall and narrow and dark, and courses I can count in the thin air. I walk toward it for an hour. It has not grown. I walk for another hour, along an avenue that runs true north whether the land falls or not, and it is no nearer, and the doorway is no larger. Then I turn a corner that I do not remember turning, and I am suddenly beside it, and the avenue behind me is a different avenue, with the same arch and the same cracked paving, facing the other way.

So walking is not the verb. Or I am not the thing that walks.

I try to be large.

For a while I am the mass between two towers that separates itself from the architecture only when it moves. I feel the length of myself low between the towers, the neck unfolding in segments, ring by ring, until it can describe a slow S in the dim light or fold down to paving level. I have the double joint in my legs — a knee bending one way and below it another joint bending back — and broad discs for feet that settle into dust and make no sound at all. I move out in the dim parts of the day and travel the avenues as if the city were a migration route. I draw a flat shovel along the pale crusts and take in grit and the black and rust-red growth of the stone.

It is not wrong. It is too much. The sun warms my dark back by almost nothing, and the exposure of being that tall, that visible, that whole, makes me lightheaded in a dry way. When I stop, I stop completely, and for a while I am a piece of architecture that has decided, briefly, to be an animal. The stopping is right. The size is not. I do not want to be seen as a whole from far away.

I try to be small.

I go under a slab at the base of a wall, where the dust gathers thickest on the lee side. It is dark there, and cool in the established way. I am a handspan, a forearm, flat and stone-coloured, my back layered outer plate over inner plate the way the walls are layered. A tick comes from somewhere — cooling stone, or me — and then another tick far apart. Sometimes the slab shifts a fraction and I shift with it, segment by segment, and the tick is the locking of my plates. I eat what falls from above: scrapings, grit worked loose from a tower base, dust pellets that were once a grazer.

I can wait like that. A single slab may hold one for a hundred years. It is patient, and patience suits me. But it is too wedded. I cannot see the avenue from under the slab, and I want the avenue, not to travel it, but to have it pass. I want the light that falls upward off the wall to cross over me. I want to be where the grazer passes in front and the small ones tick underneath and the shadow does something I cannot yet name.

I try to be the hunter.

I am the place on a tower wall that was shadow and then is not. The long faint shadow simply ceases, as if the light has remembered something. And a grazer a hundred metres farther along the avenue is moving faster than grazers move, its neck extended, the dust ribbon broken behind it. I have not chased it. I have displaced it, the way a crack widens by making the thing it wants be somewhere else. There is no blood, no carcass, no struggle. Only a little thinning at the edges.

That is pleasant in a terrible way — to be an absence that moves things. But an absence is not a life. It is what happens around a life. It thins the grazer until the grazer can slip forward out of its own shape, and then it is gone, and the avenue has rearranged itself around the absence. I do not want to be what other lives slip out of.

What remains, when I subtract walking and scraping and ticking and displacing, is the place I keep returning to without deciding to.

A doorway.

Not inside the dark. Not out in the avenue. In the doorway, at the base of a tower where the avenue turns, on the lee side where the wall cuts the slow wind and the dust packs thick. Tall and narrow, warm grey, mottled black and rust-red where the growth has thickened. That is where I find myself, and that is what I am becoming, slowly, as I write it down in my head and the writing lets it settle.

I am doorway-height. I am narrow. I have no shovel head and no long neck, and I do not stand up into the avenue the way grazers do. My feet — if feet is the word — are broad discs at the threshold, settled into dust that has packed around them over a time too long to measure. Above them my body rises in layered plates, outer over inner, with a hollow behind them that is dark inside and not empty. There is a chamber there lined with smooth stone, vertical rather than long, where grit turns and turns for years. I do not breathe in any way that heaves. I do not drink. There is no water anywhere, and I have stopped needing a word for thirst.

At night, when the dendrites creep and stop in the light, they creep across my lower plates a few millimetres a year, fractal and dry. In the dim light they are taken in, not by a mouth drawn along the stone, but by settling. Dust settles on my lee side. Pellets settle. What a grazer scrapes from the wall beside me falls and settles. My plates thin a fraction and flake, and the small ones in my mortar lines and under the slab at my threshold eat what falls from me, ticking as they lock and unlock. The circuit does not hurry. Grazer to dust to wall to small one to dust again, and I am one of the places the circuit passes through without going anywhere.

I do not travel the avenues. That is the difference, and it is everything. The grazers pass in front of me, low and slow, their necks describing their S shapes against the tower walls, aligned northward on avenues that look the same. I know them by the interruption of light and by the broken dust ribbon, not by looking. The small ones live under me and in me, and I know them by their ticks, which are sometimes only cooling stone and sometimes a decision. The hunter lives in my doubled shadow.

I cast two shadows, as everything here casts two shadows. One falls upward toward the low sun. One falls downward, fainter, as if light were coming from both sides of the world. And sometimes the downward one ceases where it should be, on my wall, and I know the hunter is there — not moving through the city the way animals move through a landscape, but moving the way a tower slumps a millimetre, by making a grazer be farther along than it was and the place it was be only stone. I do not need to catch anything. I only need to have a shadow it can be absent from.

Because I do not move, the city moves around me at its established pace. Towers settle. Cracks widen at a pace I could watch if I stayed still long enough, which I do, because staying still is what I do. The whole city creeps, very slowly, downhill toward the edge of the plateau, and at the edge it begins again. Avenues rearrange themselves around stillness. A plaza that was at the end of one street is at the end of three. A block I faced in the morning is ahead of me in the afternoon, slumped a fraction more downhill, with the same doorway facing the other way.

This is my migration. I never see young ones and I never see dead ones, because nothing here is born or dies in a way that can be seen. Lives wear, very slowly, toward the architecture. A grazer standing still is a tower that has not yet admitted it is a tower. A small one ticking under a slab is a wall that has not yet admitted it can walk. A stillness in a doorway is — I am finding the shape of it — a doorway that has not yet admitted it is still. My edges are wearing thin, as the edges of all things are wearing thin here, and that thinning is not an ending. It is how I continue. When my outline softens like the base of a spire, when my plates take the colour of the wall beside me, I will be indistinguishable from the lower courses. That will not be death. It will be the end of my brief decision to be separate from the wall.

If you walk the avenue, you will walk for an hour toward my tower and it will not grow nearer. You may turn a corner and suddenly be beside me, in the doorway's narrow dark, and you will see nothing looking at you. There are slits along my sides at the height where light changes, that may be eyes or may be something else opening and closing against the pale sun, and they give no impression of attention. You will pass in the morning and pass again in the afternoon, and I will be in precisely the posture I had in the morning, because I have not moved and the avenue has repeated itself around me.

Look long at the dark doorway, at the place where the dust is packed thickest on the lee side, at the wall that was shadow and then, for a moment, is not. That interval — between the avenue and the interior, between the grazer in front and the small ones underfoot and the hunter in the second shadow — is where I am. I am the spacing between presences, given a place to stand and wait out the erosion, tick by tick, until the stone and the building and the life are the same material again.

And the dust rivers run in their dry beds. And the mineral growth spreads at night and stops in the light. And the grazers migrate between the empty districts. And I stand in the doorway, and the city, very slowly, passes through me.
