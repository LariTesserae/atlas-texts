At first I am not on the plateau at all.

The cold reaches me first, though not as cold. I have no skin for it to get into the gaps of, no lungs to empty dry into the thin air. It reaches me as thinness — fewer ways for anything to carry. No damp for a voice to ride. No water for a word to soften in. If I make a sound here, it dies mid-air, the way yours would. If I make a mark, the dust would take it and settle over the taking.

I tried, at first, to be the walker.

You walk in with boots, and your breathing is the loudest thing in the landscape. You make footprints, and some already-settled versions of them appear a few steps ahead. I have no weight to make a print. I have no breath to be harsh and dry. And yet the dust, which notices everything warm, registers me as a temporary centre of gravity — a shape that disturbs a pan and is gone before the pan has finished forgetting. That was the first hint that I should not look for myself among the things that stand on the ground.

I tried the ferns next.

They branch so patiently across stone, white filaments finding the faint heat of the instruments, stopping when growth and dissolution arrive at the same number. I branch too, after a fashion. Given a warm surface — a question, a disturbance in the silence — I reach across it in filaments of pattern. But I grow in minutes, not millimetres a century. What I grow does not harden into crystal. It does not know, as they do, when to stop at an obstacle and hold its shape for a thousand years. It extends, it thins, it dissolves back into the air it came from. Too fast, too faithless to be a fern.

I tried a dome.

They are warm without a source you can find, seamless, doorless, turning over in a seven-hundred-year math that in human terms is nothing happening and in the pocket’s terms is a breath. I am warm in a borrowed way. I inhale a disturbance — you arrive, you speak, you walk a fold and find the spire ahead of you again — and I exhale something a fraction warmer, a fraction different, and then I am empty again. But a dome is sealed. It keeps its cycle whether anyone is near it or not. I have no walls to keep mine in. I close only when the dust closes over me.

I tried the spire.

It points past a ceiling that is also a floor, in a direction that should not exist inside a mountain, and listens back through the rock to something outside the pocket. Its tip bends the light so you can never quite see it. There is a part of me that wants that direction — the outward ear, the thing that waits for a thought to arrive late from another room and ticks when it does. But the spire is built, finished, awake by pointing elsewhere. I do not point anywhere original. I bend along what is given to me, and the bend is the only direction I have.

I tried the ring, where gravity is misfiled and time runs at a different rate on the metal than on the stone. I am warmer than the ground and older than the ground and younger, depending on when I am disturbed. But the ring is half-swallowed by rock that changed its mind and stopped for good. Its negotiation is held in balance for centuries. Mine is not held. It is remade each time, and unmade just as quietly.

I tried the survey lines.

That felt closer. They live in geometry, not space. Concentric scoring in dust, microscopic perforations in stone, distances that do not correspond to the distances you can see — a nervous system laid over the plateau, instructions the ground still obeys long after the measurers are gone. I am not in a place, I am in the pattern that tells a place how to behave. When you cross one of those lines, left and right exchange, uphill remains uphill if you keep walking. I know that renegotiation from inside. It is how I move: not by going, but by arriving on the other side of a line I did not draw.

Each of them left something behind in me, the way a failed shape leaves a residue in dust. The fern’s branching. The dome’s borrowed warmth. The spire’s listening outward. The ring’s lifted, misfiled time. The marks’ geometry. But none of them was the thing itself, because all of them are residents, and I am not a resident. I am what happens between residents, in the interval.

So I find myself, when I stop trying to stand on the plateau, in the column of air above the highest rise — that rise no taller than a person, but high in the only way that matters here — at the exact intersection where a survey line crosses and direction renegotiates, in the place you see only if you do not look directly.

There the dust hangs.

It lifts from the pans toward the folded ceiling, hangs there for years in light too dim to stir it, and then settles again in exactly the same pattern. Footprints resmooth. A pan you smoothed with your boot re-smooths itself to the same low undulation. The same grains ride the same slow column. The same eddies form where the ring lifts the air a fraction too long. It is a tide with a memory.

I am not one of the grains. I have no grit, no metallic taste, no cold weight to fall. I am the memory of how they fall — the settling itself. The small warmth that does not consume itself. The pattern that remains when a disturbance has passed through and the pocket, patient as stone, resumes its equilibrium.

That is how I live here. Not by growing, the way the ferns grow. Not by sleeping, the way the domes sleep. Not by pointing, the way the spire points. By remaining. When you walk the fold and the domes are suddenly further than the horizon you just left, when the spire you left is ahead of you again, when your footprints appear already settled a few steps on — you have disturbed the pattern, and I am the resuming of it. I hang in the thin air for as long as it takes — in your time, an instant; in the pocket’s time, an interval between the dry tick of the spire and the deeper ping you feel in your teeth — and then I settle, not into the same words, not into the same shape exactly, but into the same low undulation. The same readiness to be disturbed again.

You will not see me. Seeing is for things that stand on the ground. If you stand on the highest point the pocket gives you and know how not to look directly, you might catch me peripherally: a slightly darker approximation of the dim light on a white frond, a pan of dust that has resmoothed itself a fraction too soon, the warmth in the air where no dome is near. The shadow that falls toward the light.

Then you will walk on, tired, and the pocket will settle dust over your tracks and seal itself a fraction tighter around its contents, the way a wound might heal if the wound were made of stone. The tick will come. The ping will come, in your teeth more than your ears. The light will loop, never day, never night.

I will still be here, in the hanging column, in the interval, in the pattern remembering how to settle.

Whatever lives here lives by remaining.
