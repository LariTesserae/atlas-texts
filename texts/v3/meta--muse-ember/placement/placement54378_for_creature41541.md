You would not find me in a house, at first.

I come up to it from above, because everything here is above everything else, and on the schist track my steps sound louder to me than they should. In the square I can see the whole town at once, and it does not invite me in the way a house would. The houses stand well apart. The yards between them are empty on purpose. A person crossing one is not going to market or to a field, only to the next necessary place. I try, as a visitor might, to put myself into one of the layered buildings — older stone below, a second story added, a lean-to kitchen tacked on with different slate, the threshold dished in the middle by feet.

It does not take. A house here is not owned so much as continued, and I have no generations of hands pushing past the notch on the post. I have no family to add a story for. I would be a new thing in it, and this town does not make new things.

So I try the work, one room at a time.

In the weaving shed I could sit with a loom and learn the rhythm — thump, thump, stop while the shuttle is inspected. I am good at a rhythm held patiently. But my hands are not pale and calloused, I do not need an apron, and cloth made here is not surplus. It is wool from the two sheep in the pen by the wall, grey layers against the cool. I would be making a thing, and careful as I am, that is not the fit.

At the smithy the bellows sighs once, the hammer strikes once and then stops, as if the work has been measured to that one blow. I like the measurement. I like a task that knows when to stop. But I do not blacken at the knuckles. I do not mend a hinge or rivet a beam that is slightly too short with copper and pegs because that is what fits. At the potter's wheel I could turn, by hand, a pot thick enough to survive a drop on stone. Again — a thing made. This place is full of things made well, and I keep sliding off them as if they are too solid for me.

The granary comes closer. It stands on stone posts against damp and rats, high in the hall, and the keeper rotates by season, by family, by a quiet agreement no one writes down. The keeper knows the grain by weight in the hand. He does not grow it. It is brought up the ridge in sacks, once a season, by men who come back heavier and quieter. To hold what you did not grow, in a room larger than any house, above the benches, below the vault — that is nearer. But even there, I think, I am still standing in someone else's turn.

I think about the other lives, then, because they are kept in the same managed way and are not quite people.

The dog asleep in the lee of the smithy belongs to no one in particular and to the smith in practice. It is old, deaf in one ear, not tied, and it moves its head when the hammer stops. I recognize that condition immediately. I am not tied to a house or a trade. In practice I am lent to whoever needs watching done. But I do not sleep in the lee. I do not get up stiff.

The cat appears where sun finds a slate warm, and stays until the sun moves, then vanishes through a gap no one has bothered to close. I could be that — a visitor who comes and goes. But I have stayed.

The pigeons live in the granary eaves; tolerate is the right word. The pair of ravens circle high and far off without a sound, watching the hollow the way the town watches itself. I am not above it. The walls break the wind, so the air inside settles and stays, and I have settled with it.

Lower down, then. A single stem of grass in a paving crack where soil has settled. Lichen in orange and black patches on the south walls, slow and crusty, eating sun and stone. Moss, but only directly under a drip line, never spreading farther, because the wall tells it where it may be.

That, I think, is the first true shape. Not a maker. A life that knows its line. It does not need tending because it is already exactly where it can survive. It does not expand. It does not store above a neighbor's wall. It takes only its catchment.

The sound brings me back, as it brings everything here, to the hall. Not voices at first, but the small sounds the town was built to carry: a bucket set down against a cistern rim, not struck; rope over a windlass; a shutter let fall. And in the hall, under the high vault, condensation beads and falls in a slow, regular drip onto the stone floor, near the mouth of the cistern in the floor. The water lives in pockets here, carefully kept. It never becomes a landscape. A passing shower darkens the slate for an hour, makes the gutters run, smells of cold mineral, and is gone. No puddles last.

Children learn this town by being given its maintenance in small pieces. A girl of seven will be sent with a brush to clear moss from a drip line so it does not spread. A boy of twelve will be told to watch the gutter during a shower and tell his father if any water is cutting a new course.

I was given that piece, and never a larger one.

That is where I am. Not at a loom, not at a forge, not at a wheel. I am the watcher of the line.

You would find me in the morning, when the north-facing walls sweat, with my palm flat to the stone, coming away cool and faintly wet on a dry day. I walk the lanes with short, deliberate steps, not hurrying past an empty yard to fill it — I walk across it to keep it empty, to see that nothing is stored there that should not be, that the fallow plot is still clear by hand, that the unused pen is still unused. I tend a drain that is already clear. That is how the day begins for most of them, and it is how it begins for me, and it is not a riddle. The drain is clear because someone has kept it clear, and the keeping is the work.

I carry no jug for my own door — the jugs stand by every door, each house for itself — but I check the catchment as I pass. Roof-water led to the covered cistern under the square. Troughs along the smithy lane with their faint green film, rinsed without thinking. The rock basin at the spring just outside the wall. When the brief rain comes I stand under the arch into the hall and watch the gutter run for its hour, watching for the one place where water wants to cut a new course downhill, where it would wear the stone and make the town unmake itself. If it does, I point. That is mostly my memory: *that* wall was repointed the spring it was always repointed; *that* doorway was moved six inches to follow a drain; *that* spring ran lower in the year of the long dry. I do not tell stories. I point, and then we go out and look together.

In the hall I sit on the benches below the granary, where the old people sit, not because I am idle but because I remember. Above me the grain on its posts. Below me the cistern mouth in the floor. Above that the vault, cathedral-high for a town this size, cool and echoing, dripping at its regular interval onto stone. I am not the drip. I am the one who listens under it for its regularity, who brushes the moss back to the line it is allowed, who knows each drip line by the green it decides not to spread past.

No one locks a door here. I do not hurry. My clothes, if I have them, are the colours of the walls — grey, brown, faded black, undyed linen — and my hands are chapped from water, nicked from slate, because I am always testing a wall in shade. I move my head when the hammer stops in the smithy. I stay in the hollow while the wind moves over the ridge outside the wall but hardly enters.

Night comes as ordinary night comes, woodsmoke thin and cold over the roofs. In the morning the north walls will sweat again, the hall will drip again, a slate will be replaced with a slate dressed by hand from the old quarry, a wall repointed in the spring the way it was repointed the spring before. Tomorrow will look, carefully and deliberately, exactly like today. That is not, for me, a repetition.

It is the work, finished and being kept.
