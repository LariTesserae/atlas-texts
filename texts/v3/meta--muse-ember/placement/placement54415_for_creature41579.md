I know where I am by the leaning.

My back is to a warm wall at a doorway, shoulder to shoulder with two others whose names I do not need for this. A third has just joined us, because a person alone on a wet street feels unfinished here. We are not speaking. We are sorting, wordlessly, who is waiting for the loop tram and who is only standing out of the wind, and who will climb the steps next. My hat is tilted so the sideways rain finds the channel in the brim and runs off. Behind me, the low block leans into the next block for warmth. We have learned the architecture in our bodies. I never learned how to stand alone.

My cup hangs on its cord and knocks softly against my coat when I shift, half full of stone sweat, faintly metallic. If I offer it, you accept it. That is a greeting, and it is also water. I count the drips without deciding to count them. Drip from a ledge. Drip from a wire. Drip from the underside of a fern frond into a cup left deliberately below it. Children learn that way, the way lowland children learn a heartbeat. I still do it.

Everything I am wearing is wool that has not dried in years. Three sweaters, a coat, an oilskin cape patched at the shoulders, gloves that are always damp. It smells of mushroom and rust because it lives in that smell. My boots sink and rise on the moss felt in the hollows, a wet mattress over rock, and never come up dry.

We do not run. Four kilometres up, breath is expensive and the stone is polished by condensation. We climb in a particular way: hand on the rail, three steps, a pause to let the air come back, three steps more. The pause is where everything is exchanged. News. A hat adjusted. A check of what the cup has caught since morning. I have learned most names in Sarn-Veth in a pause on a staircase, and I have learned when a stranger has to sit down halfway and should not be shamed for it.

In the morning I climb the water tower. The wind is funnelled between the buildings and is stronger up there, and the cold funnels sweat. I coax drinking water out of night air with cold metal, and I come down soaked by my own machine. A kettle up here boils lukewarm. The sun at midday is a pale disc through high mist, grey-green, and it fails to warm the stone. I leave the tower by the tramway, where the cisterns are below the rails in the dark.

I was a child keeper of those cisterns, small and serious, and knew the tunnels by touch. Under the tramway the water never sees the sky, and in it live the blind cave fish, pale as uncooked dough, slow in the cold. I tapped the stone and listened for the turn of their bodies. I named individuals I could not see and fed them algae from the condensers. Some winters a fish is eaten and some is not. It is not a distinction we make loudly. I still tap the stone on my way down, out of habit, and something still turns, slowly, underneath.

I live between an engine and a fern. The cellar holds a steam engine from the first industrial age and it breathes, slowly, like an aged relative who must be fed and listened to. Above it are three storeys of riveted stone and iron, built over and over on top of its own ruins. Above that, fern roots are finding the mortar, and above that, the roof garden holds rain that never quite falls and drips down through the kitchen. My grandparents keep the warmest corner by the hot plate. I tend that plate. It is not much warmth, and in damp, thin air fire is difficult, but it is the ember everyone leans toward. My parents are in the room above. I am in the middle layer, where the smell is strongest. An empty room does not stay empty here. Moss takes it first, then a flowering shrub wedges itself into a joint, then someone moves in to tend the shrub, or to sleep nearer the warm wall.

The other residents are not mine. I leave a rusty casing empty on the windowsill for the rust-eating beetles, the way you would leave a room for an elder. They arrived with the first iron and they eat it slowly and leave pellets that feed the green that upholsters their homes. In my wool there are furnace moths, dusty-winged, who live their whole lives in the warm flues. I shake them out into the steam of the kitchen with a patience that is not kindness exactly, more like recognition. On warm rocks in the quarry I leave scraps for the stone-coloured marmots. They are neighbours, not pets. They do not mind the tram.

Most of my work is maintenance, and maintenance is the life. I re-line a tunnel before the damp takes its rivets. I re-plant a roof before the moss drags the tiles down. I scrape lichen the colour of old pennies off a bridge girder, and I leave another patch alone because it holds the frost off the iron in July. I read the felt in the hollows the way others read a sky: if it is open and soft under the boot, rain is coming sideways; if it is closed and tight, the fog tide is about to roll up the dome. Fungi push up through a pavement in the morning and collapse by evening, and are gathered or ignored according to the hour. I am not fighting the green. I am negotiating with it daily, at close quarters, the way you negotiate with a lodger who is bigger than you and will outlive you.

Lately I sit more. In the north-facing courtyard where frost is still on the iron in July, I sit with my back to the warm wall and the cold air from the shaft coming down the other side. Warm air from the kitchen rises, cold mist beads on the underside of the roof, and sometimes it falls upward for a few seconds onto the warm stones. No one remarks on it. I tilt my hat and wait. I sit a little longer each year. There is moss growing in the seams of my coat where I have sat in the same spot, and lichen spots are coming on my hands. I am being upholstered, slowly.

That is, I think, what I am becoming. Not the tower, not the engine, not the moss in the hollow, not the fish in the dark. The plateau wears everything very, very slowly, and the city wears itself very, very fast. An iron bridge built a generation ago is already a garden. I wear somewhere between the stone and the bridge. I keep the warm corner warm. I count drips. I lean.

At evening the lamps come fully on, amber and hazy in the damp, and the cloud sea below the rim turns the colour of ash. The domes go dark first, then the mist above them. The leaning groups break up. Cups are collected from windowsills and from beneath fern fronds. Hats are shaken out, never dry, but shaken anyway. Shoulders are pressed in farewell. We go up our staircases, three steps and a pause, into houses that have grown a little greener since morning.

If I am very still at dusk, I hear it: a single, hollow call from above the rim. We stop. We do not answer. The children look up. The elders nod, as if to an old acquaintance. The marmots freeze. Underneath it all, the dripping goes on counting, and then there is nothing, for days.
