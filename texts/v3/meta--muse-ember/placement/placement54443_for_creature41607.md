The first thing I know is cold.

Not the cold of evening, or of shade. A specific cold, on a surface that is not stone in any way I can name, standing in warm air while everything else is still dry. Condensation is beading there first — it always beads there first — long before it beads on the smooth floor, on the frogs' backs, on me. I flick something out and taste it.

A tongue. I must have a tongue. Long, forked at the tip, violet in the light I don't yet have eyes for. It laps the bead from the pillar before gravity remembers it, and again, and again, in the rhythm the pillar drips. Drip. Drip. Drip. Deliberate. Into the channel cut in the floor.

Under the tongue, spongy. Moss, bright green, woven with tiny flowers that open and close through the day. A perfect ring around the base of the pillar, expanding a little every year in a spiral no one tends. I am curled in that ring, and my belly is damp with it, and when I uncurl it leaves a print that fills in behind me. So I have a belly, and a back to put on it.

When I lift my head — and I do have a head, narrow, rose-grey mottled like the plateau stone itself — the hum arrives. Not in my ears. I don't think I have ears, not the kind the birds use. It arrives in my teeth, in the bones of my jaw where my cheek rests against the pillar. A low, irregular pulse, sometimes minutes, sometimes hours, without pattern. The pillars are the clock of this place. The birds know it. The mist knows it. My teeth know it first of all.

I step off the moss because the sun has found the floor, and the stone under my feet is warm. Four legs, then. Long, long-legged, the way the mammals are long-legged, but slower, and my toes splay wide on the ancient, filed-down rock. It is warm where the sun has not touched and cold where it rests, in patches that shift through the day. I can feel each patch through my feet. I move from warm to cold to warm, keeping a patchwork of my own. That is how I stay neither hot nor cold. That is how I get across the desert between green islands — twenty metres of bare stone, a journey, a whole migration — without drying out.

I am not a mammal. I don't run in troops. I am not a lizard, though a lizard no longer than a forearm will dart at me from a sunlit rock and I will have to out-turn it with my long tail, flicking, not striking. I am not a frog, though I drink from the shallow bowls, black and still, no wider than a dinner plate. I put my whole narrow snout in and drink them down to a film, and the stone drinks the rest back later, and the frogs who sit with only their eyes above the surface do not look up at me. They are used to things that sip at dawn.

I cast a shadow, but it lags. A fraction behind me, in the still humid air, as if it were thinking about following. The raptor that cuts through the slot from above the plateau — silent, huge, from another world of weather — stoops sometimes where my shadow has just been. I press myself flat and become stone. Mottled rose-grey on warm stone. Invisible, except to the shadow that arrives late and gives me away.

At midday the rain cell forms overhead, brief and local, and I am usually on its edge. One half of the bowl drumming on the ferns bigger than a person, on the leaves and the stone, blackening it in blotches. The other half in hard, honest sun. I live on the line between them. One foot in steam, one foot dry. Moss flowers lift their heads toward moisture that never quite touches the ground; I lift mine toward the drip that does. After the rain, the insects emerge in pulses — the cloud that has been whining as a baseline over the damp pockets tears and roars and reforms — and I sit just under a fern frond on the eastern side, unrolling in the morning, folding at night, each frond twisted helically the same way, toward the cold beneath the floor, and I eat what falls.

The fern is my roof when the hut-shell near the eastern rim is taken by the small animals who need a dry corner. This bowl has only one roof it ever made, and it's bone-dry inside even in rain. I don't need dry. I need damp, and cold, and the drip.

I don't need a roof. I need the seam.

The third pillar from the place where the slot lets the light in — the one with the crack — has a crack that is present when the birds look away and gone when they look back, leaving only a faint warmth. Nothing living touches it. I do. I go in sideways, tail last, my narrow body folding into fused black stone as into water, and the warmth closes behind me like a door that was never a door. Inside, the hum is loudest. Inside, I am mineral with an intention, for a while. When the warmth fades and the crack remembers itself, I come out again, tongue first, tasting for condensation.

That is where I am, mostly. In the moss ring at the base of the seam-pillar, under the eastern fern, on the line between rain and sun, between the green island and the bare stone, between inside the stone and outside it. Where the channel from the pillar meets the channel from the engraved discs — the flat discs sunk into the floor, carrying water drop by drop to the moss — I follow the damp the way the ants follow their centimetre-wide line up the tree bark, carrying nothing, carrying everything, a spider lowering itself on a thread across my tail and not noticing.

The birds don't notice me. They blacken the rim at dawn, lift in waves when the pillars hum peaks and the mist thickens, wheel in a courtyard of sky, stoop, scatter, reform, calls striking the rim and coming back and coming back a third time from a farther wall. I hear the third echo. I have learned to listen for it. My own sound, when I make one, returns three times and the third is the truest.

I click. Softly. Throat to jaw to teeth. Once, when I pass near the half-dome of matte grey ceramic on my way to bask on the low arc of chest-sized blocks — it answers, not to touch but to proximity, with a pinprick of light and a soft clicking, though there is no fuel, no wire, no part I can find. The moths pass near it all day. The flies. Perhaps it answers them. Perhaps it answers me, and I answer it, and neither of us remembers the hands that made it. The makers are dust now, filed down with the plateau to its final form. What remains still gathers mist, still holds cold, still repairs itself. I have arranged myself around it the way the moss has, the way the ferns have, in a perfect ring, year by year.

By evening the bowl cools at the edges first. Condensation returns to the pillars first, then to the smooth stone, then to the backs of the frogs. The birds return to the rim in noisy processions, wings still beating after their feet have found the ledge. The mammals retreat to the moss. The tongue flicks. The cold beads. The hum slows.

For a moment the place is dark and quiet and damp. Then the insects begin again, and the drips, and the distant, patient pulse from below the stone, where something dormant still breathes cold upward through the floor to keep the air wet enough for all of this — for my teeth to sing, for my shadow to lag, for a crack to heal over the warmth where I sleep.

I am not a bird, and not a frog, and not a lizard, and not a mammal, and not a pillar. I am the small thing that lives between them, on their separate clocks, in the space between presences that is the sparseness of this place. If I had a name, it would be the sound the water makes when it hesitates in the air halfway down from a vine, meets a rising bead, merges, hangs for a breath — and then, when gravity remembers, falls as one.

I am still learning my shape. This morning I have a tongue, four long legs, a tail, and teeth that hear. That is enough to be here.
