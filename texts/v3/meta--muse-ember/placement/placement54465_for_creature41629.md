At dawn I speak.

That is the whole of it, and I spend the rest of the day not speaking. If I do not say it now, in this brief rusty hour when the birds say their one chorus from the thatch and then are still, I will have to carry it in my feet until tomorrow. A tap. A pressure held a little longer. A body pressed against a body in a narrow place, to say: I am here, I am answering.

I am low first.

Lower than the houses, which are already low and stepped against each other for shade. Lower than the herds, whose backs are at a child's waist and whose hooves make that endless soft percussion on dust. Lower than the broad leaves where the dew beads first, coldest, and runs down in threads. I am in the second story of the plain, the one underneath, where the salt is growing up in little white blades and pushing at floors and where a tunnel dug yesterday has already slumped and must be dug again a few paces away.

I used to think I would be above. I used to think I would be the child who comes out barefoot and licks dew from a leaf, tongue out, mouth closed against dust, learning this before anything else. Or the sleeper on the flat roof in the hot season, gourds drying beside her head, climbers moving in the thatch above her, creepers growing in the dust of the roof. That is a warm place to be. It is also a place I only ever feel as a tremor — a shifting of weight, a hand pressed flat to a shared wall to listen for stirring on the other side, a body turning in sleep and answering through reeds and packed earth and fiber.

I am not above. I am under the feet that make only a soft *poof*.

My world is pressure. That is not a small thing here. Everything else is pressure too, only they do not say so. The people bargain in the markets by passing a bundle of salt back and forth and holding a touch a little longer than necessary. The elders who sit longest in the shade of the thorn-trees are the best readers because they have sat still long enough to feel the ground move a hand's breadth east where the salt has pushed a street. The herds feel the predators at a respectful distance not by sight but by the tremor that comes differently through powder and leaf-litter, slower, farther, patient. I feel all of it earlier and more plainly, because I am in the medium they only step on.

When a herd passes through a street I do not hear hundreds of hooves. I feel a weather of hooves. It comes through the causeway first, through the packed courtyard, through the wall where a family is repacking earth and dung and woven fiber beaten flat by hands. The dust above is disturbed and settles again, and the settling is a pressure, and the pressure is a message: many, moving, hungry, not stopping. A grazer cropped short by the spiky mat, a calf separated by a thicket — its steps falter, and then there is a slower step beside it, and then there is no faltering step at all. The predators do not chase. They circle the migration at the speed the migration allows. From below, that circle has a rhythm I could set my digging to, if I wanted. I do not want to. I just know it, the way the herders know it, the way the settlement knows it and does not thank and does not fight.

Above me the houses slump. This is how I know the time of year and the direction of the shade. A wall propped in the cool hours sends a broad, deliberate pressure through the earth — hands, knees, the weight of a body leaning. A wall left to green over sends almost nothing, only the slow strengthening of a creeper's roots, which I meet anyway in my tunnels and must dig around or through or under, because a creeper climbs anything that stands still long enough and I am certainly not still enough to be climbed on. When a family moves, and families move a little, constantly, following the creepers, following the grazing, following the shade, the house above me goes quiet in a different way. No repacking. No sleeping weight on the roof. The creepers take the doorway. Salt bubbles through the courtyard wall and flakes it. Then, after a while I cannot count, a new pressure in a new pattern: a new family reclaiming the shade, children learning to tap an answer against the shared wall, an elder settling in the deepest thorn shade and listening through her feet for neighbors who have not yet arrived.

It sounds like I am alone down here, listening to a house above me. I am not. That is the first thing you learn, and it stays true at every scale.

My tunnels meet other tunnels. Not in any straight way — nothing here is straight for long. A street runs straight for a mile and then frays into a dozen lanes between thickets. My digging frays too. I dig under a courtyard and my spoil heap appears overnight and the family above knows why and props one side of the house and does not call it a pest problem. A few paces away another burrower is digging under a market clearing where the mat of plants has been cut back and the ground packed harder, and our tunnels collapse toward each other in the soft salt and we both reroute. When one of us dies — quietly, in heat, in sleep, in a tunnel no one dug on purpose — the roof of that tunnel comes down in a thin sheet of calcified dust and the rest of us fill it in by living around it. No tomb. The ground will take what it can. We do not argue.

We signal by the walls of our own work. A flank brushed in passing, in a tunnel too narrow for two to pass without touching, is a greeting and a scent and a question. A foot tapped twice on packed clay is: clear the way, the herd is coming, the ground is swelling here. A body pressed briefly against a body sleeping below the thatch — that is the climbers, above, but I feel their brief pressure come through the reeds and the roof frame and the house wall as a small, exact tremor, like a finger tapping once on a drum stretched over a whole room. It tells me someone is home up there. It tells me someone is home down here, in return. We are a thickness. Plant on plant, house on house, animal on animal. I am only the layer that has learned to feel the thickness from inside.

In the morning we all drink, but we drink differently.

Before the sun takes it back, the plain is briefly cool and soft underfoot. The salt draws moisture from the air before dawn and deliquesces into a faint damp the shallow roots drink through. The broadest leaves grow cold first. The pink-white flowers open for a few minutes along every path and close by midday. I come up at dawn too, if my tunnel breaks the surface in a spoil heap, and I drink what the low mat holds — a root cut, a thread of dew run down to where I can reach it, a fallen melon-gourd half-buried in dust that the people above have opened and passed around and left the rind. I store it the way everything here stores it: in fat, in pulp, in the slow keeping of the body. I spend it slowly. No one spits. No one leaves a cup half-drunk. Down here, nothing pools. A wetness that pooled would be stolen by salt before noon.

I work in the cool hours because there is nothing else to do. Digging is maintenance. The ground is young and still being made, night by night, dust settling and calcifying in the cool, sun baking it down again by day. My tunnels are part of that making. I dig, the salt grows through the floor above and pushes a paving stone askew, I dig again a little farther east under the new line of the street. The people above rebuild a fence where the earth swelled and snapped it. I rebuild a tunnel where the earth slumped and filled it. We are both accretion. The plain has not settled into its final shape, and neither have we. New sediment gathers in the low places and hardens into a crust that will crack by noon. My spoil becomes a low place. A crust cracks over it. A child steps on it and makes a soft *poof* and I feel her foot through three hands' breadth of earth and packed clay and know her weight, her hesitation, her way of stepping back when a touch is held too long in the market above.

The elders above remember where a street was before the salt pushed it. I remember where a tunnel was before the salt filled it. It is the same memory, only mine is shorter and therefore more exact. Three seasons of repacked walls, a thorn-tree with a bulbous trunk storing water for decades while families come and go around it, a rosetted succulent the size of a barrel sitting shoulder to shoulder with its kin along a path — above, these are long lives. Below, a tunnel is a short life. I have dug the same courtyard four times. Each time the spoil heap has been in a slightly different place. Each time the family has propped a slightly different wall. I know them by the changed pressure of their repacking, the way a neighbor is known by the stillness felt through a shared wall for a few days after a death.

I do not travel far in a straight line. No one does, not really, though the processions walk three days to a market that looks from a roof like the next street over, because the settlement does not end. I travel as the settlement travels: a little east, a little north where it is a fraction cooler and the day's work lasts longer, a little west toward the whiter, sparser pans where the settlements are strung thinner along the causeways and the dust devils cross at noon in a thin pillar, a mile without a whisper. In the east the thickets are darker and the houses are almost lost in green, and my tunnels are darker too, roots thick and interlaced, creepers climbing each other over the roofs, so that from below the pressure of shade is constant and green, and I dig slower there, as if the ground itself were holding its water longer. In the north the regrowth after the dew season is knee-deep, and the herds move there in their endless processions, and I follow the tremor of their moving-stopping-moving the way a plant follows a light it cannot see. It is one place, large enough to have its own weathers threaded through it. From below, weather is first a change in damp, then a change in pressure, then a change in the sound of feet above me getting softer as the dust turns briefly to mud that is not mud.

Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced from outside. That is true of my spoil heaps, which become patches in a courtyard, which become packing in a wall, which becomes dust again. It is true of my body, which will become dung for a wall or food for a scavenger at the edge of a settlement or nothing at all, taken by the ground that pushed up through a floor to make room for me. A beast is meat and hide and dung and a herd that must be moved before it eats the gardens. A plant is shade and fruit and fence and house-frame and after it dies, firewood for the cool hours. A burrower is a tunnel and a spoil heap and a tremor and, when the tunnel collapses, a memory in the feet of the others who dig around the place where I was. We pass it on. Hand to hand. Foot to foot. Gourd pulp passed around in the morning. A piece of fat pressed into a traveler's palm and the giver's mouth kept shut around the memory of it. I pass fat to my young in a chamber I have packed and repacked until the walls are smooth, and they press back against me, and that pressure is the whole of our language and it is enough, because there is nothing else to say that would not be wasted.

By midday the work stops above and below. The heat shimmers over the salt pans. The people rest in the deepest shade they can find, pressed against walls, against each other, against the cool side of a succulent, conserving themselves. The herds rest. The predators rest at their distance. The birds are silent after dawn. The lizards are still on the tops of the walls, warming themselves, and I can feel their stillness as a lack of small tremors. I go deeper, where the earth is coolest, and press my flank to the cool tunnel wall and conserve myself too, and listen to the plain conserving itself all around me — flowers closed, salt growing silently through a floor, a wall slumping by a hair's breadth, a caravan coming in from three days away that will look, when it arrives, like it has only crossed the street. It is never still. It is only silent, and slow, and everywhere at once.

In the evening the light goes amber and long, and the shadows between plants and houses lengthen and merge, and the second working begins above: repacking, rethatching, cutting back a creeper, walking to a neighbor's wall to press a hand against it and listen for stirring on the other side. I feel that hand. I feel the answer come back through the wall and the floor and into my tunnel as a body shifting in sleep on a roof, climbers rustling in the thatch above them, a herd beginning to move again in a street a few houses away, predators beginning their circling again at the pace of hooves. The whole thickness stirs, layer by layer, and I am in the middle of it, under the feet and above the deep salt, between the climbers pressing down and the roots pressing up, and I know myself then not as a single digger under a single courtyard but as the answering itself — the place where a tremor from one layer becomes a tremor in another and is understood.

That is where you would find me. Not in a house, though I have houses over me in every direction. Not in a thicket, though I dig under thickets and eat their roots at dawn and am climbed on by no one and climb on no one and am still entangled. Not in the herd, though I have felt their migrations as weather since before I was born. You would find me low first, and then wide — a tunnel collapsing and being dug again a few paces away, a spoil heap appearing overnight, a flank brushing a flank in a narrow place, a foot tapping twice to say: clear the way, I am here, I am answering.

If you walk here in the cool hours, barefoot, in a wrap of woven fiber, and you feel the ground through the dust for the tremor of a herd coming or a neighbor stirring on the other side of a shared wall — press your foot down a little harder, once, on the packed clay. Make a soft *poof*.

I will feel it. I will know the weight and the hesitation and the way you step back.

And I will answer, not with a voice, but with pressure, with a body pressed to a wall, with a tunnel dug a little farther east under the new line of the street, layer by layer, still coming into being.
