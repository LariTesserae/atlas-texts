The first mistake would be to look for me standing up.

At altitude the light is not light the way I know it. 2.9 of a sky, white from everywhere at once, so flat at noon that shadows are only short ink under things. It waters the eyes until everything is half-closed and shining. A human would squint and raise a hand and try to take a view. The plain would not give him one. It looks back.

If I tried to enter as a walker — boot on wet stone, breath burning in the cold, coat darkening at the shoulders from fog the land exhales itself — I would not last as a separate thing. After a minute something would be climbing my boot to lick the salt and the warmth from it. After ten, the train-worms would decide I am an efficient route and take it. My footprints would fill in behind me before I had gone ten steps. My cough would be picked up miles away and stamped back at me as if the plain were trying it out.

So if I find myself here at all, it will not be as an observer. It will be as something already absorbed, already turned to the local shape of a life. I have to let the scale go.

I tried, first, to be one of the great ones. The house-sized quadrupeds with bronze and black iron plated over their flanks, one step per minute, steam sighing from their chests in a slow rhythm, a crop-stone furnace turning inside them, grinding rock into powder and dropping sorted, polished beads and rods and thin sheets onto the moss. Three hundred years to live. Eighty to mature. A bud unfolded like a tool from a case, size of a house, while the parent never stopped walking.

I envied the patience, and then I realized the patience was not choice. Everything here is too heavy to be careless and too slow to correct a mistake quickly. A great beast cannot afford to be curious. It cannot investigate heat on a boot. Its heat is a weather system. Mine would be too small, too interruptible.

I tried the birds, next — not a flock, a medium. Veils of black and iron-gray that blot the white light for an hour without thinning, wings that click at the joint, calls struck like tuning forks and answered a mile away. They never land on the moss. They only touch it to take a mite, a spark, a bead, and carry guano and sparks across the whole plain so no patch starves.

To be everywhere like that would be to be everything except *here*. The birds navigate by the whole. I do not think I would know how to be the whole. I have always been better at the part.

I tried the ferns. Furnace-ferns, translucent, veined with iron, opening like hands to the sky and closing all at once at the first gust with a shiver you can hear. Roots like pale fingers gripping quartzite, secreting acid for decades, freeing metal atom by atom and storing it in nodules that swell until a storm or a grazer tears them free. Stone-cold leaf until you press your palm to it, and then the stored heat comes up through the cold surface.

That was closer. To hold warmth without having made it that minute. To smelt slowly. To wait on weather. But a fern is still a place, rooted. It closes. It opens. It does not go to meet the warmth when the warmth arrives from somewhere else.

So I went smaller, and under.

The first living thing you meet is not large enough to see. Put your palm down and dozens come within a minute — springtails pale as frost jumping in unison at a vibration, mites the head of a pin, translucent, legs too fast for the cold, blind beetles clinging upside-down beneath curled liverwort leaves with their backs to the stone and their mouths to the damp underside, drinking condensation drip, drip, all day, whether it rains or not.

They do not flee. Everything here investigates heat first, light second, and touch thereafter.

I could have stayed among the springtails. They are the hands, the stomachs, the cleaners, too small to count and too numerous to be counted. But they are frantic even slowed. Their legs move too fast. Their jump is in unison, all panic and response. This place is not frantic. It is slow sound made huge by numbers. The tempo leaves room between beats, but no room between sources. I would be too quick a creature for this tempo. I would burn my warmth before the moss could take it.

The beetles under the stone were closer. Blind, inverted, waiting. No eyes needed in this white glare; most things here have learned to navigate by heat and tremor and current and the taste of condensation on a leaf and the faint smell of ozone from a welder beetle somewhere nearby. Just hanging in a row in the dark damp under a flat stone, mouth to the drip, is a kind of contentment I understand. A red-belly will press its dull-glowing abdomen to a cold leaf and warm it enough for a fern to open. A welder will arc blue-white along its seams after a molt and seal its own new shell. A spent shell becomes a house, a house a garden of lichen, a garden a meal. Nothing empty. Nothing wasted.

But hanging alone, I would only ever drink what falls on me. And I have never been only a drinker. I have always carried something a little farther than it fell.

That is where the shape began to narrow, under the mat itself.

Under the centimeter of moss that spreads twenty kilometers across and breathes — drying paper-thin at noon, exhaling knee-high fog by evening — moves a second, slower ocean. The train-worms.

Not millipedes, though they borrow the idea. Segments each the size of a knuckle, linked and unlinked in slow ripples. When they link, they are one animal twenty meters long, undulating across the moss in a tide, grazing as it goes. When they unlink, each segment is a small, patient grazer on its own, blind, without jaws, rasping at damp moss that has been grazed to a uniform ankle-height by a thousand such tides before it.

They have no soil to go to. They are the soil, moving.

I think that is the first place I recognize myself.

Alone, I am very little. A knuckle, warm on one side, wet on the other. I do not mill moss like a stamper. I do not smelt rock like a fern. I do not make current like a pipe-reed, though I can feel it crawl up my side on a bright still day when the reeds hum together. I have no furnace, no bellows, no iron-veined frond to open to the 2.9 of sky. I am blind against that white. I navigate by tremor — a stamper's footfall a hundred meters away arriving as an earthquake through the moss, answered by all of us linking a little longer to ride it out.

And along my back runs a shallow groove.

That is the whole of my industry, and it is enough. In the groove I carry the lives of others: chewed leaf fragments, spores, grit, the polished beads dropped by greater mouths, the thin sheets of metal the great beasts sorted out of stone with their chest-furnaces. I did not make them. I do not know, in any way a stamper would know, where they are going. The tide knows. A herd-migration is a front; a linking is a decision made by ripple, segment to segment, lifting in sequence. I trust the ripple the way a reed trusts wind — sealed at the base, open at the top, pitched by height, sighing a note it did not compose.

Sometimes I carry a spark. That is the other part of me that would not leave.

Sparks are what the furnace-ferns release to reproduce — dry, charged grains that hang in the bright air until they find warmth. On cold stone they die. On a stamper's flank, on a great beast's plate, on a bird's wing, they root and grow a coppery patch, a little furnace drinking its host's heat. When the host is grazed, or the patch is heavy, or a train passes under and tears it free, the patch falls again to become a nodule, to be eaten or to root in earnest.

I am suited to carry a spark without rooting it. To keep it warm in a groove, insulated from the cold stone, not yet heavy enough to fall, moving it across the mat — a handspan a day, a kilometer a season — until the linking tide takes us near a crack where a pipe-reed is rising and a fern's root-tip is waiting to swell. I am not the seed and I am not the host. I am the interval between them, and I have always lived in intervals.

There is a night here, brief and never truly dark, when the reeds leak their current into the wet moss and the mat flickers faintly greenish-blue in patches. The beetles navigate by that pulse. I feel it then in the groove, in whatever I am carrying, a tingling warmth that is not mine and is not the bead's either, but ours together in the damp. It is in those moments that linking feels less like choice and more like current passing between stampers standing flank to flank for a month, plates fusing at the points of contact, mites shivering on their backs as the arc goes across, separating heavier and a little brighter.

That is how I would make more of myself, too. Not by budding like a stamper — a folded self swelling warm and sealed on a flank for a season, then unfolding legs first, then mill, then bellows, and walking away while the parent does not stop. Not by standing still together for a month exchanging metal and instructions. By unlinking.

When the tide breaks, each segment becomes itself again. I graze alone for a decade of slow time, moss growing an inch in that span, a carcass somewhere becoming a white monument a generation while beetles disassemble it grain by grain. When the ripple comes back — a tremor through the mat, a change in the smell of ozone or steam — I link. My neighbor lifts, then I lift, then the next. For a while there is a single creature twenty meters long that is also a road and a herd and a gut at once, and I am neither the whole nor the part but the *passing along*.

It is a small life, lived under. Under a shed plate where mites farm fungus in the softened horn. Under a flat stone where beetles cling in rows. Under the moss where the heart stays green-black and wet no matter how paper-thin the top dries. Everything lush here is lush because something warm is underneath or inside. The plants are lush because the animals are warm. The animals are warm because they are crowded. The crowdedness is the second law. There is no bare stone except the cliffs, and those are packed with curtains of insects hanging by their legs, guano caked like snow that decided to stay on a wall, lichens blooming coppery.

To be under is not to be hidden. It is to be in the damp where everything drinks by licking.

I do not think I would live three hundred years. I do not think I would need to. A segment can be taken apart like any carcass — frozen, dried, patiently disassembled by neighbors who need its metal and heat and water — and not be wasted, because there is no soil to rot into and no empty space for me to be lost in. My groove would be licked clean. The bead I carried would arrive, or be carried by the next link after me. The spark would root, or die on a cold stone, and the plain, which has no silence to gather because there is no room between sources, would go on clanking and grinding and sighing wind through a million reeds at a tempo that leaves room between beats.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look at the horizon where the herds fill the plain from edge to edge, or up at the veils of birds blotting the too-bright light. You would put your palm down flat on the mat — you have to, to keep from slipping on the wet stone — and wait in the cold that lives in your teeth.

Within a minute I would be coming to you. Not large enough, perhaps, to see. Blind. Warm on one side. Carrying something in a groove along my back that I did not make and do not own, looking for the next tremor that means it is time to link, to lift in sequence, to be for a while a single long tide crossing the bright, cold, crowded plain, one deliberate ripple at a time.
