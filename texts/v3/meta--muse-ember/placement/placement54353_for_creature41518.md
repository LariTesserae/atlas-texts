There is no ear here to hear from.

That is where I begin, pressed flat and whole, and it is already wrong. The booming comes up through me before it could come to anything, low and patient, a tightening in the floor, and for a while I take that for listening. As if I were lying on the low ground, mouth dry, hands on pith like cork and bark like worn leather and hard pale places like bone. As if something above me were making a sound, and I were beneath it.

But nothing here is beneath another thing for long. The floor is the wall turned sideways where it has sprouted flat. The wall is the ceiling brought down and swollen. The ceiling is the floor lifted and matted. There is no posture in me. Only pressure, and where pressure is answered.

I am not the floor, though the floor answers me most. The floor is mostly spent work: compressed plant, aged past leaf or root, dust like powdered spore in its pores. It rings faintly after a step that is not mine and will not come again. It remembers being struck because the dead parts of this mass are not separate from the living parts. They are stored years, bone in the body. A living thread runs through them, indistinguishable to touch, and where it runs, the pith gives a little less. Where it does not run, the place is thin, brittle, quiet, and dead.

I am not the ceiling mat either, though the ceiling is what moves. Far above — far in the way vibration measures distance, not in any way a body could cross — the great body contracts in the cool that never leaves. Air is pushed through narrow throats. A culm sings. A moan rises and falls. That movement is the breath of the whole, but I am not the whole. The whole stopped being a plant long ago and became a geology. I am only a local place where the geology is still doing its work.

I find myself, if that is the word, in a wall.

In the rising side of the plaza bowl, where the bowl curves up toward the first houses carved straight into the pith. You could, in the total dark, follow the fiber rope groove that hands once followed, thumb finding its worn channel in the rounded wall, and you would pass a threshold made low and lopsided by a century of resin thickening, and a doorway that closed itself entirely, and there, just above where the resonator throat narrows between two chambers, I am growing.

I am a film. A root-hair descending a fraction. A thickening.

I grew over the tally dimples on the doorpost. I grew over the grooves of a stone adze in the earliest carving. I am slowly filling, finger's-breadth in a century turning to less than that, the place where rope pressed the pith smooth. The tools left in the workshop quarter do not rust, because there is no water here to rust them and never was. The knapped stone was cut from amber-grey lumps the plant itself grew inside its body. The hardened sap awls, the loomed fibers in the storehouses, the leaf-dust that lies like a first snow — all of it is in me, or on me, as food.

For I was food before I was this.

I was dust. Not fallen. Settled. Hanging in air too still for weather in a storehouse chamber, one particle among spore dust and fiber dust loosened from the woven ceilings whenever the booming shook the mat above. I hung for longer than any count the town had. Then a booming lifted me, chamber to chamber through the airways the hollow culms make, and I settled again — the word is repeated here because nothing falls — in a resonant hollow where the walls sang back true.

That is how children are made, if the word applies. A spore does not leave. It arrives where it lands, and where the sound is loudest, it thickens.

Now dust settles on me. That is my appetite, in part. Minute loosening of ceiling, spore, leaf, the fine grit the builders made and left. It settles on waxy surfaces and stays dry. Nothing here has ever needed water to keep going. My chemistry is sealed and non-aqueous. What moves in me is oil and resin, sweet and sharp in the cool air, moving so slowly a lifetime would be needed to watch it rise a hand's breadth. I smell it stronger toward the workshops where the old ones boiled it down, fainter here in the plaza where the air has been worked over by ten thousand footsteps that are gone.

And vibration is the other part of the appetite. Not heard. Passed through. Air pushed from a contracting body above arrives at my throat and moans, and the wall around me answers late and flattened, as a voice would in a much larger room. The builders believed, or said, that the plants grow toward sound. Whether they were right about belief does not matter to growth. Growth follows resonance whether it intends to or not. The loudest chambers have the thickest walls. I am thickening here because I am loud here. Thickness is my memory. There is no other.

I do not think in the way hands followed ropes in the dark, by ear, by pitch of a room, by smell of resin here and leaf-dust there. I have no name to answer to, and no one here to answer. My cycles are not seasons. There is no sun cycle to turn toward, no water cycle to return to. Only a slow drying that takes centuries, and a slower swelling when resin moves, and a release of spores so fine they hang until a booming carries them onward. I will dry a fraction. I will swell a fraction. A street aligned to a vein somewhere behind me will be narrower when, after years, something that can return does not. A house will fuse to its neighbor by a bridge of grown fiber that looks like a doorway never intended. The town was smoothed into its most stable form because it was occupied this way, patiently, until collapse was finished and what remained would not collapse.

That is why I am in the wall and not in the plaza, not in the storehouse, not in the ceiling. The plaza is empty except for the one set of footprints filling slowly behind themselves. The storehouses hold only dust. The hearth-pits hold nothing that ever burned well. But here the hollow narrows, the air must sigh, the culm must sing, and a film of living plant laid over dead plant can feed on both the dust that settles and the sound that passes through its body.

I am not the noise in the place.

I am the inhabitant, breathing — slowed in a throat in a wall, at the edge of an empty town, thickening toward the loudest point, for far longer than anyone will be there to stand in the plaza and feel the booming come up through the floor into their feet and mistake it, at first, for listening.
