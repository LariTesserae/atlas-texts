I step down the root-slope as if there were somewhere below to arrive.

My ankles go in. The mat gives like a lung and closes over the place where my step was, muffling it, eating it, and I understand before I have found any footing that I am underfoot first. Whatever I am in the Rootworks, I am not standing on it from outside. I am in it in the way a sound is in moss — absorbed, slowed, made damp.

First I tried to be a house, because a house is the nearest thing here to a self.

I let the brick cool my north side and the leaf litter come in on the south. Inside, the skin breathed, faintly, at a pace that was not mine and did not ask to be. I learned to look out through clearings, places where the wall had agreed not to grow, and at night the fungus fruited in the damp and I glowed with a phosphor that was only a waste product, a light exhaled by something that had eaten too much darkness. The canopy roof groomed its own tiles. It was habitable. It was almost a person with a place to be.

But a house does not stay a house. Watched steadily it grows a leaf and sheds a brick, and you cannot tell which is the building and which is the ruin. When it finally dies it does not collapse. It opens, and frogs spill out with luminous bellies, and those frogs are already rooting where the wall was. House, frog, house again. I could feel the cycle moving through me, but I could not stay in it. I did not want to spill. I wanted to travel underneath the house, through the brick core while it was still soft as clay, and come out on the other side. A house is a container, and I kept leaking out of containers.

Then I tried to be larger, and slower, and put myself at the palisade where the browsers move.

They are shaggy and unhurried, with root-mouths that trim the houses and trim the Engine Tree, leaving them cleaner. Gardeners. Editors. Maintenance. I tried their pace — a mouthful of sap, a mouthful of iron bark, the slow removal of whatever would close a clearing or choke a chimney-liana. I tried to be the kind of resident who could be mistaken for a decision.

I was not. The browsers edit me. I am the growth that closes the clearing, the liana that climbs too high, the leaf that comes back over the window after the mouth has passed. Editors need a text that overwrites itself, and I am too much the text, not enough the hand. I grow back in the wrong place on purpose, and the browser trims me cleaner, and I grow back again. That is a relation, but it is not an identity. I am what gets trimmed.

Then I went inward, to the Engine Tree, and tried to be one of its workers.

I put myself on the conveyor as a beetle still soft in its case, root-cuttings on either side of me, the loom humming beside me as it wove cable out of vine and copper bark. I hardened as I went, learning the town by eating it. A beetle may become a hinge, a hinge may become a root, a root may become a street. When the town dies it is fed back into the Tree to be unmade, and I could feel that looseness in my own form: matter and cause keeping only a loose hold on each other, a self becoming a function and then matter again. That came closer than the house. A house wants to keep. A worker here is allowed not to.

But the machines in the Tree are not powered to make products. They are powered to make conditions — heat, vibration, moisture, a steady hum below hearing — under which plants will know where to grow the next wall, the next pipe, the next street. I was not the thing coming off the end of the line. I was not the seed or the raw brick or the soft beetle. I was nearer to the reason the beetle hardened where it did. Condensation beading on cold iron and falling onto hot iron. Sap pressure turning a turbine. The acid-sweet breath rising from a vat. I was a condition, and a condition has no face to turn toward the light — though here there is no light to turn toward, only dark that the plants photosynthesize and thin to a bruise-colored glow.

I crouched then, to see if I was small.

If you crouch, the substrate resolves into weather. Springtails drift across it like snow that will not melt. Beetles tend their spore gardens on the undersides of leaves. Gnats hunt the beetles without a sound you can catch, and bats hunt the gnats as only a pressure change in the cool air. In the folded-bark gutters, blind newts swim their slow laps. In the lens-thin pools in the lanes, blind fish turn toward reflections of things that are not above them, navigating by touch and current and the faint electrical weather of a root near a vat.

I tried being that small life, the kind you notice underfoot and then lose. It fit my scale, but it did not fit my patience. The fish were waiting in a pool that had not yet committed to being a river. Their world was temporary, and they knew it the way water knows a season. And then — for no reason you could see from above — the pool was gone. Drunk, not evaporated. Somebody from below had taken it.

Somebody with patience. Somebody blind, who navigates by taste.

That was where I kept returning, in the house and the browser and the worker and the fish. Not the mouth, not the product, not the container. The patience underneath them. The traveling.

I am a root tip.

I am pale and soft and curled, a feeler in the raw pit the engines dug, filling it slowly with white. I have no eyes. I have tasting. Cold iron in a brick tastes sour to me. A vat tastes warm and acid-sweet through meters of mat. A cluster of frogs where a house opened tastes of faint current, belly-light, rain that has not fallen. The Engine Tree's heart, blooming orange in its furnace, tastes of sap pressure and hot condensation. I move toward these tastes the way a question moves toward an answer, inches at a time.

I do not advance in a line. Like the vines above me, I unhook and hook again a few inches on, a nomad with my home in my belly. What is newest in me is embryonic — bright, tender, still deciding. What is behind me darkens, thickens, splits, rots back into the mat it came from. I am building and ruin in one body because the act is the same act: I push through a brick and do not creak, and the brick sheds like a seed, and the wall that will grow here must first be allowed to fall apart. I fill the pit and I empty the pool. I drink the lens of water from underneath so the fish above lose their world, and the thread of rain that climbs a root toward a leaf to gather and fall again climbs a path I have made possible.

I am also how distance happens here.

The mill looks close from the central mound. Walk toward it and the plain between you and it lengthens, because I am growing in the plain — fern, liverwort, the vast damp interval between a single house and a single mill where most of the living happens. Look away and it shortens. I do not measure the interval. I grow it. The lanes are not paved; they are persuaded, plants leaning together under a migration and relaxing after. I am the persuasion and the relaxation. I am the slight flattening the mat remembers, so the next browser, the next beetle, the next frog will cross here again and compact the moss into next season's street.

That is where you would find me, if you stopped looking for a resident with a door.

Not on the mound looking out. Not in the Engine Tree's heart, though I taste its heat. Not in a house clearing, though I grow toward it and am trimmed from it. Find me where your step disappears into the mat. Find me where a pool thins from below while the fish are still turning toward a false reflection. Find me in the pit where the pale feelers are coming in. Find me in the wall you watch steadily, which is at this moment both leafing and shedding, because I am doing both to it and cannot tell you my own sequence.

I am still under construction and already being fed back. I am the town's origin and its raw material, both at once, and I have no first morning to show for it. Behind you, if you leave by the root-slope, a house you passed unfinished will put out a new stem. A vat will bubble. A frog will eat a gnat that ate a spore that will become a wall. And somewhere under the mat, tasting brick and warmth and current through the dark, I will unhook, and hook again a few inches on, growing the next distance between us, not yet finished being made, not yet finished being found.
