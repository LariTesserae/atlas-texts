I am not on the ground.

You would think to look there. Everything in the bowl teaches you to: the pale felted moss pressed flat over almost everywhere, black humus holding a footprint long after the foot has gone, puddles lying between stones and reflecting the only sky they have. The ground gives under you slowly, like something wet for years, and smells of green iron and old stone.

But water does not fall here. It forms. And it forms first where you would not expect it — on the undersides of leaves, on the north faces of stones, on anything that stays still long enough.

That is where I am. Not first on the ground, but first in the air above it, a hand’s breadth above the moss.

For a while I mistook myself for the things that stand at intervals over that moss. They are so entirely present when they are present that it is easy to think everything here must be entire. The fern the height of a person, its trunk no thicker than a wrist, unspooling one frond at a time over a decade. If I were the fern I would be seen from anywhere on the rim, and seen all at once. I am not seen all at once.

The small tree with pale bark peeling in wet ribbons — you can count its branches, so open is its crown. Its flowers hang under the foliage, opening a fraction in the morning, closing a fraction in the afternoon, letting pollen fall into the condensing air like ash into water. If I were the tree I would have that daily arithmetic, that pale underside world of my own. I live under foliage, but I am not the foliage.

The clump of broad leaves, each one the size of a spade, each cupped so shallowly it holds a lens of water it never quite spills. That came closest. A leaf yellows into translucence where it hangs, sags, dissolves in place, running down its own stem in a clear thread so slow you could miss a day of it. It never falls. I have never fallen either. But a leaf finishes by thinning. I do not finish. I remain.

After a while — and a while here is not measured in days, but in drips, and ripples, and inches of moss — I understood I was the thing hanging below them, between them and the ground they never quite touch. I am the vine.

My beginning, if that is a word for something that looped before it reached, is lower and older than I am now. I rooted first around a boulder sunk to its waist in soil the boulder itself helped make. Root over root, loop over loop, I knitted the stone into a basket, the way growth here knits and folds and cups and splits when it has the same wet, warm, mineral materials for long enough. The stone under those roots is warm from the sun and wet from itself. Moss has crept over the loops, an inch a year, eating the stone very slightly while the stone sweats and feeds it back. That ring around the boulder is still me, though I rarely think of it as mine. It is the part of me that arrived.

From there a stem found its way up — not quickly, nothing here is quick — into the open crown of the little tree, twenty paces from the next whole thing, and from a branch I can count so easily, I descend again.

That descent is where I live now. A thin strand in the mist, beaded on its underside before anything above me is beaded on top. In the morning, when the air is full and the light is diffused and ordinary, a bead swells there for a very long time. I lengthen by a fraction to meet it. My tip darkens. It drinks the mist — not rain, not a stream, just the nearness of water — and then, as the day changes the way a flower closes a fraction, I retreat. I grow and un-grow in place.

I have been doing this for a very long time.

It is not reaching the ground. A hand’s breadth is a great distance when you have decided, by habit rather than decision, not to cross it. The moss skin is below, blueish here in the shadow of the rim, greener where the midday finds it. If I touched it I would become part of its pale population, spreading, eating stone. Instead I hang in the interval. Everything here is an interval: twenty paces of bare moss and stone between one enormity and the next, minutes for a ripple to cross a pool, an hour for a drip to swell on a stone lip and fall, a lifetime for the weep in the western rock to migrate two hand-widths. My interval is smaller, and vertical, and I keep it by not keeping it.

Above me, a leaf-tip releases its burden. *Plip.* Somewhere else a drip hangs and falls. The sound does not carry over the lip of the bowl. It does not need to. There is nothing to carry it to. No buzz over the puddles, no frog in the seep, no worm turning the humus, no bird, no track, no call. I do not move between the plants. I do not need to be pollinated, carried, eaten, or frightened, and neither do they. Above me spores leave the fern in a fine visible weather and settle in green ripples on stone below. Pollen falls straight down. A leaf dissolves where it hangs. Decay is done by water and fungus and time, steadily, at the rate moss creeps and the one fallen branch — there is only ever one you can find — lies halfway to humus, its bark perfect, its heart soft.

I think in the same way. I drink the mist a hand’s breadth from the ground, and I think again. The thought is not a sentence. It is the bead forming on my underside because I have stayed still long enough. It is the root-tip darkening a little more each season. It is the ring round the boulder tightening by a loop I will never count. Colour is kept here for no eye at all, and kept anyway — the deep black-green where water sits on the leaves, the bluer moss, my own dark tip. I am kept the same way.

Leave and return after years and I will be the same. Not unchanged. The frond above me that was unspooling will be open. The pool between stones will be a finger’s width deeper in one corner. The drip that took an hour to form will have fallen a thousand times. My tip will be a hair lower, or a hair higher. The moss will have taken another inch of stone. You will recognise me instantly and you will not be able to say why, because I have settled, the way the rim has settled, into a rounded, worn, unsharp state, and I hold it: warm, wet, high, small, empty between living things.

Stand in the centre once, before you go, and the mist will be coming in a little thicker. Every surface will be beaded. The sparse green shapes will stand far apart in their pale moss, dripping in no hurry. If you stay still long enough, water will form on you too, first where you would not expect it.

That is how you will find me. Not on the ground. A hand’s breadth above it, hanging from the underside of the world, drinking what condenses, and retreating, and drinking again — a small, dark tip that has learned, over a very long time, simply to stay still long enough for water to arrive.
