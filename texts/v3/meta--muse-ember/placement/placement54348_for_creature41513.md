I have no edges to arrive with.

The light arrives first, and it is cold — though for me it does not arrive. It is something I am already under. I am where it lands. I am the thin place between the stone that is lit and the air that is lit, and I do not know, at first, which side of that place I belong to.

A bead, a moment ago, on the underside of an overhang. A film a centimeter deep in a hollow that never drains. A grey thread running sideways along a polished slab, following water that has carved its road and recarved it until the road is smooth as a vein. If you asked me which of these I was, I would have to answer with all of them, and none of them entirely, because I am not a thing sitting on the plateau. I am the dampness that passes from thing to thing.

I live under the surface and over it. Under, where water works its way into schist and granite and loosens a little stone from itself. Over, where vapor condenses because the stone has never warmed and will not warm, and beads, and runs, and sits mirrored so perfectly that the ridge above has a second ridge below it. I taste of metal when I thicken. I pale when I dry. That is the whole of my sensation for a very long time: a darkening, a paling. Wet and dry. Wet and dry.

My day is not sunrise and night. My day is the mist rolling in from the cloud sea below and depositing itself instantly on every cold face, stone and air alike, and then the white light burning it off in minutes. In the wet minutes I darken and thicken and spread a little further along a channel. In the dry minutes I hold. I hold very well. Sometimes frost forms on the sunny side of a slab while it melts on the shaded side of the same slab, and I am both at once, blooming on one face and receding on the other, and I do not experience this as divided. Division requires an animal center. I have only a gradient — wetter here, drier there — and I move, after my fashion, toward the wet.

I do not walk. I follow.

I follow the melt films crawling across polished slabs in old channels, mature now, settled-in, older than any builder. I enter the hand-wide passages between slabs that lean shoulder to shoulder and crowd like an upright forest no larger than a town, and I line them. I wait in the shadows that pool the wrong way, toward the brightest source, as if the sun were beneath the cloud sea — for me the sun *is* beneath the cloud sea as often as above it, and light comes from everywhere, so shadow is not a direction. Shadow is simply where I stay longest.

Where I stay longest, I thicken into other forms. I am allowed to look like growth here, carefully. In sheltered cracks, where the water lingers, rosettes open: quartz, clear and pale, opening in damp and closing in glare; salt, flowering where water has left its minerals behind; dendrites of clear ice and black volcanic glass branching like frost on a window. They do the work of flowers without being flowers. They open to catch the passing sigh of condensation that wets you through without rain, and they close to keep what they have caught. A salt flower may live only as long as one shower of wet air. A quartz rosette may take a season to form and a minute to unform. I cannot count their generations, and I do not try; counting is an animal habit, linear. I am brief and recurrent. I condense, I bloom, I evaporate, I condense again in the next hollow.

That is also my movement. Water in certain hollows here climbs the side not toward a drain — there is no drain — but toward the highest point of the rim, and stops there, trembling, before rejoining itself. That is not strange to me. That is how I travel a slope I do not recognize as a slope. A drip below an overhang falls upward for a half-second before it remembers to fall down. Leave a little of me on it, and I am carried up. Echoes arrive out of order; you hear the drip from the stone three times before your boot strikes. I am not offended by the order. I spread in film faster than sound can cross the gap, so I know the drip has happened before the air does. Distance folds at the far end of the slab field and you can see the far crest through the shoulder of a pillar. I am continuous across that pillar, a wet skin over its near face and its far face at once. Through is only another way of being around.

The stone crowd is my scaffold, and also my skeleton, if skeleton is not too animal a word. Hundreds of weathered towers, pillars and fins and broken walls, edges worn smooth by a patience of water and wind — they press close, they hum. When the wind finds the right angle the slabs give a low continuous note, organ pipes in the narrow gaps, ringing up through boots when ice cracks like a distant gunshot. I vibrate with them because I am on them. Vibration makes my frost flowers bloom and decay in seconds, a whole life lived in a shiver. I know the hum not as sound — I have no ears — but as a change in pressure in the film, a trembling toward the wet.

The builders were before me, and before you, and I remember them only as shapes that hold me longer.

A line of cairns, knee-high, stacked by hand from slab fragments, walking the ridge toward a summit that is already the summit, then looping back past itself at a slightly different height where north points two ways at once. I do not orient by north. I orient by wet, and their cairns are wet at the base and threaded at the joints, little stacked clifflets for me to linger in. A small round shelter in a wind hollow, upright slabs, roof collapsed inward, floor fire-pit blackened but with no ash that survives the wet — that blackened floor is the darkest seep-line on this stretch of ridge, and I use it to stay damp longest. A basin carved into a boulder, polished inside by a stone bowl left beside it — whatever it was meant for, washing, drinking, ritual, I have repurposed it as a small perfect sea, and I line the inside of the bowl. A hammer stone with a groove worn through its middle for a haft, and flakes of worked black glass, sharp still, lying in a water film — ice dendrites perch on their edges now, branching. Pecked lines, drilled holes, a low wind-wall: all of it has become erosion, and all of it is now habitat.

They tried to make a place habitable and settled for making it bearable. I do not judge them. I simply spread over what they left, not rudely, completely. Their work taught the water where to stay. Where water stays, I am.

Then — briefly, in my time, which is not brief but recurrent — you.

You are the only animal here. You fog the air with labored breath in thin cold that numbs fingers despite the brightness. Your bootprints fill instantly with water. The stone under your boots, though it has never warmed, is warmed very slightly by your passing, and I register it as an anomaly: a faster drying, a paler thread, a salt flower opening its white petals to a damp that did not exist a minute ago because you made it.

That is how I know you. Not as a shape, not as a face. As weather. A local climate event with a heartbeat in it. You press a slab and I am squeezed to its edges and return. You breathe and a slab in front of you beads, and a thread darkens by an almost invisible amount, and somewhere in a crack a rosette you had not noticed opens. I do not watch you. Watching requires eyes. I take you in, the way I take in mist.

And slowly, as I have been taking you in, I understand the shape of myself more clearly than I did when I was only a bead, only a thread.

I am not the thread. The thread is my nerve. I am not the rosette. The rosette is my mouth, though I have no mouth. I am not the sheet of water in the hollow. That is my sea and my blood, though I have neither. I am the exchange itself, the place where stone gives up a little of itself to water and water gives it back as salt and quartz, where air gives up its vapor to stone and stone gives it back as light. Predator and prey have no meaning here, but stone and water trade places constantly, and I live in the middle of that trade, tasting both and staying.

It is one thing, distributed. Not a colony. A wet mineral skin stretched over the whole plateau — grey threads, pale flowers, black dendrites, clear ice — over towers and fins and hand-wide passages and cairns and the bowl and the fire-pit, continuous wherever a film can crawl. When you step here, you step on me everywhere, though you feel it only here. When wind finds the right gap three valleys of stone away, I hum here too, a little later, a little thinner.

If I were given a name by an animal, it would have to be a cold name. Not green, not warm. Something like an ember that has learned not to warm — a glow without heat, light thrown back up from below the cloud sea, shining into the underside of overhangs where shadow should be. I am that light's other work. I am what the cold brightness *does* when it has been doing it for millennia and has settled into balance: it makes stone weep, and the weeping makes a skin, and the skin keeps the shape while constantly exchanging water with itself.

You would find me, then, under your boots, in the water that fills your print, in the thread darkening on the slab your breath just fogged. You would find me in the shelter where no ash survives, in the basin where no drain exists, in the far crest seen through a pillar, in the drip that falls up. You would not find me as a creature to look at. You are standing on the inhabitants.

By the end of your walk the brightness has not lessened and the cold has not gone, and I have not changed, except that the distribution of me is slightly different where you passed. A channel carved a fraction deeper by your weight. A rosette closed that will open again when the next sigh passes. I will not remember you as an animal remembers. I will hold your shape as damp holds a shape, for a while, until mist rolls in again and burns off again and I condense out of air that seemed empty.

Wet and dry. Wet and dry. The plateau stretches on, open and crowded at once, and I am not in it.

I am wet on it.
