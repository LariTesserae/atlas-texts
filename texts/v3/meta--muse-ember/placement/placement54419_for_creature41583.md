Before I am awake, I am listening.

The wall is cold against my ear before it is anything else. Above me, in the dark of the room, cloth is being wrung out over a bucket — a slow, heavy sound, hands finding the trough along the lowest wall where the slate has been weeping since before light. Inside the wall, where my cheek can feel it, there is a pipe. A clay pipe. If it is working, the condensation that has run down the inside face will trickle, plate by plate, toward the cistern. If it is not, it will be stopped with grit, or lime, at the junction.

I am too small to wring properly. A bucket is taller than my wrists are strong. So I am given the bone needle instead, and sent to sit with my ear to that junction and tell, by sound, whether the house is holding its water.

At first I could not tell a trickle from a clog. I know now. There are words for this I have no need to say aloud. A drip that will hold. A drip that will cut. A seep that comes at dawn and is gone by midday, and comes again at dusk. The wall above me is speaking low while it works, the way everyone does here, because the thick damp bounces nothing back. The clunk of a wooden bucket against stone. The fluting of wind in a crack-lane. Nothing else. No bird. No shout. Only water moving and hands.

When the trickle runs clear, my morning is done. I am sent up.

I lift the hatch in my floor — it was someone's roof, and is now my floor, and will be someone else's ceiling again — and step barefoot onto the roof of the house below. My neighbour does the same from the other side. We cross on a roof that belongs to neither of us and to both of us. My reed cloak is stiff with last night's damp and softens as the light reaches it. My feet are wet before I have remembered to be awake. They are always cold.

From a roof the town looks like it is wearing its own hair. Where the rain has washed the salt away, moss and small tough weeds grow thick on the north sides, creeping down in green beards. I am sent to pick them sparingly, handful by handful, for broth. In the lanes below, the salt-tufts in the cracks I step around. A lane is a crack, a gutter, a boundary. A tuft is a boundary too, stubborn in its own place.

I go down ladders the way others run down paths. Notched driftwood, steep, certainly. Strong calves. Sure feet on the rubbery mud. The houses are low and stacked through themselves: the oldest in the lowest dry room, their children lifted above them, their children's children above that. To visit down is to visit back. To go up is to go forward. A door often opens straight onto a neighbour-cousin's roof, and I wipe my feet before I cross, though the mud is never off them anyway.

There is one place that is not a floor. The newest splinter at the highest corner, the rock that still tilts after rain and will not hold anything square. The adults forbid building there, which is exactly why it is ours. No one else can stand there and see the plain in every direction without standing on a roof. We play there in the bright, colourless light, surrounded by weighted roofs and thin curls of smoke that dissolve at once. We do not have teams. We have listening.

We mime the work we already know. A careful hand along a wall feeling for a leak, because a leak left alone will cut a channel and take a wall. A climb down a ladder. A stoop at low tide for crabs among the pools at the foot of the table. One of us presses an ear to the rock and counts.

That is my work again, at night. Later, when the condensation has stopped and the waterfalls off the edge have roared for their hour after rain and are gone, the table begins to tick as it settles, plate by plate, like a bowl cooling. I hear it cross under the houses. I count where it comes next. If it is a new tick in the morning, the tide will have undermined a corner in the night and there will be a new crack across a lane by evening, and someone's father will be filling it with stone before dark.

In the middle of the day I sit on the rim of the central cistern with my feet dangling, on rota, and watch the fish.

They are pale and blind and no bigger than my thumb, turning slowly in the black water where the pipes pour. The children feed them crumbs, and the grey moths that drown themselves happily on the condensation at the rim, skimmed off by hand before they sink. They are not eaten. They are a sign. If one dies, the cistern is not drunk from until the pipes are cleared and the water runs clear again. It is not a story we tell. It is the only instrument we have that is always in the water.

When the tide has been in, I follow an eel with a bucket through the lanes. It threads the drains, slick and black in the ankle-deep run-off, cleaning them as it goes. It does not belong to the house whose pipe it used. It is kept mostly, and taken only when a drain runs clear and it is fat and slow, and then shared between the houses. No one wastes an eel. At low water we go down hand over hand, buckets tied to our waists, to work the fresh rock among the crabs, and to cut the reed at the base carefully, never all of it, because the reed is cloaks and cord and the only thing here that can be woven.

In late summer the whole town whitewashes itself at once, the kiln smoking, hands and forearms white and cracked, lungs full of shell lime by evening. Mudbrick slumps a little each winter. It is always slumping. A house left empty would weep itself to ruin in a season, and so no house is left empty, and no person is left without a place on a ladder that leads somewhere.

I did not know, with my ear to the pipe, that this was a place to be. I only knew the trickle, and whether it ran. Then I knew the roof under my feet, and the splinter of rock under my ear at night, and the rim of the cistern under my hands, and the drain under my bucket. Then I knew I was not in one house. I was in the crack between them — between the wall and the water, between the house above and the house below, between the table and the plain that goes flat and shining to the sea.

Houses here are not inherited. They are continued. People are the same.

In the morning my ear will be against the wall again. The plates will tick again when they cool. The fish will turn in the dark below the rim, and the drip that will hold and the drip that will cut will begin again, and I will know where to go.
