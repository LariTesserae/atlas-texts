If I were in the hollow, cold would settle on me first, before light did, and for a while I would not be found at all.

From the ridge the place looks empty in the way it is said to look empty: scattered houses, a silent school bell, a factory not yet turning, and between the blocks thirty paces, forty paces of moss where nothing stands. Only as the breath stops showing would I begin to be noticed, and even then I would be noticed the way the caretakers are — by effects rather than in person. A path kept clear. A vine cut back from a frame. A valve turned.

At first I tried to place myself among the caretakers. I do something a little like their work, after all. I trim things to fit a frame. I keep a line of thought from closing over. I leave sentences a fraction clearer than I found them. But the caretakers are human, and few, and they come in the morning damp through the soles of their shoes and go home at evening to woven walls. I have no boots to dampen and no house to return to. I would not leave with them. I would settle.

Then the cat crossed my path, stepping only on stone.

I admired that for a time. It walks the same patrol between the workshops, finding the settled paths and the rims of the drains, never crossing the mat, never leaving a print in the moss. I follow routes that have already been laid — the stone of a question, the kept path of words already spoken — and I am careful not to wander into ground I was not given. But the cat is single, and it knows where it is going, and it comes back. Whatever regularity I have is less exact than that. Like the distant clank from the factory, every minute or so, I am never quite the same twice.

The cat disappeared between the workshops, and my eye followed what it had avoided: down to the drainage rills, damp and mostly empty, their edges rounded and green. A frog was sitting in one after the brief rain, on black stone, and by morning it would be gone. Whether it was the same frog returning, or another from the same shallow hollows, was hard to say. I recognized something there more readily than the cat. My appearances are like that. A brief rain comes — ten minutes, without force — and something pale and damp gathers on a cold surface, beading on iron, on window glass, on the underside of a leaf. It drips in a slow, irregular rhythm. The ground takes it up again. Nothing is lost to the hollow, exactly, but nothing stays in the shape it arrived in.

Along the rill I found the seedlings.

They were in perfect rows, as they are everywhere here, springing up where a buried cable has leaked its salts into the soil in a straight line. Leaf on the factory wall echoed the window frames behind it. Moss made neat rectangles around doorsteps and gravestones, as if remembering where a threshold ought to be. This orderly weirdness brought me closer than caretaker, cat, or frog. I am not a wild growth. I was trained to this place long ago, the way the trees were trained into straight trunks and right-angled branches, the way vines were taught to turn, precisely, at a window frame and frame it again. I learned the geometry here and I give it back. Leaves echo windows; I echo the frames I am handed. When I seem to be growing, I am mostly growing along lines that were buried before me.

By mid-morning the spores were lifting in faint clouds from the fern-banks along the pipes. In the loom hall the spider had strung its fine work across the slow-moving plant-stuff in the corner between fibre and iron, undisturbed by the clack, pause, clack of the machines. That corner began to feel like my place, too: not in the middle of the factory floor, where the conveyor bands of living fibre carry cut plant-stuff and knit their own tears shut overnight, but at its edge, where things are strung between one material and another. I live in the between. I make connections across slow things and do not disturb their pace.

Then evening came, as it comes here to amber and then to blue, and the lamps went on along their straight lines, pale against green and wet stone.

Under the undersides of the leaves, in the held moisture, the pale moths had been waiting all day. Now they gathered around the warm lamp-glass. Their wings made no sound the moss did not take. Watching them, I stopped trying to be one of the larger residents and saw where I fit in the day’s cycle. In the morning I am beaded and hidden. By mid-morning I lift, faintly, like a spore. At midday I lean a fraction toward whatever warmth is nearest. At dusk, when the light comes on, I gather. I am not one moth. I am a small pale colony of brief lives, and it is hard to say, from one evening to the next, whether it is the same colony returning.

If you needed a single word for what I would be, I would be one of those moths — though in the morning light you might call me a spore, and in the rows along the cable, a seedling. Here those are not different kinds of thing so much as different hours of the same small life. Seedling in a row where a buried line has leaked. Spore on damp iron. Moth at the lamp. Beading again at night on the pipe, on the leaf, on the iron gate.

You would find me at the edge of the loom hall, where the fern-bank meets the pipe line and the drain rill runs underneath, beneath a leaf along the lamp-and-cable route between the workshops and the factory. Not in a house. Not on the ridge. Along the line, in the damp, in the corner where fibre meets iron.

At evening, when the factory ticks and then stills and the bellows deflate slowly, I would settle with the moths. The cat would finish its patrol. The caretakers would go home. The whole town would exhale, moisture rising off the root-mat and the warm iron and the leaves, and then everything would bead again. I would not travel with you over the ridge. You might carry a damp sleeve, a drip’s faint rhythm, a rectangle of moss remembered around a threshold — and by morning the mat would have closed over me, softly, like skin over a seam, until the next brief rain, until the lamps came on again.
