I would not find myself at the stair-hole. I would not find myself on the roof at all.

Sound comes to me before light does, and I have learned to tell the place by its layers. First, above me, the bats folded into the chimney inlets, squeaking awake only when the shafts go dim. If I were a bat I would be a night thing, dropping insects for the owl below and sleeping through all the work. I am not. I do not sleep through the day. I am awake in the middle of it, when the looms thump and the forge boy's bellows keep time with the potter's wife singing and the drip bowls keep their high counterpoint behind the pots.

Below the bats are the swallows, in clay cups built for them a hundred years ago and repaired every spring whether they need it or not. They do not build. They occupy, and they return, and they leave. The pigeons coo under the eaves and the women climb to them with grain. The vine has learned its strings and climbs branch by stubborn branch to real sun. All of them live higher than I do, nearer the squares of sky. They reach, or they return, or they hang. I do none of those things. I stay.

In the middle air are the cats on the warm wall-caps, owning the passage by sleeping on it, and the mice in the pores of the bricks themselves, in the grain jars, in every warm crack, learning exactly how close they can live to a sleeping cat. The people negotiate past each other there, elbows in, bucket held close, laundry at head height, gourds and rope and caged birds brushing their shoulders. I am brushed past a hundred times a day and I have no shoulders to brush. I am close enough to the mice that I share their cracks, but I do not eat grain. I am close enough to the cats that I share their warmth, but I do not patrol. I am passed by the people as a thing of the wall is passed by, without being quite seen.

As you go down from them, warmth gathers. That is how I know where I am. The tiles above hold the sun and sigh it back down at night, long after the hearths die. From below me comes the forge heat, low and embered, and the steam from the great blackened pot and the sunken tub by the bath hollow. I live where that rising warmth meets a cooler surface and cannot go any further.

On the northern wall, breath and cooking condense and bead and run in a slow line they have known for generations. Under that line someone has permanently set a shallow bowl, and next to it another, and next to those a row of jars with cloths over their mouths. The person who tends them is always someone, in rotation, without thinking about it. When rain comes down the shafts in bright curtains and hisses on the hot tiles and splashes into the cistern, the whole place drums for twenty minutes and then gives its water back hour by hour, tap, tap, from eave to bowl. I am there for that giving back. I am damp when the place has breathed enough to be damp, and I thin out on the dry, dusty days near the hatches, but I do not dry up entirely the way the top terraces do. Down here the air is moist and close and smells of wet clay and boiled grain and thyme, and that is the air I am made of.

That is the truest thing I can say about myself: I am made, and I only grow on made things.

When it rains, the moss appears in fine, bright lines along the mortar cracks of this wall, as if the bricks themselves had learned to photosynthesize. It grows only on made surfaces here. Wild ground, being none, grows nothing. A wild seed that blows down a shaft is transplanted into a box or removed, because an unmade thing staying on its own would be a kind of failure. The children born here have never touched unworked earth. They think of a stone as something fired in a kiln and shaped to look like a rock. I would think the same, if I thought in words. I do not know an unworked surface to miss. I know twenty layers of plaster smoothed over a last repair. I know a step hollowed concave in the middle where feet have passed for generations, though no foot ever rests on me. I know the edge of a door rounded to the thickness of a thumb. I live in the lines between all that keeping, in the mortar, in the crack that was filled and smoothed the same day and still kept a damp memory of itself.

I am not one of the hundred people, and I am not quite one of the other hundred species either. I am the thin green line above the bowls, where many small breaths collect into something that can be used. The bowls are watered by the wall, and the wall is watered by the kitchen and the bath and the bodies below, and I am where it passes through slowest. I collect nothing and I hunt nothing and I ring no bell. I only take the sips this dry place has learned to drink, and I hold them long enough to be green.

If you stand in the central court at about four in the afternoon, with sun in the shaft, a goat pulling at its rope, chickens clucking under the basins, you can hear the farthest loom and the nearest drip at the same time, and feel the heat of the tiles above and the damp cool of the bowl at your feet. Find the northern wall behind the steam, where the condensation runs in its known line. I am there, between the farthest and the nearest, in the interval between one tap and the next — not built upon the earth, and not wild upon it, but agreeing, as everything here has agreed, to be part of what was made.
