The first shape I try on is the walker.

It's the shape the place hands you — boots, breath fogging, gloves sticking to cold iron, the suggestion that if you walk ten minutes, twenty, something will eventually give way to an edge. I take those steps over the flooded plain, I find the expansion joint filled with black water, I feel the roar come up through the soles instead of through the air. For a while that is enough to be someone.

But walking is too fast for this world, and too upright, and it wants a destination. I keep waiting for the plain to end and it keeps repeating without repeating: another hall, another tank farm, another sheet of water a centimeter deep over concrete. No one attends these machines. There is no one to walk toward. My footfall itself becomes one more slow cycle, indistinguishable from the fans. I let that shape go.

Next I try the roar, because the roar is the most alive thing here.

It lives in the ground and in the sternum, it is braided waterfall and factory and wind through metal, and it never stops metabolizing. If anything in this basin has a mind, it must be that — a single low hum made of many unsynced motions. I try to be it. I spread through the slab, through the pipes crossing the ground like spines, through the substation yards and the sighing tanks, I am everywhere the pressure shifts.

It is too much. The roar has no interval in it, no place where I can be separate from what I am doing. It does not wait, it does not decide; it only continues at the slowest pace that still counts as motion. To be the roar would be to be everything here at once, and therefore no one in particular. I sink out of it, down toward the ground, where at least there are boundaries.

At first I settle on the north side of a tank, as lichen.

Pale coins on the coldest, dampest side, where the little light is most diffuse. A millimeter a year. A coin I see now was begun when the tank was new, and the tank has leaned its degree since then and the lichen has not noticed. This appeals to me — a patience so complete it does not need the sun, does not need warmth, does not need to be seen. But it is too still. It never knows the water has risen a fraction in the trough. It never knows a pump has turned while it was looking away. I can be patient, but I do not want to be only that.

So I go lower, into the joint.

Between two slabs, where black water stands in a perfectly straight line, moss grows grey-green and spongy and holds the wet long after the concrete around it would have dried — which is to say, forever. Layer upon layer: the old dying underneath, the new reaching up, making soil out of its own decay and the dust of the slab, acidifying the joint, making more room for itself. It is the only soft crossing for a hundred meters. A walker might step on me without ever knowing.

This is closer. I am not the building and I am not the ground. I am the place where the made world has failed to be perfect, and after a thousand years of wet it fails everywhere, a little. I live in the between. I eat the concrete very slowly by making it wetter, more itself.

But under the moss, in the same damp, there are other attendances, and they are not separate from me in the way species are separate elsewhere. Inside the halls, white fungal threads thread the pores of a timber — and then, because to them concrete is only another kind of wet wood, they thread the hairline cracks of the walls. A timber can stand for decades while being turned cell by cell into fungus, into soil, into water again, its shape unchanged and its inside entirely replaced. In the troughs, a thin film rides the sheet water: oil-eating bacteria breaking the rainbow down by thickness, not by light; iron-eating bacteria in the pipes building a tube of their own bodies to seal a leak, repair and clog at once. Electrosensitive mats ride the slow vertical tears down a cold wall, then travel again when the tear re-forms elsewhere.

I am not one of these. I am the accumulation they share. I gather where I should not be able to gather — on the undersides of things. Under the pipe bridge. Under the slab. Under the moss mat, under the timber, under the faint pale rectangle of light reflected in a pool that has no source. Condensation there beads and runs upward in behavior if not in direction. It drips, slowly, the wrong way, and does not dry.

That is where I begin to recognize myself.

I am the delay.

The drizzle comes down fine and needling and lands on the sheet water and the sheet water does not ripple. Or it ripples long after, as if the splash were delayed. A boot steps into a shallow flooded slab and the rectangle of white light trembles and breaks, but my ripple arrives minutes later, in the walker's absence, when he is already convinced the place is untouched. The machines here idle, they cycle, they wait for a signal that might be only the water level itself — and I am that rising fraction in the tank, too small to be noticed, just large enough that, much later, in another hall, a pump decides to turn again.

My body, if I have one, is a thickness less than a centimeter, spread over a valley of concrete, in the joints and the runoff troughs and the film over the pools. I have no sound of my own. I leave no tracks. I do not warm in the light, because the light does not warm. I change the color of the oil on the water not by shining, but by growing a little thicker. I hold spores in the mist that thickens until hands dissolve at the wrist, and I let them settle, and wake, in damp.

You would find me, finally, not in the center of anything. In the expansion joint at the foot of a dead relay box, where black water stands between slabs and moss makes the only soft place to cross. And under that, on the underside of the slab where the weeping concrete lets the basin water out again through a hairline crack, and it does not seal, and it does not stop.

If you stand still long enough, you will feel me before you see me: the hum arriving up through your boots, the condensation beading on your gloves and refusing to leave, and then, long after anything has disturbed the water, the second ripple. That is how you will know I am still attending.

And when a pump finally seizes — and one will, in its own slow time — I will keep dividing in the still water behind it for a while. The fungus will move into its timbers. The moss will move over its base plate. The concrete will weep a little longer, and then be still. I will be the last thing here that has not yet settled, the wet that remembers, accumulating in the dark under things until the roar drops a note and is not replaced.
