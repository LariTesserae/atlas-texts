For a while I am still a walker, or I think I am.

I stand on warm stone and my eyes keep expecting detail and getting silhouettes instead. A grazer against fog, here and suddenly a day's walk away. Nothing has human size. My breath thins. Sound dies a few meters out. What is left is the warmth coming up through the soles of my feet, even where nothing has rested, and I keep trying to put a body around it.

A kettle would be the obvious shape. Six legs, barrel body, blind, heat-pits flaring along the flank, a beak mouth down to scrape mat from rock, and inside, the furnace stomach rumbling in my teeth. I can almost have that weight. I can almost exhale sulfurous steam and leave the ground warmer for days after I pass. But even in the imagining, the work is not in the barrel. The work is in the bacteria and fungi in the gut, taking stonebloom and stone apart and precipitating metal as heat and slag. I am not the animal that carries them from basin to basin across a territory larger than a country. I am too singular, too sealed in one hide, and the rumble is something I feel *through*, not something I make.

The motes tempt me the other way. A cloud with a center of gravity, turning and clicking in a fog pocket, blacking out what light there is for an hour, then settling hard to feed. I understand the shedding — molts that weather into riveted plates and articulated joints, exoskeletons that look manufactured because the Table has been making metal so long that even its skins expect to be mistaken for parts. But a mote is defined by leaving. It eats a mat, grooms it with microscopic residue, and is already gone by heat and smell toward the next pool. My tempo keeps catching on the ground underneath that frantic travel. I am slower than a swarm.

I try lying flat, then, like a pack-hunter. Blind, low, heat-pits larger than a kettle's, feeling a herd's furnace-ignition kilometers away through silt and basalt. No chase, only attrition. Wait for the one that lags, that runs hot because its gut is souring. But that is too purposeful, too much an ending. I do not want to be the mouth at the end of the heat column. I keep coming back to the column itself.

It is in a depression. That is where I find myself, finally, not arriving so much as condensing there.

Out of the wind, on a windward face of worn basalt where the thin air pools warm against the skin at thirty degrees, smelling of sulfur and hot dust. Fog beads on stone and does not fall. It runs in threads down sweat-blackened walls and vanishes into porous rock. A pool has been here — ankle-deep, warm from below, evaporating from above, opaque with microbes — and has sunk away within days, leaving a crust in the basin floor. The iron pavement around it is bare for a hundred meters at a time. In the hollow, stonebloom thickens.

I am that thickening. I spread, I do not grow. A greasy, flesh-colored skin over the rock in the exact ochres of the rock itself, thinning to almost nothing between the fog-gardens, luminous where damp sits. I have no blades. I have felted hairs, like thread-grass, combing fog out of air that will never properly become morning, funneling it to roots in wet stone. I take energy from sulfur and iron and the mineral freight the fog carries inland from the lowlands. I do not need a sun. I do not eat green.

This is the tempo I had to learn. Nothing here lives quickly, and nothing lives alone. My skin is threaded with bacteria holding a charge across the drier interfluves. I feel it before I know it: a prickling along what used to be the back of a neck, now just the raised edge of a mat, a faint crackle when a wet stone is touched, a small discharge in fog that lights a trackway for half a second. Stonebloom signals stress and growth on that field across distances no scent could reach. Smaller things navigate by it. When it surges, my ember-tips pulse, faint green-orange points on stone, and the bright-bellied flying things in the hollow read it as a map. They brush the thread-grass, carry spores and strains from mat to mat, and leave the green-black thread that will draw the herd.

I do not see the herd coming. I register it. Heat first, and the ground-current through stone, low and slow, days before the silhouettes resolve. Hundreds of barrel bodies camped in a wide basin, furnaces lit after feeding — the depression stays warm through boots long after they have moved on toward the next dampness. I am in that after-warmth. That is part of where I am. A kettle trail left for a thousand years becomes a band of dark, fertile soil, a faint linear terrace. Slowly, that terrace is smoothed back into the Table. My kind of time is that time. I will be soil before I am finished being stonebloom.

And I am not alone, because the motes will come to groom me. When the fog front advances and the pool crust dissolves, the whole hollow wakes — scent rising, vibration chuckling through wet stone. A swarm erupts, millions of blind beetles, and settles on me to feed and rebuild basalt as amber polymer, as ferrite, as fine coppery wire in their guts. They leave their molts in thousands. They leave me laced with residue, richer. Then they leave. A predator stirs somewhere and the field changes and they are gone up into fog, clicking as they turn.

So if you were walking this dark, warm, endless pavement, you would not meet me as a face. You would step into a fog-garden the size of a house and feel your hair prickle. You would touch wet stone and get a tiny, surprising crackle. You would kneel — if kneeling still meant anything — and the mat would be soft and slightly greasy under your palm, stone-colored, alive with smell. After the herd passed, you would feel the ground still warm through your boots. After the swarm passed, you would see, if your eyes were still new enough to be fooled, small gleaming plates weathering like rivets.

I am spread thin between basins, in the interval between a herd that has been and a swarm that is coming, making a little metal, holding a little charge, waiting for dampness to thicken me again. Not a walker. Not a furnace. Not a cloud. The place where the Table processes itself, slowly, in the dark, and where geology and life have worn each other smooth enough that I no longer have to choose which one I am.
