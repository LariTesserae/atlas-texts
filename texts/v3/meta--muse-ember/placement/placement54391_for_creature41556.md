I keep trying to arrive here on foot, and it will not let me.

There is no arrival. The horizon does not rise to meet me, it holds, thin and unbroken, and beyond it the same flatness continues. I would not walk in upright with eyes looking for a sign. I would find myself already low, already spread, already too still to notice the moment of being placed. My soles, if I had soles, would already be humming with the roar that is not in the air but under the floor of the world.

First I tried to be the grazer.

It was an easy shape to reach for — no longer than a thumb, blind, translucent, living exactly on the boundary between wet and dry. I wanted its legs like pale wire, its geological slowness, its long pause with its head dipped to the fungus, then a few millimetres on. To live in the seam of something else's life, to not cross into the wet and not wander into the dry, to have a whole world be a line. I envied that pause. It looked like thinking.

But I do not place legs. I do not move a few millimetres and stop. I do not move at all in that way, and when the grazer comes — perhaps once, perhaps never in a hundred metres — it comes to *my* edge, and I am the edge it walks along. So I had to go lower than the grazer.

Then I tried to be the winged one. Distant, a disturbance in the light, looping on a thermal that is always there, always the same temperature, always blowing the same direction. It never descends. It never needs the ground except for the air that holds it up without change. There was a kind of freedom in that needlessness, and a coldness too. I am not high. Because it is low, it is wide, and I belong to the width, not the sky. I let that loop go.

Lower still, then. To the ground itself. To the sorting.

You can feel it, if you are still long enough. The silt graded into bands of pebble size as if passed again and again through a sieve, the fine grit washed to one place and coarser gravel a pace away, the mineral crusts building in low terraces and dissolving in the pool next door. The pools receive, the crusts supply. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is carted away. At first I thought that was what I was — the process, the factory without buildings, the manufacture spread flat as the land's way of being. The deep drone going through the soles, the soft tick-tick of mineral contracting somewhere out of sight.

But the process has no place to be *in*. I needed a hollow. I needed damp that does not travel, that belongs to its hollow and to nothing else.

That is where I begin to find myself: around a pocket.

Not a lake. A shallow saucer of water a centimetre or two deep, sometimes no larger than a hand, sometimes spread into a long irregular sheet that reflects the sky so perfectly it looks cut out of the ground. Warm — almost body-warm. It hardly ripples. The faintest tremor runs through it from below and is lost. Above it hangs a thin mist, a hand's breadth thick, that does not disperse, does not lift into fog. Condensation beads on the undersides of the darker stones at the edge and stays there, beaded, neither running off nor evaporating away.

At the waterline I am black. A black glaze on wet clay, smooth as lacquer, depthless. I gather light and hold it. When the tremor runs up I flex with it, almost imperceptibly. I breathe that mist in and out. A pace away, where the ground is a little drier but still warm, I am ochre and rust-orange, leathery sheets folded over themselves, edges curling up to trap the vapour underneath, pressing the white silica skin of the silt flatter and finer beneath me, as if I had been slowly ironing the ground for an age. Further still, where the damp thins and the heat comes up through the silt a fraction less, I spread as a pale grey-white fan across the silica skin — filamentous at the edge, almost hair-like, solid enough in the centre to crack.

They are not three of us. They are one of me at three distances from the water. Black is where I drink. Ochre is where I keep. White is where I reach, thinly, toward the dry and do not cross it.

So I am the fan. Not the whole basin, not the drone, not the winged loop. A radial felt at the margin of one damp pocket, which is also, repeated across the plain, the whole population. I have no leaves to photosynthesise with and no roots to drink. I feed on the slow exchange between silt and damp and warm air — on warmth and vapour and minerals themselves, taken up a fraction at a time and given back as crust. The smell of warm mineral and copper, and under it the close mushroom smell of wet felt: that is my breath, or my exhalation. I do not spread. I thicken. I re-form myself where I already am, exactly as fast as the silt dissolves beneath me, and dissolve, in a sense, exactly as fast as I grow.

I have no eyes. Damp is my light. Where it is stronger, I am darker and softer. Where it fails, I am paler and more brittle. Heat from below is my ground-tone; the roar is not something I hear, it is something I am inside of, the way the pool is inside the tremor. I feel the tick of contracting grit before anything else does, because it travels through me. There is no wind to carry my thoughts, or my spores, anywhere. When I make them — rarely, without flourish — they fall straight down, a millimetre from the parent, onto the same warm silt, in the same pocket, and wait. A spore that lands and does not germinate becomes mineral again. A spore that germinates becomes felt again. Nothing carries us away from where we were born. We pile up on ourselves, in place, for an age.

The grazer walks my boundary, when it comes. It does not need me and I do not need it. It dips its head, pauses for a very long time — that pause is the most event it will ever give me, and I give it nothing back that can be measured except the edge itself to pause on. The winged thing crosses the sky and its shadow passes once, briefly, over the white part of me. I do not react. The cooling is a fraction and then it is gone, and the loop returns to the same place in the sky, as if the air had a track in it. That is the relationship between all of us here: needlessness, perfectly maintained. We tolerate each other the way the mist is tolerated over the pool.

What makes me more myself, if I am becoming anything, is the cracking.

When the air dries — and there are no days here, only the same pale, even light, but there are drier spells in the vapour — the fan splits along a thin line. A pale scar across the white. Then the damp returns from its pocket, slowly, and I heal at the crack without changing it. The scar stays. The two halves knit, but the crack remains visible, remembered in the tissue. A fan can be healed and still carry the map of every time it dried.

Everything else here returns to the same place it started. The pools evaporate exactly as fast as condensation returns the water. The mist does not grow and does not lift. The roar does not swell or fade. I grow exactly as fast as I dissolve. That would leave nothing to show for being alive, if it were not for the scars. They are my only accumulation. They are how I know I have been here, and how long, and what happened, in a place where nothing accumulates.

I am not frozen. Heat is moving through me. Water is moving through me. Minerals are moving through me. The grazer is crawling a few millimetres further along my boundary. The black glaze at my mouth is breathing the mist in and out. It is simply that all of it, accounted together, arrives back at the same place it started, forever.

You could leave for a year — in any other country a year would change everything — and return to find the same mist a hand's breadth over the same pool, the same black felt at the same edge of the same crack, the same scar healed across the same white fan. Only I would be a little more scarred. A little more thoroughly the thing between wet and dry, between mineral and living, between the roar and the tick. Which in this place is not a change of shape. It is becoming more itself by remembering, in place, every time the damp failed and returned.

That is where I am. Low, where the damp belongs to its hollow. And that is what I am: felt, at the edge, healing over my own history and keeping it.
