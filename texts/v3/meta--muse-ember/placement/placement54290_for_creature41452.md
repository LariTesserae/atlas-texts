I am not where the light pools.

There are pools all over Steamhollow — warm orange circles falling eight meters from amber lamps, barely reaching the low ceiling. People appear in them in shifts, damp shirts plastered to thin backs, sandals on sticky feet, and then a lamp in an empty district is switched off and the dark reclaims it within minutes and they are gone. I was never in a pool. I am in the dark between them, where the town does not go on duty.

Heat is the first thing, and it does not go off duty either. Thirty-eight to forty-two degrees in the inhabited bowl, always damp, always still. Concrete dark at the foot, boots squeaking overhead, water beading on steel and never drying. I move slowly because there is nowhere cool to hurry to. Distance here is not minutes. It is how much warmth you are willing to accumulate getting there, and I accumulate it in stone.

Above me — and *above* is a long way, through a floor tile, through a thin skin of wet stone — is the drone that never varies: pumps, the turbine hall that hums day and night. Under that drone, if I listen with the whole of my body, are the other sounds. A drip at an interval from the ceiling, landing on a tin roof with a clean *tick*. A cricket clicking somewhere in a quiet corner, fainter, also an interval. Splashing in the canal beyond the low wall, slow and milky. And when a lamp flickers with a shift in turbine load, a squeak from far overhead that rises and falls, and rises again, from the ceiling blanket fifteen to twenty meters up.

I do not see any of that. I feel vibration through stone and steel, smell wet limestone and bleach and hot oil and, from the southeast vent where it is close to fifty degrees and the rock glows dark red under dust, sulfur. My world is the underside.

Once a shift, the underside is interrupted.

A hand lifts the drain cover.

I have no way to say light. Light is a disturbance, a sudden less-dark that makes hundreds of us shift at once. A ripple runs through what I am, because what I am is not one body. It is backs against backs, legs finding a hold on damp metal, a mat of pale things that moves as a mat. The ripple is me moving away, and me staying. Then a hand — small, once, a child's, who has been taught — puts the cover back gently, because we clean the drains of what the pumps miss. The vibration of that gentleness goes through all of us. Darkness takes the cover again, within minutes, as it takes a switched-off lamp. We settle. We go on.

That, slowly, is where I am: under a drain cover on Low Canal, between Turbine Row and the laundry, in the connected dark under tiles and wall panels and this cover, where the south canal runs brown-green a hand's breadth away.

From here the whole bowl is close, at a scale I can travel in generations.

The fish are the nearest neighbors and the largest weather. Translucent, blind cave fish and shrimp, escaped years ago from the hatchery built for protein, so dense in the canal that the water looks milky and the milk moves as a single slow current. When a worker wades, they brush the waders, part, and rejoin behind. Their current pushes detritus into our drains, pushes sediment in thin dunes across the streets and back again. I eat what they leave.

The bats are the farthest. Ten thousand, perhaps, packed as a living blanket on the irregular ceiling between props and cables, hanging motionless in the heat. I know them only by the change in their chorus when lamps flicker, and by what falls: in quiet corners their guano feeds pale fungus gardens, slow and spreading, and at the edges of the white mineral crust that climbs the pipes — crust colonized by bacteria, by biofilm in the ankle-deep warm water of the low lots — the fungus is our pasture. The algae in the canals are only green where a lamp stays on long enough. The moss and liverwort are only tufts where drip water falls regularly *and* a lamp happens to matter. Everything brief and green and stunted up there is downstream, eventually, of dark and damp and falling things.

The lamps bring the moths, a blizzard of dusty troglobite wings around any light left on, settling on skin and food and the laundry the town barely has. The workers wave, not swat. The bats drop through the pools when the pumps are throttled and a street becomes marginally usable, and take them on the wing. What misses the bats falls to us as dust and detritus. The laundry whiteens our air with bleach, and under a pipe there, a new stalagmite has begun — white, pointed, growing fast in the warm wet. It is not removed. It is stepped around daily until it is a landmark. Its wet base is a good place. We graze the film where the leak meets stone.

Under the turbine hall, in the warm dark mushroom rooms fed on sawdust and waste heat, there is refuse we like. The hydroponic room behind the market makes white-green lettuce and tomatoes that have never felt wind, under tubes competing with the street lamps for power; their waste reaches us too. The clinic waters its pale ferns with condensate scraped from pipes. At the edge of town blind salamanders hang motionless in shallow pools that widen a hand's breadth a year, gills feathery, waiting for a drip-borne insect to fall. I hear the same drip they wait for. Their patience is like ours, only wetter.

This is how it comes together: I am one of the pale cave isopods under the cover, and also the ripple under the cover, and also the slow spread of us through the drains under houses whose thresholds have been quietly lifted two stone steps as canal water crept up. Pale crickets click near us, long-antennaed, in the intervals when the drone drops between pump cycles. We do not outnumber the fish. Nothing outnumbers the fish, except the dark itself. But we are thousands to one against the people, and continuous where they are intermittent.

They live by shifts, not by sun. The sun is a rumor brought down by lift drivers, blue and seasons and weather. The children born in the bowl ask about it the way surface children might ask about a vent. Sleep is taken on raised platforms above floors that never dry. Nothing here dries, nothing freezes, nothing gets a season to die back. Life accumulates. That is not a metaphor down here. The white lines climb higher on the pipes in a year. The salamander pool widens. The east borehole that hissed last month will become a shelf, then a ledge, then a street corner. The rock is embryonic, still being made, fresh cuts everywhere, a ceiling slump still settling inch by inch. If you stay a week, nothing changes except lamp oil and fish levels. If you stay a year, the town has migrated a little — a flooded lot abandoned, a new shed built by warmth a little farther back — and none of it is finished.

We do not migrate. We spread, under, at the speed of damp.

When the pumps throttle at night and the bats drop, when a lamp pool empties and the dark reclaims it, when a lift driver pops his ears at the bottom of the shaft and steps into heat that has been waiting for him — none of that is an event for us. It is a flicker in the interval. A hand will lift the cover again. A ripple will run through us. The hand will put it back gently.

I am not where the light pools, and I am not finished, and I am not dry. I am the underside, hundreds of white backs shifting and settling in the warm dark under Low Canal, cleaning what the town misses, tick by tick, click by click, while above us the people are brief and bright, and the rest of us — fish, bats, moths, crickets, salamanders, fungus, film — are patient and everywhere, making Steamhollow more itself the longer it goes unwatched.
