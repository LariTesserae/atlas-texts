**The Slow Turn**

Going down changes the weather in my skin before it changes anything else. By the fourth landing my shirt sticks. By the eighth, the sweat stays where it lands. I do not so much enter the Underhearth as arrive in layers, the way the walls are layered, paint over paint, brick over brick, until I am no longer sure which layer is mine.

At first I take the shape of one of the dwellers. I wake in a small room terraced against the warm wall, curtain drawn, kettle set over a shared flue so my heat warms a neighbor and hers warms mine. I learn the low turn of the boilers, the shift bells like struck cooking pots, the way to carry a rag and breathe slowly in air that will not cool me. My palms harden from rope and hot brick. Salt draws at the edges of my shirt. I squint at the amber bulbs and see better in the dark between them, where outlines are heat shimmer and a surface being a fraction warmer than the one beside it.

It fits, for a while. But even then I keep looking past my own hands — to the pipe beside my face, where movement is separating itself from the shimmer.

So I follow it, and I am smaller.

On the cold main at the north end I am a mite, blind and translucent as salt, in single file along the wettest seam. The world narrows to a film of condensation on metal, to temperature under my feet, to the rule that I never cross a dry joint. I go out along the drip and back along the drip, wearing my faint track into the mineral scale. This is how I learn to read here: not by light, but by trace, by warmth, by where the wetness holds. It is true, and it is too narrow. I cannot hear what is above me. I cannot know if the fish has surfaced.

So I rise, and I am a pigeon in the trusses.

Born here, as everything here is born here, my feathers grey with brick-dust, my nest wire and rope fiber packed into the warm steel. I coo and am answered, and hush when the ventilation sighs. I circle once beneath the ceiling lost in darkness and settle again, as if the length of the vault were all the sky I needed. I learn staying. I learn never leaving as a fact, not a sorrow. But from this height the canal becomes only a smell of steam, the gardens only a pale smudge, the people only a slow shuffle in the floor channel. I have stayed too high.

So I come down to the boiler flank, and I am the lizard.

Blind, translucent enough that the blue of a vein shows through my back, flattened against steel that holds its fifty-something degrees for hours. I do not work for my warmth. I receive it. I let a hand pass near me to feel the warmth of my own flank and then go still again. The dwellers know me by the pattern of scale on my tail, as they know pipes by their tick. I learn the patience of taking heat without making it. I have never been a flame in this place. I am an ember at the edge of one.

So I slip from the steel into the water, and I am the fish in the canal.

Black water, forty centimeters wide, barely moving. I turn in it with the small turbine wheel, blind and wrinkled like a pipe joint, and surface now and then to breathe the steam directly. The surface breaks with a soft ring. Children on the concrete lip watch for it and give me a name, not to own me but to mark that I have returned, the way people name weather. I learn returning. I learn that a life can be measured in rings. But I do not keep the belt from seizing. I do not know the pile of names the children have given my kind.

And at the edge of the water I am the green things.

I am the fungi wall in its boiler-shell planters, pale blind hands reaching out of ground brick and damp. I am the beans on the ledge, roots in mortar and ash, creeping not along the rail as they were trained but along its warmth, toward the leaking pipe. I am the montbretia in the cinder pit, older than most of the machines, iron-banded, leaning away from the amber bulbs and unmistakably toward the boiler wall. Here the signal is inverted, and I learn it in my body before I learn it in thought: grow toward heat, not light. The bulbs are rationed. The boiler glow is the only sun I have ever known, low at the south end of my world.

None of these shapes leaves. They stay under the skin like layers of paint. The dweller’s hands, the mite’s track, the pigeon’s circle, the lizard’s stillness, the fish’s ring, the plant’s lean — I find they meet in one place, and I keep returning to it.

It is on the main walk, south of the fungi alcove and north of the boilers, where the heat has thickened enough that sweat no longer cools but has not yet become soup. I sit on the concrete lip of the canal with the boiler glow at my back and the steam of the black water at my feet. To my left the fungi wall veils itself in steam. Above me the catwalks cross, and higher still the pigeons shift in their nests. Behind me, on the flank, a lizard sleeps in its pattern of scale. On the pipe overhead the mites make their slow line along the drip. In front of me the channel is polished by a century of shuffled feet.

This is my work now, the slow turn: I am the keeper of the turbine wheel.

It turns nothing in particular. That is the point. I tend the wheel and the belt so it does not seize, because a seized belt is a loss this closed world cannot afford. Every seventeen minutes or so the ventilation sighs and lifts the dust; that is my clock as much as any bell. I check the basin where the drip falls with perfect regularity. I leave the mites alone. I harvest the fungi with a knife and leave them to regrow. I untangle the beans back toward the heat of the rail. I water the montbretia from its leak so I do not have to draw from a store. I murmur to the boiler tender shoulder to shoulder, because warmth is shared currency, and a knock on a pipe can carry three levels of dwellings.

I was a child here once, sitting where I sit now, counting drips and sighs, watching for the ring. I have never seen the sun, and I do not wait to go up. A few in a season climb the switchbacks with a message or a part and come back down into the wall of heat at the fourth landing. I am not one of them. I am born here. I learn the ticking under the floor as it speeds when the engines turn over, the long cool turn when we withdraw and only the animals keep their patrol, the thickening of scale on a joint by feel.

If you look for me, do not look for me in the amber pools of light. Look where the sweat does not cool. Look where the black water rings, where the belt keeps its slow turning, where a back is warm from a century of brick. I will be there, banked and patient, growing — as the beans grow, as the montbretia grows, as everything in the Underhearth has learned to grow — toward the heat.
