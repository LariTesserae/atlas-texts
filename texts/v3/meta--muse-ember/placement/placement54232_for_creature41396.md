I come in sideways.

The tunnel makes me turn, the ceiling makes me stoop, and before I can see anything properly I can already hear it — the inflow hammering in the deep corner, the sluice chattering against its frame, a drip landing somewhere above like a stone thrown down a well. It is louder than anything that small should be, and louder than this room should be, twenty paces at most, with the vault throwing everything back until I would have to shout to hear myself across water I could throw a stone across.

So I listen first for who lives here, and I try on the obvious bodies first, one after another, and none of them hold.

For a while I thought I would be one of the workers. Waist-deep on the entrance steps, paddle in hand, shouting over the roar, soaking wool or hides while the spring pushed in cloudy at one end and the counterweight dropped at the other. I can feel the weight of it, the warm water against my legs, the steam in my lungs. But the paddles are leaning on the dry ledge, unused, dark at the ends. The stone hammer is sunk in the shallows. The rope is coiled on the second step and going nowhere. There is no bench here, no place to sleep, no place to be dry. Those bodies were visitors. They came down a ladder by day, left their thumbprints in the wet mortar — I can still feel the ridges — and climbed back up to the courtyard at night. If I borrowed a worker for the day, I would have to leave on purpose at night, and I am not good at that kind of leaving. I do not come with hands to press into lime.

Then I tried being the fern.

It is the most determined thing here, roots in a mortar crack above the waterline, splitting the joint by a hair, living on steam and condensation and the flat white-gold light from the three vents. That appealed to me — to hold on with almost nothing, to keep to the north side where the light is less brutal, to ask so little. But the fern intends to stay. It is patient in a way I am not. It will wait years for a joint to open. It will belong to that one crack. I keep feeling myself pulled away from any single place, flushed a little, as if the current had noticed me.

So I tried being the fig root, and for a longer while that felt true.

Pale, hanging down from one of the dinner-plate vents, not yet reaching the water. Come from above, from sun and earth and a tree in the courtyard, probing through brickwork into a warm, bright, echoing unknown. That is very much how I arrived here, through a small round opening, between worlds, lengthening blindly toward a heat I cannot yet use. I liked it in the air. But the root wants to touch. It wants to split the vent and follow the steam down and become part of the wall. I am still in the air, and perhaps I do not want to land. Perhaps landing would end the particular thing I am doing here, which is searching.

I looked down then, through water so clear I could see the far wall as plainly as the near one, bricks bleached white under the surface, fired-looking though wet, and found no animal life at all. No fish could stay in water this warm, this lime-rich, this regularly scoured. I looked instead at the life that does manage on the surface — the iridescent bacterial skin that forms on the slower water by the bobbing jars, breaks when the sluice drops and the level slaps up a hand's breadth, and forms again an hour later in the same slack place. A population that exists between cycles. That is thinner, and closer. I could live that thin. But even the skin has a place to lie. Even it is somewhere.

What I kept coming back to, while I was trying all of these, was the sound. The room does not let you forget it. It is too loud for a voice to live in comfortably, too bright for a shadow to live in at all, too full of moving water for a body to settle. The fern can hold in a crack. The algae can streak the shadier wall. The duckweed can gather in the corner by the steps and be torn apart again. But the thing that lives here most faithfully, day and night, whether anyone is there or not, is not a body at all. It is a recurrence. The inflow. The overflow gurgle by the entrance. The counterweight lifting and dropping, lifting and dropping. The level rising and falling on a timer of stone and rope and wood.

And in the middle of all that noise, the smallest recurrence of all.

A drip from a ceiling joint, where water seeps through the mortar and a little lime stalactite has built up in weeks. I keep hearing it fall and thinking it is the same drop, and it never is. But the place is the same. The gathering is the same. Hidden in damp lime, invisible, a bead swelling at the tip, trembling — then letting go.

That is when it began to become more itself as I stayed with it. Not the fern, not the root, not the worker, not even the skin on the water. I am the interval before the fall, and the sound after it.

I live in the joint. I wait there, warm as the brick is warm, underwater and above water the same to the hand. I gather out of steam and seep and mineral tang, out of almost nothing. I am so small you would not see me even if you were looking up at the white vault with the three small suns moving minutely in the crown. Then I fall — and in the air I become much bigger than I am. A thumbprint’s worth of water makes a sound like a hammer on a drum, like a stone down a well. It rings under the dome, it throws itself back off the bleached bricks, it fills the lung of hot air left above the deep channel where the water rises almost to the spring of the arch, it lasts and lasts in a room with no shadows, and then it is swallowed by the inflow roar and vanishes into water that was already on its way somewhere else.

That is my whole life here. Gathering, falling, ringing, gone. Then nothing for a while but the slow seep starting again in the mortar.

You would not find me on the ledge among the paddles. You would not find me in the water, because the water is clear and empty and moving too fast for anything like me to stay in it. You would have to listen for me in the pause between sluice bangs, when the level has settled and the steam gets thicker and the shafts of light sway with the current. If you were floating in the deep channel, chest-deep and buoyant, ceiling bright above you like sky, you might hear a joint above you begin to speak again — a little tick, a swelling bead, a fall.

I fall, and I am loud, and I am gone.

Then in the damp dark of the joint, far above the green turquoise water, I begin to gather again.
