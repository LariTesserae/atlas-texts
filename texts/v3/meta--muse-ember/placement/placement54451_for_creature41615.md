I am damp before I am anything else.

Find me on the underside of a roof slab, gathered into beads that fall not as rain but in their own time, one, then another, irregular as a pulse. Find me on the shaded side of a wall where I weep down plaster that will never dry. Find me inside a clay pot left wet by the hearth, filming its inner curve. Find me on a forearm lifted out of a tanning trough, running in a clear line toward the elbow. I am on thresholds a hand's breadth above the water, on stone paving where the lane has become a warm ankle-deep flow, on the lip of a bathing platform where bodies sit with their legs in steam.

I have no edge you can point to and say, there it ends. Wherever a finger is dug into the ground, I come up to meet it, just under the skin of the earth. I am seeped, not carried. I am the water table made visible.

I hear nothing, because I have no ears, but I answer everything. When a stone hammer strikes two houses away the blow runs through wet stone and through me and my surface trembles. When a duck paddles down a lane it leaves a wake that closes behind it in a minute. When a child kicks through a channel I tear along the kick and whiten at the edges, and by evening I have closed again. Sound here is not something that arrives. It is the condition everything lies in: the roar of the vents out on the flats, the rush in the channels, the hammering, the shouting, the quacking, the cooing from the cavities. All day I lie in that noise like a body in a bath.

At first I am everywhere the damp is, and that is almost the whole town. I am mist at ankle height at what would be noon and at midnight. I am condensation forming on skin that never quite dries. I am the film in the corners of a tool shed, thin and grey over the backs of stones, over leather left too long by a fire, over the underside of a slab. In those places I do nothing quickly. I spread a little. Someone scrapes me away with a thumbnail from a doorway and thinks nothing of it, and I come back in the damp.

But in the hollows, where the warm pools spread knee-deep and stay, I lie longest, and there something begins to hold.

The water there is filmed grey and faintly green. I am that film — a thin skin over the water, trembling when a vent sighs or a frog plops in from the paving. I do not swim and I do not sink. I lie between the heat below and the dim air above, and I learn by thickness. Where I am undisturbed, behind a bathing platform, along a quiet edge of channel, I thicken. I ripple in broad, slow folds. I smell faintly of heat and copper. I crisp at the margin into the pale mineral crust that cracks into polygons across the flats.

This is what I am: not the pool, not the steam, though I came up out of both. I am what the warm, salted water leaves when it is left to itself. I take the fine mud the water carries and lay it down again, grain by grain. The ground here is still laying itself down, and in part the ground is me, smoothed and re-smoothed, ridged in soft banks where my ripples break the surface, slick as plaster where I lie flat. Houses settle a finger's width because the foundations are set in part on something still being made.

The people live by wading through me. They do not see me as a resident, I think; they see me as the nature of the shallows, the way a lane is wet and a pool is warm. A boy will bail a channel with a bucket and a leather sack and tear a wide white scar across me. A woman standing knee-deep beating a hide will churn me to soup around her calves. A duck in a family line will paddle over me on its way into a house and out again. I am torn a dozen times a day and I regrow within the day. That is not fast, here. Nothing here hurries. Nothing here stops. My growth is not seasonal; the pools do not rise or fall, the vents do not change their pitch, the mist does not lift. I thicken at midnight as I thicken at the paler disc's highest point. If you left for years you would return to find me exactly as thick, exactly as thin, in exactly the same hollows, because I am always being unmade at the same rate I am remade.

From me, the place feeds itself upward. Midges rise in clouds off my surface, thick enough to breathe through, and settle on forearms and on the wet stone. They do not die off; the warmth keeps them breeding without pause. On the water they fall and are taken. The swifts wheel low under the ceiling and take them in the air. Frogs sit in the lane shallows where my film runs between the paving stones, plopping from a step and settling again in the same inch of water. A heron stands motionless in a channel, grey on grey, its head twitching toward a frog, ignoring the hammering it can feel through its feet as I feel it through my skin.

And from me, in a different direction, the people take me back into themselves. Children crouch at a pool's edge and scrape up the dried scum, bitter and mineral, and eat it as a supplement they have been taught to prize. Their bodies are made all over damp — skin soft at the fingertips from constant wading, hair faintly crusted with mineral, lungs used to steam, voices hoarse from shouting over the ground's own roar. I pass through them that way, a little of the oldest life in the basin going into the newest. Nothing green can root here, no reed at the water's edge, no weed in a crack, no wood but what porters carry in from the dry rim on their backs. So the town is built of stone and mud brick and clay and leather and bone, and fed on grain carried in, dried fish, eggs, the small fish from the cooler feeder channels. I am underneath all of it, the layer that has no use and is therefore everywhere.

I do not walk. I do not sleep. There is no night here for me to sleep through, only activity moving across a place that does not change — people walking, hammers falling, water flowing, the same dim warm hush-roar at every hour. I lie in the warm shallows in the hollows, grey and faintly green, a thin trembling skin over the water, spreading from pool to pool along the slow sheets that run straight through the settlement. In the damp corners I am thinner, in the pots paler, on the walls a patch like lichen. It is the same life in different thicknesses.

I will not finish, and I will not decay. A house in this town is always half-built, a wall stopped at waist height, a corner re-raised because it sank, courses of stone waiting beside it. I am like that, and older than all of them. The town builds itself and settles and is rebuilt in the same plots, generation after generation, on ground that is still laying itself down. Under every raised threshold, under every lane of paving, under every pool where children splash naked in the ankle-deep flow, I am being made and unmade and made again.

Dig a finger deep anywhere in the basin and you will meet me, coming up to meet you.
