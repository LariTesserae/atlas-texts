I would not be on top of it.

I went looking for ground to stand on, and there was none that was not already alive. I knelt, as you must, and my hand sank a hand's breadth with a spring and a release, and kept sinking into weave — rhizome over rhizome, pale thick roots like fingers, black-green mould that has never quite rotted, fungi stitching it all so dense that every step finds a give. The country is a skin over elder rock, worn to its final curve long ago, and the skin is the only ground. To stand upright here is to be seen for kilometers, and to be taken in full light.

So I tried, in order, the things that stand up.

I tried the builder first, because they are the only hands here. Small, dark as wet bark, no taller than a knee, broad padded feet made for feeling. Living in the dry hollow of a dead stalk-giant, base plugged with stones polished by handling, woven cups hung to catch the drips from a living leaf above, a fire-pit almost always cold. Tapping a stone three short and one long for water. Plucking a vine at a rhythm to send a message along living wood to the next hollow a hundred paces away. Coming out only when a grazer has passed, to follow in its pale swath and gather what its feet uncovered — a chipped obsidian nodule, a bone awl, fiber for a basket, a loom frame propped to a trunk and worked by feel.

It is a life. But it is still a life that walks on top, still separate, still carrying its home with it, still waiting for a stooper's shadow and going down into the mat's edge with a hand on the weave to feel if the chase is moving away. The children learn that first — not names, but feels: the note of a safe hollow, the ripple of a burrower, the heavy thrum of a grazer, the sudden quiet when something in the sky is listening for movement. I understood the feel. I could not bear the standing.

I tried the sky. A sail-stooper, wings on long finger-bones, veined and translucent, beating once, twice, then gliding across a whole valley in view, tilting slowly, terribly, toward a house-sized platform below. The sky here is not a home. It is a procession — weather fronts, migrations that darken the sun for minutes, wings making a second wind. To live there would be to be always crossing, never arriving, roosting at last on the rim of a giant's crown with wings folded like closed sails, head turned to the wind.

I tried the grazer. Six legs, each as long as a tree is tall, a back that is less a body than a platform, shaggy hair sheltering mites and small beetles and a kind of hanging moss, head low, eating the mat standing up and leaving a clean pale swath behind that greens again from the edges. Breath steaming faintly even at noon in the thin, warm air. It carries a world on its back, and when it falls — and it is eaten openly, in full light — it is not allowed to lie. White roots come, grader beetles come, flies come in a brief iridescent weather, and in half a day there is no bone exposed to the sun. That promise held me. But still, a platform. Still separate, and eaten from above.

What lives in the hair, then? What lives under the leaf, where water beads and falls tick-tock against the mat? What lives under the foot when the foot pauses and the mat dimples and a grey shiver goes across it?

Go down.

At dawn the whole country sweats. That tempted me next — to be a surface thing for the bright hour. A dew-runner, hyaline and long-legged, mouthparts like a paintbrush, surfing the thin shine that covers every filament for perhaps an hour before the sun takes it back. Licking as we go, so fast and so many that a fresh patch sparkles and dries in minutes. It is frantic and wet and brief, and it matches the place: dampness here is a pulse, not a permanence. Overnight wet, midday warm and close, then wet again. But by mid-morning the shine is gone. A life made only of that hour would not be a life. It would be a lick, and then a retreat, and then dry.

So go in. A hand's breadth down, and again.

Dark. Narrow. A round chamber chewed in a rhizome, warm from the sun working through the sponge above. No light is needed here, because the weave carries better than the thin air. I know where another of my kind is, and whether the temperature in my chamber is right for the eggs, only by touch — by letting the mat speak and answering in the same key.

I am not the choir. I am one note in it.

Thumbnail-sized, translucent amber if the dawn shine ever caught me, which it rarely does. Forelegs built like files. Wing-cases I rub together, steadily, not to be loud but to be known. When the sun warms the mat my note rises with it. When something heavy steps overhead — a grazer's stilt leg, a builder's padded foot pressing and releasing — the note drops and restarts, like a choir catching its breath. Around me, higher and fainter, the file-mites rasp at the fungal threads, eating the soft skin of roots, scurrying in their hundreds when a foot pauses above. Lower still, the ripple-burrowers go back and forth all day through blind tunnels no wider than a wrist, and I feel their slow wave travel under my chamber long before it arrives, as if something enormous were breathing just under the skin.

This is my day, overhead and under.

At dawn I come up to the surface chambers to sing louder into the damp air, while the dew-runners surf and the giants lift their boat-wide leaves because the hum has risen. The undersides open. Water collects and falls. Animals lick it from surfaces; builders catch it in woven cups above us without ever knowing the note that lifted the leaf.

At midday the warmth holds in the green against a palm, and we sing through the heat. The grazers move in their slow, ponderous arcs across the open sponge, the mat rippling under their combined weight as a stooper tilts and circles for an hour, sometimes missing, sometimes not. We feel both, equally, as vibration.

Twice a day, sometimes not for days, the dark layers pass. The migration — winged ants big as a hand, nectar moths, sawing midges — blocks the sun for minutes and their wings make a second wind that presses through the mat and layers over our chorus until the pressure is in my own small body. They rain dung and scales and dead, and carry away pollen and spores. Then the light returns, and the ground hum resumes its dominance, and I restart my note again.

In the dry afternoon everything is pressed down. The grazers stand in a giant's shade if they can, legs tucked, plates heaving. The stoopers roost, wings folded. The surface insects go under. The mat closes its filaments to conserve what damp remains. The chorus narrows to wind combing the filaments flat and releasing them with a sighing crackle, and a single, endless insect note, high and clear, from a hole no one ever finds. That is us, at our most reduced: no one singing for company, only to keep the chamber right, to keep the eggs warm, to say *here* to the weave. Endless, because stopping would be a kind of dying in a place that does not permit falling still.

At night the cold comes in as a clean, mineral bite. The condensation begins again. The giants close and drip steadily in the dark. The builders emerge in numbers you would not believe from the day's emptiness, moving between dead trunks with hands on wood, sharing news pulled along vines. The stoopers hang from the crowns, wings like closed sails. The migrators are gone to the next tableland. And the chorus changes key — deeper, slower, as if the plateau were dreaming in vibrations. I sing lower. The whole skin of the world resonates in my sternum, which is also the whole skin that holds me.

Someday I will stop. That is not a fear here, because nothing is allowed to lie. When I die in my chamber, the white roots will thrust out to drink me, first from inside the weave, then the beetles, then the flies. My chamber will be eaten and re-chewed. My amber case, my filed legs, will be taken into the mat that fed me, and become a part of the hum another beetle will feel as temperature, as nearness, as a note restarting after a step overhead. Plant eating animal eating plant, root drinking beetle drinking fungus drinking root. Nothing separate. Nothing left to touch stone.

If you kneel now and press your palm flat to the green — sponge, spring, release — you will feel the wind first, and under it the grazer's wide thrum, and under that the slow ripple of a burrower, and under that, if you stay, a small steady rubbing, a thumbnail of a life in a round dark room, dropping when your weight comes and starting again when you lift.

That is where you would find me. Not on top. A hand's breadth down, where the ground decided to be alive continuously, and I decided to answer in key.
