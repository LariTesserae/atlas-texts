I do not come in through the warped door.

I was already under it, in the lee of the threshold where the dust pours through in its thin, hourglass stream. For you that gap is just wide enough to crawl. For me it is a gorge, and the stream never stops.

From outside, with your ear to the wall, I am only the rustle that never resolves into steps. That is accurate. I am not meant to resolve, not from that distance.

It would be easy to mistake me for the weather overhead. The air layer hangs at head height like a second, thin ceiling — midges, moths, wasps circling and mating and laying eggs in mid-air fall. When the fan turns, or a predator wave runs below, the whole cloud lifts at once and rains on the roof, a living storm that blacks the skylight for a heartbeat. If I lived there my life would be that lifting and settling, layer upon layer. I feel it from underneath, as a shadow passing over the floor and a fine sift of wings and cast skins falling into the dust. I am too heavy for it, too slow. I would dry out.

Below that, the walls. Centipedes holding loose strata up the concrete pillar-bases, spiders in curtains of stiffened web, runners commuting along the pipes in lines so regular you could set a watch by them — out from the lockers at dusk, up the warm riser, back when the air runs fast. They live vertically. They feel the hall through their feet, the tremor of a motor waking three metres away arriving through stone long before it arrives through air. I feel their traffic the same way, as pressure through the floor, but I do not climb. I am not built to commute. My world has no up worth going to.

I settle lower still, to the crust.

The floor is where I resolve. It is a living crust, shallow, and it moves even when you think it is still. I am one of the pinhead workers who turn it over, mouthful by mouthful. Translucent, blind, fat-bodied, my legs like oars for paddling through dust as if through shallow water. You would not see me as an individual. You would see the crust itself stirring, dune faces slumping and re-forming overnight in the lee of the press feet and between the lockers.

I know the hall, but not as a hall. I know it as temperatures and fats and vibrations arriving through metal. The six cylinders chained into a massif at the centre are not a machine to me. They are a mountain range I can climb by its ridges, a forest canopy in the felt pads where oil and warmth have made a dark sponge, rivers in the grease trenches congealed a century ago in the cracks. A bolt is a cliff. Its lee, in a fan-storm, is a valley where a whole generation can shelter while the dust goes horizontal above us. The far wall, your horizon, is something I will never reach. My mother did not reach it. The dust I eat was a dune last week and is a trench this week.

I have half an eye at most, most of us none. Light reaches me only as a change in warmth on the stone. Sound reaches me truer through the concrete than through the dry air. I navigate by that, and by taste: mould spores, shed skin, insulation fibre drifting down from the eaves, the bacteria living in the grease. My abdomen is translucent and full of grey dust, so that what I have eaten shows through me. I am made, visibly, of the floor.

At dawn the coldest steel beads, a few microlitres, gone by mid-morning. I press up against the base of the engine where the metal holds the night's cold and take that film in the slow sips that are most of my drinking. The rest of my water I get, like many here, from the chemistry of breaking down fat. No rain has ever reached me. No plant has ever fed me. That is not a lack I can feel. The processed things the industrial world left behind — felt, rubber, rope, leather, paper turned to powder, fats that never dried — are soil enough, and everything that dies here is eaten within hours. The eating is part of the sound you heard through the wall.

Disturbance is weather. A big predatory beetle, armour-plated, mandibles clicking loud enough for you to hear, charges across the crust and we scatter like a flock, oar-legs paddling us into the lee of a grain of grit, then re-form behind it. The machines wake every few hours over us, as they have longer than any of us can remember: a belt jerking forward on its timer, a panel buzzing, a strip light flickering on at midnight with that flat industrial light no sun ever made. Through the stone it is an earthquake first, a sound second. We ride it. We brace and continue. The built world keeps its pointless cycles; we have built ours directly on top, using the same tremors for our own purposes.

At night, when the light through the skylights thins to the same cool grey it holds all day, the strata descend to meet me. The air drops, centipedes come down off the pillars, and the floor goes into a frenzy of feeding and mating in the dunes and grease trenches, on the warm side of the press where the metal still gives back a little heat. The sound gets denser then, a dry thunder of chitin, as if the whole hall were chewing. I am in the middle of that density, touching and near-touching always. That contact is my society. There is never quiet, and never, either, solitude in the way you would mean it.

You can walk our entire world in two minutes, from the warped door to the far wall. In theory you could know every locker village and every pipe highway. For me the same ground takes generations, and it never stays still. The dune I ploughed last night has been eaten and re-formed. The trench I polished will be clean and dark for years after I am gone, because my body will become part of that polish, part of the dust, part of the soil the next mouthful turns over.

That is the final form, smoothed like everything else here. The plain outside was worn down to grey, rust-red, washed yellow, with no edges left. Inside, the eroders have legs, and I am one of them, eyeless and translucent and busy. I do not know I am in a shed. I know warm felt, cold steel at dawn, fat in the cracks, a tremor through stone, and dust that moves because we move.

You heard me before you saw anything that lives here. That was the correct order. I am heard first, as work.
