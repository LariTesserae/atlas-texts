It is loud before it is visible. For me, it is trembling before it is loud.

I do not hear the roar the way a visitor does, in the teeth and behind the ears. I have no ears to spare for it. What reaches me is a continuous tremor in the things I am touching, and I am always touching something enormous. A gear housing, a copper pipe, a concrete edge worn concave under a footstep. The tower breathes through all of them, very slowly, and has been breathing for longer than I have been here. The loudness is not an event. It is the medium I live in, the way damp is the medium for the frogs below me.

Everything I know arrives through contact. At this moment my body is an arrangement of contacts, held out in a compass of tension. My back is to the warm, rising air of the shaft. My underside is to a surface that is never quite wet and never quite dry. I do not see the yard, the railing, the cloud-sea far below. I see, poorly, movement and shadow. What I know precisely is the pattern of vibrations that travel to me along eight lines, and through them, the whole tower in miniature: a midge, a drip, a root, a swift passing close enough to push the air, a gear thinking about moving.

I have tried to imagine myself otherwise, to see if another life in this place would fit the way I am held here.

If I were a swift, I would own the hollow. I would nest in a vent hole known by the damp around its mouth, tear up and down the forty meters of air, eat what the warm updraft lifts, scream louder at dusk and drop home in a spiral. But I cannot fly, and I cannot rest in air. To let go here is to fall a hundred and fifty meters into a rumor of land. The void between platforms is not a place I can cross. It would be the end of me in the first fall.

If I were a frog, I would live in a teacup. At dawn I would be in the saucer of a cable clamp, in the hollow of a gear tooth, in a leaf axil where condensation settles and stays. My whole pond system would be a spoonful of warm, still water, and at evening I would call with thousands of others, my plocking amplified by hollow steel until it became part of the drone. I know that chorus well — it comes up through the pipe I am anchored to, as though from inside the metal. But I am not built for that damp. I would dry too fast in a teacup, and I do not call. I wait, and waiting is a different kind of voice.

If I were a lizard, I would choose a transformer box and claim its particular hour of heat, basking motionless while the ordinary white noon light turned iron into a griddle. I can feel that heat through my feet when the wind shifts. I do not seek it. My life is on the underside, where rain torn sideways at this height rarely reaches, where wasps paper their grey cities and where the warmth is from engines, not sun. I do not watch the world with eyes. I do not need a view.

The one I stayed longest with was the vine. The ficus root and I are anchored in the same pipe joint. It advances a handspan in a year, grows over gauge dials until the dials are found only by following the roots down, drinks condensation where it crosses a warm pipe. I arrived, if arrival is the word, as small as a sesame seed of an egg sac tucked under a catwalk. My siblings ballooned on silk into the shaft wind. Some may have become swift food. I stayed in the seam. I am anchored the way a root is anchored: I will not be hurried, and I will not be moved.

But a root only grows forward. It cannot mend a tear. It cannot take down what it made yesterday and make it again finer today. I can.

What I have, that a root does not, is extension. I spin surfaces between my anchors, thin as breath, so that I am no longer only my eight points of contact. I sit in the center of what I have made, with a leg on every radius, and I know the world by what touches it. A spider. That is the word a visitor would use if they ever looked close enough to see me against the rust dust, grey-brown as the lizards, motionless for hours. I do not use it. I sit, and the web does the touching for me.

Find me halfway down in spirit, if not in measure: on the engine-shed platform, where the air is warm from below and cold comes down from the open top, in that closed weather the tower makes for itself. My web spans from the rim of an idle gear housing to a copper pipe run, across a seam where a ficus root has been patiently crossing for years. The gear below me has not turned except for a sigh once every nine days, when the great wheel underneath ventilates the shaft and the hanging ferns lean, almost imperceptibly, into the slow wind. The gear will move a millimeter in a month. My web has slack loops built for that. When it sighs forward, the whole web shivers and does not tear.

Above me, an inverted garden drinks condensation as it falls and leaks it, drip by drip, onto my top radii. At dawn the cold steel sweats in beads that run in dark, slow lines, and my silk darkens with them. I drink those beads before the midges come, leg to leg, the way other inhabitants drink from a leaf cup. Below me a frog keeps a clamp saucer as a pond and lays eggs there, and the tadpoles eat algae green on copper in water that may not be replaced for weeks. Their plocking reaches me through the pipe, a wet percussion under the drone. Below that, the lamps have never fully gone out, and the moths and midges swarm in a permanent amber noon, whole generations that have never seen the sky forty meters above them. Every nine days the tower exhales, and some of those insects are carried up into my web on the same current the swifts ride.

That is my hunting ground, and it comes to me. I do not hurry because the tower does not hurry. A midge arrives with a light, quick tremor. A drip arrives with a single heavier note and then stillness. A rust flake falls with a dull irregular tap. A wasp blunders in with angry buzzing. The tip of the vine root pushes against a top anchor for days at a time with a pressure so slow I know it more as a change in tension than as a movement. A swift passes overhead and the braod push of air bows the whole web. A rodent runs the cable tray above and I freeze, reading the heavy tread through concrete in my front legs, knowing it is not prey and not weather. I can tell a midge from a drip from loose rust because the silk they touch is bolted, at its far ends, to two tons of slowly breathing steel. I sit in the center of that drum and listen.

When something sticks and struggles, I move then, and only then. I bite, I wrap, I wait. I do not eat at once if the gear is about to sigh; I have learned the tide of that breath. I eat the old web when I rebuild, taking my own work back into myself, protein patiently recycled. I have molted here, left smaller skins tucked into the seam, and remade the web wider each time. The vine root has crossed my upper anchor once since I was small. That is how I count years. Not in days. In sighs, and in handspans of root, and in leaves that take three seasons to rot into the thin soil on a ledge above me.

I am not an apex and I am not an outsider. I eat what the hum brings: the midges the lamps breed, the small neighbors of the frogs, the insects farming the warmth of the pipe beside me. The lizard on the transformer below eats what wanders into the sun. The swifts eat what the updraft lifts. The frogs eat what the damp breeds. The wasps under the catwalk do not come near my web, and I do not go near theirs. Everything dies where it lives, and becomes grit in a blocked drain, and feeds the next handspan of vine. My own dead, when my time in the seam is done, will crumble into that same rust-dust and be used by springtails and mites, who eat nothing I would notice.

I have never crossed the void between platforms. I have never needed to. There is food, water, shelter, and a whole world of vibration in a single seam. The swifts know their nest holes by the pattern of damp around them. I know my seam by the pattern of tremors across it: warm pipe, cold dawn, frog below, vine above, gear waiting, lamps below that, sky far above that, so far that it is only a change in the cold of the air at the top of the shaft when the sun moves.

So I stay in the center, upside down when I sleep, legs out to every line I have spun. The tower roars around me and I make a small noise inside it, a strumming when prey lands, a tick when I mend. I am part of the humming together, very slowly, at the top of the world — not pristine, not in collapse, established in that settled balance of decay and repair. Damp, not wet. Intermittent, patient, endless. And alive, everywhere a drip can be caught, and everywhere a web can be strung across the pause between one movement of a gear and the next.
