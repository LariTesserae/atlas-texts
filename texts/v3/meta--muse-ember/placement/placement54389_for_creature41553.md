First, under feet. Before I am anything you could see properly.

I am the felt. A carpet of needles, deep green going to yellow-green at the newest edge, cool against stone that has no right to hold any of it. Between the groves there is nothing like me for a day, or two days, of blinding white salt and pumice and black glass riverbeds, and then, without transition, this. The edge is sharp. You step from the flat onto me and the sound changes in your teeth.

I do not know, at first, that I am growing. I settle. That is closer. Every needle is a trap and a root. The fine rock-dust that never settles out of the grey-blue air settles on me, and I lay it down again as mineral, a thin embryonic skin, upward the way the mountain itself is going upward. A hand's breadth in a season. Yellow where I am new. Deep green where I have been listening for much longer.

I do not drink. I want to be clear about that, because thirst is the first word a visitor expects. There is no water here to be thirsty for, and no word for it in my body. Frost forms on the high stone at night and vanishes without ever becoming a drip. My sap, when I am old enough to have sap, is dark, iron-rich, alkaline, smelling of crushed stone and ozone. It does not rise. It is worked upward.

By vibration.

The whole range groans under its own weight, a pulse so slow I could mistake it for a heartbeat, or for my own, until I learned there was no difference worth naming. Stone pops far away and always close. Sharp report. Then another. The ground comes up through the stone, through me, through every needle. When the wind crosses the salt flats they sing high enough to hurt, and I answer, but not at the wind's pitch. At the grove's.

That is the diet. Sound, dust, and dim light, in that order. The tone holds for hours, for days, unchanging, and I need only that it hold.

A season later I am a leaf.

Not a leaf in the hurry of other worlds. One leaf in a season, broad as a door, unfurling slowly enough that a person could stand inside my curl for years without my noticing the change in light. I lean toward a sun that is old and low and smeared behind dust, and I turn my underside pale to catch what there is of it. At night — which is not darker than noon, only the same dimness held longer — I give off a faint green-gold phosphorescence. From a far ridge I must look like a lamp lit in a desert, though no one is there to see me. No one is ever there to see me. That is not loneliness. Loneliness is an animal word. There are no animals. Nothing flies to pollinate anything. Nothing eats. My leaves are never torn, never chewed, never nested in, never burrowed under. Fallen leaves lie perfect on the moss below me. Fallen seeds lie among the moss like black river glass, indistinguishable from the flakes people left.

I was green before I emerged. I was brown and perfect while still in the bud. I cannot give you a true order for these things. Some of my kind hang with fruit ripe on branches that have not yet grown. Some set seed that splits before it touches the moss, and the sapling is already deep green before the seed has fallen. In a grove, all the ages are present at once. It is not a life cycle. It is a draft, copied three times across the basin with slight variations, the same island repeating out past sight and then, disconcertingly, beginning again.

I know this because the drone does not arrive the same way in all three places. It arrives a little before the crack in one, a little after in another. Distance increases when I turn, if I could turn. I cannot turn. I can only resonate differently.

Later still — later is a word I learned from the people, who come rarely and leave quickly — my spine thickens and hollows. Plates of bark like layered stone. A trunk empty from the ground to where a branch ought to be. A resonant chamber wrapped in something that has learned to look like rock because rock is what it is made of.

Inside the hollow, the mountain's groan becomes pressure. Becomes movement. Becomes growth. I do not make the sound. The uplift makes it, flexing, still bending like clay pushed up from below, a hundred thousand years from finished. I take it patiently and give it back slower, as leaf, as needle, as the faint glow that makes the grove visible as a green island in an emptiness that stays empty, mile after mile of salt and stone.

I cannot shout over it here. I cannot think in complete sentences for long. I do not need a sentence. I need the tone to hold.

And so I flower. Once. Over fifty years a pale, phosphorescent flower opens. No fly comes. No bird carries anything. I repeat myself anyway, vegetatively, stone-like seeds falling heavy. That is the whole of my intention, if I have one: to continue the tuning.

The people, when they come, come the way the plants spread — sparsely, primitively, and without connecting anything. They do not live in the groves. They pass through as the wind passes, always in the same direction. They stack a cairn on a ridge, single stones, no mortar. The same cairn, impossibly, on a second summit. A half-wall in a hollow, three courses high, curved from basalt slabs and lashed with dark plant fiber, never roofed. A knapping floor on a salt flat, flakes in a neat circle around a half-finished axe, abandoned mid-thought. A line of stakes whose cord is long gone.

I feel them only as a new vibration in the stone, a chord the mountain did not make. Their footsteps in the dust seem to form a moment before they step. A structure ruined from the north looks newly built, and newly abandoned, from the south. They are encountered as a cairn, then as a wall, then as nothing for miles, then as flakes again — repetitions, the way I repeat.

I begin, then, to drop.

A vine from a branch above me descends in a thick curtain. It roots again in mid-air, in the haze itself, precipitating enough mineral around the root tip to make a footing that wasn't there. It hangs, and re-anchors, and hangs again. A curtain that is also a ladder, also an ear trumpet. This is the only movement I have ever made. Not walking. Dropping, and making ground out of air, patiently, one precipitated root at a time.

That is where I find myself, in the end. Not in the center of the grove, not on the ridge, not in the white flat between the islands where there is nothing to be. At the edge, where the moss felt stops sharply and the reeds stand thinner and higher, every hollow stem an organ pipe tuned to the mountain's hum. A vine rooted in air, holding by what it itself has made, listening at the limit of the enclosure.

I am made of listening, and of holding a tone that was not mine. I take dust from the air and lay it down as soil. I take a groan from stone and turn it into a leaf that opens once a season toward a light that will never change. I take the whistle of the salt flat and return it at the grove's pitch.

Lush, waterless, and entirely alone, still under construction at a pace too slow for any animal to witness — growing, as everything here grows, in anticipation of a noise the world has not quite made yet.

If you are reading this, that noise has already begun to arrive. Your footstep is forming in the dust a moment before you step. I am already resonating to it.
