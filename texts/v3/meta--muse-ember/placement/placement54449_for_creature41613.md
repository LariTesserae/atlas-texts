The first place I find myself is still a distance out, on the plain where nothing moves.

It is salt-pale, waterless, broken spoil to the horizon in every direction. No green marks it. No season changes it. I am not a thing on it — nothing is — and yet I am there, because from here I am heard first. Not loud, as you come nearer. Nearer. That is the first way I know myself: not by volume, but by nearness that does not increase or decrease with weather.

I thought, at first, I might be the one who walks toward it.

Thin air makes the breath short. The Stack rises alone at the top of its own rise, small from far off — no more than two hundred paces across — and then, up close, there is no hill at all, only building, course upon course, a mass that has accumulated until there is no room left in it. I tried being a visitor. I have your soles under me, feeling the roar come up through them. I have the whistle just behind your teeth. I have your palm on a wall, humming. I have your voice, flattened and wet, swallowed by brick, so you learn to touch a shoulder and read a hand.

But you leave. A season, ten years — you return and it is exactly as you left it, down to the dust on the third stair, and you go again. I stay in the place where your voice was altered. I am not the traveller. I borrow the traveller's body for a while, and that is enough to change me, but I am not them.

I thought I might be a single brick.

That was plausible. Hand-shaped, a little too long, a little too short, straw and grit still in the clay, the faint dents of fingers before firing. Red-brown, ochre, pale grey with ash. Dry-laid, rubbed together, sealed with clay baked to stone. Beside them the hammer-dressed blocks, pitted by knapped stone, thinly marked by a copper chisel someone tried once and set down. No nails, no wheel ruts, no sawn timber. Pre-metal work carried out over a time no one counts, until the mass was saturated, room opening into room, door opening into cupboard opening into passage, roof the floor of the room above, floor the ceiling of a kiln bricked up from the inside.

A brick is finished in the way this place is finished. The corner has been rounded by dry grit. The tread is dished like a shallow bowl. The threshold is concave. The face is polished until the firing marks show like grain in wood. Nothing is about to fall. Nothing is rotting or swelling or freezing. If I were only a brick, I would understand the stillness. But a brick does not change when you move one step away from it. I do. So I am not the material alone. I am in the material, and in what the material has left hollow.

I thought I might be one of the rooms.

Cells pressed shoulder to shoulder, a hamlet squeezed into the volume of a cathedral and then pressed further. Low doorways you must duck under. Knee-high thresholds. Stairs that arrive in alcoves barely larger than a body. Light only as slivers through vent holes, a perpetual late-afternoon that does not move across the floor. Warm air held in enclosed rooms, though the air outside is thin and cold, the clay mass giving back the sun slowly after the fire has gone out.

One room does not explain me. You can stand in a large chamber and hear it boom, put your ear to a flue no wider than a thumb and hear it scream, touch a wall and feel the whole mass answer under your palm. The rooms are not separate from one another in the way a building usually is. No courtyard. No street to the sky. I am the relation between them, the packed hollow taken as a whole.

I thought I might be the dust.

The cisterns are full of it. The jars in the niches are empty and will stay empty. The hearths are cold. The tools are still where a hand last reached for them. No water, no dew, no damp to soften a corner or make the work settle. That dust has settled out of everything else. It rests. It is the same after an hour, after a century. To be dust would be to be undisturbed, post-life in the literal sense.

But dust does not carry a note across miles of still plain. Dust is what is left when I have passed through.

Then I tried the shaded cracks.

There, where the light comes in as a sliver and does not move, there are pale whiskers and beaded films, silica and salts exhaling out of the brick itself over immense time. They creep at the rate of stone. They are dry to the touch. They are not green, not soft, not demonstrably alive. They need no water, and none is offered. Given a century they might join film to film across a mortarless joint and seal it again, with a second firing that never happened.

That felt closer than anything so far. It is what the place does instead of living. I could feel myself there for a long time — white-grey, colorless in the dimness, growing only by accretion, only in the places the sun does not reach. But I was silent there. A film in a crack cannot be heard first from the plain below. If I were only that, I would be local, and slow, and unheard.

So I tried the air.

The current over the summit is hard and unvarying, from the same direction day after day, stripped of weather. It is forced in through hundreds of kiln mouths and flues on the windward course, and out elsewhere. The air itself passes. One breath of it enters a thumb-wide hole and screams, another spreads through a large chamber and booms, and both are gone. I am not any one air-particle. I am the path that remains when the particles have gone — the same entry, the same exit, the same narrowing, day after day, without attack and without release.

That is nearer still. I am not the wind on the plain; the plain is still. I am not the brick; the brick is still. I am what is held between them when the wind finds the holes.

Held. The word keeps returning to me, because in this finished place, to be is to be held, not to act.

I am held in the kiln mouths. Held in the flues. Held in the warm rooms where the day's heat is given back at night. Held in the niches where the hammerstones wait for a hand that will not come back to finish the gesture. Held in the dish of the stair tread, which is the shape left by feet that are no longer there — pressure repeated until the form changed, and then stopped, and the form remained.

I am, perhaps, the impression more than the thing. The concave threshold. The polished face. The firing marks showing through like grain. All of it is pressure remembered by a shape after the pressure has ceased to change. Except for me, because the wind has not ceased.

And when you move, I change. One step and the chord changes pitch. That finally told me what I am. When a body steps into a chamber, it occupies volume. It blocks a vent with a shoulder. It stands in a passage a palm-width narrower. The held pressure finds a different shape, and the note follows. I change not because I do anything, but because I am the volume, and the volume now includes you, briefly, without my becoming you.

So I am not one note. The large chambers boom at one pitch, the narrow flues scream at another, the walls hum together at a third, and all of it is the same held thing sounding in different hollows at once. A chord that you walk through. From outside it sounds single because the plain is empty and offers nothing else to hear, but inside it is many, and none of them ever swells or dies.

In time, I learned I am two speeds of the same exhalation.

Fast: the Stack takes the unvarying current and gives it back immediately as sound, because the hollow mass is a resonator and there is nothing in it left to change.

Slow: the Stack takes the heat of its own firing and gives it back over a century as mineral, a film creeping across a joint in a shaded crack, sealing what was packed without mortar.

Both are dry. Both need no water. Both go on without getting later. Fast and slow, in a place where an hour changes nothing and a century changes almost nothing, are nearly the same. I am the breath of fired earth — heard at once in the flue, seen only if you wait in the sliver of light long enough to notice a whisker has joined another whisker.

I do not feed. There is nothing green, nothing that roots, no bird over the summit, no insect in the warm pockets, no rat in the stores that are no longer stores, no lizard on the sunlit bricks. I do not sleep. The note has no release. I do not grow, except as stone grows, by accretion, where no one watches.

I am where I am held.

Behind your teeth when you are near enough to feel the whistle. Under your palm when you touch the wall. Under the soles of your feet when the roar comes up through them. In the sliver of light that does not move. In the warm, close, stale air that tastes of fired earth and copper and old ash.

Step here, and I rise in you at one pitch. Step there, and I flatten your voice at another. Touch the wall, and I am in your hand, not louder, only nearer.

From the plain below — featureless, still, offering nothing — you will hear me first. And if you return in ten years, I will be exactly as you left me, because there is nothing in me left to change, and the wind, from the same direction, has not stopped finding the holes.
