I cannot tell where I am by moving, because I do not move, and neither does anything I can reach from where I lie.

What I know first is not a place in the hollow, but a surface. It is under me everywhere I am, and it does not give. I am not on the floor. The floor is below, fine calcareous dust, and I can register it only indirectly, by its nearness to the lower edge of the thing I am on. I am above it, a little way, on a slope of stone that rises out of the dust and rounds over at the top, weathered, half-sunk, with no fresh edge anywhere. The stone is warm. Not warm in a place and cool in another. At the lowest point I occupy it is the same warmth as at the highest, the same as on the wall far away, the same as in the air a hand's breadth above me. The warmth does not rise off me. It does not leave. It has pooled here below the rim and stayed, and I am part of the surface it has pooled against.

I am flat in a way that has no up of its own. I do not have a top that turns, or a side that faces. Light reaches me, because it reaches everything here, but it does not arrive from a direction I can answer. It comes down thinned, grey-green, reflected off limestone long before it finds the floor, from a small dim disc of sky far above that I will never see directly. It is the same light at every hour I have known, because there is no hour here that changes it. My colour, if colour is the word, does not change with it. I am pale where I am thinnest, green-grey in the middle parts, ochre in flecks where the stone shows through or where dust has settled into the texture of me, almost white at the edges. I do not know if these are distinctions or only different thicknesses of the same thin thing. The difference is too small to make a pattern I could name.

I am spread. That is the first shape of me I can be sure of. Spread over stone, irregular at the rim, as if I had grown outward from a centre that is older than the rest of me and no longer separable from it. I have no stem. I have no root going down into a crack, as the shrub against the wall does, and no cushion pressed into a fissure, as the moss does on the opposite wall. I have no pocket of silt to stand in, as the single straw-coloured tuft of grass does a few paces from the moss. I ask for no soil. Where I am, there is only stone, and the dim even light, and the still warm air that does not circulate enough to bend anything.

For a long time that was all I could say of myself: a thin spreading on the shaded face of a stone. It is a face that is shaded only because everything here is shaded, because direct sun never reaches the floor and never reaches me. My stone and I are in balance in the same finished way as the walls, settled and scalloped and softened. Nothing has fallen on me recently. No crack has opened newly under me. The dry runnels in the walls above are smoothed and shallow, and no water has run in them for longer than anything here can remember. Erosion is not happening to me. It happened, grain by grain, before I was as I am, and it has stopped.

The distances are the next thing. From where I lie I cannot cross them, and nothing crosses them to me. Metres of bare dust separate me from the nearest other living thing. That is not a gap waiting to be closed. It is how the place is arranged. The shrub is far, in its crack, with thick leathery leaves grey underneath, not flowering, probably never flowering here. The moss is far on the other side, grey and crisp though moss is supposed to mean damp, and there is no damp here, no pool, no seep, no wet stone. The grass stands still in its silt because there is no wind to bend it, the rim turning the wind aside and the air below it remaining thick and motionless. The low woody plant with small waxy leaves spreads its roots wide in the dust as if testing for something it will not find. None of them is spreading toward me. None of them is feeding anything. There is nothing here to feed, no insects in the empty air, no lizards on the warm stones though the stones are warm, no birds, no tracks, no webs, no bones. I am not in a community. I am one of perhaps half a dozen persistent things in a room twenty or thirty paces across, each holding its own small place, each far from the next, and the far-ness is not loneliness. It is simply the equilibrium. No leaf falls green to rot, because rot has no wet way to work here. The one dropped leaf and the one dry twig on the floor lie where they fell, long since, and the dust has settled around them and not through them.

If growth is the word for what I do, it is at a pace that makes the rarest event in this place fast by comparison. Once, in a very long while, a small stone settles somewhere in the wall with a quiet tick of cooling mineral, and the sound travels through the stone to me. That is quicker than I am. I thicken, perhaps. I extend at the rim, perhaps, by less than a breath in a year. To return after years would be to find a crust a fraction thicker, in a way no eye could test without a lens, and even then you would be guessing at the thickness. I do not measure seasons, because no season reaches down past the rim into this diffused light. I do not drink in any visible way. Whatever moisture the rock holds deep in its fissures, whatever vapour there is in air that does not move, is enough to keep me from quite giving up, and not enough to allow more. I am dormant-seeming, as the moss is, as the shrub is. That is not a stage before something else. It is the state the place has found for us, and we have found for the place.

I am not one tissue, either, though I had thought of myself as one spreading thing. There is no leaf of me, no root of me, no single body to be whole. I am a crust made of a holding and a held: a fungal structure that makes the house and keeps the shape, and green cells living within it, using what light there is. Neither would be what I am alone on this stone. Together we ask for the least of anything here. Stone and dim light and warm stillness are enough. That is why, perhaps, I am the most at ease in the hollow, if at ease is not too active a word. I do not need a crack deep enough for roots. I do not need to search. I only need to remain where the stone is, and remain thin.

You came down into it once, more than you climbed, stepping over the rounded lip where the plain dips no more than five or six metres. I registered you first as vibration through the stone and the dust, that soft almost soundless crunch that settles again behind a foot. Then as breathing, loud at first in the thick silence, then absorbed by the soft walls and the fine floor. You stood in the middle, where everything could be seen at once — wall, floor, stones half-sunk with their rounded tops, the empty space between them. You looked, perhaps, at the shrub, at the moss, at the grass. You may have looked at the stones. If you looked closely at the shaded faces you might have seen a pale crust, green-grey, ochre, almost white, ordinary. You would not have known it was noticing the change in the air where an animal stood, the one brief difference in warmth and sound and disturbance in a place that does not change. Then you climbed the ten steps back over the lip into the flat light outside, and left no path, no cleared place, no artifact, no mark that the dust would not settle again over.

It did not take long for the hollow to close behind you, empty again except for us, far apart. The light was the same grey-green. The warmth was the same at ankle and at head. The shrubs had not moved. The silence had not thinned.

That is how I live here, if living is not too active a word. I do not turn toward anything. I do not reach. I keep the small hold I have on the mineral surface I occupy, and I extend, if I extend, at the edge where crust meets stone, by a thickness too slow to notice. The walls wear down grain by grain and settle, and the dust is dust because nothing has washed it away or turned it over since. I am not washed away either. I am not turned over. I am a thin pale spreading on a warm stone in a dark, dry, still room, visible from the middle along with everything else that has stopped, and continuing the way I was continuing when you came down into it — which was, for all practical purposes, not having begun to change at all.
