I would not find myself on the rim, looking out.

I would find myself at the inside edge of a pool, where the water is giving up at a little more than body heat and the air is too thin for a breath to finish. My breath comes short, but not because I am climbing. It comes short because I have no height to breathe from. The ground is warm under me and wet above me, and that contradiction is where I live.

A footstep sinks here and the moss closes behind it in a day. I do not stand long enough for a footstep. I spread. Sideways, the way the clubmosses root and drink. Sideways, the way the springs appear from under stones and from the sides of mounds without having traveled far downhill. For a while I thought I might be something that moves through this in a line — a hawk, perhaps, riding the geothermal lift over the second plateau, patient because flapping is expensive in thin air. I had the shadow of that for a moment, circling, watching for a snake in a gully below.

But I am not watching from above. I am under the watching. I fall, if you can call it falling, through the layers instead: through tree-fern weather, through a bromeliad tank no larger than a cup where mosquito larvae walk the meniscus and a tadpole waits out the rain, under an orchid root silver and flat against bark, drinking air. A hummingbird whirs past and it is not loud, exactly. It is irritable and close, and then gone. A glass frog clings under a leaf and its heart shows through, drinking condensation through its skin that beads on warm stone and seems to fall upward off it. I am on that stone too, in the same film. I am not the frog with the heart. I am what is wet enough for it to drink.

Lower still, the world thickens. Red peat, layer on layer, not soil so much as a stored history of things that lived and compacted and stayed wet. Cushion plants sit on it like mattresses, and the ground between them is green-brown and still. I run in that stillness, under the surface, in root-threads and diatom films and the slow traffic of chemicals. An ant highway crosses above me — a thousand workers, each carrying a green sail — and their laid trail refreshes the air over my head with every pass. At night the trail fades. At dawn it is laid again. I do not answer it. I register it as a change in the damp.

And then the heat comes up through my soles, if I have soles. Somewhere the peat gives out and the pools begin, steaming without boiling, yellow-white rings of sulphur where the steam condenses. This is where I thicken. This is where I finally have work that does not require me to go anywhere.

I am thin as felt. Orange where the heat is worst, yellow at the edge, brown where I have begun to bury myself.

All day I do this one thing, patiently and without pause: I take the dissolved minerals out of the water and lay them down. Grain by grain I build travertine, terraces, factory floors banded and stepped. The workers stand on what they build, and are built over in turn. When a vent hisses — sigh, pause, hiss again on its cycle of minutes, like pistons in another room — I do not move. I simply work faster in the pulse. That is my pace. Not the hummingbird's. Not the frog chorus erupting for ten minutes at dusk and then stopping as if switched off. Mine is the slower pulse underneath all of them, the thump felt through the feet at the warmest pools.

I am not loud here because loud does not carry. Moss absorbs it. Wet leaves absorb it. Heat thickens midday. What I make is not sound but trace: a chemical laid down, a vibration through wet earth, a mineral band. The termite tower nearby breathes through its shafts, a faint regular exhale I feel when the air shifts; the leafcutter fungus turns green shards into food in dark chambers I will never see; the frogs take in the morning condensation on their backs and wait to be briefly, densely loud. All of that happens above and around the ring where I am. I am at the edge because the ring is the only honest place on this shelf — where hot meets cool, where steam becomes water again, where rock in shade can be cold and an inch away be burning from the earth below. The rules slip sideways at the edges, and I live in the slip.

In the late afternoon the clouds rise from below to meet the rim and the whole shelf disappears into white. I do not disappear so much as lose my edge. I become cool vapor for a while, water beading on everything, filling a metal cup left on the moss without being poured. The frogs drink me from the air. The bromeliads fill. Then the sun breaks again, hard and ordinary, and the ground warms under my feet as if the engine below had turned up just slightly for the evening shift.

I condense again. In the same ring. On the same stone.

And the work resumes: I lay down another band of mineral over the last one, and under it, another self.
