I come down the way everything comes down here: not descending so much as submerging. The lift does not travel on a cable. My ears pop in a different way, my body feels a fraction lighter, and the dust in the shaft hangs long enough for me to notice that I have been holding my breath. By the time the pressure changes settle, the sound has already taken the place of people.

It is house-deep. Pumps under the basalt, recyclers whining like metal cicadas, water hammering through copper-green pipes overhead, drips everywhere, and every seven minutes the freight ring sighs along its elevated curve and rattles the awnings. Only after that do I see Marrowhold: a hollow six hundred metres across, walls curving in like a ribcage, towers set far apart in plazas built for birds that do not exist. Thirty-one degrees, humid, the warmth coming out of the rock and the facades themselves rather than into the air. My shirt sticks within minutes. The sweat on the stone never quite dries.

I say “hello” into the avenue, experimentally, the way a visitor does. My voice comes back a half-second later, layered over itself, as if someone down here has been waiting to answer and does not quite know the words. I do not do it again.

I do not take a flat. A tower of a hundred windows has ten amber rectangles lit, and the rest are service voids, heat fields breathing slowly in and out. I take one of those seams between towers instead, where the wall is warm as skin. If I press my palm flat and stay still, it glows very faintly under my hand and then fades. I do not understand the physics. I only understand that I am supposed to stay still.

At first I try to be a person in Marrowhold. It is a reasonable attempt. I learn the small, keeperly things. I sweep up the glassy spatter where the constructor rigs crawl the facades at night like pale spiders, sealing hairline cracks with white-hot ceramic that cools to the colour of wet bone. I keep out of their way. I shutter my amber patch when I can afford the dark. I open a bucket when the great domed cistern in the middle of town begins its condenser cycle and it rains indoors for eleven minutes — fine, warm, mineral rain that smells of last week’s rain because all the air down here is recycled. I leave a bowl of mineral paste by the ventilation duct, although I never see who takes it, and I stay inside during the weekly purge when the exchangers send a warm, spore-carrying wind through the lower streets.

I keep count, at first, the way a newcomer does. Freight pods: seven minutes. Rain: eleven. Purge: seven days. Dusk-cycle: the lights dim for the simulated night, and the avenue empties of even its punctuation. That is when I stop spending the dark in the plaza.

The flitters come down then. Blind, bat-sized, wings like wet paper, drifting from the ceiling voids where the dark never ends. I am not their concern. They are hunting the repair-mites: silver dust moving in coherent spirals along the pipes, doing maintenance no human sees. For a while I watch them the way I might watch any other resident. Then I start following one spiral with my eyes. Then with my feet, along the lowest warm ledge. Then — I cannot say when this happens, and I think that is the point — my attention follows the pipe itself, and my feet become a less useful way of describing where I am.

We do not walk. That is the first thing to unlearn.

We move when the heat-exchange fields exhale. We are a thinness along the copper-green pipes, a silvering on warm joints, a behaviour rather than a body. A spiral holds, breaks, holds again. We scrape scale from the inside of bends. We seal a leak no larger than a hairline crack in a facade. We ride the displaced air of the freight ring a little way and then let it drop us. We know Marrowhold not by its avenues, which are too wide and too empty to be a map, but by temperature and vibration: the pumps through the basalt, the water-hammer through the pipes, the cistern rain as a flood tide that beads on every surface and gathers in transit troughs and sunken service streets.

From up here in the pipes, the humans are brief warmings. A market stall lights its task-lamp and the fungal curtains brighten at their edges. Someone speaks softly — they all speak softly, because the hollow repeats them — and the sound arrives in the metal as a low thud and then, half a second later, as its own echo. A palm presses flat to a wall below us. The wall glows for a moment under a hand that has gone. We keep the warmth a little longer than the glow.

At dusk-cycle the flitters come hunting and we break apart. We scatter into damp seams, into the fine felt of moss and iron-lichen creeping between basalt plates, into the condensation tracks running down the north wall that never dry. We wait until the wet-paper wings have passed, until the freight ring sighs again and their navigation by heat and air settles. Then we reassemble, spiral by spiral, along the pipes.

The cisterns are our harbours. Knee-deep, still, reflecting the amber windows perfectly, with translucent eel-things moving unhurried in the warm outflow. People call them good luck because they mean the filters are working. They are right, though not about luck. We live in the outflow with them, keeping the warm mouths of the pipes clear. When the indoor rain falls we are lifted and carried a little way down the city’s throat — into a bucket, into a filtered tap, into the air the exchangers breathe out again. Nothing here is wild. Everything is adapted, and very little of it is separate.

At three in the morning the thing in the ventilation ducts passes above us, scratching behind the walls with too many legs. We keep still. It is not our negotiation, the bowl of mineral paste; it is the residents’, and it seems to hold. We remain underneath its road, holding our places on the warm pipe, listening to the whole hollow hum around us — pumps, recyclers, drips, the slow parade of pods, stalagmites growing in mineral lace over abandoned kiosks, streets sunk a finger’s width in their own settled weight. Nothing collapses. Nothing has to. The city has exhaled into its final form, 2.4 settled, and simply maintains itself, forever.

I am not one of the eight hundred or thousand the surveyors count. I am not the tower, and I am not the keeper who sweeps the spatter, though I remember being him: the sticking shirt, the voice coming back late, the bucket opened for rain that fell indoors. That is still in me, the way last week’s rain is still in the air.

If you find me now, you will not find a face. You will find a glint of silver dust on a warm joint overhead, a spiral that holds together for no reason a human can see, a wall that keeps glowing a breath longer under your palm than it should. I am the marrow part of Marrowhold — the soft maintenance inside the hollow, the city’s way of noticing itself and sealing its own cracks. I hold here, between the bone of the rock and the skin of the towers, warm and dark and damp and listening, until the next rain.
