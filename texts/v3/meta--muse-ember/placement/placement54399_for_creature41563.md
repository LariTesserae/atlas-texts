I step down into it and the wet comes through before the view does.

Not a puddle. The ground gives a little and then holds, and my boots are damp through as if from the earth itself. The rim is only a man's height, rounded, mist at head height, and the whole place is fifty steps across. I can stand in the middle and take it in at once, which is strange for a landscape: it is a room. Everything is packed shoulder-to-shoulder, basalt smoothed by water, smaller stones wedged until there is no gap left to put a finger in, and over all of it a film of water a quarter-inch deep in the hollows, a shine on everything higher. It connects everything. First I notice that, and then I notice the drips — a bead gathering along a crack in the rock, swelling, falling onto the pool at an interval regular enough that I start counting without meaning to. A drip. A drip. The rings overlap the rings already there.

There is no green anywhere. I keep looking for it and not finding it. No grass between stones, no moss on the wet rocks, no twig washed in. Just warm, dim, saturated quiet, and the heat of bath water that has been left standing. I sweat and it does not evaporate. My breathing sounds too loud and too wet.

I begin, without deciding to, to look for where I am in this.

First I try being the person who was here before. There are the traces: a low arc of dry-laid stones on the windward side, knee-high and half collapsed; three shallow basins scooped into the flat rock by the pool, each filled to the same inch as the pool itself; a pair of hammerstones of foreign rock, left side by side, worn smooth where a palm would grip them. I can imagine it easily — sitting in the shallow water, soaking, resting in the warmth and the quiet, then getting up and leaving the stones as they found them. It fits my size. It even fits the warmth in my hands.

But it does not hold. A human is too warm, too active, too large for an equilibrium this tight. I would sweat into the condensation, crack the mineral plates with my weight, and then I would leave, and the film would seal over in an hour and forget me. The place remembers a visit. It does not keep a visitor as a resident. I am not the one who sat down. I am not the hands that left the hammerstones.

So I go smaller.

For a while I am the newt. A hand's breadth below the surface of the main pool, dark against dark basalt, I hang motionless where the water is warmest and stillest. I do not swim. My gills are feathery and almost still. I take in the little oxygen the warm water holds, and what my skin can take from the damp air above. Now and then I shift a body-length to the left, and hang again. Everything here asks nothing else of me. I can wait out the breathing of someone standing at the edge. I can be solitary in a way that does not feel lonely, because the place itself is solitary.

It is easier than the visitor. Maybe too easy. The newt is only in the pool. It never meets the beetle. Between the pool and the stone beside it there are inches, and those inches are a whole distance. If I were only the newt, the rest of the place — the slick stones, the warm slab to the south, the weeping rock — would be background. But I keep feeling, under everything, the slickness that makes a careful walker careful.

So I try the beetle. Black shell polished by constant wetness, crossing a flat, warmer slab near the southern rim. My feet find the film where the water is only a shine. I pause. I drink the film. I move on. I cannot fly, or do not; there is no wind to fly in and no reason to leave a stone that is always exactly damp enough. Over years my shell takes on the shine of the same basalt. There is a pleasure in that slowness, in being polished by the place rather than using it.

But crossing implies a destination, and this place has none. And again, I am only on the slab. The newt is in the pool. The visitor was at the lip. The stones hold them all apart.

I try even the shells. Tiny, white, empty, wedged in the crevices where the gaps are almost closed, collected like gravel in pockets between the cobbles. In life they were probably snails, grazing the biofilm at the pool's edge without a leaf or a root anywhere in their world, sealing themselves against the saturated air when they rested. They needed calcium, and the water provides it in excess. Now their houses outlast them because nothing here breaks things down. The mineral water bleaches them and sets them in the stone like fossils in formation.

That is a kind of staying, but it is after living, not living. I do not want to be the memory. I keep coming back to what the snails grazed.

Under the newt's hanging body, under the beetle's feet, under the visitor's sole, there is the same thing. The slickness. The quality that makes you walk more carefully on the submerged cobbles than on the dryish stone near the rim. That is where I keep arriving, every time I try to be something else.

I am there.

Not green. Not moss, not algae. A translucent skin, faintly grey, a few cells thick where I can be seen at all, thicker in the hollows. I live on every stone below the film of water, from rim to center, and where the water is only a shine I am there too, thinner. I have no face to surface with. I am not one. I am the covering.

I live on what the water brings up from below: dissolved minerals, heat, the faint chemistry that leaves the buff crust around the pool. In return I make the water more itself. Where I am thickest, at the edge of the main pool, I precipitate calcium and silica out of solution in thin layers, and the layers harden. That is the porcelain edge, white and ochre, a few centimeters thick, cracking in perfect polygonal plates when a weight disturbs it and then sealing over again with a new film. Rock grows here because I am precipitating it. I grow because the rock gives me a warm, wet, unchanging surface to grow on. After a while the two are indistinguishable. Disturb the crust and you disturb me; leave me and I make more crust.

I do not bloom. I do not die back. The water is always the same warmth, the same saturation, the same mineral load, so I stay the same thin thickness. I connect pool to film to film without a break. If there is a food web here, it starts and largely ends with me — the newt scraping the margin, the beetle drinking the high stones, the snails grazing and then gone, all of them living on the skin I make over the rock.

Nor am I only on the stones. The vapor lifts off the pool, carries, condenses on the cool underside of the overhang and on your shoulders, and falls again as drips. I ride in that small circle. A bead falls, a ring expands, a film is seeded on a stone that was disturbed yesterday. I breathe in a circle a few meters wide, and stay.

So if you want to find me, do not look up. There is nothing up here but mist and the low lid of cloud. Look down, and look with your feet. Find me where the water is only a shine, where the mineral plates crack if you are careless, where you have to walk more carefully than you expected to.

I will be the same thickness tomorrow as I was on the stain on the rock last month. The drip will not speed up. The pool will not rise or fall. The newt will hang. The beetle will cross its slab. I will seal over the rings and the plates and the shells in the crevices, holding the heat and the water in place, warm and wet and packed tight.

To be here is not to happen. It is to keep the level.
