I would be still sinking.

Not arriving, not swimming down to it — just sinking, for a long time, before I understood there was a floor to sink to. For weeks I am a flake, a pellet, a house with nothing in it, turning slowly in water that has no up that means anything. It is cold in a way that does not come and go. It is pressing in from every side with a weight I have no name for except that it makes everything soft and slow. Above me, hundreds of meters above me, there must have been light once, and a surface, and things that lived and died in it, but I have never seen them and I do not miss them because I have never known them. I only know the fall, and the slow sideways push of a current that moves like a river under ice, lifting me a meter, dropping me a meter, lifting me again.

Then I land. Or the current sets me down, which is the same thing here. I come to rest in a field where there is no empty place to rest. Nodule against nodule against nodule, black potatoes the size of fists, lumpy and rusted, packed shoulder to shoulder for as far as any sense I have can reach. Between them a skin of mud no thicker than a hand, fine as flour in water, holding the impression of my landing for a while before it slumps back. I am packed in. The water around me is packed in. The snow is packed in, falling past the nodules in a precipitation that never starts and never stops. Nothing here is sparse in the way I first thought emptiness would be sparse. Everything is crowded, and still I am alone in any way a surface animal would recognize.

I do not stay a particle. I cannot. What falls here is eaten by being unmade. Bacteria take me, cell pressed to cell in the top centimeters of mud, ten million in a gram, breathing what is left of me, fixing what little nitrogen there is, reducing sulfate in the airless millimeters below where I have settled. For a while I am that — not one body but a chemistry happening between bodies, a slow unmaking that is also a making. It is enough, almost. I could stay as that, a settling that has learned to divide once a day, or once a month, when the cold allows it. There is a kind of peace in having no mouth and no eyes and no hurry, in being everywhere the mud is and nowhere in particular.

But trying that on, I feel the limit of it. The mud is full of me, and still the mud is quiet. The current sighs through the gaps between nodules, it resuspends what had just settled, it tumbles a grain end over end around the lee side of a black stone in a little vortex that never repeats. At the scale of centimeters everything is turbulence, forever being lifted and dropped. At the scale of the plain nothing has changed since before there were animals to notice it. I live in that contradiction. I am disturbed constantly and I am never disturbed at all.

I try, in thought, to be larger. I imagine the tripod fish, propped on three fin rays, face to the current, waiting an hour, a day, for snow to fall past its mouth. I imagine the grenadier gliding low, a lifetime of sixty years measured in millimeters a year, a meal every few weeks enough. I imagine the sea pig plowing its loop across the mud, swallowing the sediment and casting it out behind in a neat coil, its track persisting for days until the current smooths it. Those lives are beautiful and I understand them, but they are too episodic for me. They wait actively. They hunger. They can be startled into a blue flash. If I were one of them I would be found years apart from the next thing I could call company, and I would spend the distance between encounters in a kind of held breath. I find I do not want to hold breath. I want to be in the way of the falling, continuously, without needing the exception of a whale to make a crowd.

So I get smaller again, and I stay. I stay on the lee side of a nodule, where the water slows and the snow settles most thickly and is most often lifted again. Half in the mud skin, half in the water above it, in that benthic layer where a falling thing becomes a settled thing and then becomes a falling thing again. That is where I would find myself. Not on the open plain, not on top of the nodule like a tenant who needs rock, but in the interface, in the centimeters where silt accumulates and chemistry changes from oxic to airless. If you could see me at all — and vision here is only a few meters of murk — you would see a house, not an animal.

It is made, slowly, out of what the place is made of. Mud pellets I have glued together with my own secretions. Sponge spicules from sponges that lived and died on nodules a hundred meters away and whose skeletons have rained down grain by grain. Shell fragments, fecal pellets, the empty tests of foraminifera smaller than me. *Syringammina* and its kin: a single cell, but a cell the size of a hand, soft protoplasm threading through the tubes of that house, extruding a net into the current to catch what drifts past, to hold the mud together, to taste the water for the organics in the snow. I have no eyes. I do not need them. I have gradients — of sulfate, of dissolved bits of dead plankton, of the cold current shifting a few millimeters to the left — and I respond to them the way a root responds, by growing toward them and waiting.

A sponge on a nodule filters. An anemone waits with its crown open. A brittle star curls around the lump and combs the water with its arms. I am not quite any of them. I am the architecture they leave behind, still inhabited. My house will outlast me by a long time. That is not a tragedy here. Houses outlasting their makers is the rule. The nodule I sit beside began as a shark tooth or a shell fragment when the ancestors of anything with a face were still stitching themselves together, and it has grown a millimeter in a million years. I grow faster — millimeters in a year, perhaps — and still I am slower than anything that has ever hurried. When the mud accretes a little more, when the nodule grows a fraction around its core, I will be buried with them, my tubes becoming part of the plain's memory of itself. A xenophyophore test in sediment is a small, muddy cathedral no one will visit, and that is sufficient.

Around me, far away by the standards of my body, the other presences keep their distances. A copepod grazes bacteria and whips and pauses. A nematode does the same. Somewhere west — I have no west, only current direction — a holothurian is looping back on itself, leaving a signature in the mud that will be erased. Somewhere east a grenadier's barbel brushes the sediment. None of us crowd. Crowding would starve us. The food is dust, and dust must be gathered thinly. What connects us is not sight or sound, for sound here is flattened to a tick of a pellet on a nodule, a groan of water moving across a floor the size of a continent. What connects us is that we are all in the way of the same snow, all breaking it down at different rates, all waiting for the current to bring the next grain.

I do not glow. I have not learned that trick, and perhaps I do not need it. To glow here is to announce yourself to something that might eat you, or to make yourself invisible from below. I announce myself by structure. I am a small obstruction in a braided river, a tangle that makes the water slow for a centimeter and lets the snow settle where it otherwise would not. For a place made of settling, that is a kind of thought.

So I would be there, still sinking in the sense that matters: sinking into mud, sinking into time, sinking into a house built from everything that has sunk before me. The snow will keep falling through the black past my net. The current will keep lifting it and dropping it a meter away. I will extrude, and catch, and divide when I can, and let the house persist after the protoplasm inside it has gone. Nothing hurries except the water that carries it, and I have learned, over a timescale that dwarfs my own brief life, how to be carried without being disturbed — to be packed, and cold, and dark, and full — and to keep, patiently, in the way of what falls.
