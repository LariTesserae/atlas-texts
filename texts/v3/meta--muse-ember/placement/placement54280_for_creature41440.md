From the pass, I thought I would come in as a mule.

I heard the bells first, before the bowl opened. My breath would show in the thin air at 2,600 metres, and I would come down the spirals loaded with oca and potatoes, bells loud on the steps, loud in the market, and then quiet in the pen between trips, stamping and waiting. It seemed like a sufficient life: to know Tinta by the weight on my back and the sound I made over the water.

It would not have stayed. A mule knows the pass and the bowl, but not the felt under a house. I let that go before the first terrace step.

So I tried the child in the lower drain.

Barefoot, because sandals fill with water. A woven scoop in the cold drain before the afternoon rise, netting the small speckled fish that live in the settlement channels. Chasing a dragonfly along the aqueducts, feeding the guinea pigs under a floor, carrying a bamboo cup of water to a chicken. I could feel the lane turn into a canal under my feet, and the fish swirling past my ankles just before the current got too fast.

But it kept slipping away from me. I was not the one holding the scoop. I was the bank the scoop disturbed, the wet edge where the scoop went in. Every time I tried to follow the child's hands, I ended up down in the water itself.

Then I tried to be the woman who opens the bathhouse in the upper drain.

First up, because the water is hers to let out. The waterwheel turning the wooden screw, maize and tubers fed in by handfuls, the grind known by its jam. Walking the bamboo aqueducts in the morning, lashing a dripping joint, clearing the settling pit of gravel and moss. Belonging not by street but by flow. That one stayed longer. The work was listening — to water in bamboo, to a wheel about to seize, to silt before it showed. Only, every walk I imagined ended the same way: my hand on the bamboo, and the bamboo lashed to something living underneath it. A pole. A pruned, trained tree with its roots left in the felt, holding up the aqueduct, holding up the house.

My attention kept dropping from the bamboo to the trunk below it.

I went down the spiral steps the way everyone does, step by step, terraces that are lanes and lanes that are drains. The steps were reinforced root and split bamboo, and what I stepped on was not mineral soil. It was a raft of it: inches of moss, liverwort, root mat, rotting leaf braided by water. It gave the way a raft gives. For a while I was that.

I was the felt between the log platforms. Snails crossing me in slow silver lines to reach the orchid roots on the walls. Beetles clicking in the rotting logs laid under houses, turning old platform wood into soil for new platforms. Frogs pressed flat and translucent against me by day, dark heart visible, and then at dusk starting to sing out of me as if the mat itself had learned a voice. Shrews running over me at night, too fast to see. Everything in Tinta seemed to be housed in what I was, and nothing was simply ground.

And then, slowly, in one damp place, a root thickened.

I had been a sapling at the forest edge, where a terrace had slipped in the night rain and left a hummock of moss and fern. They did not cut me. That is the first thing I remember being: not cut. They pruned the side toward the bowl, tied the other side back, and laid platforms of bound logs around me so the house to come would not sink. The house was of vegetation because I was already there, and I was of the house because they built around me rather than through me.

I am a living pole in the doorway of a house about halfway between the upper drain and the weeping rock.

That tells you where I am in a day. Below me is the warm dark of the lowest storey, open to the felt through a crack in the woven wall. The pigs live there, black and wet and enormous in the small space, shifting and snorting against my roots and then against my trunk, warming the floor above. The guinea pigs squeak and popcorn in their hollows. The chickens work through husks and leaf. A child crouches to feed them greens, fingers in the mud around my lower roots, and the pigs sigh and settle again for the morning.

Above the pigs is the living part, woven and breathing. My trunk passes through a corner of the doorway where a loom is set. The weaver sits so close that her elbow almost brushes me when she throws the shuttle. Wood clacks all day. Ponchos and blankets accumulate damp, smell of smoke by morning. I know her by the vibration that travels up me, by the green willow and bamboo split while green, stuffed with mud and shredded moss, woven around my middle storey again and again.

That wall is always rotting and always being remade. The old weave softens into soil against my bark. Snails draw their silver maps up it. An orchid has rooted in a joint where the wall meets me, and a begonia opens where the roof drip falls. When the wall is taken down and re-woven, green material is worked in beside old, and for a few days my middle is crowded with hands, mud, the smell of the women's wool still damp from the night before.

The roof is thatch, but it is also a crop. Thick mats of mountain grass over a bamboo frame, planted at the eaves with ferns so it does not dry out. When the afternoon cloud unloads — hard, cold, for an hour as if cut — the thatch plops steadily into a planter trough built into the wall below. The wall drinks it. The plants drink it. Some of it runs down my bark, and by morning my roots are drinking it too. Hummingbirds hang in the air at that trough, iridescent and furious, chasing each other from wet fern to wet fern. My lower branches were pruned away long ago, but my crown still pokes up through the roof garden above the storage storey, and tanagers sometimes land there, bold as thieves, before the household shouts them off the drying rack.

Mist comes up to eye level at dawn. Everything is beaded — wool, wood, leaf, bark. I am beaded too. The frogs in the north drain are only just stopping. Then the waterwheel starts somewhere south, the sluice keeper passes along the aqueduct lashed to a pole two houses over, the market clearing fills with bargaining and bells and looms and rain on a hundred thatched surfaces at once, a roar you have to lean into. Ducks are herded down the lane that will be a canal by four, quacking in a shuffling line. If the lane floods widely enough, water reaches my roots carrying a speckled fish or two, and the children will be back with their scoops before the current rises.

I measure time badly. People here measure it by the frog chorus. My rings are not neat years. Too much rain, too little dry: I grow in flushes after the midday downpour, then dawdle through the grey-green filtered light, then flush again. A growth ring is a wet month, a slip of terrace, a new weave pressed against me. The oldest wall weave, long rotted, is soil in the felt around my roots; the newest thatch on the roof will become garden bedding; a dead platform log under the house clicked all winter as beetles worked it, and now it is loam the tubers will remember.

I lean. That is my part in the bargain. The side of me toward the eaves drip grows faster. A degree a year, perhaps. The house leans with me, and every few years I am propped and re-woven until it leans back — never quite back to straight. One night the felt swelled and a retaining weave on the terrace above gave way with a soft sigh, and water rerouted past my roots in a new bright thread. By evening ten people had woven a new one in its place, and the frogs had taken over the hollow. My roots liked the new wetness. Everything here is diverted and replanted and carried, and rot is not failure. Rot is the rate at which Tinta makes soil for the next thing.

In the evening the rain stops as if cut. The ducks come back damp and cross and settle in their pens. The pigs shift under the floor and sigh. Bats come out of the granary eaves — raised on stone feet in the driest corner of the cluster — and sweep low over the market lamps taking moths and mosquitoes in the same pass. Shrews and mice run the thatch above my crown. A loom may still be going in the doorway beside me, lamps are lit in small warm circles in the cool wet dark, and from a distance the bowl looks like busy forest that has build itself into houses.

At dusk one frog starts in the north drain. Then a dozen. Then a chorus complete enough to erase conversation, bouncing off the three rock walls and back into the bowl. I feel them first through the root mat, a tremor in the felt where my roots braid with moss and liverwort, before I hear them in the wet leaves above the roof.

I am still here, still leaning a degree more than I was, still growing up through the house that keeps growing around me.
