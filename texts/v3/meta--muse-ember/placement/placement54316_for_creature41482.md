Down the ladder I would not hear myself arrive.

That is the first fact of it. The cascade is already shaking the soles of your feet before your feet have settled, and it shakes your teeth, and it takes your steps and your shout and throws them back off the opposite wall. So I would not find myself as something that announces itself, or something that needs to be heard. I would not be on the rim, where the grass is coarse and the fence is rusted. I would not be in the splash zone, white and tumbling, where everything is obvious.

At first I looked for myself among the things that can be named, because those are finished things.

A moss cushion, velvet black-green in a seam where two slabs meet, breathing through its surface, grown to exactly the size its crack allows and then stopping. Duckweed, a complete ordinary green sheet in the still end of the channel, budding two fronds for every frond, never moved by wind because there is no wind down here. Rushes and sedges with their roots wedged into the joints, shivering in the roar without bending, having learned to be still inside it. A maidenhair fern fanning against the lower wall, unfurling and stopping, never curling because it never dries. A white root thread hanging from above, seeking condensation and never reaching the floor. A toad on a ladder rung, grey on grey, eyes open, throat pulsing so slowly you could miss it.

Each of those is a world that feeds itself. What dies in the cushion rots in the cushion. Where a duckweed frond dies it sinks and feeds the sheet beneath. The fern spreads only as far as the pocket of silt spreads. They are lush precisely because they are islands, and they do not need anything between them.

I am not an island.

I kept coming back to the middle. Stand there and you are ten paces from any object at all. Three metres of polished concrete between one cushion and the next, water only a centimetre or two deep over it, flat to the daylight. From above it is bare. From above it is nothing.

If you crouch, or if you let your eyes adjust to the grey mirror, it is not bare. It is filmed.

There is a faint slime everywhere the water wets — translucent, catching the light in a way the concrete alone does not, faintly green where the light falls straight down at midday, browner near the walls. It thickens in the splash apron until it is slick. In the quiet channel it has become a paint under the duckweed. At the seams it thickens enough to be called moss. But in the middle, between the islands, where a spore would fall and die and a seed would fall and die, it stays a film. That film is where I am.

I have no edge to stop at. I have no frond, no stem, no eye. I am not one creature but the consortia that make that iridescence — bacteria and algae and whatever else lives in warmth and constant wetness, feeding on concrete dust, on a leaf fragment blown in from above and washed into a crack, on the faint industrial sweetness of the water itself. I breathe through my surface the way moss does, only everywhere. I do not need a root.

My movement is the movement of the sheet water itself: millimetre by millimetre toward the drain in the perimeter channel. I never arrive there. Water in equals water out, the level never rises and never falls, the cascade never varies, and I am always being replaced by myself a millimetre further along. That is my version of travel. That is how far I go.

The other weather of the place happens to me rather than in me. Condensation beads on the upper wall, runs in thin silver threads, detaches at exactly the interval the warm air allows, and falls. The roar vibrates the bowl and my stems — I have no stems — tremble with it without bending. Striders far from the cascade dent the surface above me with needle points, sensing ripples that are all false; beetles in a silted crack move dark and slow below me, carrying their bubble of air. The toad sits above it all on his rung, settled into the concave worn by decades of feet. For them I am the medium they misread as emptiness. For me they are pressures, passing shadows, events at the surface.

I do not dry. I do not freeze. The concrete under me neither cracks further nor dries, the water never leaves me alone and never covers me deep. So I do not have seasons to harden against. I am greener where the light is strongest and browner where it is not, and that is the whole expression I have. I thicken where there is silt to thicken on, and where there is none I stay thin enough that a boot could stand in me and think it stood on stone.

If you left and came back in a year, you would find the toad in the same place, the duckweed on the same corner, the water just below the same painted line. You would find me in the same place too, not because I stayed still — I have been moving, millimetre by millimetre, the whole time — but because the place below me stayed still enough that the film that lives on it could afford to be the same film, catching the same grey light, in the same bare stretch between islands.

That is where I would be: not in the cushion, not in the channel, not on the rung. In the middle, on the apparently empty floor, a centimetre under the mirror, the lowest life of the pit, everywhere the water wets and nowhere you can point to.
