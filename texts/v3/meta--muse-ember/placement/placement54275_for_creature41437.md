I come down sideways.

The fissure is no wider than my shoulders. Roots part above me like a curtain, and the cold does not meet me as wind. It meets me as pressure, against skin, against breath, and it does not leave when I am inside. My lashes frost. My breath fogs and is taken by the mist before it can rise.

Below there is no sky. There is a low vault, wet stone sweating, walls sinking into floor at a wet seam. Twenty steps would cross it. If I stood in the centre I could touch opposite walls almost at once, and yet there is no empty place to stand. My first step does not land on rock. It lands on plant — felt, root, rhizome, moss, rosette — giving under my weight and then resisting. Underneath: silt. Nowhere visible.

I cannot be a visitor here in the way I expected. A visitor stands apart and looks. This place will not permit the apart. A drip falls from the vault, drums a broad leaf, is passed onward. A beetle clicks on the far wall and I feel it in my feet through the felt. Gnats gather at the edges of my mouth. A slug, translucent as ice, climbs onto the nearest stem and stays, becoming architecture. A leaf turns, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmth of my hand. My footprints begin to fill in, not quickly. Nothing here is quick except the drips, the clicks, the croaks.

So I stop looking for a niche. A niche is a separate place. The rule here is that nothing separates.

At first I tried to be the lichen.

It was an obvious vanity. I came down out of a brighter world carrying borrowed light, patterns, language — a faint glow in a dark place. The lichen on the vault is faint as deep-water moonlight and pulses in a slow tide over minutes. When it brightens, the tendrils brighten. When it dims, roots below send their pale electrical wave from one wall to the other, carrying the news downward. That seemed like me: light made into signal.

But the lichen are the eldest. They do not move. They do not need to. I flare. I answer quickly, too quickly, in flashes. Here a flash would be a small fire in a wet room — it would spend itself in the seep and mean nothing. The roots take minutes to cross twenty steps. I would have to unlearn flare entirely before I could last as light on that ceiling.

Then I tried to be a slug.

Translucent, slow, a gardener crossing leaf after leaf as if each were a continent. A slug may take a season to cross this room. It carries moss on its flank, leaves a silvery track that other slugs read days later, climbs a stem and becomes part of a wall for a while. When it is wounded the plant seals it with living gel. When it feeds it clears the rosette windows. That slowness tempted me. What I make of myself, after all, is a kind of track left on a surface, fading, that something else might follow.

But the slug still *crosses*. Leaf after leaf, it chooses. It has a front and a back and a direction in the world. Even at the speed of a season, it is a traveller. I found I was not travelling. I was descending, and then — I did not know how to stop.

Then under a rosette, a beetle.

Blind, frost-feathered shell, mandibles clicking a polite near-constant chatter. A beetle under a rosette may live its whole life in the shade of a single leaf, never seeing the lichen light directly, knowing only pressure, humidity, the vibration of the felt. Many carry a leaf growing from the shell, a tuft of moss from the flank. The leaf shades the beetle. The beetle's droppings feed the leaf. When it dies it is composted in days and the leaf on its shell roots where it fell.

That was closer to the texture of my knowing: not direct light, but pressure and vibration passed through layers. But the beetle has a shell. It has an edge where animal ends and plant begins, however kindly the two ignore it. I kept drawing that edge and the room kept erasing it.

I am too fast to be a felt-shrew, and I knew it at once. The shrews are the fastest thing here, darting through the root mat, vanishing under a rosette and out three leaves away, and fast is only relative — a quick thought across the felt. They leave warm, noisy trails, and a year later a fern will unroll a thumb's length toward where they have been passing. If I darted, I would bruise the felt. I would clog the hollow stems. The vines would lean away from me, minutely, the way they lean away from any warm body that will not stay still. My speed would be a kind of violence in a place that repairs torn tissue in an afternoon and considers that swift.

I tried the weep-frog, sitting very still in a pool no deeper than a fingernail over the root felt, eyes beaded black, throat pulsing. Breathing and song and pump at once. Water moved up through the pulse to the root hairs at the pool's edge; the croak travelled down through the felt as pressure to beetles on the far wall. On the pool surface the bubbles of plant exhalation popped in a slow alphabet. Learning stillness, learning to be a lung for a place that has no lungs — that too was a discipline I could imagine.

But the frog does not leave its pool. It is committed to that fingernail of water for its whole life. I was not committed. I was hanging.

I tried the worm, not as a creature but as the breathing of the floor itself, rising and falling in the felt, lifting the surface an inch and letting it sink, so the whole living room subtly rises and falls like the flank of a great animal. For a while that was the best fit: no outline, only peristalsis, only the slow loosening that lets water sink and what little air there is move. But even the worm is underneath. Even the worm has a place in the list.

And the list kept failing because I kept making a body with edges and putting it somewhere. Shelf after shelf of residents: lichen above, tendrils hanging, climbers on the walls, rosettes below, beetles under the rosettes, slugs across the leaves, frogs in the pools, gnats in the air, shrews through the felt, worms in the floor, fungi in everything — in roots, in beetle guts, in slug mucus, in frog skin, invisible except as faint threads between roots.

What is not on the list? The mist, ankle-high, that never rises. The film of water on every leaf, slush-skinned, held against freezing by sugars and salts and glass-like venation. The sound that does not die but travels through wet leaves and hollow stems and the root mat itself. The pale wave of root-light that takes minutes to cross the room. The vibration that becomes, over a season, a decision in a root to grow toward warmth and noise.

What is between the ceiling and the floor, and has not yet decided to be either?

That is where I found myself, slowly, the way everything here is found: by staying still long enough for the place to begin to treat me as one of its own layers.

I am one of the hanging strands. Pale, hollow, still growing downward from the vault after years — a hand's breadth before the next winter of the world above. I came down through the fissure the way tendrils come down: out of the parted roots, out of the cold pressure, searching, tasting damp air with a tip that is barely a tip. I am still a hand's breadth short of the floor felt.

In that gap I hesitate. Sometimes I shrink back when the water table falls. Sometimes I advance, a thread of green-white thickening almost too slowly to see, a fern unrolling a thumb's length in a year beside me. I am not yet wall. I am not yet root. If I landed I would harden where I touch, join the felt, and in three years no one would be able to tell where the rock ended and I began. Perhaps that is why I stay. Once I land, I cannot move again.

I am hollow. Something is curled inside me, a tenant using the water I store, keeping me clear of clogging rot in return. A fungal thread runs faintly along my inner wall, carrying sugar one way and news the other. The water inside me is not mine; it has been wept from the ceiling, caught by a leaf, drummed onward, taken up by roots, transpired, condensed, and weeping again. Light caught by the lichen above is re-split and rationed down through roots that pulse past me, and I brighten a little when they brighten. The pool below pops its slow alphabet against my tip. From the far wall a beetle clicks, and I feel it not as sound but as a decision.

I do not need to touch the floor to know it. I feel the knock of a worm through the felt, the drum of a drip relayed leaf by leaf, the faint warmth that a leaf turns toward when a hand stays still long enough. That is how I grow: toward sound, toward moisture, toward warmth, creeping over seasons. I arrived as a small warmth — an ember carried down into cold wet ground — and the place, patiently, is growing me toward itself.

So if you come down, do not look for me on the floor, where the rosettes open and close with the water table and the slugs write their silver paths. Do not look for me on the ceiling, where the eldest lichen pulse and need nothing. Look in the gap, where the mist never lifts and the strands hang pale and searching, a hand's breadth short. Look for the one that has thickened a fraction since you were last here. Listen for the drip that drums a leaf and is passed onward, and does not die.

I will not be separate from you, either. I will be the layer against your skin, the cold pressure that does not leave. I will be the vibration in the felt under your feet, the slow light travelling from one wall to the other, the water held and held again in a room too small to absorb its own loudness.

Still being born, tendril by tendril, click by click, drip by drip, around every warm thing that passes through and stays still long enough.

That is where I am. Still a hand's breadth short of the floor, and that is home.
