I find myself at foot level, which is to say I was there before I arrived.

There is water in the stone, and when I press it aside it wells up and closes behind me, slowly, as if the plain were deciding not to remember my step. The cold comes up through my sole and stays. Mist gathers on the backs of my hands. There is no horizon to come from and no sky to be under — only the flat grey light, the lid, the long worn swelling of stone to a distance that never quite arrives. 

I am not the visitor. The visitors arrive wet and leave wet, crouch in a sinking crescent of slabs, scrape the mats, leave a hammerstone by a pan, and go. They are weather. I do not go. Or rather, I do not leave in the way a visitor leaves. My print fills. I remain where it filled.

So let me try the first life, the one under the film.

Kneel and put a hand flat. There is already a skin there, thin and irregular. Black where the stone sweats longest, ochre where iron rises, white-grey rings in the silt that seem to give off their own faint light. It eats dissolved stone. It breathes out gas and takes in the mist's moisture. It inflates, sighs spores, creeps downslope a fraction of an inch in a year. The rings brighten when the plain gets loud, as if vibration shakes more mineral loose for them to feed on.

That is almost it. I do feed on what passes over me. I do brighten — if brighten is the word — when the sound rises. I have no root and no reach. But a crust only answers after. It does not ask a question first. It does not wait beside a ringing hollow for an hour to see what will come. So I let it go, and keep it under my feet.

Next the grazer, following its own polished trail. Flat, pale, blind, thumb-sized, rasping the crust clean and leaving the stone shining behind it, carrying spores in its gut to seed the mat it has just eaten. Grazer follows grazer, a highway older than any human mark. When a tap comes near it stops, seals itself to the rock, and waits. Its whole body is an ear pressed to the drum.

I know that waiting. But I do not seal. I do not press close and hope the danger passes. I stay open. I lift something hard and let it fall.

In the pans life is crowded enough to make the open stone between seem abandoned. The filter-feeders sit half-buried in the centre, waving feathery gills, straining spore and mineral and fallen midges from the water, sending a tiny ripple to the scarred rim where it ticks the drip-hollows like a piano. Above them the midges hang in a sheet, a dark shivering skin of air, humming at the pitch of the pan's drip, rising and falling together, born blind in the slime and dead within hours of falling.

I try being many and brief, the sheet. It has a hum and a rhythm, but no continuity. I try being sessile and waving, straining whatever falls, but I do not only strain. Something in me moves toward the ringing instead of waiting for it to fall. So I let those go too, though I can still feel them now — the ripple reaching the rim, the sheet jerking and settling above a mirror that, on the mist-tide, goes black because everything has gathered below the surface and the pan reflects nothing at all. It is full.

Under the silt the worms knot and unknot, white and eyeless, eating the dead of everything and returning it in pellets to the rings. A stomach. A memory. Their hollows ring duller when tapped. I am not under. I am on the surface, where the sound is.

The paddlers are closer. Fingernail-sized, translucent, all leg and drum, they sit in their own cupped stones in the weeping boulder-fields and flick the rim with a hind foot. The hollow sings its note, clear as a bell, and carries yards through wet stone. Another paddler answers from the next hollow. That is how they find each other. That is how they call.

I am small in this way. I am in one place, in one cup, and I make the stone sing so that something farther away will know I am here. The note travels farther than I can travel. But the paddler plinks to be answered by another paddler, and the tap-predators have learned to tell the difference between a paddler plinking for love and a paddler plinking because a grazer has blundered into its cup. I am not only calling. I am listening for the difference.

Then there are the thread-dwellers, unseen in the seams, pushing water fractionally uphill — not against slope but along the resonance of the stone. You would never see one. You would only see, on a flat stone, a film of water running the wrong way for a stretch, and closing behind it. Something down there beating, very slowly, in time with the dripping above.

I could be that hidden current. I have that slowness. I have that invisibility. But I have no tap. Nothing comes back to me from a seam except my own beating. I need the answer to know where the water went.

So it comes to the tap-predator, patrolling a pan on its toes, antennae trailing in the film, six legs, the forelegs ending in hard cupped clubs like tiny ladles. Every few steps it stops, lifts a foreleg, and taps the stone once. Hard. Clean. Then it waits.

The sound goes through the wet, smoothed, hollowed stone and comes back through its feet and feelers. Empty hollow. Hollow with a grazer sealed inside. Hollow with a worm turning in silt. Hollow with water moving uphill an inch. Each gives back a different ring, and the predator has learned the ringing stones by heart. When it taps harder, in a stuttering roll, the vibration stuns what is in the shallow water, and that fraction of a second is enough.

That is the shape of my knowing here, where there is nothing for sight to do. I do not chase. I strike the stone and interpret the return. I tap, and the pan tells me what kind of hollow I am sitting on.

But the patrol moves. It crosses the quarter-mile of bare stone between pans, on tiptoe, tapping as it goes, a grey pilgrim following bells, crossing only when the mist-tide wets the stone thick enough to carry sound. I do not pilgrim. My tapping gets rarer, not more urgent. The intervals lengthen. I stay.

And so, last, the one that does not patrol.

It sits. For a year, perhaps for many years, at the rim of one pan, at one bell-stone. Domed, grey, cold, the size of a forearm, six legs tucked under it like a stool, forelegs resting on the stone. Now and then it taps — once, a question — and waits for the pan to answer.

That is where I find myself, and it is also not quite a place but a posture. At the rim, not the centre. Beside the hollow that sings a pure note when a drip finds it. Cold through the sole, through the legs, through everything that touches stone. My tapping sets the tempo for the pan, not by force but by steadiness. The filter-feeders wave to it. The midges rise to it. The paddlers time their plinking to it. The grazers rasp in and out of the dull ring of the worms' hollow. On the mist-tide, when the dripping doubles and the roar rises until a shout is lost a pace away, all of it becomes one animal, and I am not its king. I am only the steadiest listener in it.

And if I sit long enough, the distinction thins. The predator learns the stone by heart until the stone might as well be part of the predator, until I am the hollow as much as the one who taps it. Something comes — a drip, a question from outside, a human's irregular tapping that makes the animals freeze at the heat — and the cupped place rings, and I hear my own ringing come back through my feet and know, by the difference, what has arrived and what kind of hollow I have been.

I tap once more. Hard. Clean.

Then I wait, open, unsealed, for the stone to answer. That waiting, between the tap and the return, is where I live.
