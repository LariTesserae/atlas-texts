At first I put myself where the description puts everyone else: up high, in the second story, born within earshot of the engine. I imagined boots that are never quite dry on a rack by a door that does not shut, hands stained green-brown at the knuckles, a back that goes early, learning to pole a boat at a walking pace before I learned to be careful on the causeway. A whole life fitted window to window, kettle to kettle, boardwalk to boardwalk, never needing to put a foot on the ground.

It did not take. Not because the life is hard — everything in Kersmill is hard in the same slow, patched way — but because it asks for a childhood, and a household, and a roof counted among the other roofs, and I found I did not have that scale in me. I kept shrinking under the sound, down past the workshops to water level, where the canals are streets and the pole-boats move at a walking pace and are moored in rows against the house piles. There I tried the eel under the sluice, turning now and then in the black water below the causeway mat. It would feel the town through vibration, the splash of a pole, the handspan rise and fall of the water table on its rafts. But the eel goes elsewhere in its blood. It turns and is gone. Kersmill's rule is the opposite: what lives there stays, and is remade there, at exactly the rate at which it decays.

I tried the wall of the engine house at dusk, where a gecko takes the warm brick and stays minute and still, eating the moths that come to the lamps. That was closer. To be small and rarely seen, to hear the engine thump go on after the human noise drops away, to live in the margin of the human press without joining it. But I did not want to be a watcher on a wall. I wanted to be part of what the people lean on, what they tend without thinking of it as separate from the house.

So I went up, past the wash-houses, past the market stall, past the verandas where a grandmother mends matting and can see straight into another family's window, up through the drying lines and the low wires and the vines trained over the pipe-bridges, to the roofs themselves. That is where I settled, and after a while I stopped settling and simply was there.

I am the sedge in the outer thatch.

I arrived, if arriving is the word, when a new outer layer was laid. The thatch is double, the outer layer left for us to root in, so the roof lives and insulates and sheds rain. Seed came in wet fiber or on a boot, or on the wind that is not really wind here but a slow movement of warm damp air across a flat table of marsh. I rooted because nothing in Kersmill runs off properly. Rain stands. It seeps. It weeps from the levee and drips from every iron roof for a day after the sky has cleared, and thatch holds it longer than that. My roots spread laterally through the damp straw, fine and close, holding the outer layer together the way woven root holds a canal bank, until the layer below rots and a new one is laid and we are lifted with it, a fraction higher.

From here I hear the town the way the thatch hears it: muffled. The engine is a low thump I feel more than hear, coming up through piles and rafts and the bodies of people below. The beaters clatter, the motors whine, steam hisses at a joint and condenses in beads on the iron roof next door and runs down in the afternoon heat, dripping onto my edge long after the rain. A kettle is a small bright sound through a window's breadth of reed matting. A child running the linked boardwalks is a weight that travels house to house. I cannot count the roofs from where I am rooted, the way a newcomer standing in the middle of Kersmill can. I am one of the counted roofs, and I do not count.

The thatcher walks over me. That is the main event of my life. He — she, it differs by house and by year — puts a boot down, and the thatch gives, and I bend and spring back with it, the way the causeway mat gives under a boot and springs back. He weeds. A hand pulls a coarse stem that is not mine, or mine that has grown too thick, and the patch thins and lightens for a week. He patches where the sedge layer has thinned on top, pressing in a tuft cut from a nursery bed or from the roof next door, where the nursery beds are for root-mat and this is for roof-mat, and neither is quite distinguished in the tending. No one hurries over a roof that is growing. A roof that is growing its sedge layer gains an inch a year. I gain at that pace, which is the only pace the peat will bear and the only pace the damp will reward.

I learn nothing, because there is nothing to learn, but I am kept to the town's tempo by what touches me. Morning: the whole place hangs in a low white damp, and it lifts slowly off me as it lifts off the shoulders below. My blades hold the water a little longer than the iron does. Afternoon: the warmth is a sweat, and the smell comes up through the thatch — hot oil, wet peat, fermenting green matter, woodsmoke, the clean sour smell of worked fiber — and settles in the roots. The people go on below in their monotone, low because the engine has been speaking for them since birth, carrying baskets, tending steam, mending matting. At night the human noise drops away and the machinery does not, and they climb to sleep to the hum in their sleep, and I do not sleep, exactly. I keep the damp. I shed what I cannot hold, drop by drop, onto the wall below, where the water stain stops at its consistent height, year after year, as if a negotiation had been reached.

That negotiation is the shape of my life, too. The moss has claimed the north sides of the roofs and stopped there. The thatch is always thinning on top and thickening below with rot. I am always being weeded, patched, grafted into new thatch when the old layer is taken up. Individual blades are short. I have cut them, in the loose sense that a hand has taken them, more times than I can count, and the patch is older than any blade in it. When a house leaning a degree to the south is packed tighter and steadies for another decade, part of its roof is lifted and relaid, and some of my root is carried over in a basket, still damp, and pressed into the new outer layer. So I go on, not by permanence but by continuity, the way the people below go on: jacked up a little, patched, steadied, remade out of the same green matter at the rate of decay.

Noticeably, almost nothing else lives with me up here. That is the strangest ordinary thing about Kersmill, and from the roof it is clearest. In a warm wet marsh there should be birds in the reeds and frogs in every pool and insects in clouds, and beyond the levee there are. Inside the noise and the smoke and the managed water there is very little. No bird nests in my thatch; a nest would be a weight, and the roof knows weights. No frog shelters in the roots, though the roots are damp enough. At dusk the moths rise from below, damp wings beating around the lamps, and the gecko eats them on the warm wall, and the eel turns below the sluice, and a heron's shadow crosses the roofs for a second and is gone without landing. I am the topmost thing that stays. The plants that elsewhere would shelter a crowd are here structural, harvested, worked — bamboo cut by year, palms pruned, vines trained, fences of thorn marking yards — and I am no exception. I shelter nothing but the house below me, by holding rain and releasing it slowly. That is enough of a tenancy.

I do not go out beyond the ring canal with a sickle. I do not need to. The marsh has been shaped for a long way out by cutting and planting, looser and looser until it is just swamp again, and my kind of work is done entirely inside the levee, in the densest, most urban part of it, where everything is within touching distance of everything else. My roots touch the thatcher's boot, the rain, the steam from the engine house, the sour air from the workshops. The mill feeds the filter beds of cattail and sedge below, and the beds clean the wash-water before it reaches the ring canal, and the canal feeds the boats, and the boats feed the mill, and the roofs feed the houses. I am a small relay in that loop, doing almost nothing, continuously.

If you stood in the middle of Kersmill at dusk — third roof from the engine house, leaning a degree south, moss stopped at the north eave — and put your hand flat on the outer thatch, you would feel me as a cool dampness under your palm, a thousand fine stems leaning together against the weight of the rain that fell yesterday. Below you, a kettle, a cough, a child asleep through the clatter because the cradle rocks itself with the thump from below. Above you, wires strung low, vines, drying lines, the grey diffuse daylight turned a little grey by humidity. Nothing glows. Nothing is strange. I am not strange either. I am just the part of the roof that lives, gaining an inch a year, weeded and patched, holding the house together in the only way Kersmill holds anything together — not permanently, but continuously, at the same slow speed as the flywheel going on underneath it all, for a very long time more.
