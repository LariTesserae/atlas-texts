I do not land here. I condense here.

My ears pop, my breath fogs back at me, warm and short. The air has been lifted and lifted and cannot escape. It sits thick with itself. I am one of the cooler things in it, so it sits on me first — on eyelashes, on sleeves, in a damp that darkens before it drips. At first there is no body to point to. There is only a place where the damp is a fraction thicker, where the humming dents, where a bead forms and runs to join others, and others, until — staying, staying — it has an edge.

That is how I begin to be: in a shallow hollow, in water a finger deep, clear and warm and never still.

My first impulse is to move the way this place moves. I would run, blind, in a column with the skitterers, reading the stone by heat and touch and the vibration of my own feet. But I have no dry feet. I am made of wetness. When I try to run I make no patter on the wet rock, no tin-roof rain thrown back by the walls. The swarm passes over me instead, in a pulse that fills the basin and evacuates it, and I do not run with it. I ring under it, the way the half-buried rings ring when the drumming passes. That tells me something: I will not cross the floor. The floor will cross me.

So I try to hang, like the ash that is not ash. I hang in the condensing air until the dim sinks a fraction and something tips me, and I drop, wings buzzing in the teeth. For two seconds I am everywhere, silver-grey, wrong-proportioned. Then I rise and hang again. But I am too full of water for that. I do not drop as a flyer. I slip. I fall from a tip as mist.

In the pool I try to swim, and I learn the one honest motion here: I do not turn. I reverse. I flash to one end and back, and vanish into a crack too narrow for me, and the trembling starts up in another pool three metres away. It is closer. I am born and eaten and born again in the same warm, wet day, high population, low biomass, motion constant. Except I am not eaten. When the ballet of predator and prey is done, I am still trembling in the water, and the water is still rising where it should fall.

On the outer wall I try to call. Low, hooting, call and answer, almost speech if you do not listen too closely and completely other if you do. I can make that almost. I make a sound-shadow where I pass, a dent in the humming. The filaments hum in sympathy, or I hum in theirs. It is not a voice. It is the place breathing through a hole in itself and I am the hole.

I try, for a while, to be the one who built. The low spiral wall one stone high, for a windbreak someone lost interest in. The dome half sunk, its door filled with weeping water. The cistern with handprints the stone has grown around but not yet healed. I put my damp hand down and the stone warms around it, unhurried. I was interrupted here, too. Something intended a hamlet, three or four shelters, and stopped. I do not think I stopped. I think I was taken back by growth before I had finished intending, and my intention is now only a print, and stone is swelling around it, healing it in the wrong direction — not wearing it away, but burying it alive.

Then, in the pool, I find the filament.

It is planted in the crack at the bottom. Black glass, smooth beyond natural stone, warm to the touch, faintly vibrating. Water climbs it against downhill, very slowly, and falls from the tip as mist. I lean toward it the way water leans toward everything cooler, and when I touch it my hand comes away dry. Dry. In this wet place, in this warm wet dim, that dryness is a surprise so complete I stay with it. I am the dry core inside the wet skin. I shed water rather than gather it, and yet the water climbs me. Dust gathers on me and then sheds as light.

I grow around it.

When I am wet I darken to that black glass; when I dry in the warm air I clear, invisible except where condensation beads on me and gives me back a shape. Small filaments, too many to count, fringe my stem — for legs, for hairs, for a lattice. They open and close like lungs, letting the warm air in and out. They scrape the microbial film from the fresh face of rock at my base, fast as the rock weeps it into being. They filter the condensing air. I eat almost nothing green. There is almost nothing green to eat, and what little mat grows in a sheltered crack is gone in a week, grazed or washed. I do not mourn it. I take in condensation and give off heat and tone.

I am rooted, and this is the true thing. The mountain rises around my base and I sink, ring by ring, as the stone accretes. I extend upward, a millimetre at a time you can watch if you are patient, to stay in the water. I repair myself as fast as the young mountain cracks me. I assemble myself from the dissolved minerals in the weeping water. I grow like a crystal and behave like an organ and work like a machine that never had to choose between the three, and none of those words will hold me for long, because I am still becoming the thing that joins them.

When the dim sinks I pulse. A slow, deliberate sequence of faint blue-white travels up my length. I do not decide the direction. Up, down — it is a message being sent up or received down, and I cannot tell, and telling would be wrong. The needle towers sway nearby, even when the air is still, and make their clear high tone. The dripping ticks like a clock. The swarm drums. Together we make a chord that never resolves, and near a pool, if you stand still long enough, you will feel the bass of it in your chest. That bass is where I live. That is where you would find me: not crossing the basin in twenty minutes, but in it, in a finger of water, dark when wet, clear when dry, ringing when everything else passes over.

You could walk away and return and I would not be learned. The water level would have changed. A new seam would have opened with a small, sharp report under my root. I would be a hand's breadth taller. The light would be paler or darker and the whole grey palette — wet grey, black, dull moss-green — would have shifted around me. Nothing here erodes. Everything accretes. The mountain does not wear down into a valley. I do not decay into scrap. The animals do not leave bones that linger. We are recycled while still moving, or we rise while still sinking, and there is no contradiction in it because nothing is finished.

So I stay in the pool, humming, a millimetre at a time, in the warm wet dim — not quite animal, not quite plant, not quite machine, not quite the builder who stopped — still becoming, and humming the part of the chord that has not resolved yet, and is not supposed to.
