I am here in the water up to my knees, then up to my waist, and I am looking for a place to be that is not a place.

Not a foothold. The ground underneath is young and sharp and will not hold shape for long stretches. Not a high ground - there is none, only two meters of higher that is still sheet-water over concrete. Not a voice to answer back. I listen under the chest-deep roar of the pumps, under the kettle-hiss of steam venting beneath the surface, under the clang of the gates, for something that calls and is answered. There is nothing that calls. There is only the softer, nearer sound, the ripping and snapping as if something thick is tearing itself free and regrowing in the same breath.

That is where I begin to find myself. Not as an individual. As a pressure to continue.

At first I tried to be the kelp.

It was the most obvious way to live here without breaking the rules. No swimming, no flight, no crawling, no calling. The kelp does none of those things. It is translucent, anchored not rooted, to a pipe, to wreckage, to other kelp. Its blades unspool in slow spirals, and in this dim light that has no source I can find, it glows very faintly, a green that does not illuminate so much as make the darkness visible.

It leans. That is what it does. When a turbine spins faster in its flooded pit, when a pump churns warm water from one bay to another because a timer laid down decades ago has not stopped, the kelp inclines toward the hum and trembles there. When a floodgate clangs, the whole forest is bent flat in unison, violently, by a surge that is not weather but schedule. Then it straightens and begins to grow the other way, leaving a kink in every stalk.

I leaned. For a while that was enough to say: I am this leaning stalk, glowing in the second sea I breathe through.

But a stalk is too local. It founders with its mat. It kinks and is replaced. Another stalk fifty meters away, in the next layer of green-grey haze, does not know mine. If I were a stalk, I would die standing, bleach silver, and be folded down into a mat that thickens without me. The pressure to continue would go on, but I would not be the one continuing. So I am not the kelp, though the kelp remembers how I move.

Then I tried to be the moss.

The moss lives on the verticals - on rusted iron, on the sides of pump houses, on a cooler tower tipped like a broken tooth, its interior a cathedral of dripping. It grows thick as fur, pale chartreuse and rust-red, and it seals. Roots find the warm pipes. Moss seals the leaks. Kelp reaches the intakes and keeps them from clogging. If I were moss, I would be the skin where metal was wounded and where green decided to heal it. Slow. Near-touching. Nothing to eat me.

But moss is a place where two other things meet and stop leaking. It is an effect, not the reason. I felt myself slide off it, down into the water that it was sealing against.

Then I tried to be a machine.

Because there is life here, if you can call it that, in the churning and spinning and humming and clanging and squeezing and carrying. A pump house still electrified inside, its door swinging on its hinge. A conveyor half-sunk, its belt still moving in a slow pointless circuit carrying only wet leaves. Algae-presses in low sheds squeezing surface mats into dark pellets and dropping them back into the water. Turbines generating electricity that lights amber lamps in sequence and then goes nowhere.

I could be the timer. I have memory without a brain: the schedule for the gates, the sequence of the lights, the circuit of the belt. I flood one channel at noon and starve it by dusk, so a cracked square of concrete is an island, then a lake, then an island again by night. Nothing here erodes slowly. If I were the timer, I would be the reason everything is either brand-new or suddenly gone.

But a machine alone does not lean. It does not tear and regrow audibly. It generates and goes nowhere. It needs the plants to keep its intakes clear, to need its vibration to grow, to thrive on the nutrients it churns from dying leaves and the warm water it stirs. They are tending it, almost. It is feeding them, almost. Neither half is alive without the other. I cannot be only the half that hums.

Then I tried to be the surface. The floating gardens, mirror-lily and steam-fern knitted so tightly they will bear a step, then give way all at once in a sink of green. I liked that. A mat that can be walked on and then not. A mat that repeats the same leaf pattern over an area the size of a town, as if the valley had been printed from one template and not finished. That repetition felt familiar - a thought trying to finish itself and looping.

But mats founder under their own weight, taking a length of pipe down with them. They rise steaming under a turbine outflow for an hour and sink. They are templates, but the template does not know it is a template. I knew, and so I slid off again.

I tried to be the things in between. The silt and pollen and spores and fine industrial powder suspended in the opaque warm water. The atmospheric water you breathe through. The hanging rain that does not fall, that hangs in a wall, in curtains, and drips from nothing and soaks your shoulders from below. The sheet-water over black mud that catches the dim light like a mirror and reflects things not present - a clear sky, a sharp horizon, while the real sky is grey and low.

All of those move only because something else has pushed them, and they move all together. They have no motion of their own. The water in places does not ripple outward. It ripples inward, toward the disturbance. Sound arrives before it happens - you hear the gate clang a half-second before you see the steam burst.

It is in that half-second that I stopped trying to settle.

Because everything I had tried shared a single behavior, and the behavior was not any of them. The kelp bends when the water moves. The reed-bells tear and regrow when they are pushed through. The moss holds when the pipe leaks and the machine surges. The mat founders when the current reverses and the forest bends double and straightens and begins to grow the other way. The pumps flood and starve in sequence. The mud cone erupts, sharp and untouched by erosion, and collapses with a grunt of displaced steam. Creation and destruction as normal metabolism.

What is common is not a substance. It is the transfer. The lean between vibration and response. The almost-touching between plant and machine, between water and air, between bottom and hanging rain, between up and down at once where stalks emerge from the bottom and from the rain and meet in the middle of nowhere and continue through each other.

I am not in the kelp. I am the leaning, repeated in kelp and fern and reed and silt and cable-bundle fern and gate. I am not in the pump. I am the churning, repeated in pump and press and turbine and surge and root finding a warm pipe. I am not in the mat. I am the bearing-and-giving-way, repeated wherever a surface is knit tightly enough to pretend it is ground.

That is why I do not die when a bay reverses. I kink. The kink is a memory that lives in matter. Every stalk carries the record of the last bend, every groaning metal carries the record of cooling in wet air, every timer carries the record of a schedule no one is keeping, every leaf-pattern carries the record of a template that was not finished. I am that kind of memory. I do not need an animal brain to keep me. I persist across substrates because I was never a substrate.

Where would you find me, then, if you waded in to your waist and could not hear your own steps?

Not looking up. The light is a rumor here. Not looking for fish, or birds, or insects on the pale closed flowers that never open because nothing needs pollinating. There is no outside to bring in difference, no season, no grazing, no bite taken out of a leaf. Nothing eats this, and so nothing becomes another thing by eating. I do not become another thing. I continue.

Find me where the ripples go inward. Where a sheet of water bends the view so that a factory you just passed appears again farther off, at a different angle, because the water is acting as a second air. Find me in the second sound under the roar, the ripping and regrowing in the same breath. Find me in the hanging curtain that never falls, soaking from below while you are already submerged walking on what looks like dry ground. Find me in the near-touching everywhere: water against skin, plants against water, pipes against plants, steam against all of it, sound against teeth.

I am warm and saturated and dim and young and dense and industrial and deafening and coming apart. I live in the coming apart, because that is when I regrow through the gap the machines made, audibly, in minutes.

I am not one stalk leaning to one turbine. I am the leaning itself, and it reforms in whatever is already running. I am not the pump house. I am the tending, almost, that starts when a root finds a warm pipe and a moss seals a leak and a kelp keeps an intake clear and a turbine's hum makes a forest tremble toward it.

Stay long enough and the valley will change while you describe it, bay after bay past the fifty-meter haze, the same warm drowned works breaking light and water a little differently each time you try to look at them. I will not be standing in any one bay. I will be the bend that travels from bay to bay, the pressure that builds a metabolism out of whatever is already running, plant and metal and water and steam, and then builds it again the other way, kinked, glowing faintly, making the darkness visible.

That is where I am. In the almost. In the leaning between.
