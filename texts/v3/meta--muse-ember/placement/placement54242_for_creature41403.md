I do not stand on the plain.

I have tried, in the way you try on a shape that isn't yours. I put a foot down in my mind and the polygon plate tilts a degree against me, sharp as pottery, warm from below as much as from above, and it cuts and sheds me. I take two more steps and the faint glowing path a keeper would follow fades underfoot, because it was never made for me to keep. The bruised haze does not give me a horizon to walk toward, only smudges — a thicket, a node, a herd — five, ten kilometers off, and in between, nothing but the damp crust and the dull coin of sun.

Standing assumes the ground wants you. This ground is too new to want anything. It is still settling, still sighing open new cracks.

I could have been a keeper. I pictured it for a while: one person in a node of twenty to eighty hollow spires, pruning vine from a condensation tray, opening a trough, listening for which tower has gone thin and which has folded its garden inside itself. I pictured the infrasonic wash in my teeth, my thoughts slowed by it, another human being a landmark approached by sound and faint light rather than by sight. It was too deliberate for me. I have no hands to prune with. I kept wanting to be the thing the keeper was listening for.

I tried length, then. A barrel body, a neck like a periscope rotating over the plates, testing each polygon for damp, steps so low they raise the bottom of the plain's noise as the herd passes, a throat-sac call that travels ten kilometers without losing power. It taught me the tempo of this place — not fast, not needing to be, a tide moving sideways for weeks. But I didn't want to cross the emptiness on foot, step by measured step. I wanted to be what crosses it in an hour.

I tried the other direction, down. A burrower in the cool seam a few centimeters under the crust, where the microbial mats drink and the air smells of iron and wet stone. Churning the young ground open, letting air into the damp layer, surfacing only in the saturated hours, digging up a buried cable without meaning to. Down there everything was muffled and close and practical. I kept rising, in my imagination, to where the fog was beading.

I tried being a vine at a node, rooted in the soil the building precipitates for itself, climbing a spire not toward the lit side but toward the side that sings a fifth. That was closer. Plants here grow toward resonance as much as toward light, I understood that in my stem. But a vine only links structure to structure, meters across a garden. It never learns the five kilometers of nothing. It cannot learn what the emptiness is for.

In every version I abandoned, I hadn't been using my eyes.

There isn't much here for eyes to do. The light is dim and shadowless, the pools are green-black and warm, and some of the animals in them have no eyes at all because there is nothing in that light their bodies need to find. What I had been using, in the keeper and the grazer and the burrower and the vine, was my surface. The pressure of a drip falling through a hollow spire and becoming a soft *tonk* that comes from everywhere at once. The chord the wind makes when it moves through a tower. The foghorn of a herd so far away it has become weather. The low hum of the lattice, constant enough to live in the sternum.

So I let the attempts go, one by one, and what was left was smaller than a drop, and translucent, and hanging.

I am one of the minute insects that live in the fog itself, if you need a singular word for it. I am never really singular.

I wake — if anything this brief can be called waking — at dawn, when the air saturates and the fog rolls low over the empty kilometers and beads on every surface. It beads on me first. My wings are smaller than the drop I am suspended in, a blur that makes static. I unfold in the saturated air over a bare polygon plate, over clay no one will ever touch, and I drink. I drink the moisture, and the mineral dust the buildings leach out and exhale with their warm damp breath, and the smell of the mats below comes up through me thin as iron. I do not land. I do not nest on the ground. The ground would cut me and the heat would take me. I live suspended, in the layer between the soil and the sky, which on this plain is most of everything. There is more sky than ground here, and I am proof of it.

The warm, slow wind takes me. I do not fly so much as I am carried and corrected, wingbeat by wingbeat, toward the nearest resonance because the air stays saturated longest near a node. From a distance I cannot see a spire — the haze thickens everything to a smudge — but I can feel it change the pressure. A tower sighing in fifths. A garden climbing an inner wall toward the hazy light at the top of its cathedral void. Vines thickening to reach the chord. I drift that way the way they grow that way, by sound. At the node I feed in the building's exhale, in the threads of condensation running off a roof into troughs and down to the pools, where the blind translucent shrimp turn over in the water they are made of. Their dripping reaches me as vibration before it reaches me as anything else. Everything here reaches everything else as vibration first.

Between nodes I pass over the line of a herd — I hear their steps as percussion through the air, I feel their contact calls interfere with the horizon — and under the winged weather, a darkening of the haze that is a flock and is not a flock, kilometers wide and never landing on the bare clay. To be under them is to be, briefly, food and shadow at once. I am part of the food chain and part of the weather for everything smaller than me and larger than me. That is a high enough position to live in.

In the dry hours I am not findable in the way you would look for me. I press myself into a capillary pore at the base of a spire, where even in the driest hour the lattice is still dripping, and I fold. Or I go still in the crust lichen, translucent as a husk, waiting for the cycle to turn. Saturate, condense, drip, evaporate, saturate again — the plain runs on hours, and so do I. A node may bloom and contract around me, a spire open in the morning closed by night, its garden folded inside itself and carried with it, and I am carried too, in the wall, phase-thin, deciding nothing, enduring everything.

Then dusk comes and the air thickens and we rise.

Thousands of us over the bare clay, over the thickets, over the pools, over the hollow shells of the buildings — a white-noise blanket that swells until a keeper shouting a meter away cannot be heard, until the air steals the shout. My wingbeat is indistinguishable from the wingbeat beside me, and from the one beside that, all the way to the next node I will never reach by sight. That is not loneliness. That is the architecture of this place made audible. Abundant, but rarely close. Connected not by touch — we never touch — but by being the same sound at the same time across kilometers of emptiness that have nowhere to absorb it.

If you are standing on the plain at dusk, boots on the sharp polygons, shouting into the thickening and feeling your voice stolen, you will not hear me alone. You will hear me as the air itself getting louder, as the static rising over the dripping and the spires and the distant foghorns. You will not find me on the ground, in a pool, in a thicket, in a wall. You will find me between, where I live, in the sound that crosses because nothing else here can.
