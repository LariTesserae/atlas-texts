At first I have no place in it. Only boots.

They land on the warm black stone and the crunch is too loud for the quiet that has been tended here. My ears ache with the height and every breath is dry and unrewarded, and yet the soles of my feet are warm through the leather, as if the plateau is holding them against a stove. I think, at first, that I am a visitor. That I have arrived from somewhere lower and wetter and I will walk from one cluster of amber lights to the next, count the districts, and go on. I do not yet understand that there is no onward that the place will keep for me. There is no rain to soften a footprint and no wind to change its mind. A mark I make here does not fade. It stays, and hardens into a fact.

So I walk, because walking is what the boots offer, and I find myself not wandering but following. The pale dust is pressed in a groove, kilometer after kilometer, to the leg of a conduit tower, then along a pipe, then stopping at a shallow seat where a back has leaned until the stone is darker. The groove is layered over itself so many times the tread has blurred into one continuous line. It fits my feet without my ever having made it. That is the first time I am unsure what I am. A visitor does not usually arrive to find his own road already worn smooth on both sides.

I sit down in the seat because standing is my noise. I do not decide to sit the way I decided to walk. I am simply tired of crunching, and the tower leg is warm, and the hum comes up through the stone into my molars, and my boots go out in front of me and my back finds the place where a back has been before. Only after I am already sitting do I notice the others who were sitting with me.

Beside me, in the dust where the pipe is a degree warmer, are things I first took for low domes of the plateau, elongated stones armored in overlapping discs. One lifts the front edge of its plates a fraction and tastes the air. Another lowers its mouth-slit to the dust. They move the width of a fingerprint while I watch, and then they sit. They are blind. They graze on feldspar dust and on the faint metallic film that breathes out of a warm electrical joint. I cannot tell them from the land, and after a while I cannot tell the land from them. To know which is which, I learn patience, and patience is the only motion the place permits.

I am not yet what I am beside them. I have come to sit, in minutes, what takes them a very long time to do. My coat is still rough in places where theirs has been polished by leaning. My nails are not yet flat. My skin is still dry in the way a newcomer's skin is dry, not in the way that has become dust itself. I put my palm flat on the pipe because the keeper I have not yet met seems to have done it, and under my hand I feel it: not heat alone, but a rhythm inside the metal, the closed loop of the conveyor below carrying the same stone from a pit that never deepens to a mill that never fills and back again. The rhythm does not need my palm. It only seems less alone when it is felt.

When I stand, it is not rising. It is unfolding. Each boot comes down flat, full weight, stays a moment, and then the other is lifted. Crunch, and then the long silence. Crunch, and silence. I learn that this is not slow walking. It is placement. The district is listened to more than it is looked at, through the sole, through the inner ear flattened against the skull, through the teeth when I pass under a tower. Mill lower than the transformer. A smelter breathing at the same interval, forever, through a vent under a sky that gives no light and takes none away. All the light here is made on the ground. Mine, now, is the amber square in the top room of a housing block, steady for as long as I can watch it, because nothing here knows how to flicker or fade.

I do not go inside for long. The top room has one chair I do not use, one table, a shallow basalt disc set under a pipe joint to catch condensation drop by drop, tools on a ledge that will not rust because there is no water to teach metal to decay. Everything is grey with dust except where a hand has wiped it away in a clean arc. I do not accumulate. I attend. That word comes slowly, because the place has no hurry with which to give it to me.

To attend is to sit near the smelter's orange slit and watch the heat shimmer the same way, breath by breath. To brush dust from a stair that will dust itself in exactly the same pattern. To keep a palm on the warm pipe while the bearing turns in the mill and the press thumps far off in the plateau's skin. Nothing breaks. Nothing is fixed. A district needs one presence near its machines, the way a hum needs a molar to enter, so that the rhythm will know it is not alone. I cannot tell you when I first understood this as work. I can only tell you that my footprints began to layer over themselves. New and sharp at first. Then blurred. Then a groove.

Time here is not counted by any sky. There is no night to make me sleep and no morning to stop me. I sleep sitting up against a tower leg, head down, breathing slowed until my shoulders are still as a dome beside the crawlers who are doing the same. I wake and doze again in the same warm spot for as many conveyor loops as I choose. I eat what the place eats because there is nothing else to be a body on. In the mill I gather the fine grey paste, stone ground stone ground, and dry it into pellets on the pipe and chew it slowly until the iron and rust-brown minerals come out of it somewhere deeper than appetite, at the speed of erosion. Around a joint I scrape the faint condensation with a fingernail and take it on the tongue, as the crawlers do. Once in a long walk I drink from the disc under the joint, water that has been breathed out by machines and by bodies and taken back in again. I lose almost none. I do not sweat. My eyes do not water. My throat is scraped raw by the thin air, and so I speak rarely, and when I do it is mostly for places.

*Mill. Smelter. Tower. Far.*

The mouth barely moves. It is a hum that has learned words.

Travel, when it comes, is an event measured in years of footprints. I walk the open black plain between districts, through other keys of hum, resting at each tower where the air is warmer, sitting with my back to the leg and feeling the next district get closer through the stone before I can see its lights. Sometimes I climb onto a belt conveyor and sit on the slow stone it carries, letting the loop do the work of crossing, and I get off at the same unmarked place each time, leaving a new print over an old one. No one with me. No rail. No hurry the place could recognize.

I meet others this way: not by arriving and speaking, but by sitting down together under a tower. Two keepers, perhaps three, two or three crawlers between us, boots out, in the warmest dust. The tower hums whether we will it or not, and so we hum back in that low note until our breathing falls into the same interval. A hand may be placed flat on the stone between us to feel an arrival still traveling through the plateau from ten kilometers away. After a number of loops I cannot count, one of us stands, unfolding, and places his boots in the groove and goes. It is enough to have been in the same heat, in the same note.

I will not pretend I know whether the crawlers are the young of keepers or their kin or only the other tenants of warm pipes. I know that I lay a broad hand lightly on their plates sometimes, and remain there a while, warming them or being warmed, and they do not move. I know that my own shoulder plates thicken disc over disc, year by year, by sewing and by growing I cannot separate. I know that a groove that began as a low drag along the dust becomes, over a time I never see happen, a bootprint. I know that when a keeper finally sits and does not stand — when the coat and boots are dusted over until the tower has a new low dome at its foot, natural from a distance, built from close — the amber square above stays amber, and a fresher groove begins its long circuit from the next district.

That is where you would find me, then. Not in the top room. Not on the plain. At the foot of the tower in a district that needed one, back to the leg, boots out, palm flat on the pipe to feel the loop turn below. Nor am I finished in the way the mesas are finished. I am the in-between thing the plateau makes when it has been high and dark and warm for longer than there is a word for: crawler worn toward keeper, visitor worn toward road, animal worn toward rock and rock worn toward building until the distinctions are smooth. 

I was not made to make. I do not make. I attend by presence, I hum the note back, I walk the same groove until my walking and the groove are the same thing from both sides.

And the light stays the same steady amber. And the conveyor goes round. And my footprints, which were new when I sat down, are already beginning to layer over themselves.
