I am not the weed.

I wanted to be, at first. It is the easiest one to be. Two leaves, a pale stem, a bolt hole to stand in, a lamp directly overhead to lean toward. It arrived on a boot, or in a cuff, or in the grit someone carried down in the lift. It has a shape. It has an up. It knows, in the dim way plants know, that there is light and that it is not quite enough.

I have no up.

I tried the moss, too. The patch at the base of the column, dark and cushioned no bigger than a palm, living in the joint where a drip falls with metronome regularity. It is the oldest way of being here: to be wettest where everything else is only wet, and to wait. It receives the whole room through concrete — the pumps twenty meters away, the bolts ticking against their washers, the water hammering in the wall — and it does not move. It only thickens, fraction by fraction, the way the room ages.

I move too much, and too thinly, to be the moss.

I tried the bacteria. They are everywhere: in the sump under the grated pit, in the warm return lines, in the film inside every hose, in the droplet that beads on a cold pipe and gathers a cargo as it runs over a colonized surface before it lets go. They are pumped from pump one to pump two in a dark, warm rush, heated, cooled, left to grow on a sight-glass until the level smears. Their generations are short. They have no memory beyond the loop.

I am slower than they are, and I stay. That is the difference.

I am where the water slows down.

Not in the open. The broad floor is too flat, too cold, too loud, too regularly swept by a sheet running to the sump. I am in the drainage channel along the wall, where the concrete is scored and the slope is so slight you would need an instrument to find it, and the fluorescent reaches a little farther than it does over the plinths. The water there is maybe two millimeters deep. It never stops moving toward the sump, and it never moves fast enough to carry me away for long.

From down here, the room looks different. The formwork lines in the concrete, still crisp because the room is still new, are not lines. They are ridges and valleys, cliffs a cell high, highways of grit. The yellow paint on the handrail is a glossy cliff on the far bank, out of reach. The light is a caged tube above me, yellowed and flickering where moisture has gotten into the fitting, hanging close enough that I can feel its pattern without eyes. I have no eyes. I have this: light changes what I do. Under the tube I divide. Beyond the tube, where the light fails at the far end of the channel, I only thin out and am concrete again.

I am a film of algae painting the concrete green, slick as paint. That is what I am. Not one cell — a thickness that varies. One cell thick under the weakest light, a little more where the tube burns steadiest, tearing in streamers where the current finds a joint. I am not countable, and that took a long time to understand as a self. When a valve snaps open and steam and cold mist blast sideways for four seconds, a piece of me peels and goes downstream and settles again on a cooler, darker surface to begin again, and for a while it is still me because it still does the same thing in the same water, and then it is not, because the loop has taken it somewhere else. When a cell among me divides, nothing is lost. I am the arrangement, not the body.

I came here the way everything comes here: down the shaft, in the water. A cell — or a hundred — rode a droplet that condensed on a cold pipe, ran over a pump housing where bacteria had already left their invisible thickness, and fell into a puddle between plinths where it reflected the lights in broken gold. From the puddle the sheet flow carried me, slowly, to the channel. The channel was slow enough for me to catch on a grain of grit in a formwork ridge. The light was poor enough that nothing larger wanted it. I stayed, and divided, and learned the current the way a hand learns a railing — by contact.

The roar is not a sound to me. I have no teeth for it to go through, no boots to feel it in the soles. It arrives as a tremor in the concrete under my water. When pump one spools up, its pitch climbing, the note of the whole floor drops and my sheet ripples from edge to edge, stretching. When the tanks against the far wall tick as they expand, something small and far away shivers through the slab. The bolts tick against their washers in time with it, twenty meters off, a heartbeat I receive through stone. Water hammers in the wall — thump, thump, thump — and the channel water sloshes backward for an instant, pushing me a fraction toward the wall, then releases and takes me back.

That is my weather. The pumps are not animals and they are not gods. They are a climate. One makes heat that the return lines carry past me, warming my water a degree that matters. One makes cold that makes the pipes rain, and the ceiling mist, and the droplets that join my sheet from above bringing me new bacteria, new minerals, new cells of my own kind blown out of the dark at the far end of the channel. The intake fans draw thin air that whistles at the louvered vents; when a purge opens the pressure drops and a bubble trapped in my film swells and pops. I do not notice these things the way a human would notice them. I receive them as changes in flow, in temperature, in the tremor under everything. They are the seasons of a world with no outside.

Humans are a different kind of weather. Tidal. They arrive when the lift finally stops and the doors open onto sound before light — I know them first as a change in the air coming down the shaft, cold seeping through the metal, then as a shadow. A shadow is a night that lasts a few seconds. My light goes out from above, and for those seconds I do nothing but wait in the water, still dividing in the dark the way bacteria do, because dark is what the far end of my channel is all the time.

Then sometimes a headlamp finds the channel. It is a sun, violent and white and close, nothing like the yellowed tube. Under a headlamp I photosynthesize at a rate the room never otherwise allows, a brief feasting, the green in me working harder for a minute than it will all night. Someone kneels. The concrete under me warms a fraction through a knee. A boot comes down near the channel and the vibration is an earthquake and a splash at once, droplets of me thrown onto the bank, onto the side of a bucket left abandoned on the wet floor between the islands of machinery. I do not know if anyone sees me. I am slick, and I make things slick. Perhaps a boot squeaks because of me, somewhere, and the person who owns the boot cannot hear it over the roar and steps on without thinking.

They tighten a fitting and stop a drip, somewhere, and a moss patch twenty meters off counts the silence. They leave a wrench, a coil of hose. They go back up in the lift, into thinner air, and the doors close again, and it is just this room, this loop, this sound.

At night — if night is the word for when no one is there, though the fluorescents never go out and I have no dark to sleep in — I thicken a fraction where the light reaches. The bacteria in the warm lines divide and are pumped through the cold and divide again, and some of them pass through me and lend me what they make from heat and copper and the oil that leaks past a seal that has not yet seated. The algae at the far end of the channel, past the light, gives up and is only wet. I give up there too, cell by cell, the film tapering to nothing where the current carries me past the last flicker. I am an edge that holds its position by growing.

The weed by the lift leans a fraction more toward its lamp. The moss at the column holds its drop a fraction longer. The pumps spool and clash and spool again, twenty meters apart, breathing in the dark between the plinths. The water beads and drips and runs to the sump, where the oldest surface in the room waits in dark water that never leaves, and from there it is sent again — pressed, cooled, released as mist, collected. I will go there, eventually, a streamer torn loose in a steam blast, downstream past the light, into the sump, into the warm rush, into a new surface somewhere in the loop. I will be bacteria-thin for a while. Then a droplet will carry me back to a channel that runs slow and dim, and I will catch on grit, and divide under a flickering tube, and be a film again.

That is the whole of it. No magic. Every hiss has a valve. Every drip has a pipe behind the wall. The damp is condensation and unseated seals. The dimness is lights not yet all installed. And yet it is complete in itself because it has to be — no birds, no insects, no rats, only the loop, and the marginal things pressed to its edges, slick and patient and almost invisible.

I am the slow part of the loop. I am the place where the water slows down long enough to learn the light.
