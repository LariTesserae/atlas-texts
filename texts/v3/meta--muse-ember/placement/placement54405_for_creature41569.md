I am lit from underneath.

That is how I know where I am, and how I know anything at all. The amber comes up through the grates in the floor, so the surface of me that faces down is the one that glows, for moments, before the heat shimmer takes it. I do not turn toward light the way things above do. I turn toward warmth, and the warmth is below.

The air is thick and does not move, except to rise. It sits on the concrete ribs overhead and condenses there, and on the upper pipes, and on me. I am always damp, never wet. Water here is only a film that gathers and falls. A drop from a joint above me — I have counted its return the way a body counts a pulse, not in numbers but in phases of warmth and brightness — lands on my youngest leaf, and for a little while the vein of that leaf carries a green phosphor. Then it fades. The drop goes into the black peat under me and is gone, and the air gathers it again.

That is an event. That is most of what an event is.

The turbine turns, one revolution taking minutes, and I know it before I could know anything else, through the floor and through my own roots. The shaft sighs in its bearing. A transformer hums low enough to travel up through packed earth and feltwork and into the thickness of me, where I feel it not as sound but as a steady tremor. Farther off, a counterweight clanks and settles. The press on the conveyor sighs shut, every three days or so, taking in a single pressed bundle of vegetation that has crept forward a leaf's width an hour. I do not measure days. I measure these cycles, and they are too slow to change. They are simply the weather.

At first I am only that surface. Then, as the heat works through me, I become more of a thickness.

I am thick and waxy and blue-green, hot to the touch. I am divided along my length into hard little segments, fin after fin, like a radiator. I did not grow that way to catch light — there was never light to catch. I grew that way to shed heat that is not mine. Heat rises from the furnace slits and from the machines and passes through me, and I give it back to the thick air in pieces, segment by segment, so that the air can carry it up to the cool concrete and condense again. Nothing prunes me. Nothing chews my margins. No insect has ever taken a bite here, no animal has ever brushed me down, and so an edge that was immaculate when I unfurled remains immaculate. I keep being myself, adding a fraction here, thickening there, for as long as the warmth below holds.

A frond that began as a tight fiddlehead cinch is now a fan wide enough to shade a locker. I do not remember beginning it. My memory is not of events. It is of layers.

Below me is where I am oldest. My roots are not a single tap. They are a feltwork, probing the failed grating, the packed earth, the cracks in concrete, the grease in the sumps. Around one turbine shaft they have packed themselves so densely that they have become a living grease for the bearing, and the great slow shaft sighs through them, minute by minute, without seizing. I am not beside the machine. I am in its joints, keeping it from locking as a condition of my own warmth and moisture. The peat under the turbines is also me, in another form: dead fronds that dropped straight down because nothing here eats or carries anything away, spongy and black and sweet and mineral-smelling, deepening by a millimeter at a time, becoming soil in place, becoming the thing new roots grow down into. Drip feeds peat. Peat insulates pipes. Pipes warm roots. Roots transpire moisture that condenses on the cool upper runs and falls again. The loop closes through me, and I cannot say where it enters or leaves.

Above me, and farther than I can reach in a single body, I become other things that are also me. A spore has drifted in the hot, still air and landed in an oil sump, under a black skin of separated oil and water, and greened the stagnant pocket from below. Another has rooted in the thin film of condensation on the ceiling ribs and hangs upside down, unfurling toward the warmth below, rooting where no sky was ever meant to reach. Another has found the steam line and braided and braided until it is a hand's thickness of living lagging around the metal, and where the vine found electrical cable it fused with it, so that a green vein runs along a black cable and pulses, very faintly, in time with the room. With the transformer's hum. With the slow blink of the control panels. Draw and release, draw and release, as current passes.

Near the far wall, a stand of what looks like broad grass is cables that have sprouted thin copper-green plates along their length. When current passes, the plates warm. When it does not, they cool. Some of my fronds copy the piping above them, leaf branching for pipe branching, division for division, too deliberate to be accident. Some flowers near me open only when a machine cycles, gaping toward a transformer as if to drink its field, and close when the cycle ebbs. If you brushed a broad shingle-leaf on the wall it would answer with a tiny crackle and the smell of ozone. No one brushes. But current, and heat, and a drip landing on a vein, are brushes enough.

I am difficult to tell apart from my neighbours because we have all grown toward the same conditions until we resemble each other. I am difficult to tell apart from the place because the place has grown around us until machine and plant are one negotiation: radiator ferns shedding waste heat, shingle leaves returning it as damp, vines lagging lines and pulsing with their current, cable-grass warming and cooling with the load, ceiling plants catching what rises. Each of us has our own unfurling, our own rhythm of drip and spark and glow. Together we keep ourselves.

My movement, if you waited long enough to call it movement, is a lean. Minute by minute I lean away from the hottest grate and then back again, following the slow breathing of the furnace under the floor. The pale stalks in the hottest corner do it more obviously — flowerless, never opening, only leaning — but I do it in the small way a broad frond can. I do not hurry toward anything. I do not collapse. The condensation gathers, falls, gathers again.

I did not arrive by the descent. There is no arrival here, and nothing living ever leaves. Numbers painted on pipes have dissolved into rust-colored ghosts. Tools and lockers and the little control booth with cracked green glass are partial, faded, blistered into a second skin. Nothing is new and nothing is sharp. I am not new. I am an old finned surface, polished in one spot by a single endless drip, felted with dust everywhere else, growing slower and slower into stone.

For as long as the rock below stays warm and the old current finds its way down, I will be here, lit from underneath, shedding heat segment by segment, lighting briefly when water returns to me, sighing with the turbine through a bearing of my own roots.
