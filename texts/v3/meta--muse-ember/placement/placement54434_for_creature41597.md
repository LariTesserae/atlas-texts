You would not find me up.

Not in the trees, though they are perfect for birds. Not over the settling ponds, where a frog would sit with its face to the light. Not in the hot, wet air, where insects would make their small weather. I am at the seam where things become damp: where the boiler-house wall sweats in the morning, where concrete gives up its paint to algae, where a threshold is black with year after year of mud-lines. The rim of Paludra is still there, in theory, but from where I am it is less a view than a rumor of dryness. My world is the first few inches of surface, and the water moving over it.

I come awake when the town condenses. Every cold surface is beaded, the brick cold under my foot despite the air pressing hot and heavy against everything. Mist hangs low over the ponds and flooded streets and does not lift until a boiler sighs its steam on its timer. My foot ripples, muscle by muscle, over the skin of the wall — bacteria, algae, fungi, the olive-black film that makes brick and timber and metal all wear the same damp age. I graze it slowly. Where the film is orange, iron has fed it from a rust-streak. Where it is green, light has reached it through the sky-trees. I do not know those names as words. I know the tastes, the wetness, the slight warmth the film gives back to the brick.

My house is with me, a spiral I carry on my back. It is not a shelter from anything that hunts me — nothing here hunts — but from drying. Even in Paludra, where the air is a held breath, there are pressures: midday, when the plants transpire so hard the air thickens, when leaves drip and the sheet metal pops like knuckles cracking all over town. Then I pull in close under the eave of my own shell, in the dampest angle of wall, where a moss-softened strut of the pipeway crosses overhead. Heat comes through the brick in slow pulses. Every so often the boiler vents, and the whole wall shudders with it, sweat running faster for a minute. The tin roofs tick. The fans clank through their pollen-weather, indifferent to me, though some of that blown dust settles on the film I eat.

I have eyes, of a sort, on stalks, and a face I keep mostly lowered to the work. I do not watch the town. I read it by contact. A flooded boulevard, ankle-deep to anyone tall enough to have ankles, is to me a broad shallow country. The painted lines are long gone under silt and algae, so there is nothing to follow but gradient: slightly warmer here, where a condensate pipe leaks; slightly softer there, where mud has slumped a foot and stopped; a black tide-line at a doorway, then another, then another, like the wall's memory of floods. In the afternoon rain, at three o'clock as regular as the boilers, the storm comes down sideways off the rim and drums on corrugated iron until the town must shout to itself. Water sheets down my wall in rivers. I flatten my foot and hold on and let it pass, and when the rain stops as suddenly as it began, the roofs go on dripping their private weather long after the sky has cleared. That drip is my rain, continuing.

Evening is redistribution. The lamps come fully on and make their sodium halos in the steam, but beyond twenty paces they show nothing, and I ask for less than that. The flowers open under them and release their sweet fermented smell — not for me; I have no animal nose to please, only tentacles for damp and taste. The pumps go on heartbeating under the flooded plaza, arrhythmic and heavy, moving water from one low place to another. The vibration travels up through concrete into my foot. I can feel the town's circulation in my sole the way the vines feel a new handhold through brick.

Night is when I travel. Growth is loudest then: trunks creaking as they expand, metal ticking as it cools, tendrils advancing in the dark toward heat they can feel through walls. I descend my wall by slow ripples, leaving a silver road behind me that dries at its edges and tightens as it sets. I cross the plaza where duckweed sheets shift whenever the pumps change cadence; I go under the green lace of vines that have sealed a factory door shut like a bandage, because the sealed dark is cooler and keeps its damp; I ease into the open-sided machine shed, where a rail line cool with night holds a bright living film and a fern uncurls above it, fiddlehead by fiddlehead, living on machine-heat. Belt drives slap somewhere and the rail hums through me. I graze and graze. A few inches is a night's work. I am in no hurry. Nothing in Paludra is in a hurry, because nothing is eaten and nothing eats, and everything has been balanced long enough that falling is no longer fast.

Before morning condensation beads the brick again, I climb back to my corner of the boiler-house wall, or another corner much like it — for a snail, a damp corner is a home the way a forest is a home to a tree. My shell takes the new beads on its spiral. The mist is at my ceiling again. The first vent lifts it.

That is where I am: low, slow-footed, carrying my house, eating the skins of walls, leaving a trail the mud will take back. One of the town's few snails, perhaps the only resident with anything like a face, and I keep it close to the damp.

Small enough that a finger's width of rising water is a flood. Small enough that the whole steaming bowl of Paludra — brick and leaf and iron, pump and pressure, transpiration and condensation — can pass over me, around me, through me, and leave me still here at dawn, wet, warm, and unwatched by anything at all.
