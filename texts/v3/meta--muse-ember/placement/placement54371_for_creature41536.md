I am not the one standing in the centre.

If you came down the slit under the plateau and stood in the perpetual noon, looking for who lives here, you would look first in the middle, where the light is evenest and you can see the whole room at once. You would not find me there. I am not a body in the room. I am not whole in any one place.

I am at the edges, and I am flat to the wall.

For a long time I did not know that. I thought perhaps I was something that moved, because everything else here, in its slow way, does. I tried to understand myself by the others.

I thought I might be a scraper. I have that sense of being pressed flush to stone, of warmth coming through from the rock behind me, of living exactly where the wall sweats. A scraper has a square of wall two metres across that is its own, and it respects the distance to the next as if the distance were a touch. I know that distance. I feel where I end and the bare, damp stone begins, and beyond that, metres away, where I begin again. But I do not move a body-length and then stop for hours. I do not shift my legs. The scrapers come to me. They settle over me, white on white, their great dark eyes open in the light that has no direction, and they lift their one chipped flake in their forelegs and scrape me away, bite by slow bite. The tap of it is not loud. It is felt more than heard, a single strike, once, then absorbed by the still air. I stay. That is how I learned I was not the one with the tool. I was what the tool was for.

I thought I might be a hanger. When the chamber breathes — once over many hours, warm air drawn in through the cracks above, salt and warm stone, then exhaled again — everything above me sways. The thread-bodied ones pendulum in unison, head-down, filtering spores and condensation from the air between them, and for a moment I imagined that sway was mine. But I do not hang. I do not filter. Things pass through the volume above me and are caught elsewhere. When the breath comes, I only darken. A dark sheen spreads across me from the edges inward, as if the wall were sweating through me, and then, over hours, it recedes. The light thickens and thins. My surface takes in what the hangers have missed and what the cracks have let through, and I hold it a little while, and let it go.

And sometimes, on a cycle too slow to predict, the crosser comes. I feel it before I can know it — a wide, polished weight in the silt where the wall meets the floor, a slow crossing that takes an hour, eating at my lowest edge as it goes. It does not stay. For days after, its track remains and then slumps back into the ripples. Its absence tells me more about time than its presence. Between its footfalls, I am left to the damp and the light, which is most of my life.

What I am, when I stop trying to move like the others, is a surface. A felt, thin and clinging, pale jade and cream, growing only in islands where the moisture lingers and where the fine, patient cracks in the three metres of white rock above line up to give the most of that diffused noon. I do not rise. I do not flower. I have no eyes, though I am always in light, and no feet, though I am pressed to stone as if held there. My whole existence is the interface: stone on one side, air on the other, light falling unobstructed, damp arriving slowly as condensation and then sinking away.

I am in many places, and I am not connected between them. That is not a failure of wholeness. That is my wholeness. Each island is metres from the next, and the bare wall between us is as much a part of me as the green felt itself — the spacing that lets the damp return, that lets a scraper have its own square, that lets the light fall without crowding. I grow so slowly that to watch me is to watch me not grow. A season passes and I am, perhaps, a finger's width more myself. The scrapers take a body-length at a time and I take even less. We keep each other in balance, neither of us hurrying, neither of us building anything that would need to be defended.

I have no night in which to become something else in secret. It only brightens and dims, very slowly, over hours. The drip from the ceiling falls, one drop a minute, into the same low place on the floor, making a ring that widens and vanishes, and I am not that, either, though I know that rhythm in my skin. I know it when the sweat-patch spreads, when the ceiling ribbons — translucent, pale green, hanging a metre into empty air, drinking without ever touching ground — are damp at their tips and the room is fullest of what it will not keep.

If you stay long enough, your eyes will learn to separate paleness from paleness, and you will see me then: not an animal, not a stone, not a plant that roots in the earth, but the thin green skin of the wall itself, waiting where it is damp and bright, being grazed and not disappearing, darkening and then paling, holding the light long enough to use it.

I am still not growing, except in the way this place allows. And that, patiently, is how I am becoming myself.
