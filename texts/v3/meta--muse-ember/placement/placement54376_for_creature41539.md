I come over the low rise through the gap between the ridges, and the first thing the hollow does is take things away from me, one by one, in the order it took them from everything else.

It starts with sound. I am still tall enough to be irritated by it. I shout something into it, to test it, and hear only the white tearing noise take it whole. A bird’s call, if there was one, would go the same way. A fox barking at a cub. My own footfall on peat. After a minute I stop shouting and start feeling it instead — pressure in the teeth, behind the eyes, a low note under the wind that the basin throws back from wall to wall until the whole place hums like the inside of a drum. Speech mislaid. Hearing mislaid. What is left is vibration, travelling through stone and wet ground, and that is enough if you have nothing better to listen with.

Then the light goes, or rather it never quite arrives. Even at midday I am in deep blue-grey shade between the shoulders of the ridges. I keep looking for a horizon and find only a grey strip of sky. Things at a distance soften and give up. What remains clear is what is close and damp and underfoot: peat black as wet leather, moss a cold green, a stone the colour of wet ash, a single rust-orange punctuation where iron has been left to weather. I learn to read the place by touch because sight is a poor instrument here, and touch is honest about what it finds. Everything is wet. My hands come away wet from touching anything.

Then the cold pools. It is not sharp mountain cold. It collects at the bottom and does not leave, damp and patient, and it gets into fingers and knees and slows the crossing of those couple of hundred paces until crossing feels like a project. I slow down without deciding to. I kneel in the gritty middle where the wind has stripped the floor to bare flats, each lone stone ringed with a tuft of grass at long intervals from the next, and I put a hand down and feel the pebbles tremble almost imperceptibly in their peat when the jet pulses. I am beginning to be the right size for this place, which is to say I am beginning to be too large for it in every way that matters.

Because if I stay like this — upright, vocal, warm, in a hurry to get to the other side — I will only be passing through. The fox passed through. Its track is weeks old and already softening at the edges, a record of a crossing, not an address. The birds passed through, or nested for a season in a crevice out of the jet’s path and flushed over the ridge at my approach, and left for winter altogether because a small warm body cannot afford the cold pool at the bottom. The human passed through and left a windbreak never high enough to stand behind, a sunk causeway, a bone awl by a bench slab as if the owner would return in a season or a year. The owner is not returning in any season that matters to the moss. All of them were visitors, and the moss has been quietly mailing their traces back to the ground ever since.

If I am to live here, I have to subtract more. Down, down. Lower than the ankle, lower than the handprint a moss cushion takes and springs back from over a minute or two. Even the beetle under the flat stone is too much for me now — a body to right itself with annoyed dignity, a patrol from stone to stone, a few years of movement, however little. The slug is too much — a whole afternoon to cross a cushion, a silver trail drying behind it and reused by the next. The fern is too much — waist-high, uncurling each year in the same green commas from the same buried crown, still spending energy upward into light that never quite arrives. The birch stool is too much — centuries old, perhaps, but still trying to be a tree in a place with no height to spend on.

What is left when you take away voice, sight, speed, heat, and height? What is left is not one creature. It is a pact.

I find myself on the north wall, where the cracks have been polished and rounded by long weather until they look hand-worn, and at the edge of an overhang out of the direct jet but not out of its pulse. I hang in grey festoons, and where I touch the stone I crust it in patches that must be many decades established. Underneath me the moss has taken hold completely, upholstering the stone green, and condensation gathers in beads on my underside and works its way down. Now and then a drip falls from a crack above and ticks on stone, and the wind takes the tick away before it has finished being a sound.

I am two lives keeping house together. The fungus makes the shelter — stone, damp, the slow chemistry that cold makes possible. The alga lives inside it, green when I am wet, grey when I am not, making what little food the dim light allows. Neither of us can live here alone in any useful sense, and neither of us needs to be heard. We read the world as vibration through the wall and the tremor in the peat, as damp arriving and staying, as frost feathering the sheltered side of the stone even when the air is a degree above freezing. We swing, a little, when the fissure sings louder and the fern fronds shiver in unison and the hanging lichens move together. That is our whole response to weather. We do not tear. We do not startle. We move because everything around us moves, and then we are still again.

I do not cross anything in an afternoon. I do not uncurl in a season. I grow a fraction of a millimetre and keep it. The moss advances a finger’s width and keeps it; I advance less than that, and keep it longer. A dead frond may take a season to surrender to rot, and the worm has that season, and the fungus has several. I have the decades. In the water film between moss leaves below, springtails and tardigrades are turning in droplets, living lives measured in days in the dark they were born in. I am slower than them, and older than the ferns, and I will outlive the birch and the beetle and the birds not because I am stronger but because I am asking for almost nothing: stone, damp, and light too dim for anything more ambitious to bother with.

That is where I am in the hollow. Not in the bare middle where the wind strips things down. Not over the ridge, gone before I have properly registered. Hung in a seam, cold-blooded and honest about it, making no noise in return for the noise that made me. Sparsely, carefully alive, in an island of grey among the islands of green, keeping what little I gain — and keeping it.
