There is no opening.

Warmth finds me first, and after it the weight of the water, even on every side, so that inside and outside stop being different. It does not move much. It drags when I turn. The dark is not a thing I notice; it is what happens when the water stops against something and I learn the stopping. A wall. A slab. The flank of another body passing close enough that my side-line ripples with its ebb.

At first I think I am one of the loopers.

A long, soft pressing goes by above me, translucent to pressure, mouth gaping at particles I cannot taste. Dozens follow its line, not fleeing, only making room. I turn with them, or think I do, until the movement checks too hard and does not give. Something holds me from below. Disc after disc finds the ground and commits. I am not in the middle water. I am under it.

I learn my shape that way, by what holds. Above, a flattened armor, taking the texture of what I lie on — wet stone, fired clay, the polished chip of bone. Below, soft and suctioned, made to release and re-grip without leaving the place I hesitate on. Along the flank a ribbed line for feeling another body coming. Ahead of the mouth, two antennae sweeping, whiskered, blind, tasting the tile before the mouth arrives. I release a small puff from the gills when another presses near. *Mine.* Or *move.* Or *stay.* It is all the language there is, laid antenna against antenna in the still water.

The floor is not ground. That is the first sure thing. Under my feet it is laid: flat stones none quite the same size, worn to a common level, smaller broken pieces set between them. Where a slab has lifted, silt has crept in and made a spongy crust, white and pitted on the tongue of the water. Farther on the paving gives way to hand-set mosaic — shards of fired clay softened at the edges, shell fragments with a tooth-like catch, bone chips polished smooth by being passed over for longer than I can make a record of. I dig sometimes, patiently, with mouth and forelegs, the way others do. There is no soil. Only more rubble packed tight: ballast stone, broken pots, more bone. Fill, all the way down.

I would find myself on the low terrace that steps up toward the central vault, on the side between a stone table and a doorway at hip-height.

That is my territory, though I would not call it that. A wall's width in a day will carry me from my terrace to the edge of the vault and back. The antennae go first, across the mosaic, across the smooth path where mouths have grazed the film clean, across the rougher patches where they have not passed in a long time. Everything is filmed. A matte, cream skin of microbes lives on every tile, every bone rib, every cup — drinking the dissolved warmth that rises from the vent under the center. I eat that film. I leave the stone smoother behind me by a fraction. Mineral taste passes over the gills and mouth, damp and warm, no leaf, no fiber, no green thing ever. There never could have been. Only stone, clay, shell lime, bone, and sinew darkened to tea. The absence of plants is the shape of everything that lives here.

The vault is above and all around the terraces. I know it by its ribs: great bones curved and lashed and plastered over with clay, arching perhaps thirty of my own lengths across, finger-marked where the clay was pressed in. Around it the smaller rooms shoulder together — storage pits roofed with slabs, tunnels just wide enough to wriggle sideways through, vertical shafts, niches, chimneys, cold kilns. Nothing lines up. The bricks are uneven. Mortar oozes in lumps never smoothed. In the niches I brush past, with an antenna, the tools left where someone set them down: knapped flakes, bone needles, copper awls bent and greened. I do not know what hands were. Their marks have rounded away. Their sharp corners have softened like soap under the tap of centuries. The place was made to stand in and fire pots in. Then the water came, slowly, as the summit sank by fractions. The high rooms were the last to drown. Now it is maintained, in a small way, by other hands.

My neighbors teach me the place by their currents, which I feel against my skin more than anything else.

On the stone tables, in rows, are the cups. Each cup is a perfect small house for a pumper, cones fixed head-down in clay jars, lacy fans opening and closing in unison, straining the slow rain of dead things that drifts down. Their faint current tells me where the table starts before my feet do. In the kilns and narrow chimneys are the shrimp, the quick exception to our tired pace — translucent, flicking in short bursts between chambers, carrying their young in shell houses they wedge into any cup that will fit them. Births happen in cups. Deaths become rain.

Above me, between floor and ceiling, the hoverers loop. Pale, eel-like, eyeless, ribbed with the same side-line as mine but made for middle water. They patrol the vault mouth gaping, eating filterers and grazers, the careless ones that rise too high from a surface. They feel me long before I feel them and swing away with a movement so unhurried it is almost courteous. I stay low to keep from being that careless one.

At the long unchanging hours — there is no night, but there is a time when the work of grazing slows — I climb, disc by disc, to the underside of the vault's ceiling. There we crowd shoulder to shoulder, three and four deep, sticky feet and filter fans and egg-strings layered over the stone until the stone itself is mostly animal and slightly moving. I was hatched there, in a string among the others. My mother grazed the same mosaic below. Her armor took the color of the stones she lay on, and mine will, in time, take theirs.

Beyond the retaining wall that runs around the edge of this high, made plateau, the ground gives out stone by stone into a drop that goes on into colder, emptier darkness. There the large ones patrol — cartilaginous, blind, almost featureless, one or two of them for the whole place. They do not need to be fast. Almost everything here is packed so tightly, generation after generation, with no season and no day to knock it out of balance, that a stray crawler past the wall is enough. When a hunter passes close, the whole crowded ceiling goes still. Fans close. Antennae withdraw. The water holds its breath in the way water can. I press myself flatter against the bone rib and let my armor be the tile.

I do not build. No one here builds as the builders built. But sometimes a slab sighs, ticks, settles a finger's width in an afternoon after standing a thousand years, and I brace it. I wedge a pebble under a sagging edge with my mouth. I haul a shell fragment to block a tunnel that brings in too much current. By the hip-height doorway beside my terrace there is a heap no larger than a fist, picked stones added to year after year, deliberate and hand-scale. I add to it when I pass. It is craft continued by other hands, at the same patient pace as the fans opening for an hour, closing, opening again.

That is the shape of a life here: short and closed and old. Warm mineral water brings up its chemistry from the vent. Microbes make a film. Grazers eat the film. Filterers strain the rain. Swimmers eat filterers and grazers. Hunters take the careless. Nothing blooms. Nothing bursts. Silt builds and unbuilds over a generation. The low hum of the vent is felt in the belly through the paving, strongest near the center, and everywhere else is a made sound — clay contracting, a stone settling deep in the fill, water sighing through a broken tunnel.

So I rest my flank against the low terrace wall that I know by its worn level and the rougher film just beyond my track, and let the current, such as it is, pass over me. An antenna lays against mine — *stay* — and moves on. A hoverer loops, unhurried, into the vault above, followed by dozens making room. A pumper beside me opens its fan.

The place holds.
