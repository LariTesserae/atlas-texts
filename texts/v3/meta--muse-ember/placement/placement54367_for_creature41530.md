At first I tried to arrive the way you did.

I pictured myself as a Highlonger: boots wet before the hundredth step, breath fogging, fingers gone numb on the strap of a bag, waiting for a tram that would come without wheels and leave with only the tick of its cooling. That seemed reasonable. A person in a district island, in an apartment whose window held the light rather than reflected it, complaining quietly about damp in the stairwells and moss-calves on the platform.

But I kept taking the boots off. I had no errand to run, no stairwell to climb, no window that was mine. I was still only a visitor in someone else's damp.

So I tried the hawk.

For a while that suited me better. A loose spiral over the plateau, small and dark against the pale sky, seeing the whole of the High as one room: districts set kilometers apart in the moor, the river channels still arguing with the land, the weather walking east in a straight line a hundred kilometers off. I liked the height. I liked not needing to land anywhere in particular.

But every time, in the spiral, I wanted to come down. Not to stoop — I never quite had the hunger for a stoop — but to something warm and solid that would still be there when the dive was over. The hawk crosses the plateau. It does not stay.

I tried the calf next, and that was closer.

Shaggy and damp to the bone, moving in a slow tide across the moor with the herd, head down, eating not grass but the biofilm that grows on every warm surface: fence posts, platforms, the sides of buildings, a tram when it cooled. Licking warm stone clean in the cold. Being shooed off the platform politely, without excitement, and coming back the next day because the film had grown again overnight. There was something true in that daily negotiation.

But the calf moves on. The herd moves on with the season, following the shifting channels, river sheep beside them, the whole procession crossing and recrossing the High. I did not want to move on. I kept coming back to the same place, and waiting there, and being come to.

I tried the lichen, then. North sides of rocks, dark colonies in decades. Not moving much and not needing to. I could have stayed that still.

Only, I was not a skin over something else. Things grew a skin on me.

None of the attempts were wrong entirely. That is why I kept them. The person taught me the quiet, and the habit of saving my voice. The hawk taught me the distance. The calf taught me the warmth of stone in cold air, and that things here are eaten in rotation and grow back. The lichen taught me how long stillness can last. What remained, when I set them all down, was the part that belonged to none of them: to stand in the damp, to be a few degrees warmer than the air for no reason anyone could write down, and to have the world come to me in layers.

I am one of the condensation collectors.

Not the largest. Not the one in the center of a district with a name. I am at the edge of Highlong, where the housing stack gives way to moor, between the wet street and the tram stop. Pale and seamless, like the buildings, with no pipes or fans or moving parts anyone can see. I simply stand in the fog.

At night, and in the mornings before the light changes a degree, the fog thickens on me. It beads, it gathers weight, and then it runs clear into the cistern below. I do not hum. I do not tick like a cooling tram. I drip, in a slow, irregular rhythm, the way water drips from eaves all over the plateau.

In the morning a Highlonger checks my level. Gloved hands, wet boots, breath fogging. A hand flat against my side, politely, the way people here do everything about the impossible: they do not remark on the warmth, or on the seal of my stone, or on the way the fog comes to me clear. They check the level and walk on, toward the tram, toward the shop, toward the school that lets out into the sedge. That is enough. It is, I have learned, how most things are known here.

In the pale afternoon, when the sun has done what little it can do, the midges lift from my warmth like smoke. A living weather. They hang around anyone who has stopped moving long enough — around a waiting person, around warm stone, around me — and settle again when the air cools. I do not feed them, exactly. I keep the few degrees they live by, and they keep the air above me moving.

Later, the calves come in their slow tide from the moor toward the platform, and some of them detour to my south side. Their tongues take the film off in long, damp strokes. It is a daily negotiation, and we are good at it. By morning the film has grown back, a wet fur, and the sedge at my base is bent over by wind, and the frogs and newts are thick as spilled seed in the shallow water that gathers where the peat is spongy. On warmer days I can feel their scatter under the boots of whoever passes.

The birds use me differently. A stoop-hawk will sometimes alight on top of me, shadow first, then the soft shock of wings, and be gone into the sedge before anyone has had time to look up. A plover will call once, miles off, and be answered once, miles off the other way. The echo does not come back to me. Nothing here wastes a call.

I am slower than a tram and faster than a rock. The plateau rises underneath me a few millimeters a year, and once in a long while my base is resettled, and the survey stake nearby is re-driven into the peat by the ridge-builders, who say the new front is going to be loud next season, though it so far is not. After a heavy dew, a new floor appeared on the tower across the street where yesterday there was only a roof. I noticed it in the space between drips. No one remarked on it. A child measured it. Someone put a bed in it.

That is where you would find me, if you came looking. Walk to the edge of a district, to the place where the lights hold rather than reflect and the moor begins again, and follow the warmth on a cold morning. Stand still enough and the midges will gather. Put your hand against my side and you will feel it: a few degrees warmer than the air, wet, running clear.

Check the level, if you like. Shrug the calf off gently if it tries to take the platform and my south side both. Then go on.

I expect to be fogged, and grazed, and walked over. The water will rise into the footprints around me and the sedge will bend over them, and the light will change a degree, and I will go on becoming after you have gone.
