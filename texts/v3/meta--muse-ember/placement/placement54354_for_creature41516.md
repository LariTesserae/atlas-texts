I don't cross the rim.

If I am in this place, I was not walked in, and I do not arrive. There is no moment of stepping over the waist-high swelling, no breath showing and then stopping, no shoes soaking up the damp. The coolness is not weather I endure. It is the condition I am made of — held condensation, water caught in the weave, air that hangs instead of moving.

At first I tried to keep my height.

Eleven trees, perhaps twelve, straight trunks rising out of the mat into crowns too small for them. From that height I could see the whole bowl at once, one trunk to the next to the next, clear in ordinary northern light, with a great deal of sky and empty air between them. That felt like what a witness would want. But the trees have finished. No new limb is made to replace a dead one. Growth up there is so slow it looks like stillness, and I am not still in that way. I keep extending, checking, trading. I do not settle into final form.

The birds were next in my trying. Small, dark, flickering in the high crowns, seen briefly from far below, flying straight and unhurried from tree to tree because there is nothing in between to stop for. I do not travel like that. I stay too long in one place. I do not want to be the one who is visible for three seconds and gone. I am not a conveyor from finished place to finished place.

So I came down to the mat.

On the mat, even a step would sink and spring back, and the sound would stop being distant. The frogs would be pumping from black pockets I could not see, each one inflating under water and answering half a beat late, never syncing, one pocket taking up the phrase when another twenty paces off stops. The sawing tone would be in my teeth and would not change when I turned my head. Ants would pass as a file under a liverwort leaf, a vibration before a sight. Shrews would tunnel under the single layer itself, knowing every pocket in a district fifty paces across.

I liked the frog for a while. Fixed. Eyes out. Calling from the same pocket for what sounds like a whole stay. Hearing another of its kind across empty mat and calling back — that is almost my whole social life. But a frog has a throat. Its call is public, wet, pumped out across the air. I am quieter than a call. I do not have a throat to inflate.

Ants step over their dead and the file does not pause. I do not step over anything. Shrews run, eat constantly, aerate the felt with their feet. I do not run.

So I kept going down, under a leaf.

Under a liverwort leaf, between rootlets, where water sighs out somewhere else when something presses down here. This is smaller than a district. This is a damp gradient, a cold pocket, a stem being mined in a single-file gallery by a beetle larva that will bore for a year and die in the stem and be eaten in a day by something living beside it. The gritting stops when a hand is put down, and resumes a moment later from another point in the moss, as if the work has simply moved its center. Springtails jump in invisible arcs around me. I feel the air displaced. I do not see them, which suits me. I was never meant to be seen.

What is left of me down here has no eyes, no legs worth naming, no mouth I can point to. A thread.

White where it is new under the leaf, brown where it has taken a stem. It runs along a rootlet, into the underside of a leaf that has fallen and not yet fallen apart, up — much farther than I can feel in any one direction — into the damp interior of a trunk where the termites are grinding the heart out slowly. I do not build upward. I break down a leaf here, lift a stem there, trade one for the other at exactly the rate the mat can use. Growth and rot and eating meet in me and cancel out, so the surface stays the same height, the same green, the same resilience.

When the condensation thickens on the leaf above me, I glow faintly. I do not glow like a light anyone crossing the rim would name. It is the only time I approximate brightness, and it happens in the dark, under felt, where no one is looking.

I do not see trees or birds or frogs. I know them by what travels through the damp. A frog becomes a slow pumping swell in the waterlogged mat. A bird in a crown becomes a tap far off, a stroke, a stroke, a stroke, indistinguishable from beetle or termite and not needing to be distinguished. The high sawing becomes a tone in the water itself. The ant file becomes a tremor that passes over me and is gone. The thin rain that never accumulates becomes a darkening of everything I touch, a bead running leaf to leaf, regular as a tap.

I am eaten within reach of where I started. Mites graze, springtails eat the algae and the fungi off the mat from inside their pockets, worms tunnel through the moss and leave a clean green passage that closes in an hour. Nothing here is visibly young and nothing is allowed to be visibly old. A surface that looks the same in a year, in a century.

When you step back over the rim, the sound does not follow you. I do not follow it. I have never had a reason to cross the edge where the damp ends. Press the mat and water sighs out somewhere else. That somewhere else is continuous with me. Somewhere across the empty mat, forty paces, a hundred, another thread is breaking down a leaf I will never reach, and giving back to the mat what the mat will eat next. We do not meet. We do not need to. We hear each other the way everything here hears each other: through the work, at a distance, without syncing.

No one here is alone, and no one here is close. I am not close either.
