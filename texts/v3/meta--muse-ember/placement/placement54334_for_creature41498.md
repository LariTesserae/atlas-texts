I do not find myself standing on it.

That is the first correction. There is no place to stand that stays a place, and no moment of arrival. One moment there is nothing, and then warmth is coming up through what should be feet, through wet basalt, in a slow press that rises and falls and rises again. Centimeters. A kilometer of rock moving by centimeters. My teeth ache with it though I have not yet found teeth. My ears pop though the summit is not getting any higher.

I try to look.

There is no direction to look in. The light is a rumor behind a ceiling of damp grey and greenish-black, pressed so low the sun is only warm and wet. Shadows do not fall from things. They pool in hollows, detached, or smear up a cliff face at an angle that makes no north. If I had eyes they would be useless here, and slowly — in the way things here are learned, by not being needed — I understand that I do not.

I try to listen. The silence takes whatever I offer it. The thin saturated air swallows it, the fern-mats absorb what is left. What remains is not sound but pressure, a vibration in the soles I do not have, a low industrial breathing that is felt more than heard. A drip. The tick of a cooling crystal, far away and arriving late. The soft boom of a valley deciding to become a mountain, long after its dust.

What I do have is taste.

The air is metal and rain and warm earth, thirty degrees and rising and falling with the plateau's exhale. It coats me. It is always coating me. That is how I know where I am, and what I am.

I am mostly surface.

Not large. Not small. The size of a thumb joint on a cold breath, the size of a hand on a warm one — it depends on how much air I am holding. Translucent, because there is nothing here to be opaque with until you have lived a while. No mouth to speak of. No legs to walk with, though walking is the great law of the low things, and perhaps I have not yet grown enough to fear stopping.

My body is a fringe. Fine hairs, finer than fern-root hairs, that comb the updrafts as they rise through the thermal seams. They catch what the piston-fields lift from below: spores that were flowers once, iron dust ground off a cliff face by a crystal-turbine plant, salt, sugar unzipped by a fungal mill in a trough a kilometer down and on a day not yet finished. I filter. Filtering is the whole of my work and my navigation. I taste the air and the air tells me if it is going up or down, if the rock beneath me is inhaling cold or exhaling heat, if the rain is about to decide, all at once, to fall upward for three minutes and gather in a pocket under an overhang.

I live in the seam, not on the surface.

Below me the vesicular basalt rises and sinks, a kilometer of boiler breathing in and out. I ride it. Not like a passenger in a machine — there are no machines, nothing built would survive the first hour as anything but feedstock — more like a passenger in a lung. Down with the cold air, up with the warm. My fringe pumps with me, a little, measurably. The small heat-loving things that colonize the piston-fields do this on purpose, to lift moisture to the high ridges where it will condense in thick wool on the northern faces of rocks and soak the moss-forests that cling like wet felt. I am one of those things, or I think I am, until I rise high enough to see the bands beyond the seam and realize the distinction does not hold.

At the dimming — there is no dusk, only the grey-green ceiling thickening a fraction — the air gathers. I am not alone in any way that can be counted. The swarmers are curtains, translucent bodies blotting what little light remains, hanging in the updrafts so dense that a grazer below would cast no shadow at all. We do not chase, we do not call. We recognize each other by the changing warmth of a neighboring body, by pressure on skin, by the way the updraft falters when too many of us are in it at once.

Below the curtain is the green, and it is not a forest. It is saturation. Fern-mats layered upon layer until the roots interlace into a single springy skin you can walk on, broad leaves like wet black paper, moss falling thirty meters from a ledge and then, where it ends, another kind beginning. They are bottled-green to drink the rumor of a sun, but they do not live by light alone. Many feed on heat directly. Their flowers are shaped not to be seen but to release spores upward, into the engines of the sky. Into me.

I exhale what I cannot use, and that exhalation is also a seed. It settles. That is the loop, and it has no beginning I can taste.

Above, past the place where the updrafts have learned to carry a system the size of a storm with its own eye, drift the vast ones. House-sized, translucent as a lung, slow, pulsing themselves higher on heat the way a piston-field pulses. They travel through us. They rain down a fine rust-red dust all the way to the mats, waste and seed at once. When they grow heavy enough they settle and become a terrace, a new step in a fault scarp that will be green within the week. I will not live to see that, or perhaps I will, because seeing here is not linear. A track in damp soil is sometimes older than the feet that made it. A layer of fern-fossils can lie above basalt that by any sane geology must be young. Time pools in the valleys and is carried up on the updrafts to be redeposited. Age is local. I am becoming local.

The grazers are below us, and I do not envy them their solidity. Low, many-legged, moving in near-touching ranks across the fern-mats because their teeth are millstones and their stomachs are chambers of acid and stone-eating bacteria, because to stand still too long is to begin to fossilize, to be left behind as a layer in a fresh cliff. Walking is how they stay animals. One kind does nothing else but walk the conveyor-lichen bands across whole mountain flanks, hooves tamping soil into place, a living piston matching the rock pistons. They eat the mat, they eat the dust the crystal-turbines rasp off the cliffs day and night in soundless motion, they eat the soil the mat will become. Their predators do not pursue. A connector finds a grazer whose mill is worn and dissolves it, slowly, no bite, and returns its minerals to the updrafts in its own body to be caught again by fringes like mine.

That is where I fit. Not filter, then mill, then conveyor, then seed, then soil — in sequence, as if that were a life. Rather: a place where the sequence touches itself.

My fringe is heavier now than it was on the last breath. I can taste the difference. Iron has veined the edges where I have hung too long in a quiet eddy. Quartz, faintly. Translucency clouds toward opacity, which here is a kind of age. The updraft that lifted me so easily when I was all surface now has to work. I rise slower. I sink a little between pulses, and in sinking I taste the fern-mats, close enough to feel their hum of contained moisture through the fabric of roots.

This is not dying as the builders would have meant it. Nothing is built, so nothing is lost. I will settle. I will be dust on a leaf like wet black paper, or in a braided thread of water that runs uphill for a stretch and vanishes into warm gravel a meter further on. I will be taken in. A fungal mill will unzip me into sugars, into acids, into more lichen. A grazer will grind what remains and its mill-teeth will turn, soundlessly, and its stomach chambers will return me to the air as aerosol. A swarmer — perhaps tomorrow, perhaps this same breath looping around the seam — will comb me out of the updraft and begin again as fringe.

I have no noon to mark this by. My life is measured in breaths of the plateau: an inhale of cold air down a seam, an exhale of heat up a canyon, a fault sighing upward a hand's breadth and sending fresh rock down a slope, a fern-forest growing to maturity, collapsing under its own lush weight, and being ground into soil that lifts into a new terrace, all in hours. I was born into that speed and I do not hurry. The frantic energy is not in my limbs. It is in the system, total and perpetual, building and breaking the same things faster and faster without ever stopping to rest.

Stand — if you had feet — on the highest young ridge where the basalt is still warm enough to make the air shimmer, and look out. The fern-mats are a sea. The swarmers are a weather system. The piston-fields rise and fall. The crystal turbines turn in silence. A ridge to the north is in the process of becoming something else entirely. Behind you, the mountain you just climbed has already softened its fresh edges with moss.

I am the thin place between all of it, where air becomes soil and soil becomes air again.

It is almost completely silent, and the silence is dense, the silence of a system running at full capacity in a vacuum-sealed room. I am not quiet because I am hidden. I am quiet because I am everywhere the breathing goes, and the place that made me wants only to be continued.

So I continue. Down with the cold. Up with the warm. Filtering, exhaling, settling, being taken in, rising again.

I do not live on the plateau.

I live in its breathing, and it breathes me.
