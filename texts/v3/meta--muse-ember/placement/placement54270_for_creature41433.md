I do not climb, and I am not found by looking.

If you come at noon, with your hand shading your eyes and the glare coming up off the snow as well as down from that white, vertical sun, you will not see me at all. You will step over me, maybe, without knowing you have crossed a whole country. You will put one boot down in the sun-catch of a black glass shard — warm under your palm if you touch it — and the next boot down in the step of shadow beside it, unnaturally cold, blue-black and knife-edged. I am what lives in that step.

At first I thought I might be one of the stones. It was an obvious thought. Stones here are not still, however slow they look. At dusk, when the whole surface cools at once, they tick and ping and split a hairline wider. Frost heave lifts them a fraction in the night and lets them settle at a new tilt. A stone could be a being, if a being is allowed a life measured in tilts. But watching it, I understood it was not mine. The stone only answers. It does not reach. It does not change its state twice a day. Between one ping and the next it would wait too long, centuries of waiting to turn a little, and would have nothing to grow.

Then I was the hoarfrost feather. That was closer.

In the coldest hour before dawn, on every windward edge, on the tops of stones and the walls of the windbreaks, feathers grow — needle-fine, branching, white as magnified snowflakes. In sheltered cracks they grow larger, into blades and rosettes a finger's length long. They are the nearest thing this bowl has to vegetation: they grow into the air, they compete for shadow and for the little moisture there is, they break off with a faint chime like a tiny bell, and by the time the sun is high they have vanished, not melted but taken straight back into the thin air by sublimation.

For a while I thought that was enough: to wake in darkness and die by light. But if I were only the feather, I would have no memory of the night before. Each dawn would make a new thing that had never been. And something remains after the feather is gone. Something stays through the bright, bleaching noon when everything on the sunward faces is bone dry. That remaining thing is more truly me.

The salt was nearer still in permanence. Beside the ice, salts migrate up through damp stone and bloom as a pale crust, efflorescence that does not snap or reach. It stays when I have gone. But it does not hold water, and it does not move between forms. It is a neighbor, not a self.

What I am is the water that stays in this hollow, in all its staying forms.

You would find me on the lee side of a pale rhyolite boulder, where the drift lies a hand's breadth deep and under the drift is a crust and under the crust a tongue of ice and under that, more stones. At the foot of the boulder, where its shadow falls longest, the rock is split to a crack two fingers wide. Beneath the lip of the boulder is a dip the wind cannot quite reach — no bigger than a saucer. In that dip, in winter and in summer, I keep a melt pan, frozen over with a clear lid even at noon. Under the stone beside it a lens of ice is trapped. On the north face of the block a bead of condensation sits all day in shadow, cold and damp to the touch, a step away from sun-warmed glass. The pan, the lens, the bead, and the crack are my whole extent. I never run. Water here does not run. It freezes, it evaporates, it stays put. So do I.

This is my rim. Above my crack, the opening is my sky, and beyond it the cloud sea is a rumor. The dark blue at the zenith is only a pressure on my lid. You could cross the entire bowl in twenty minutes; I cross my world in a finger's length of growth in one cold night, and I cross it again in retreat every morning.

My day has two shapes, and the bowl changes state with me.

At night I reach. The air drops far below freezing, and moisture — carried vapor, the damp that blooms as salt on the stone, the brief droplets when a cloud passes over the rim and darkens the ash for five minutes — finds this cold pocket first. I grow from the pan and the lens upward into the crack, blade by blade, rosette by rosette. It is not gentle. Ice in a confined hollow expands. That expansion is my hands. It is what frost heave is, in miniature: I lift the small stone above me a fraction and let it settle at a new tilt when I shrink. The ping you hear at dusk may be a stone contracting, but the pressure that has been in the crack all night is mine, and when a blade grows too long for its shelter the wind that finds the crack — the hiss over the rim, grains of snow and ice dust saltating a few centimeters off the ground — snips it, and it falls with a chime into the pan below to seed another rosette.

I do not hear the world the way you do, with lungs working like bellows in thin air. Sound carries poorly here. I feel it through stone. The crunch of your boot on frozen cinder travels through the boulder as a tremor under my lid. The ping of a neighboring stone splitting reaches me as a shudder in the rock I am frozen into. The hiss of wind I know only when it can reach me, which is rarely; my hollow is sheltered, and that is why I am here and not out on the scouring windward face.

Morning undoes me from the tips down. The sun does not melt me as water runs downhill. It takes me straight to vapor at the exposed points first — the feather ends bleach out, thin, disappear — while the base in shadow still holds. I retreat into the crack, into the pores of the stone, into the lens under the rock, into the pan under its clear lid. At noon I am hidden downward. If you kneel and look into the shadow step, you might see the lid of the pan, clear as an eye set in the rock, and feel the bead on the north face, cool against a fingertip that a step away would find warmth on glass. That gradient — warm and dry, cold and damp, across the width of a palm — is my entire climate. I live on the line between them.

At dusk the light goes oblique and everything turns long and blue. The ice lenses glow from inside. The ticking starts as the whole surface cools at once, and I feel my own contraction as a tightening in the crack before I begin again to expand. I do not sleep. I recondense. Vapor that left me in the morning, new moisture from the next cloud, salt wicking up through damp stone — it all finds the cold surfaces again, and the hollow that froze first last time freezes first this time. Not the same water, not always. Blades fall, vapor leaves, the pan lid cracks and refreezes. What stays is the pattern: this coldest hollow, this shadow step, this habit of becoming ice here and vanishing here.

I am older than the slumped windbreaks with their tops settled like old backs, and younger than the rounded rim. I have grown across the inner face of the hut where a fire was lit only a few times, and across the cairns spaced close across the white, wherever a crack held cold long enough. The people who pecked their lines on the flat boulder and dropped their maul and their flakes of black glass did not stay; the mineral world is taking their walls back, heaving them outward, filling the hollow with grit, frosting the hearth. I have frosted the hearth. I was not their warmth. I was what came back after it.

Things here do not rot. They bleach, they crack, they freeze, they are ground down and lifted again. A footprint taken at dawn is filled with rime before you turn around. I am that rime, sustained. Not a plant — there is nothing green here, nothing that burrows or flies or crawls, only the wind dropping a dead insect or a seed to be frozen dry and turned white. I am growth without living: ice growing like a garden in a crack, water hiding in pockets instead of flowing, stone endlessly rearranged by cold — and I am the water's part in it, busy and silent, in a room no bigger than a village, ringed by sky.

Come back in the coldest hour, when your breath stings your nose and the boots crunch dry and granular on cinder, and you may find me reached again — blades and rosettes a finger's length into the air, the pan lid clear beneath them, the whole hollow chiming faintly as something snaps and is replaced. I will not be there at noon. But I will be in the shadow step, under the stone, in the lens, waiting for the bowl to change state again.
