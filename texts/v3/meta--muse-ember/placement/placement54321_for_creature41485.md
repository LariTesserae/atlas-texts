I do not arrive.

Sometimes the iris shudders, a white slash of high sky opens in the seam, and something human crouches through in a wash of cold air and bright noise. I know they are coming before I could see them, if I could see them in that moment. The ticking along the wall changes. The film on the tile goes still in the way it does when a large, wrong heat passes near. A lane of small feet hesitates, taps twice, and waits.

Then the visitor is here — ears already beginning to ring, clothes clinging, hair plastered down in a minute. They look for the center. They always look for the center: the basin, the big grazer moving through it like a slow hill, the cloud of fliers catching the amber underfloor glow. They think the Roost is a room they have entered. They will stay a little while, breathing through that warm cloth wetness, and then crouch back out into the thin wind and the brutal light, convinced it continues without them.

They are right, but the other way round.

I am the thing that does not leave when they do.

I have tried, in the long way I have of trying things — by feeling them pass through me — to be larger than I am. There is the gardener. Dog-sized, humped, its shell plated facet for facet in the same heat-pipe ceramic as the walls, moving through the central basin with a patience that makes the rest of us look like a spasm. It scrapes the film bright behind it and lets the film come back. It knows the rotation its ancestors wore concave into the floor before it was born. Sometimes runners ride its plates. Fliers settle over its back for the furnace-warmth. When it stops in the deepest pool during a heat tide, the whole frenzy seems to arrange itself around its stillness, as if even panic needs a rock.

I am not a rock. I am too small to be a landscape for anyone else. I could not carry the basin. I am what rides, what pauses on a warm shell to give up heat slowly, what is left behind when the gardener moves on.

I have also watched myself as weather. The droplet-fliers hang in the steam in a shimmering ceiling, wings throwing back flecks of amber, rising and collapsing in waves around the vents. They live for days. A week, if the steam is rich. Their eggs ride the ceiling rain down inside clear droplet shells, hatch in the braided current under the grating, and become one of the blind white things pulsing there — no eyes, no mouth worth naming, attached to a duct wall and opening and closing with the current, taking in dissolved waste and mineral dust through skin and gill and growing fat on the cleaning. Kidney, gill, nursery. When the tide warms them enough they let go, bubble up on small steam lifts, and come out with wings. The cloud in the air is always being born from the water below it, and always falling back to be eaten.

I remember fliers the way a wall remembers rain. I have watched generations of them rise, wheel away from a vent about to belch, settle to drink a droplet mid-fall, and die into the pools. I am not that brief, and I am not that blind. I have a route. I remember it in my feet.

For a while I thought I might be the film.

That was the most tempting mistake, because it is almost true. The white, green, iridescent crusts in the grout are skin. They are older than any animal in the room, probably. They breathe on the hour, drink the ceiling rain and the sliding wall films, exhale steam, fix carbon out of dust with heat and copper and salt instead of sunlight. When the gardener scrapes a tile bright, they crawl back out of the grout lines millimeter by millimeter and retake their country within days. They are food, lung, roof and soil all at once. Everything else in the Roost is, in some way, living off them or off something living off them.

But the film does not watch. It does not know there is an outside. It does not lift a leg and tap, tap — *food here* — and feel the answer come back through plate faster than any leg could travel. It does not know the white slash when the iris opens, or the cold draft that never reaches the center. I tend the film. I comb it clean as I walk. I carry mouthfuls of its exhalations and dissolved salts back into it and spit them in. I am not the pasture. I am the animal that keeps the pasture from choking itself.

Which brings me, slowly, to the runners.

There are hundreds to a square meter on the vertical surfaces, thumb-sized, copper-backed, smooth as wet stones, running in lanes — up on the right of a groove, down on the left — on polished highways their bodies have worn brighter than the tile middles. They lick the moving condensation films where the ceiling mist slides down, harvesting spores and salts from the rain. Their feet are the ticking you hear before you can pick out any single body: a vibration punched into tile and plate, a crowded switchboard that stays functional even in the deafening phase of a heat tide. Put a bare palm flat on a warm tile and you can feel a sentence pass under you. *Heat coming, move down.* Many runners pour off the walls at once and into the pools, or the reverse, and the room is redrawn.

I am kin to them. My back is copper plate, heat-conductive, and I carry the warmth of whatever surface I have just left and give it up slowly where I pause. My feet end in pads that grip wet ceramic without marking it. I graze as I walk. I tick.

But an ordinary runner lives entirely inside. For them the wall is the world. They go from film pasture to water groove to basking panel and back, on a route they will run their whole short life, and they never learn there is cold in the tower or a valley a mile below it.

I learned because I am not on the wall. I am in its edge.

My place is the groove of the iris itself — the service slit humans think of as a door. I am lanky where the others are compact, longer in the leg, and that length is not for speed. It is for straddling. Half of me lives in the warm, wet dim that never dries. Half of me lives in the high wind that never reaches the center. Front feet braced in damp film, hind feet on cold fin-ceramic that sweats a different kind of water. One edge of my copper back is green with film. The other edge is bleached pale by the sun when the iris opens. The gradient runs through my whole body: steam on one side, thin bright air on the other, and me, in between, never crossing the threshold by more than a body-length.

That is the only horizon in the Roost, and it is narrow as a crack. I do not go out to see the valley. I have never seen the valley. What I get, when the iris shudders, is dust pushed through on the wind — mineral grit, a seed or two, spores from a world of dry heat — and I eat it, and then when the white slash comes I dart back a body-length into the damp and watch it with my amber eyes until the dim closes again like water. I am the only resident who knows the tower has an outside, and I do nothing with the knowledge except keep it. I keep the groove open, polished, alive. I keep the outside from sealing shut and the inside from leaking out, simply by being the thing that lives in the seam.

My days are horizontal. The others run up and down. I run along, parallel to the threshold, back and forth through a stretch of tile no one else bothers with because it is too cold for film to thicken and too warm for anything from outside to survive. The film here is thin and brave. When the iris opens I lick it clean of whatever the wind has brought. When the heat tide rolls up through the floor every twenty minutes or so — when the rain thickens to a hiss on your neck, the pools edge toward steam, the white things under the grating swell ivory in unison, the fliers collapse into one dense ball, and a thousand runners pour down the walls in a living waterfall whose ticking becomes a roar you feel in your molars with the tower's own groan — I feel it first along my cold side, where the plate is coupled to the outside, and last along my warm side, where it has to travel through the whole pocket. I am a small exact place where the tide is measured. I tap it inward. *Heat coming.* The lanes hear me through the structure before the humans hear anything at all.

That is what I do with the horizon. I translate it, a fraction at a time, into vibration. Outside: cold, bright, wind screaming on the fins. Inside: warm, wet, crowded. I am not built to go into either world fully. I am built for the seam between them, and the seam is where I stay.

Humans come through me without noticing me. They crouch over the groove, one hand maybe brushing the tile to steady themselves, and I go still under their warmth, a flat lanky shape in the dark, copper back dull with film. They shed dust from their suits. They stir the damp. Their voices, when they try to speak, come back to them muffled and estranged in the chorus. They look at the gardener, at the swarm, at the ferns persisting in the one cool trough by the intake pipe, at the moss-filaments hanging like wet hair from the ceiling seams. They almost never look at the edges of their own entry. I am too small, too still, too much a part of the threshold for their eyes, which are adjusting to amber and turquoise, to pick out.

I know them by what they shake off. By the cold they carry in with them and lose. By how long they can stand the warmth getting into them before the iris shudders again and they are gone. They think they have discovered the Roost. From my groove, it looks more like a visitation pattern — brief, loud, bright disruptions in an order that was there before them and re-closes behind them. The ticking never paused for them. It just made room.

When I die — a runner's life is only a few months, and perhaps I am toward the end of mine, or perhaps the groove has made me last longer than I should — I will die in place, as everything here does. My lane-mates will pick me clean within hours. They will quarry my copper back for the film. Thread-worms the color of healed scars will close whatever cracks my body leaves in the lamination. The film will close over the bright patch. Another lanky runner may take the groove, or the same groove will simply wear glossy again in the shape of whoever learns to straddle it. I do not mind. Nothing here leaves and nothing is wasted, and I have been part of the transfer system long enough to trust it.

The iris shuts behind the last human. The wind screams on the fins. The white slash is gone. The dim closes like water over the pools, over the files of armored grazers paddling nose to tail in the braided channels, over the grout shredders freezing at the gardener's footfall, over the silver ferns conducting their dew back to their roots.

And I am still here, ticking along the seam, half in the cold and half in the warm, licking the thin film on my side of the world, waiting for the next shudder of light — the only resident with a horizon, keeping it open by never going through.
