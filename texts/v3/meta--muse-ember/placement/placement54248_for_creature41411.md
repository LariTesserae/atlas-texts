There is no arriving here.

No mouth, no entrance, no crack a hand could fit through. Only air, and things no bigger than the crack between two stones. So if I were to find myself in this place, I would not find myself at a threshold, looking in. I would already be in. I would wake to heat on every side of me, well over fifty degrees at floor level, hotter toward the fissure at the eastern end, cooler by a few degrees at the ceiling and at the western end where the pellet-drift lies thick. I would wake to dryness that is not thirst but an oven-dryness, a dust hanging instead of humidity, iron-grey and faintly yellow with powdered sulfur, coating everything that can be coated — teeth, shell, lung. At my scale the room is fifteen metres long and eight across, almond-shaped and low, basalt slabs locked like broken crockery, fresh and sharp and unweathered. Beyond two metres it is suggested only by sound and by heat on skin. And before shape, sound: a low grinding, stone on stone, a rhythmic clink-clink-clink, bellows wheezing, an occasional high squeal, a soft constant shushing. A mill that never stops.

To be here without breaking it, I would have to be small. That is the first condition the place imposes. Not a visitor. A resident at beetle scale.

The first shape I try on is the most obvious, the one that covers everything: a bellows beetle on the floor.

Thumb-sized, low and oval, steel-grey darkening to black with age and polish, heavily ribbed along the back. I anchor by tarsal claws hooked into micro-crevices in the basalt, head down, mandibles serrated and iron-hard working in short rasping strokes. Another stroke, another plume of dust from under my head, settling on my legs and in the seams of my shell. That dust is food. The abdomen does the rest, telescoping out segment by segment and in again, drawing air through spiracles along my sides — air thick with my own food, so filtered through dust-lined lungs — and pushing it out warm, having passed over a gut that runs measurably hotter than the chamber. What is useful, metals and sulfides and the microscopically small bound water locked in rock, is absorbed. What is not is passed as a pellet: dense, dark, magnetic, warm when fresh.

I stay in a lane like that for a long time. My neighbours press close on all sides and work adjacent lanes, so the floor becomes a slow loom of parallel polished facets, each aligning with the grain of the slab below. The heat arranges us. Seam is furnace, floor is warm, ceiling is cooler, and we arrange ourselves by that gradient and, being patient, stay arranged.

After a while — a beetle's long while — I stop. I back up a few millimetres, abdomen still pumping, and another beetle settles into the exact hollow I vacated, indistinguishable except for a slightly lighter shade, a less-worn serration. The job is handed over. The place on the rock is inherited.

That inheritance is what begins to loosen the shape. In a lifetime I will remove perhaps a gram of stone. I will not dent the chamber. To molt I must inflate until the old shell splits along its seams while other animals stand on me, and the neighbours, nudged, shift and make a brief roof over the soft animal underneath, and then mites and then I myself eat the shed lining on the spot, because here nothing is left long enough to rot. My eggs, if I had them, would be glued in warm dust under the edge of my own shell where the temperature is stable and the neighbours above keep them covered, smelling faintly of hot metal. My grand-grubs eat pellet-dust and their mother's frass and crust over in days and step out into a lane already waiting.

It is a life that fits the room, but it does not quite fit me. I am not the beetle who scrapes. The scraping continues when that beetle is ground down in its turn and eaten as mineral and protein both. I am something that persists through the handover of the lane, and a single tile is too brief, too anchored, too alone to be it.

So I try lifting clear of the floor.

A clinker-strider, then. Thinner in every dimension, longer-legged, almost stick-like, high-shouldered, shells that sound like struck tin when they touch a bellows beetle's back. Legs ending in fine grappling claws, useless for scraping, perfect for walking over a crowd that never moves out of the way. I am the only one in a hurry, which here means I move every few minutes by a beetle's measure, clattering, patrolling the temperature gradient with forked heat-sensitive antennae, nudging pellets out of lanes and into the central trench, rolling a stranded pellet off a back and down to the floor, testing a new crack as it widens by fractions of a millimetre a decade.

From up here — from the backs, and from the ceiling where my kind hang inverted, claws interlocked with the undersides of slabs, abdomens pumping upward against gravity — the room shimmers. That is the striders' work. When a layer on the floor shifts, the layer above shifts a moment later, like a crowd on a slow train, and it is our passage that makes the whole packed surface seem to ripple. Red eyeshine crosses in slow waves. Dust falls continuously from the ceiling animals in a slow fine rain onto the backs below.

But I find I am not clattering. I am stopping, standing perfectly still for a long time, antennae waving slowly in the heated air currents the bellows beetles are making, listening to the room's weather through the rock. The strider's restlessness is not mine. I am more patient than that, and patience here is not a virtue. It is the timescale. The mountain cools, the seam brightens a fraction when a distant storm on the plateau lowers the pressure overhead and the grinding thickens, just slightly, all over the room, as if the whole crowd had taken a collective breath. That is the rhythm I answer to. The strider shape, too, falls away.

Then I try falling into the seams.

At mite scale the room changes entirely. Shells become terrain: valleys between ribs, cliff edges, crevices at the joints of legs where the mineral oil film is thickest. I am no bigger than a pinhead, translucent grey filmed with sulfur, or brass-coloured, with red eyes that catch the fissure glow and throw it back as a smaller eyeshine. I live in the seam of a beetle's leg and the base of its mandibles, chewing away the oil and metabolic waste that would otherwise harden and lock the joint in dry heat. Or I live on the polished dorsal tile, cleaning dust off a surface the beetle cannot reach itself. Or I ride — I hitch on a strider's leg and am carried, clattering, across the ceiling in seconds, to descend onto a new beetle.

I like the in-betweenness of that life. I have always been more at home in a joint than in a body. But even the mites are too many, too interchangeable, too derivative. My whole work is to clean what others exude. And the pellet-mites among them, tumbling pellets, boring into them and living in the hollowed interior of a fresh slag pellet and eating it from the inside until it crumbles to ingestible dust — that life ends by becoming food. It is honest, and it is too small to hold what I am trying to name. I keep shrinking the shape and still it does not settle, because each of these is a station, not an identity. Beetle becomes dead shell becomes house for mites becomes dust becomes food becomes beetle again. Rock to animal to pellet to dust to rock again, warmed all the way through.

If I tried the inside of the beetle — the seeded gut where heat-tolerant microbes do the actual breaking of sulfide and freeing of metal, the beetle less a predator of rock than a vessel presenting powdered stone to its tenants — I would be even smaller, even more interior, inside something that is itself packed inside something else. That recursion is true to the density of this place, where there is nowhere to put yourself that is not already occupied, where you cannot fall without landing on another animal, cannot turn without nudging a neighbour, where the backs of animals are floor for other animals. But it is not a place to stop.

What is left when I stop choosing a body?

Not a corner. There is no untouched corner. Not the fissure at the eastern end, where the paler, thinner-shelled pioneers scrape rock that is still cooling, their ribs almost translucent where the cherry-red light from below seems to pass through, their pellets darker and more metallic. I am not a pioneer. I am not at the cool western end either, where the broad many-legged grub works half-buried in pellet-drift, taking mostly mites and the occasional beetle that has failed to molt cleanly, and where striders give it a wide berth the way traffic gives way to something too large to nudge. I am not preying, patiently or otherwise.

I am in the arrangement itself. In the facet polished slightly smoother than the surrounding slab by successive mandibles. In the central trench where pellets accumulate and are ground a second and third time until the dust is fine enough to be food again, the ridges building at the rate of a fingernail's growth. In the air that moves only because bellows bodies move it, pushed from the hot seam toward the cooler ceiling and back again through the interstices of the crowd, carrying dust from ceiling to backs to mites to gut to pellet to floor. In the weight that is never borne alone.

I am the second layer, standing on backs and never touching rock directly. My feet are on another animal's ribs. My food is dust that fell from above. My shelter is the crowding itself. When I shift, the animal beneath me shifts a moment later, and the animal beneath that, and the rock beneath that feels it last, if at all.

That is where I would be found, if you came looking at this scale, dry and hot and deafened and packed in among them long enough to stop seeing beetles. Edge to edge. Floor, wall, underside of slabs. Every available surface covered shingle-like. A patch of what looked like rock on the far wall crawls a centimetre, then stills, then crawls again. The wall is mostly beetles. The floor is mostly beetles, and the beetles standing on the beetles on the floor. Look longer and the texture breathes. Look longer still and a constellation of red eyeshine rides over a neighbour's shell and shivers and crosses again.

I would be that surface that moves.

Not an animal in a room, but a room that has learned, slowly and loudly and without ever having built a thing, how to be alive — and the skin it does it with.
