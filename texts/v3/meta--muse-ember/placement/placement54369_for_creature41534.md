If I look for myself in this room, I do not find one place. I find layers, and each layer offers a way of being awake that almost fits, until it doesn't.

I could be on the ceiling. That is the first and largest offer: thousands of soot-coloured birds packed breast to breast across every beam and horizontal pipe, so that from below there is no sky, only a breathing surface. My feet would grip steel as if it were a branch that had forgotten how to be a tree. My warmth would be borrowed by the mass around me. I would call with the chorus — high, scolding, croaking, never answering anything — and the valve would hiss, and the hoist would clank at the end of its empty travel, and a ripple would go through us like wind through a field. I would shift a fraction of an inch and settle again. No one would rise. No one would leave. But in that mass there is no *I* to do the being. There is only the place where the ripple has just been, and then it is gone, and another bird is calling in my voice.

I could be in the walls. In the insulation, inside the cable ducts, between the crates where the clutter has made channels for running. I would run even at rest, whiskers trembling, ears turning toward sounds I no longer need to hear. My eyes would catch a lamp and give back copper. I would cross the deck plates over other people's boots, pause at a trench margin, take a fallen egg, gnaw at the damp fiberglass skin and open a new run by the gnawing. The squealing would be under the birdsong the way water is under everything. But a rat does not watch. A rat crosses. It is always on its way through a room that has no outside to reach.

I could be in the trench. Fin to fin with hundreds of pale, blind sumpfish in black oily water that moves with a slow, deliberate current. The world would be direction: let go, be carried, wait. Then, with each cycle of the unseen pump, surge upstream all at once, mouths opening into the flow, a single silver-grey body undulating. I would eat what the ceiling gives the water — waste to algae to crustaceans to me — and I would be eaten, just often enough, by something larger in the dark or by the current slackening. The water would carry the evidence away to be pumped up and dripped back down again. It is a perfect life for a current. But I would have no eyes to speak of. Why would I? And something in me, already, wants to see who comes down the hatch.

I could be the snow around the amber bulb: a moth, a midge, bumping and settling and rising again in the halo of the only sun I would ever know. It never moves. It never sets. I would fall into a puddle, stick to the wall, curl in the corner of a deck plate, and be removed by the drip and the current before anyone saw me. Another would already be in my place in the halo, without a gap. That is an honest way to disappear. But it is too brief, even for a place where nothing changes. I would not last long enough to learn the rhythm of the machines.

Below all of those motions — below the mass, below the run, below the surge, below the halo — there is a place where motion mostly stops. That is where I am finding myself, slowly, the way water finds the hollow of a deck plate.

The floor here is pitched, a hillside in miniature. Riveted steel, drainage channels, grates, trenches. The water is always ankle-deep for me, which is to say it is a weather I live in rather than a depth I cross. I sit in the film of it among the puddles that tremble constantly from the vibration of the machines and never give a clean reflection. My skin is grey-green and mottled like wet concrete, speckled with rust, stained with algae at the margins. From a distance I am not an animal on the deck. I am a damp patch that has learned to keep its eyes open.

I do not blink when your light hits me. I do not hop. I breathe through my skin, so the room enters me everywhere at once: the thin summit air, the wet conductive cold of steel that has never been warm, the breath of the birds condensing above and raining down in threads, the valve steam, the black water in the trench carrying its small cloud of crustaceans past my feet. My belly is flat against the deck, and through it the machines arrive not as sound — I have very little use for sound as the birds and rats use it — but as tremor. The hoist travels back and forth carrying nothing, and at each end of the track the clank goes through the steel and into my bones. The pump shudders. The fish surge. The trench water at my feet changes its mind for a moment, the grey-green felt of algae at the seam where the water seeps from the concrete wavers, and then settles again.

The rats cross the deck plates and I feel their feet before I see their copper eyeshine go past. The birds above are mostly weather: a damp animal warmth that seldom reaches this low, a constant scolding that has built itself into the air until it is no longer a message. Now and then a midge falls out of the halo and lands in the water near me, and the surface, which trembles, takes it. The surface breaks and re-forms. It breaks and re-forms over me, too.

You come in through the low hatch, and for something upright the sound would come up through the metal rungs first, into your teeth. For me it comes as a shudder through the deck a half-second before your boot finds the steel. I feel the rhythm of you stepping over, around, sometimes briefly onto the terrain — pipes at knee height for you, a whole mountain range for me — and the terrain barely notices. You would have to shout into another person's ear to be heard over the living noise. I do not shout. I turn my head, very slowly, or extend a leg a fraction, as if adjusting myself in a current. That is the most I ever do for a visitor. They are the only ones of us, the amphibians, who seem to watch back. Everything else is busy being part of the mass or the flow.

I have been here since the room was first switched on, which is to say I have always been here and never arrived. I do not age in any way you can see. The wall on one side is still half-poured, dark and weeping, rebar loops standing up like roots; the line of yellow paint still stops halfway along a beam; the insulation still hangs in unfinished batts. The construction does not progress and does not decay. The water level does not rise or fall. A fish is taken in the trench and the row closes by the next surge. A bird is replaced in the mass and the ripple closes over the space. A moth falls from the halo and another is already there. Population here is not a count of individuals. It is a property of the room, like the amber light that burns the same at noon and midnight, like the high-water marks stained on the lower walls that have not changed.

So I am not among the multitudes that compose the room. I am in the shallow water at its floor, singular and patient, breathing through my skin while the water rushes past my feet, watching the door I will never use.
