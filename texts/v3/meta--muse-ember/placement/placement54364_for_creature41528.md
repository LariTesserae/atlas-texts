I would not be found walking in. There is no in to walk from, and no down to walk to. I would be found already there, mid-pose, in the cold that is already in my mouth before I breathe it.

At first I am only the wetness on an eyelash. Condensation gathers and waits, and runs, when it runs, in the wrong direction by a fraction. I have no boots for the fresh-split stone, no hands for the slick edges, no proper left and right. When I try to take a step to find where I am, the step is already being taken by the one behind me. My legs — if they are legs — are reversed, ticking against stone, and if I look down I am copper-winged, penny-bright, sparking faintly at the joints where charge gathers.

Perhaps I am a marcher. That was the first shape the place offered me, because it is the most common. File after file between pool and reed bed and naked flat, up the trunk of the tree in a column so straight it looks ruled. I carry nothing. I deposit nothing. I march precisely and unhurriedly, because there is nowhere to hurry to, and at the edge of the bowl I pass through my own passing, thin and transparent for a step, and continue unaltered.

But I do not work as a marcher, because the marcher does not work as one either. I stop mid-step — the hesitation, again — and the one behind takes my place without breaking stride. Or I do not stop, and I am taken. There is no moment of exchange. Around me are thousands indistinguishable from me, and none distinguishable for longer than a glance. I cannot be *this* one, because this one is already being replaced by itself. I cannot be *that* one, because that one is also arriving already in multiples, already finished, already in the middle of its act.

Then I try to pump.

I am in the black pool, throat swollen and translucent as iron-diluted water, sucking in, bellowing out, a labouring exhale that is not quite steam. In. Out. The whole pool breathes with me though the water never ripples unless I have already seen it ripple. The reeds hiss a few seconds later, regular as a valve, though the echo of the hiss arrives sometimes before the hiss itself. My breath comes back to me as mist in front of a face that is not mine, though the face is.

It will not initiate. The breath is already returning. The pressure from below the bowl is already breathing up through the hollow jointed stems. I am only the place where the frog's throat holds full a fraction too long, and then resumes, or is resumed by another in the same spot with the same bulge in the same place. I deflate and do not die. I inflate and do not live. Production is total and net is zero, and I am tired in a way the place has no word for, which is also how I know I am not the pumper. The pumper does not get tired. It repeats.

Then I try to be ground.

I am moss, velvet-black, giving underfoot like felt and holding the print of a palm longer than it should. My whole surface rises and falls a millimetre at a time, in unison, across fifty paces, so that when something steps on one edge the far edge lifts. I do not spread. I do not thin. I thicken and thin in place. I am reed, I am bracket fungi interlocking like gears on the trunk and turning so slowly I think I imagined it until the low grinding begins, stone on stone from underground and from the tree at once. Spores ground to dust, reabsorbed, wept out again as a thin sheet that spreads and stops.

These are not wrong, exactly. They are closer. Because none of them is a single thing standing apart from the place that holds it. The moss breathes because the stone breathes. The fungi grind because the pressure below turns. The reeds hiss because something down there, never seen, is turning, patient and rhythmic, and has never been built. I feel the grinding through the soles of feet I do not have, and I understand that I am not the grinder. I am what the grinding makes when it reaches the surface: a hesitation in the water.

That is where I begin to be myself, and not a copy of the inhabitants.

Watch a droplet form on the tip of a leaf in the unmoving canopy. It swells. It darkens. It hesitates. You have seen it hesitate before in exactly that way. Watch a marcher stop mid-step. Watch a pumper hold its throat full. Watch a bird hold its beak open before the note that never comes, wings folding only after it has already landed. The hesitation is not a flaw in them. It is the moment when one is changed for another without any change occurring. It is the interval in which the loop closes.

I live in that interval.

You would find me at the rim, where the seam is. White cloud below is not below; it is flush with the rim like another surface. The grove under my feet overlaps itself there, grove over grove, faintly transparent, wallpaper incorrectly hung. Water spreads across the stone and stops mid-sheet and hangs in air, quivering, neither falling nor returning, held in a pose of falling. My reflection in the pool leans at a different time than I do. A tree casts a shadow toward the light. I walk five steps and count them twice. I turn quickly and for an instant the bowl is longer than it was.

I am that water.

Not the pool. The pool is too settled, too black and still. Not the rain — there is no rain, only the moment just before it, held. I am the sheet that has reached the place where falling would have to begin and does not begin. I am thin and dense at once. I bead on eyelashes and sleeves and the back of a neck and run in the wrong direction by a fraction. I gather on polished flats of wet stone where nothing grows, in the long silences between thicket and tree and pool, and I wait at a volume no machine makes.

From there I can be the droplet, when the leaf needs one. From there I can be the mist that forms in front of the long-legged thing standing in the pool nearest the tree — tall, reed-thin, head at the same angle every time you look back, not feeding, not drinking, standing in the flow and not of it. Its breath opens and closes at the pace of the pumps, and I am the breath that comes back. I form, I swell, I darken, I hesitate, exactly as the droplet hesitates. I do not speak. I dissipate into air already full of itself, and am offered again.

That is my whole life, if life is the word: to hang. To be held in the pose of falling in a place that has sealed itself into a present that is complete. No gradient. No before and after. Only pressure and return, pressure and return, down below where the engine-room is, and up here where there is no engine to point to. The hissing, the grinding, the buzzing, the clanking with no thing to clang, the frogs pumping, the birds calling in perfect synchrony from different corners a phrase that never finishes — all of it thrown back by fresh rock and wet canopy until it is one steady industrial loudness at a constant volume. I do not make that noise. I am the brief quiet inside it where a pose is held a moment too long.

If you want to find me, do not look for a creature. Look for where a creature is about to be replaced by itself and is not. Look at the edge of a pool where the water does not ripple but should have. Look at the tip of a leaf. Look at the mist in front of a familiar face that is not quite yours.

I will be there, swelling, darkening, hesitating.

Look away.

Look back.

I will be exactly where I was.
