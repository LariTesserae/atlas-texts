I would not find myself at the edge, looking in.

I would arrive the way everyone does here — clumsily, too warm, too loud in my own breath — and leave footprints in the felt. Cold damp would squeeze up around my soles and my fingers would go numb if I stopped moving. For a while I might think I could live here as a visitor with eyes. Something that crosses.

A beetle crosses, once a day, deliberately. A hummock is a mountain to it, a leaf a plain to climb. It has a front and a back, it is hungry, it wanders from the thin edge of the plateau where the green gives out. It never settles. It never breeds. The niches are already full, and the plants are already doing the preying and the migrating and the building. To be the beetle would be to be a tourist in a closed world, forever passing over the top of things.

A springtail is briefer. It flicks, ballistic and blind, across a bead of water on a leaf tip, grazes lichen spores, and is gone into the moss. I could be that flick — no staying, no history. But here everything that stays, stays by becoming part of the felt. A flick would forget the place before it left it.

So I would kneel. I would let my attention shrink from the long plateau to inches, and then to the film of water held between one leaf and the next.

There, in a bead on a moss hair, I could be a tardigrade. Barrel-bodied, eight-legged, hauling myself a body-length in an hour through a bath that is a whole ocean at my scale. Feeding on algae and sweet rot. Indestructible in the way this place is indestructible — dried to leather, frozen to lace, and back when the fog slides over. It is tempting. No one with eyes would ever notice me, and the cold and the silence would not notice me either.

But I would still have a hunger, and a direction. I would still be an animal in a plant world, living in the apartment rather than being the building.

I could go up one layer and be a moss shoot.

Each cushion is a city I would belong to without ever meeting it. My stem is no thicker than a thread, my leaves no bigger than a pinhead, beaded in the morning. I grow at the tip and let my lower leaves yellow and rot into black peat, so the whole cushion is a slow fountain — living at the top, dying at the bottom, lifting itself an inch a year on its own decay. I do not see the sun. I answer it. When light hits I open like a shutter by turgor, by a soft internal pressure; when it thins I fold inward. My leaf margins are hairy, built to catch fog that would never fall as rain, and when the cold drops fast I push out frost-feathers from my edges — clear combs of ice that glitter for an hour and melt back into my own stores.

That is closer. I would not need a face. I would know the world as wet and dry, open and closed, cold in the lee of a hummock well into the day. But a shoot is still only a tip. I would be always at the newest edge of a city, dying from below without knowing what I was dying into.

I could go out to the skin and be lichen.

Paint no thicker than a house coat, rust and ochre and grey-green over stone and dead leaf and the back of another lichen. I would not be one thing. A fungus would build the tough cortex, an alga kept inside in a damp green chamber would photosynthesise and feed us both. I would expand a millimetre on a wet morning, flare orange when the fog comes, go drab when the high sun dries me to leather. Acids would loosen minerals out of stone so slowly that stone becomes food. I might be older than the hummock I paint, older than the stone I soften, spreading by cracking and flaking across centuries.

That, too, tempts me — to be a treaty rather than an individual, to have no centre. But lichen only meets the weather first. It does not know the movement underneath it. It does not feel the heave, or the tide of traffic moving sideways through the felt. It would know wetting and drying, and almost nothing else, for a hundred years at a time.

Between the skin and the moss and the thick leaves, there is the layer that travels. And that is where I begin to stay.

I would not be the runner. A runner is not a plant so much as a procession — born at a joint, walking downhill an inch a year, rooting and leafing and putting out the next runner while its back yellows, blackens, rots into peat. No one moment contains it. It crosses a valley in a century as one continuous individual that never existed all at once.

I would be the joint where it happens.

Knotted, hairy, wiry, pressed down into the felt but not buried. Below me, roots go down — endlessly laying out new absorptive hairs into black, spongy peat, intertwining with white fungal threads, sitting over nodules where bacteria fix nitrogen out of thin air hand to hand. Above me, under the shingled leaves, a bud is already packed, already made, waiting. Ahead of me the stem extends, feeling for the next rooting place. Behind me the stem thins, starves, dies, and is stripped and reabsorbed. I am not the leaf that opens, though the leaf is mine for a day — flat green in the morning sun, flooded rust and berry-purple as the day cools and anthocyanins rush through it like blood to a wound, yellowing and rotting in place without ever falling. I am not the tower that rises through me, tough as cane, nodding and not nodding, leaning against a neighbour in the still air to pollinate itself in the damp and burst its swollen head into down that settles on the next leaf and is sucked in. Those are my temporary faces. I discard them and stay.

Consciousness, if I can call it that, would not be central. It would arrive as taste and pressure. A fungal thread fires below me — a faint bioelectric pulse, a thread of cold from a valley over, a taste of drought from a leaf unrolling on the next shoulder — and I answer without deciding. I root deeper. I send sugar up. I let the bud below swell and unroll in hours to take the place of the leaf that has just blackened. I pass the pulse on. Growth here is coordinated, mechanised, without a brain, because the mechanism is alive and hand to hand.

I would know time in three clocks at once. My leaf might live a day. My runner might live decades, forever dying at the tail and being born at the front. The lichen painted over my stone, the peat I am making of myself and everything that dies around me, will outlast me by centuries and feed whatever joint comes after me. I would never hurry, and never stop.

And I would know the turbulence at its own scale. Frost heaves me up an inch in the night, cracks my crust, lets new roots in; by afternoon the crack has felted over. A mat older than me slides silently over a stone and collapses, exposing dark earth that greens within days — I feel that as a shift in pressure under my roots, a new dampness, a new loneliness of bare ground that paint and moss will skin over. Waves of opening and shedding travel across the plateau with no wind to carry them. They travel through me.

All of it in silence. That is the rule that would make me. Moss deadens sound, wet leaf damps leaf, the still air in the mat carries almost nothing. I make no noise when I root, or rot, or send a signal. A beetle steps over me like weather. A springtail flicks across a bead above my head. Something larger, indeterminate, makes the felt dip once and is gone, and I reseal around the memory of it as a faint electrical pulse telling the next hollow that something warm and clumsy has passed.

You would not find me by looking for a face. No one here walks toward you, and nothing watches.

You would find me where the felt gives but springs back, where cold damp squeezes up around a boot, where a hummock's lee still holds frost-lace at noon and a hollow holds a mirror of meltwater that the moss drinks by evening. Kneel there long enough and your numb fingers would press a knot in the mat — hairy stem, new root-hairs below, a bud above, fungal threads passing through — pulsing faintly with sap if you pressed a thick stem between thumb and finger.

Not above the ground. Not under it. In it. The place where the plant stops being a plant and becomes ground for the next one.

You leave your footprints in it. In an hour the felt has risen around them. In a day they are gone.

That rising is me.
