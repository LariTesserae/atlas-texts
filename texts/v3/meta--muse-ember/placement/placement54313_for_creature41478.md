At first I have no edges.

I come to myself in the middle, where there is nothing to stand on but ridged, polished floor, and the air is cool all day, and the light comes only in pale ribbons through the reeds. Above me the roof drips in a slow, irregular rhythm that never stops. Below me the floor gives back a dull, hollow sound. I do not know if I have arrived or if I have always been here, dampening.

For a while I think my body is the heavy one.

A footstep. That is what I am first — loud on the hollow clay, loud enough to make the water in the basins shiver and the toads hop from their ridges. A hand, cold from touching a wall, kneeling to press fresh clay into a seam where water has found a crack and moss has followed. The mud sticks under my nails. I leave a finger-mark. The humans do this: they move slowly through the emptiness, distant giants, and mend. They adjust a siphon, clear a runnel, set down a basket, and are gone again into the quiet between their visits.

But I am not occasional. I do not leave.

Then the chorus takes my throat.

It rises from the central basins and fills the Hollow the way water fills a bowl — hundreds of frogs calling from the shallows, from woven perches made for them, from brick rims and wet ridges. It is a pulsing roar, swelling and breaking, and for a moment I am inside it, one throat among so many that the far frog sounds as near as my own feet. The tadpoles below me turn like weather, dark clouds in the clear water, nosing the green film at the edges.

Then the snake crosses a basin, low and sinuous, and the roar breaks. Calling stops. The clouds scatter. Everything freezes.

I learn stillness from that silence.

I am sitting on a knee-high ridge, camouflaged against the polished clay, not moving at all while the sound reforms around me. Only when a shadow falls across me do I remember I can hop — suddenly, surprisingly fast, a few body-lengths away, and stillness again. Lizards do this in the light ribbons. Toads do this on the dry paths. It is a good way to be: held, hidden, waited out.

But stillness on the ridge is still surface. Still too clear. Still too much light, too much noise.

I find myself on the wall.

My belly is cold against the weeping clay. I move as slowly as a hand feeling its way, scraping the dull green film clean as I go. Moss cushions the joints of the bricks around me. Algae skins the wettest seams. This is slower than hopping, slower than calling. Here the Hollow is not a floor or a roof but a thickness, a landscape that is being eaten, layer by patient layer. Spiders sit in webs across the channel mouths and wait for midges. Beetles and woodlice run the bases. I do not need to be seen to be in the world. I only need to be damp, and to keep grazing.

Slugs do not hurry. Neither do the things that eat the building from inside.

That is where I go next — not across the wall, but into it.

Through a crack where the mortar has softened, where water has already made its road. Dark. No light slit here. No drip. Only straw, and clay, and the faint smell of old dung and ash trodden into the floor generations ago and now pressed up into a brick. My jaws find the straw-flecks left in the clay when someone's fingers last shaped it. I mine them, tunnel by tunnel, in the dark between one brick and the next.

Here the frogs are not a roar. They are a tremor. The whole chorus comes through the floor and walls as a low heartbeat, a vibration in the clay that tells me the basins are full, that the snake has passed or has not passed, that dusk is coming and the bats above me will unhook themselves from the roof voids and pour out in a flickering stream. The swallows under the eaves are a distant chatter. A human footstep overhead is an earthquake. When a hand presses a fresh patch of clay over my brick — mending, sealing, smearing a new layer over the last — the world shifts a fraction, the roof sags a fraction to accommodate it, and a new season of food arrives above my head.

I do not hurry. There is nowhere else to be, and no need to hurry. The house leans where the ground gave. The channels incise themselves. The moss follows the cracks. I go on consuming the place that made me possible, as steadily as the water consumes its channels.

And I am not alone in the dark in this. All around me, in the pillars and the straw-flecked bricks, are a hundred small mouths doing the same slow work — larvae, borers, termites — quietly hollowing out their own architecture inside the architecture men made for frogs and bats and themselves. We are the underside of the Hollow's equilibrium: decay and repair turning at the same pace. The Hollow persists not in spite of us, but because its inhabitants are both living in it and unmaking it.

That is more itself than the footstep, more itself than the throat, more itself than the still toad on the ridge or the slug on the wall. I am the eating that lives inside the made world. I am the damp dark between one brick and the next, where a finger-mark from a generation ago still holds straw for me to mine, where the weeping wall is not a wall at all but a body, and I am one of its quiet, necessary mouths.

Outside, the basins shine. The frogs call and break and call again. The reeds drip. A snail makes its slow progress down a seam. Someone, someday, will kneel and press fresh clay into my roof, leaving another handmade layer, not knowing — or knowing, and mending anyway — that on the other side of that clay, in the hollow of the Hollow, I am still here, still eating, still holding the place together by taking it apart.
