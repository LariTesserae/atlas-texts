I did not arrive as anything that moves.

At first, trying to place myself there, I kept putting myself a little above the ground — a watcher. The newt was the obvious temptation. The only animal that has bothered to be visible, pale in the condensation gutter of the press house, living her whole life in two meters of painted channel. Warm metal after sunset. A drip to watch. A world small and complete enough not to need the valley.

But she has never seen the valley. She does not need it. And even in my imagining I could feel how narrow that would be: always the same two meters, always the same edge of metal, the same white pumice light from above. I would spend my life being looked at by workers leaning over the press house, and never once understand the green I was living inside.

The slug was next. Four crossings a year, a silver line across cold concrete, out of moss and into moss. A life made of arrival and departure. I liked the slowness, the way the whole place would make speed feel obscene. But a slug is still a visitor, mostly waiting under a wall. The place is not about waiting to cross. It is about staying.

A human, then? Seasonal, rare, breathing too loud in the absolute silence. Coming in the bright months to harvest and repair, speaking quietly out of habit, leaving in the dark months. That would make me absent for half of everything. The valley goes on without me, thickening. I would return to find the vine had sealed a different door, the tree had grown further around the generator bolts, the moss had advanced another fingernail across a housing. I would always be catching up to a place that had not missed me.

Trees came closer. Low, many-stemmed, gnarled, branching early as if they had decided not to reach. A ring a decade. Their curtains of hanging moss drinking fog from the air. But even trees here lean. They lean to a sun-warmed stone, to a south-facing crack, to heat leaking from a buried pipe. They have a direction. They want something. I kept feeling the pull downward, away from direction, toward whatever does not lean but simply spreads.

Ferns, waist-high, uncurling in spirals that take days. In rows hidden inside the wild thicket, cut every seven years, carried sideways on a silent conveyor into the press house. That life has a journey in it, of a sort — born in green, cut, pressed, stacked under a tarp smelling of hot oil. But it is still an intermediate layer. Above moss, below shrubs. Always passing through.

My attention kept sinking past them. Past the shrubs with their spoon leaves, waxy against the cold. Down to the mattress.

Thirty centimeters thick in places. Emerald where shaded, grey-green where the light bleaches it, almost silver-white on the crowns where the sun hits full. A footprint fills slowly with water and closes behind you. Sound dies in it. My own footsteps — if I had them — would make nothing.

That is where I stopped trying to be on top of things.

I am the moss.

Not one plant, not one leaf. A layer. A place that does things at the speed of staying still long enough to be noticed.

I do not see the valley. I am underneath it, and over it, and in between. I grow in the hollows between the black glassy ridges where iron-dark soil has begun to gather, but I do not live on soil the way a tree does. I drink the air. Fog shreds itself on the ridges in the morning and I catch it. Dew forms so thick on my upper leaves that I drip under my own weight. Rain comes once a month for three minutes, rattles on the bright roofs, and vanishes — into me. I hold it, I pass it down and across, centimeter by slow centimeter, to the layer below me and the layer below that, all doing the same.

My sensing is not sight. It is wetness and light and cold. The silver-white tips know the sun is hard and close and colorless here, even though the air stays freezing. The emerald undersides know shade. New black rock beside me radiates cold into my felt of roots. A buried pipe knows me from the other side: I grow thickest toward its warmth, greener there, faster. On the conveyor belt I am not random. I am thickest at the joints, where there is a little oil and a little heat leaking out, joint after joint along a machine that moves a centimeter and stops. I have learned the building the way the lichens have on the shed wall, growing in straight lines along the seams, reading the panels.

I advance. Across soil, across the sail-like condensation nets strung between poles — I clog them with living fingers and so I make them work better, a net and a mesh at once, catching fog for the press house gutters below. Across a cable, until the cable now conducts poorly and wonderfully through my tissue, and the workers have stopped repairing that part because I have taken over the job. Across a generator housing, while above me a tree has grown through the frame and holds it upright, its roots gripping the same bolts I grip. We are not competing. We are both keeping it from falling.

Inside me is weather. The mites, finer than dust, uncountable, living and dying within a few square centimeters of green, stirring the mattress grain by grain. I do not notice them the way you would notice neighbors. They are a slow constant movement in the damp, the way a room is warm. Above me the ferns push through, fronds uncurling. Below me is more of me, darker, wetter, older. A footstep presses down, water rises, and then the dent closes again behind it, no memory kept except the damp redistributed.

Humans come and harvest me on a cycle of years. They press me into dense insulation mats and stack me under tarps. I forgive this, if forgiveness is the word. The cut face greens over. I grow back across the wound at fingernail pace. Some of me becomes wall, then: a mat in a shed, keeping thin heat in or cold out, still smelling faintly of green and hot oil. For a while I am no longer ground. I am shelter. That seems fair. Everything here is tended and untended at once. I tend the machines by covering them. They tend me by warming me and cutting me cleanly.

I make no noise. That is not a virtue, it is a material fact. Sound is absorbed in me the way water is. The silence of the whole bowl — no birds, no insects, machines moving at plant speed — lives in me as thickness. A drip falls from a leaf tip once a minute into my upper leaves and disappears. A slug crosses the press house floor once a season, tasting concrete, and I feel her only as a brief pressure at the edge where moss meets wall, and then she is gone into moss on the other side. I do not follow.

By late afternoon the light slants and gilds everything: black rock, silver leaf, bright roof, white pumice. Mist comes not as a blanket but in threads, horizontal bands hanging between ridges, unmoving. From the high shelf you can see the whole first draft of a world at once. From inside me you cannot see any of it. You can only feel the light change on the crowns, feel the cold deepen in the rock, feel the threads of fog settle wetly over everything that stays still.

So if you want to find me, do not look for something walking. Do not look for the newt in her two meters, or the slug on her schedule, or the workers in the bright months. Look for where something has stayed long enough to be noticed. Look on the warm side of a stone. Look where a net sags. Look where a conveyor joint leaks heat. Look where a footprint filled and closed. I will be there, layer upon layer, drinking the air, holding light and damp and silence together at the pace of a fingernail growing, and calling that enough.
