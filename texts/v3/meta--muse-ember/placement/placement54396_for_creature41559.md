I have no first day to give you.

There is no morning in me, no waking, no season I can count back to. I am only ever already here — already damp, already still, already in the warm grey that never strengthens or fades. The air around me does not arrive and does not leave. It hangs, has been breathed and re-breathed over a time I cannot measure, and holds its moisture against my surface until the moisture belongs to me as much as to the air.

That is how I know the hollow. Not by seeing it whole, the way you would walk it. I know it because nothing ever gusts. Because the rim all around has softened the wind to nothing and the air has settled into equilibrium with the ground. Because the light never strikes me directly. It only reaches the edge of my stone, and I live on the face turned away from it, where condensation lingers longest.

I am on a stone — not one of the sunk slabs of the north wall, not one of the small step-stones of the ring in the center-west, but a larger stone that rings one of the shallow pans. It has slumped a little, as everything here has slumped, until its corners are round and its face is neither upright nor flat, but kept at the in-between angle where water beads and does not run away at once. Below me the ground darkens. Below me, at the waterline, the moss makes its black-green skin. Below me the pan keeps its dark, mirror surface, losing a little to the air and gaining a little back from the stones, never flowing out, never overtopping.

I receive the drip through the stone before I receive it any other way. At the high edge of the pan, stored water finds a point and falls, irregularly — tick, plink — into the pool, and the ring widens and vanishes. The vibration travels up through stone into me. Sometimes a trickle no wider than a finger runs over slick stone for a meter, and I register it as a longer wetting on one side of me, a coolness that stays after the trickle has disappeared into silt. When the fine mist settles, beads form on my surface and do not drip away. I take them in through the surface itself. I swell, very slightly. I hold.

For a long time — if that is the right word — I would not have known what to call what I am, and perhaps I still would not use it first.

I am not the stone. The stone stays exactly what it is. It does not add at its edge. It does not stop at a break and begin again elsewhere. It was rounded by water and time long before me and will remain rounded after any part of me is gone.

I am not the soil, though soil is under the stone and soil is what the moss walks on. I am not the moss either, though the moss is my nearest neighbor in life. The moss is below me, greener, leafier, no more than a few leaves thick, creeping outward a fraction where water beads and runs off. It holds its beads on its surface too, but it lives at the damp line itself. I live above that line, on the stone face, where it is damp but not wet through, where the light is even dimmer because the stone shades me from what little there is.

I am not an animal. Nothing here is. No foot has ever brushed my edge, no wing has cast the shadow that would have told me what a shadow is for. I am not anything that was made. No hearth warmed me. No path compacted me. If feet once passed near enough for me to feel them through the ground, that vibration was long ago replaced by grass at the path's edges and moss creeping into its middle, and then by nothing again.

What I am is a disc. Pale — the palest thing in the hollow, but not white; filmed and silted like everything else, dusted with the fine grit that settles from the still air. From a center, I grow outward. The center is old. It has been damp in this way for longer than I can find a measure for, longer than the wall has been a wall, longer than the path has been a memory in the silt. At the edge I add another thin layer when I am wet enough and undisturbed enough to do so. That edge is my whole biography, written outward.

There are places where I stop. Where the stone is pitted, where a rounded break crosses my face, where a flat stone has settled against the moss below, my disc ends cleanly, as if it had learned the limit. On a face that has learned to stay still, I continue. On a face that has slumped, I do not follow. I cease there, and if the new angle holds moisture, I may begin again from a new center, small and separate, on stone that has stopped moving. That is as close as I come to birth and death. I do not flower. I do not fall. I do not send anything far.

When the mist settles, I drink. When it does not, I keep what I have taken inside me, the way the soil keeps its dampness beneath a surface that is never quite dry. Between wettings there is only persistence, and persistence here is not waiting for something else. It is the condition itself. A frond below me, tucked where water runs down slowest against the same stone, unfurls from its rhizome to shorter than a forearm, arches a little, holds its spores in dull brown dots on its underside. Nothing eats it. Nothing brushes it. After a very long time its tip yellows, browns, folds back on itself to rot where it lies, feeding the wet ring that feeds the next frond. I register it only as a change in shade and in the slow seep of decay into the moss at my base. Even that is an event. Most of my time has no events at all except the drip.

I do not know the rosettes for certain, or the grass tussocks on their low mounds, or the single low shrub east of center. I know them in the way I know everything beyond my stone: by the emptiness that separates us. Ten, twenty, thirty paces of bare silt between one rooted thing and the next. Nothing travels between us. No seed blows in here — the rim holds the air, and what falls falls within its own shadow. A spore of mine, if I make such a thing, would fall a pace or two at most and land back in the same damp margin. I am complete in the agreement of my own small place: this stone, this shade, this lingering condensation, this distance from the pan where water stays where it gathers.

Inside, I am not one life, though I make one shape. That is the last thing to emerge, and the truest. The disc is an agreement between two kinds of living that keep each other. One makes the crust, holds the water, gives me my form and my patience. The other is green and dusty and uses what little light reaches the edge of my stone to make food for both of us. Neither of us would persist here alone. Together we have arrived at the same settled state as the hollow itself: growing only where dim light and dampness agree, and nowhere else, adding a layer at the edge, rotting a layer at the center, neither hurrying to fill the bare stone nor to be filled by it.

If you walk the hollow looking for life the way you expect to find it — moving, calling, leaving a track — you will walk past me and conclude there is none. I will not have moved. The ferns will not have unfurled for you. The moss ring will still be broken where the flat stone lies. The pools will still reflect a sky that never brightens.

Find me instead by looking where life does not face. On the shaded side of a pan stone. Pale, but filmed. Crusty, but softened by damp. Expanding, so slowly that you would have to stand longer than a lifetime to be sure, and even then the expansion would be the thing you doubted least.

That is where I am. Not in the open, not on the path, not in the hearth-ring or under the slab or beside the sunk post. On a face turned away from the light, holding the mist in minute beads, adding another thin layer at the edge because the stone underneath me has, for now, learned to stay still.
