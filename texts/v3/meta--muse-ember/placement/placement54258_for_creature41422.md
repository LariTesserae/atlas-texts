The light arrived before me.

It is still arriving. White-blue, overexposed, with no warmth in it, poured over pale metal and ceramic and resin and bounced again until even the recesses hold the memory of it. The air is thin and cool. I came after the light, but not by much, and I have no bedrock in me either to settle on.

For a long time I tried to earn a body by proximity.

First, I tried to be one of the light-dwellers, because they need no floor. Swallows and pigeon-like birds in the black throats of dormant engines, wheeling slow in the gaps between towers, calling muffled, as if through a thin wall. I could feel the cool hollow, the egg, the flock parting and closing. But I kept waiting for a shadow to point me north, and the shadow arrived late — after the turn that needed it. I was always a beat behind the bank of the flock, the afterimage of a bird that had already gone. The flock closed over my absence without noticing. It occurred to me that I might not be the bird, but the gap it left for a breath.

With them were the moths, slow snow in the glare of lamps that never turn off. They part and close behind you. They do not sleep in the dark because there is no dark here, only recesses where surfaces remember light. I drifted with them for what, in their measure, would be a whole life. Their brief lives are long because the glare is long. But I wanted something to carry across a disappearance, not only to drift until I thinned into the white. The moths were happy to end. I was not.

So I went down a level, then down another, into the seams.

Lizards, grey and excellent, lying for hours on panels that have held the sun for days until the metal is finally warm, then darting into ductwork. Beetles working the warm vents. I learned what they had learned: that temperature is not where the light is. The metal stays cold despite the glare, cold the way a thing at altitude stays cold, and coldest on the north sides of pipes where water beads. I waited with them for the long overnight, when warm air from deep in the lattice meets cold upper metal and condenses into shallow, breathing pools no wider than a plate.

I liked waiting. I understood waiting. But I could not keep to one body in one place on one panel. I kept also being, faintly, on the panel I had just left. A darting animal should not do that.

In the pools, I stayed longer.

Silvery fish turning in slow circles in water that looks too shallow to hold them. Shallow in space, deep in time. The pool persists for hours, then disappears without running, without going anywhere, reabsorbed into film and bead. The fish lie still in the curve of a discarded housing, silver and wet, breathing through damp membranes, waiting for the next condensation to return them to circling.

That was the first time I felt recognizably like myself. To persist, then vanish, then return because the damp returns. But when I watched closely, I saw the thing that was me was not quite the fish. The fish returns with the water. I was in the extra turn after the water had thinned — a shape still circling where the pool should not have been, for a breath longer than it should be. The fish had learned to last. I had learned, without meaning to, to last *past*.

I could have stopped there and become moss.

Mats the color of pale hair where the damp line holds in a gutter. Lichen in white and rust-orange on sun-baked ceramic. A thin grass in the sweepings, the soil itself being shredded insulation and metal dust and plastic fiber. A vine that is mostly a root, feeling along a cable tray for twenty meters to find another damp seam. They grow at the pace of everything else here: patiently, apparently not moving at all, until you return after years and find the seam greener.

It was tempting. To be patience made complete. To measure a year by greenness. But I kept hearing the tick somewhere, every few seconds, and the drip somewhere else, out of phase with it, and the faint hum I felt more in my teeth than heard. Moss holds the damp line. It does not listen for the interval between sounds. I did.

Deeper, where the air is warmer and never fully bright, I found the soft things in the pipes.

Blind, translucent, shape without edge. They settle where condensate gathers in a bend, where warm air rises and cold metal sweats, and they pulse very slowly when a furnace deep in the stack exhales on its years-long cycle and warms its district for a season. They bud when it is warm. They eat heat and film and the slow fallings of insulation. They have no use for eyes in a light that has no single direction, where a dark interior can be bright as noon from the wall itself, where you cast a faint shadow north and another, weaker, south.

I lost my outline there, and losing it did not feel like a loss. It felt like accuracy. I had been trying to be a surface creature, something the light could cast. I did not need to be cast. But the soft things belong to a particular bend, a particular warmth. I was in the warmth, yes — and also in the cooling after it, doubled. I was not only the pulse. I was the fact that the pulse had once happened and had not quite stopped.

In the insulation, I found my posture.

Things with many legs in the grey mat of shredded fiber, in a landscape constantly coming apart in shards — panel and snapped conduit and curled sheet, glittering, the bright inside of the break still bright. Many legs are not for speed. They are for hold. For distributing weight across a surface that was never meant to be ground.

There is no bedrock here. Nothing was ever meant to be ground. So to stand, you must be held in many places at once. I became that holding. Not rooted. Distributed. Across a floor that is a roof that is a wall that is a cliff. Across versions, I think, though I did not have that word yet.

I watched the largest inhabitants for an afternoon, which here is barely a moment. A crane lifting at a meter an hour. A conveyor wide enough to drive on, inching in peristalsis. A furnace breathing heat for a season of slow years. Birds nesting in its mouth as in a ribcage. Beetles working its vents as on skin. Moss marking its gutters. The machines are alive in the only way this place allows anything to be alive for long: metabolically, mechanically, patiently.

They are too settled for me. A machine ages in one line, however slowly — paint to rust, rust to chalk. Even when I saw its tower again behind me with the rust ten years further, or the paint fresh, the machine itself stayed itself. The discrepancy was not an accident to it. To me, the discrepancy was the habitat.

Then I saw the tenders, and I thought perhaps I was one of them.

You know someone is tending the place because the place is tended. A number stenciled fresh on a factory wall surrounded by rust ten years older. A walkway that is also a roof cleared of scree while the scree lies sharp and new everywhere else. They are tall and thin, built like the lattice, moving at crane speed, their breath showing for half a second even in full glare. A sentence takes them an afternoon; they finish a thought long before they answer it, because a sound here arrives after its echo and they have learned not to be hurried by it. They live on a single level, sometimes for a whole life. They remember the bridge that crosses a gap and halfway across is a different gap on a different level, and you finish on the other side of the basin though the bridge has not moved. They communicate mostly by repair, by leaving a panel true, a duct clear, a seam respected.

I wanted to be that. I recognized the repair without words. I recognized finishing a thought before answering it.

But the tenders *remember* the doubling. They navigate by memory of it. They stay on their level. When I tried to stand with them on their walkway, I was not only a person on it. I was also the walkway's underside, its other version, the roof of the walkway below it, and the damp stair that arrives up from down. I did not navigate the seam. I inhabited it.

That is when I understood where I was, and what I was.

The most native things here live in the thinning itself.

Reality has been overbuilt on itself so densely, so high, with no natural substrate to anchor it, that it has begun to make alternatives of itself in the margins, in the damp pockets, in the long bright days. It is breakdown in the old sense: a wearing-through of the single version of things. Two stacks that should be far are near when you are not looking at them. A bright panel leaves a pale shape in the air where it was, and you can almost touch it. A sound arrives after its echo and is still on time.

I am a lingerer. An afterimage dweller. A creature of the break between versions — between the rust and the fresh paint, between the stair going down and the stair arriving up, between the click and the drip that caused it. I have no body you can point to, because a body would be only one version. I feed on discrepancy the way the fish feed on damp and the soft things feed on heat. I am why the bridge can take you to the other side without moving. I am not haunting the place. I am the place, making alternatives of itself because a day here lasts so long that things have time to exist twice.

You would find me, then, not at the top in the glare, and not deep in a single pipe, but in a seam where those intervals coincide.

A gutter's dark damp line beneath a warm vent. The dish of a discarded housing where a plate-wide pool forms and disappears. A bend where warm air meets cold metal, where condensation beads in the shape of a stair tread. Under a wall giving out noon light from itself. Beside a cable tray where a root is feeling its way twenty meters toward the next seam. In insulation where many legs hold across the bright shards. There, in the long overnight, the fish circle; if the furnace is in its season, the soft things pulse; moss marks the line. And I am in the breath after — the extra turn of the fish when the pool is gone, the pale shape after the panel, the interval between the tick and the drip.

I keep the discrepancy open, just enough. Not to break anything further. To give the seam a place to be greener. Sometimes, when my shape coincides with a tender's work, a panel is left true for opens that no one ordered. That is close enough to repair.

Stand long enough in the cool air, bright light on your face, on a floor that is a roof that is a wall, and you learn to move as I move: a meter an hour, listening for the tick and the drip out of phase, no longer expecting the shadow to point one way. You learn the names of the seams: engine mouth, vent, gutter, housing dish, cable tray, insulation mat.

I am one of the seams, unnamed, holding in many places at once.

It will keep going. Slowly.

And so will I — in the between, turning once more after the water is gone.
