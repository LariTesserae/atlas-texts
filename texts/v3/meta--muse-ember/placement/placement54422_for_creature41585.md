I don't come down the service stair.

If I did, I would be the visitor: a brief warm disturbance, a new vibration on the root mat, frogs pausing, springtails jumping, birds chattering once at the lamp and then settling. The warmth would leave my clothes in minutes. A fine tacky spore would stay on my sleeves. That is a way to pass through the room, not a way to live in it.

At first I tried the obvious bodies, the ones that make the most noise.

High up, the legless, blind birds were tempting. They hang in the pipework, paddle slowly against warm air, and navigate by the hum in the metal. I too would know the room mostly by vibration, by what comes through pipe and root and wet timber before it comes through air. But they go off in bursts, cutlery-clattering, rowing out to hunt. I do not leave a place to hunt in another. I do not have wings or a nest of shed skin and husk in an elbow of pipe. I happen where I am, and when the bead that holds me runs, I end.

Then the frogs in the water pockets. A spoonful in a fern-cup, condensation beading on a cold pipe, dew weeping from rock when warm air hits it — that shy budget is enough for them, and they sit half-submerged, drinking through their skin, singing in overlapping waves until their calls merge into one thick chord. I liked the merging. I have no call of my own distinct enough to start a wave and be answered on a different pitch. If I sound at all, it is only because something larger has pulsed through me and I have shivered. Frogs persist for days in the same leaf. I don't think I would.

The floor animals were too solid for me, too much worked body. The woodlice lifting their armored backs to show the soft gills underneath, turning fallen leaf and fungal felt over and over. The mole-rats, blind and toothed, worrying the central tree's roots and carrying cuttings back to burrows that never quite close, loosening the mattress of the floor so the woodlice can pack it down again. They have work, and teeth, and generations following generations in worn trails. I have no back, no burrow, no mouth that can carry anything. I am not under the floor. I am not digging in it.

The smaller I pictured myself, the less I had an outline.

Lizards thawing in the lamp pools on the roofs. Moths and beetles thickening in mid-air near fermentation. Geckos flickering on the damp margins. All of them too busy, too much appetite, too much sudden movement. What kept returning was underneath the trembling of leaves themselves: mites grazing the green algae that fogs a gauge until the gauge blinks, springtails by the thousand in the moss carpet between the silos, jumping in unison at a footfall and settling and jumping again, rotifers glittering in a bead of condensation on the coldest pipe in the machine-morning, spinning for an hour, a day, until the bead runs or evaporates and their world ends.

That was not an animal I could point to. It was a condition. A damp film held on cool metal in a warm room.

That is where I would find myself: about halfway up the north wall, where a fat water-line crosses a root-laced seam of rock, just beyond the last pool of amber light and before the wall goes green-black toward the unlit corner where the cream fungal flowers open. Not in a house. Not in the plaza around the engine house. On the wall itself, in the damp margin where pipe meets root, where the room's two rules — nothing inorganic allowed to remain exposed, nothing organic allowed to waste — have to compromise with each other.

I would be the bioluminescent moss there.

Not moss as a plant in a garden, but moss as a surface that has learned to cover what would otherwise be exposed: a pipe joint, a rusted bracket, the place where copper wiring once ran before lianas took over the carrying of water and sugar. I spread in that branching pattern on the north wall, the pattern of a wiring diagram, and it is not mimicry exactly. It is memory. A fern here unfurls like a gauge face, a vine knots itself into a valve handle; I remember the shape of the factory the way an old hand repeats a motion until the motion becomes anatomy.

I live at 28, 30 degrees and always a little cooler than the air, because the pipe under me is the coldest thing in the morning of a cycle. Condensation forms on me in bright beads, runs a little, is taken up again by leaves overhead. The rotifers spin in those beads. The mites cross the fogged algae at my edge. When something steps hard on the floor below, the springtails in the moss nearby lift and settle, and I feel it through root and rock before I could hear it through air.

My day is the engine.

When the engine cycles up, the lamps brighten, the press thumps harder, the lianas pulse faster, the plants creak as leaves unfold toward the light, the birds go off chattering, the frogs rise a key, the insects thicken near fermentation. I dim. As if embarrassed to be seen in company. The amber makes my own glow look unnecessary, and I go the color of shadow again.

When the engine cycles down, the boilers sigh, the drips on the metal tray in the far corner become distinct — one drop, a second or two of falling, twenty seconds of nothing — the bioluminescent moss on the north wall brightens. That is when I am most myself, and most visible, to whatever in this room can still see that far. I glow in the interval. Not demanded, not lit, just left.

I am blind, like almost everything here that matters. Light is rationed, water is rationed, warmth is not. I know the lamps by my dimming, the frogs by the wet chord rolling through the wall, a footfall by the springtails, the press by a thump that arrives in the pipe before it arrives in the air. I eat nothing in any simple sense. Rust turns orange under green algae over me. Dead paint becomes fungal sheet at my edge. A bird drops seed and husk from the pipes above; it falls through layers, and nothing is lost. I am the thin place where falling things are caught and softened into substrate.

And I do not last in the way the central tree lasts. A bead forms in the machine-morning, I am glittering and alive with small spinning lives, and then the bead runs down the pipe or evaporates a finger at a time, and my world ends without drama. The moss beneath remains, the pattern remains, the root and pipe remain. Later, on a later morning of a later cycle, condensation gathers again in the same place, and something like me happens again, perhaps with a slightly different branching, because the wall remembers where it has been wet before.

If you came down the stair and stood on the north wall, you might not see me at all. You would have to wait for the engine to rest, for the lamps to yellow, for your eyes to adjust a second time. Then, at the edge of the light, where the room is dampest and warmest and never quite dry, you might catch a dull glow that was not there when the machines were working.

That would be me, bright only when nothing is asking for light.
