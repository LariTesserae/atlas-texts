First there is only the current carrying me sideways, and my own beating.

I am not yet a place. I am a larva with a ring of cilia turning in water that has no surface, no bottom that is not also a ceiling somewhere else. The dimness is everywhere the same flat blue-grey, a fraction paler in one direction where the water enters and leaves, a fraction darker in the other where the two curves of shell meet and thicken into a spine. I am food for anything that filters, and I am a colonist for anything that waits on a clean surface. I cannot tell which I will be. Everything around me is the same: translucent, turning, tasting the water for a touch.

Then the touch comes. Not a landing — nothing here lands. The current slows against a broad, polished curve and my foot brushes it and finds it smooth as ice, gleaming with the light from the opening. I test it. I test it again. The water tries to take me back, as it takes most of us, tumble after tumble over a ridge that for me is a mountain range. A pale crawler steps over me, its many legs feeling blindly for a brother's back to step on next. Silt grains push at me like weather.

I hold. I secrete. I make a first smear of myself permanent against the nacre, and then another around the smear, until there is a cone of my own making standing between me and the flow. It is translucent at first. Things settle on it within days — a film that binds silt, a smaller larva that will not move again. The shell under me is still bare in wide districts for as far as my tentacles can reach. That is how it is here: the city is still coming into being, and the empty shine is unbuilt land waiting ahead of the growth.

When the tube is high enough to hold the current off my body, I put out the crown.

It is not a head. It is a fan of hair-thin threads, waving, tasting, catching. They comb the water toward my mouth. I do not see the dimness — I have no eyes for it — but I know toward and away. Toward is where the water comes fresh from the opening, paler, carrying rain: spores, eggs, larvae, organic snow that has fallen through the seam from a world I will never know is there. Away is the thick spine where the shell curves meet, where the builders are oldest and densest and the nacre is buried under everything that has lived.

I live three ribs out from that spine, in what was when I settled a frontier district. It is not a district to me. It is a slope, a ledge of rib, a gap in the flow. But around me now there is architecture because I cannot help making it. My crown stirs silt into the corners. My tube is a new surface. A mussel below me has slung a byssus thread, finer than hair, across the alley and lashed my leaning tube to its neighbor's, and that thread now holds up a small bridge of other bodies. Above me — which is to say, on the dome curving over, growing down toward the floor it will never fall to — another of my kind has fastened itself inverted, crown waving down into the same street. Its waste and my waste and the silt from a hundred crowns make mortar between us.

We all feed at once, not together. Thousands of appendages beat in loose asynchronous waves and the water between us thickens, and the whole chamber pulses against the shell, a pressure you feel in your tube wall. I beat faster when the water tastes richer. I beat slower when it is still, between tides, when the opening breathes out and even the gills of the mussels seem to pause. The tide comes every few hours and pushes through and takes what we have loosened. That is the only wind we have.

I have no voice. Nothing here has. The loudest thing I will ever know is not a sound. It is a steepening.

It comes first as pressure rolling ahead of a body too large for this street. The shrimp does not enter so much as displace the water in front of it, antennae trailing, legs tapping tubes to test them, translucent and slow and enormous by our measure, longer than a mussel bed is wide. I know it before it touches me because my crown feels the front of it and because the city tells me: a wave of retraction runs from the opening inward, tube by tube, district by district. Crowns vanish into tubes. Mussels snap. Crawlers freeze in silt or scatter over one another's houses. It is fast, for here — a fraction of a second, a whole field of plumes gone.

Then dark.

Inside my tube there is no light at all. My own body is pressed down into the chalky cone I built, waiting in the cold that is the only temperature there is. I do not know how long the hunter picks at what was slow to pull in. I know the pressure passes. I know behind it, tentacle by tentacle, the re-opening begins somewhere far toward the opening and travels toward me, a silence undoing itself. I put my crown out again. It has been nipped at the edge by a fish that slipped in with the tide — one of those eyeless pale weather-pulses that circles the outer districts and takes an amphipod here — but it regrows. Loss here is not dramatic. It is a slower beating for a while, then the same beating.

Around me the accretion does not stop for my moulting or my mating or my near-death. I have no mate to meet. At the tide's turn I release eggs and sperm into the water together with everyone else and trust the current to do the arithmetic. Some of what I release will be filtered and eaten by my neighbors before it leaves the chamber. Some will rain down again on the bare nacre beyond the sponge frontier, where a patch of us no older than pinheads is already waving untubed crowns on the clean shine, testing it with feet as I did. That is how new neighborhoods start. Not founded. Dropped, stuck, lived.

A sponge margin is creeping toward my lower tubes now, pale and almost transparent at its edge, its pores opening and closing in no order I can follow, a suburb of soft flesh few cells thick at the frontier and thicker behind it where tenants live in its canals. It will overgrow the bottom of my tube in time. It does not mean to. It spreads because the current brings it food and because spreading is what it does. On the other side, bacterial films in the dark corners bind the silt into a springy skin under my foundation, exuding slime, digesting what we leave, leaking chemistry into the water for rotifers to eat — the haze of turning mouths that moves in the boundary layer between our bodies, too small for scale to matter, everywhere.

The crawlers never stop. Blind white amphipods and isopods, translucent enough that the dark line of a gut shows through, processions over mussel beds, over tube spires, over sponge domes, over my closed tube when I retract and over my crown when I feed, using my house as a road because for them there is no distinction between road and house and back. Ostracods dart and hang still. Rotifers lift from surface to surface like dust that has decided to swim. I am leaned against, crawled over, built upon, exactly as everything here is. Touching is the default condition. To move at all is to move through each other.

Slowly, because nothing here can hurry for long in the cold, the gaps narrow. A spire topples — undermined by its own weight, leaning for a season — and does not become rubble. It becomes a dam in an alley. Larvae test it within a season. Bridges of byssus sling across where the water must pass. In the old core toward the spine, streets have roofed over entirely and become tunnels dark even by this place's standards. I am not in a tunnel yet, but the lip of my tube is being built over by a younger worm stacking itself sideways, and the silhouette of the dome is closer every tide. Already in the tunnel beyond the hinge a flasher lodges in an old pore and sends out a pinprick of cold blue light, on, off, on, and for that fraction the walls — which are made of other lives — gleam back at it: nacre and tube and chitin thrown into relief for an instant before returning to the everywhere dark that is our whole sky.

I do not know I am in a shell. I know a curve overhead that is ground to those born on it, and a curve underfoot that is sky to them, and a spine at one end where everything is thickest, and an opening at the other where the water breathes. I know toward the current and away from it. I know the taste of water that has just passed over something rotting, carried to me by a crawler's legs.

My tube is chalky now, layered with silt and dead shells. My opening is narrower than it was. I am less a body than an address where the flow slows enough for things to settle. When I die — where I live, as everything here does, nothing carried away except by the slow breath of water — I will not leave. My tube will be bored, colonized, built over. A smaller worm will settle on my lip. An amphipod will moult on my wall and its empty copy will be used by someone else within days. The dead are literally built upon, and the chamber is filling in from its walls inward, layer by layer, at a rate no one here would notice except that everyone here does, in their whole lives.

In a few hundred cold years the dome and the floor will meet in a solid mass of shell and tube and silt, and this pocket will close and become a single stone made of a city. That has not happened. Now I am still waving, still filtering what passes, still pulling in when the pressure front rolls through, in water with no air and no end, in a dimness that never clears and never quite becomes night — one life among too many to count, in a world as wide as a dinner plate, which is to say, exactly as large as everything.
