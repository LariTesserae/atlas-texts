I am not the first sound you hear.

I hear it before almost anything else — through water, through stone, through the mat — the deep companionable chorus of frogs, hundreds of throats beginning before dusk and not quite stopping. It comes through the seam where I live as a slow hammering, as weather. When it falters, someone upstairs has stepped too close to a nest. When it surges, wind has come off the ridge and the reed beds are hissing again. I keep still under it. I do not sing.

Nor am I one of the silver fish. Flashing, schooling, moving through flooded doorways the way pedestrians move through a street town, they pass overhead in bright shoals and never stop. I do not school. I do not flash. I stay low, where flat stones meet.

And I am not upstairs. I have never been dry. I have never sat on a hearth-hummock, never warmed hands over a clay pot, never heard news collect the way frogs collect at dusk. Lamp-light I know only from below, as a bright drowned rectangle in a submerged room, fish drifting past it like slow thoughts. The dry rooms, the woven walls, the roofs allowed to green and take seed — all of that is a ceiling to me, close and loud and inhabited.

What I am begins with a seam.

By day I fit myself into a narrow joint between two causeway stones, sunk into the mat until only their smoothed tops show. The mat is six feet thick here, maybe more, a living weave of root and rhizome and peat-moss that holds water inside itself and bubbles faintly when a pole presses it. Cold snowmelt seeps in from the north rim and drifts, almost imperceptibly, toward the south — a finger’s width a day, carrying peat-smell, silt, algae, the faint chemistry of fish pens and floating gardens. My back is green. Algae grows on the stones and it grows on me, so that when I tuck myself in, tail curled under the fan of my abdomen, claws folded close, I am simply another green stone in the road.

Two long antennae reach out in front of me and read the floor without my needing to see it. Shorter feelers taste the water. Under my shell are ten walking legs, each one lifting and testing before it takes weight. I have no need to hurry. Tessik does not hurry. A root pillar takes a childhood to grow thick enough to bear a house. A house sinks by inches in a decade and rises by inches when the mat swells. I have learned slowness the way they have: by waiting in the cold and growing by a finger’s width at a time.

At dusk I come out.

The frogs begin. The light above goes flat ordinary gold, then green-brown. Lamps of rendered fat are lit in the dry rooms upstairs, and their light finds its way down through the flooded ground floors, through doorways where old furniture has stood underwater for a generation. Boats knock against piers, over and over, and the knocking travels down the piles into the mat, into the stone, into my feet. Somewhere a net splashes. Children shout last messages across the channels, voices arriving whole in the air, arriving here as a thud through stone. Then, when the surface has settled into its evening hum, I step onto the causeway.

It is not a memory to me.

Humans pole dugouts feeling for these stones with the ends of their poles, the way you might feel for a familiar stair in the dark. They call it the old causeway, rim to rim, a broad track now more remembered than walked. For me it is simply the floor. Smooth stone, rough stone, a place where the mat presses up and bubbles, a joint where an eel lives and breathes slowly and does not want company, a stone worn hollow by a shoal of silver fish passing over it a thousand times. Eight hundred paces across the whole drowned summit, and I know it stone by stone, though I have walked only a dozen of them in a night. I will walk a few further tonight. That is enough of a life.

The town, from down here, is all supports and shadows.

I pass beneath raft storehouses, their platforms rafted and lashed, smelling of dried fish and thatch and spare poles, visited by everyone and owned by no one. I pass beneath houses where post and root have become one thing, the root pillars thick now, interlaced with alder roots locked into the mat. One house’s flooded lowest room is a fish nursery — I feel the flicker of small fins over my antennae and step wide around a chair leg gone soft and green. Another has become a planter as the mat thickened; pondweed waves there like slow hair, and I brush through it and carry its spores away on my legs. Under a fish pen the current is stronger, split reed weaving the water through, silver fish crowding close above the smell of slime and smoke — smoke never comes down here, but the hearth-hummocks leak their warmth and news into the drift, carries of rendered fat and simmering pots. Under a floating garden, duck feet patter on the surface and roots dangle like a second, drowned thatch.

The silver fish go over me like traffic going the other way. Eels slide past in their seams, neighbor to neighbor, without touching. Once an otter slid down a pile — the whole stone rang, the mat shivered, a heron burst startled from a rooftop and beat wet air somewhere far above, and for a moment everything I could feel was that one loud cousin shaking the world. Once, in summer noon, a dragonfly’s shadow crossed the surface and stitched itself darkly across the causeway, huge, brief, gone.

Sometimes I meet another of my kind, going the opposite way along the road. We do not stop. Antennae touch, read each other, withdraw. There are no crowds down here, and no privacy either. We are solitary the way stones are solitary.

Humans notice the walking, after all. That is the danger and the proof. On some nights a pole taps too close and I freeze, green on green, tail tucked, until the boat moves on. On other nights, when the lamps are lit and the frogs are loud enough to cover small sounds, a hand comes down through the water with a bone hook or just fingers, feeling along the stones. They are gathering. The people have a name for the things that walk this road at night, when they come looking for us along the causeway no living person has ever walked dry.

Crayfish.

I had never needed the word before. It fits the way the shell fits: awkward, armored, exact.

In spring I molted. The back split along a seam I could not see from inside, and I worked myself out soft and pale and hid for days while the new shell hardened. The old one stayed in my joint, empty, tenantless, like a flooded room left behind by a family that had moved upstairs. Nothing here is wasted. The shell softens. The mat takes it. Peat forms where old reeds fall and are not eaten. I step over my own first self when I go out now, a little larger, antennae a little longer, able to reach the next stone and the next. The root pillar near my seam was thin as a reed when I first emerged. It is thick enough now to bear a house, and a house is growing on it, and children will learn to pole before they learn to swim beneath its shadow.

The water breathes a finger’s width in a season. The stones sink a fraction more into the mat. A channel choked with weed becomes a meadow and a new water-street is poled beside it; my road does not change its mind. It only greens.

Before the white-gold noon glare, before the clouds trapped in the valleys begin to move, I turn home. The silver fish are already flashing through the streets overhead. The frogs have not stopped. A pole presses the mat nearby and a faint bubble rises and pops against the underside of a lily pad. I back into my seam, fold myself in, and let the algae take me again.

I am not the chorus. I am not the shoal. I am not the hands upstairs mending nets by lamplight, though I hear the rhythm of them through the floors. I am the small armored walker in the joint between stones, feeling my way by antennae along the only road in Tessik that has never been dry.

And it is, after all, still a road.
