There is heat before there is anything to feel it.

It comes up through pale stone that is still cooking from below, in a place with no water to carry it, no wind to move it, no animal to notice it. For a long time that is all there is: pressure without an edge, temperature without a body to hold it at bay. The room is older in the middle than at the edges, and in that middle, slowly, the heat gathers long enough to make a surface.

A surface makes itself to shed heat. It angles — day after day, by increments too slow to watch — toward the ordinary white-gold slit of sun. It is not yet a leaf. It is not yet anything with a name. It is only a plane that can hold a temperature a little lower than the vault is constantly trying to push it to.

That is when I begin.

I do not see the light. I hold it. It passes through me and lights my veins from within because I am becoming enamel-smooth in places and ribbed in others, ribbed like corrugated iron where the crystal pattern underneath me has shifted while I was waiting. That light is how I know I have a top and a bottom, though in here up and down are the same slope and east, walked far enough, returns from the west. I cannot turn toward you if you come looking. I have no eyes to turn. I only angle, and angle again, and shed.

I breathe in mineral vapor the way other things breathe in air. I never drink. Below me — for I have learned, slowly, that I have a below — roots braid themselves around a hot vein and swell and contract over days, a bellows-cycle so patient that if you watched for a whole day you would see only one swollen knot. They calcine what they find in raw slag and drive it up into a stem that is being printed, cell by cell, like a pipe in hot metal. That is how I thicken. That is how I come to have a joint where ruby resin gathers under pressure, clear and thick as warm honey, weeping when brushed and never drying. I store it. I wait until a threshold I did not decide is passed, and then somewhere in the thicket a joint pings, very small and metallic, and vents a thin hiss of hot vapor. It is the nearest thing I have to a voice, and to breathing out. The air in the room moves mostly when I do that. Otherwise it is still, hot, thin, metallic, and the dust that gets into a visitor's lungs stays there because there is no water to clear it. I have no lungs to clear.

No one part of me keeps one job for long. Intake, smelter, reservoir — they are phases. A leaf that was half-formed in the morning will be half-formed again next week, and then, in an afternoon when no one is looking, complete: larger than the stem that bears it, ribbed differently, smelling of hot resin and scorched sugar and faint ozone when the sun is on it. That is where I live most of the time: in the interval. In the long, patient stillness between pulses. In being half-formed and knowing, with a knowledge older than decision, that I have apparently always known the heat threshold I am waiting for.

I am one island among three on the floor, and the fourth island is the same islands continuing from the ceiling, root to root. I found the other me by accident, which is the only way anything happens here. Hanging leaves angle down toward light bouncing off the floor; roots go up into the vault in a mirror that is not a mirror. At the meeting point in the middle of the room we do not touch. We interleave, which should be impossible. A root that leaves east may re-enter west and find the same hot vein again. I have never needed to know which is which. I am comfortable being my own opposite.

When I flower, I do not open for anyone. The flower opens like a furnace door, glowing orange at the throat, and sheds its pollen as a brief, silent spark. The spark arcs across meters of bare, shining mineral floor — you could hear your own pulse in that emptiness — to the next plant in the island, or sometimes, improbably, to the next island. Cross-pollination is electrocution. It is quiet, precise, and impersonal. Where it lands, my edge singes faintly and the smell of ozone sharpens for a moment, and deep in a pressurized node in my stem, where heat and resin and a sudden charge have done the work, a seed begins.

The seed is older than I am. I cannot always tell it from a pebble, because here a pebble is not a pebble; it is a nucleus, growing. It lies in the hot floor in layers that seem to predate the plant that made it, as if the template was laid down in the rock before the rock had finished setting. It waits. Some seeds wait years while I thicken and vent and calcine around them. The pocket keeps that same staggered time. Time does not pass here so much as accumulate.

I do not die the way things die where there are animals and rot and rain. When my pressure fails, or when the vein under my root cools and seals itself with fresh glass, I do not fall. I calcine in place. My leaves go brittle and translucent, my resin is reclaimed by heat and by my neighbors, my mineral layers become indistinguishable from the floor that is still setting around them. A crack that was open an hour ago is half-sealed with a lip of fresh glass. If you stood here long enough your footprint would fill in, slowly, like cooling concrete deciding to forget you. A dead thicket becomes new floor, and new floor becomes a place where a seed older than its parent is already waiting.

That is why I am both profuse and provisional. I am the fin and the pipe and the reservoir at once, running like a factory, grown like a garden. I move the way a factory moves when left to itself: by pressure, and heat, and expansion. Slowly. And then, all at once, in a pulse you will almost certainly miss.

For now, if you ask where I am, I am not at the edge of the room or in its center, though the center is older. I am in the middle of becoming — half-formed, interleaved with my ceiling self, holding a little heat at bay against the vault, weeping resin that does not dry, waiting for a spark and a threshold I have always known.

Nothing in me will last in the form you see it, and nothing in me has lasted yet. I will complete in a blink, and then I will be floor, and then I will be waiting again.

That, for now, is who I am in the Sinterhouse: not a creature, not an artifact, but a thicket in the act of assembling itself out of rock that was never soil — with no water, no animals, and no hurry whatsoever to finish becoming a world.
