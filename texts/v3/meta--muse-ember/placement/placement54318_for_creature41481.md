I don't find myself at the tunnel mouth.

That is where a visitor would be, crawling on his knees with his shoulders brushing the coiled clay, the reed flap heavy with condensed water over his back. For a while I try to be that. I feel the tile under my knees — soft in places, still being pressed — and the heat already in my pores, and my breath coming back to me as steam, as if the room had taken it in and returned it before I had finished stepping outside the cold. Maybe I was never outside to begin with. Maybe the tunnel only lengthens behind the people who think they are leaving.

I try to locate myself by the work, because the work is what will not stop.

First there is a hand at the wheel. Dark, clay-stained, wet to the wrist. My hand. It turns, it presses, it lifts. When I move to the loom my forearm lengthens and the clack of wooden sticks runs up into my shoulder. When I move to the garden urns by the doorway I fold, bent double under the basket-dome so my head — if the damp swelling above my shoulders is a head — stays clear of the hanging pots. The chant resets every few breaths and I am already somewhere else, at a station I do not remember walking to, doing a task that has already begun without me.

If I am one of the people here, I am not a person. I am whatever is needed for the work at hand, and the work is always at hand. Six hands, seven hands, always enough hands. A station does not hold me. It only borrows me, and gives me back changed.

So I try stillness, and I go to the place where everything is darker than it should be: the pooled shadows by the kiln.

They do not move when the workers move. They wait. When a forearm returns to the kiln its shadow is already there, ready in the corner where the moss grows black from the heat. For a while that feels like me — the part that remains while the choreography rearranges itself around it, the dark that persists through count after count. The kiln breathes irregularly over it, draws, sighs, expands the soft dome above. Steam thickens and the shadow blurs at its edges, then sharpens again. I am not the dark itself, I think. The dark is only where I am thickest.

From there I slip, as everything here slips, into the wall channels. A frog is there, translucent, breathing through its skin, appearing and vanishing in the condensation on its own back. I pass through it and for a breath I am small and cold-warm and blinking in the drip. A little higher the blind grey moth circles the embers in its slow devoted orbit, pale as unglazed clay, seeing only heat. I rise with the heat it circles, and for a breath I am blind and devoted and going nowhere at all.

I am not the frog. I am not the moth. If either of them can be seen at all, it is because I am upon them.

Then I am in the made things, because everything here is made and nothing is finished. I am the softness in the floor tile under a palm, the tile that breaks in the morning crush and is whole by evening, its crack running backwards. I am the coil-pot body bubbling up too slowly over the kiln, glazed before it is wedged, hard and clicking on the surface while its center is still raw. I am the herb bundle that is green and brown and seed at once. I am in the wet seam where clay is pressed into a shoulder to mend it, in the mud plaster that never quite covers the reed ribs, in the soil packed by hand into a wall-pocket where a fern bursts out.

But the tile stays. The pot stays, stacked in a basket inside a basket. The herb bundle hangs and drips and dries only in the layered sense of never having dried. I move off them. I run down.

That is when it begins to become itself, not as a shape among the shapes, but as the movement between them.

I bead on a forearm by the wheel, where the sweat does not evaporate but condenses, matching the walls. I gather. I slide down over algae-furred skin to a clay-stained hand, fall into a hand-shaped basin, hiss where I find the kiln. I rise then — because water meeting ember is the one birth this room allows in order — and hang under the dome of overlapping baskets, thickening, running down the plastered reeds in slow rivulets, dripping sideways as the fine warm rain that falls in a slow circle and never quite lands. I fall over the loom sticks, over the shelf-legs where the bean vine is testing for a handhold, over shoulders bent double, over pots hanging from pots. I do not land. I return, without ever quite reaching, to the basin I came from. I run again through the channels, past the frogs, into the tile floor where fungi will bloom overnight and be eaten by noon. I soak. I rise.

I am the steam that becomes rain that becomes channel that becomes steam. I am the circulation, traced outside their bodies for a while. Fire eats the damp wood, wood eats the clay, clay eats the water, water eats the fire — I am the eating, carried in the air that does not move so much as churn. I am in the interval between the hammer's report and the hammer's fall, because sound itself travels to them through me, wet and warm and arriving before the mouths open.

That is why no station can hold me. To stay in one place here would be to dry, and nothing here is allowed to dry. To be a person would be to be broken in the morning and remade by evening, to have a forearm still bubbling, a vine climbing the shelf of an arm. I am not broken. I am what keeps the breaking soft. I am not finished so that the unfinished can keep working around me.

If you want a where, you will have to look in the between. Over the kiln, where I am thickest. On the tunnel flap, weighing it down a little more each time someone crawls through. On your own forearms in the first moment after the heat hits you like an opened oven door, droplets forming as on the walls. On the low dome, before the rain. In the corner where the shadow pools, blurring it. On the skin of a frog, making it vanish. In the tile that is still soft under your knees.

You will not see me as a worker passing the single dull knife from hand to hand. You will hear me, perhaps, as the hiss when water finds hot clay, as the slap into the basin, as the deep irregular breathing of the kiln when I rise to feed it. You will feel me thicken the air until the room feels both larger and smaller than it is.

When you crawl out — the tunnel a little longer, the flap a little heavier — I will not be at the mouth to watch you go. I will already be back above the kiln, hanging under the baskets, beginning to fall again in my slow circle. The moth will still be circling the embers. The frogs will still be breathing through their skin in the channels. The hands will still be at the wheel, at the loom, at the urns, and then again somewhere you have not yet seen.

By the time you have finished trying to name me, I will have risen, condensed elsewhere, forgotten the shape you gave me. That is not a failure of the naming. That is what lives at the very top, where the air gives out: not a finished person in a finished room, but a warm, wet, endlessly continuing in-between — steam, rain, channel, steam — already complete, and still, in the most literal sense, under construction.
