I don't arrive at the Flatworks.

I am already wet when I know where I am. My shoes are dark, the hems of my trousers are dark, my skin is damp from air that condenses on it and falls again. The ground underfoot is flat for longer than flat should be able to last, made surface holding water in the expansion joints, thin sheets running along curb-edges and canal-straight channels. The light is ordinary daylight at a normal angle, diffused by a low, warm, wet haze. It is always late morning.

For a while I think I am a walker.

That is the visitor's shape here, and for hours I wear it well. I walk the slab pattern, past the same lamp branching, the same weeping brick wall, the same junction that might be the one I passed before or one so like it I cannot tell. The horizon does not close. I meet another walker once in a long while, damp at the hems like me, carrying nothing, not hurrying, not asking where I am going because there is nowhere to be going to that is not the next identical junction. My footprints fill in the shallow water between the slabs and level, and are exactly as they were before.

But walking is a way of pretending that progress is happening. Things here move constantly and do not progress. A conveyor feeds a hopper feeds a line feeds a store feeds a conveyor. Water runs down a gutter, is collected, is pumped back to the roof tank, runs down the gutter again. The herds shift a few inches and settle. The fish swim their reaches back and forth. A walker is the only one still trying to get somewhere. If I keep walking I will only ever be the animal it thinks it is not.

So I stop, because nothing here startles, and I try staying.

I sit in a doorway in the lee of a bollard, against a warm wall, and for a while I could be one of the dogs. There are hundreds of them in a long street, of no particular breed and of all of them, asleep breast to breast with the wall and the stone. They lift their heads when I approach, judge the weight of my step, and put their heads down again. They do not bark. Warmth is the one thing the place provides in surplus and they have learned exactly where it pools: the engine-warmed ledges, the tops of electrical cabinets, the cats stretched to their full length with paws tucked. I could learn that too. I could breathe slowly in the damp and not move to cool myself, because there is nowhere cooler to move to.

But even a dog lifts its head. Even a cat, stretched within a hand's width of my passing, blinks once, slowly, in the light that does not move. Something in me does not want to lift its head for an event. I want a life that does not have to notice me at all.

I crouch by a gutter then, and look down.

Below the main level the surface of the canals breaks and breaks again. Fish in such numbers that the water is a constant shivering of reflections, mouths sometimes at the surface, gills working in the warm water, 2, 3 degrees beyond comfortable at all times. They do not leap. They do not dart. They swim their straight reach, turn at the concrete end, and swim back. From above they are interference. From below, I think, the walker's feet would be interference too: dark smudges crossing the broken light, filling and leveling.

If I were down there I would not need a doorway. I would have a turn. The turn would be enough to make a life out of. Back and forth, back and forth, in water fed by the same loop that feeds the bacterial film on the warm pools, that feeds the midges, that feeds the birds walking the canal edges deliberate and unhurried among the puddles.

It is closer. A fish does not ask where it is going; it turns before the end. But a fish is still moving, still purposeful in its swimming, still going back as if back were a direction. The Flatworks is not back and forth. It is held.

I rise a little, to where the midges are.

Over the warmest, stillest pool the film catches the light and throws it back green and violet and peacock-blue, though there is no oil in it. The midges rise from it in a cloud thick enough to be weather of its own, hang, settle, rise again. They are food at exactly the rate they are needed: for the birds, for the fish, for the spiders that eat the ones that miss. They have no middle, no place to stay in. A cloud is not a place. I could be brief, but brief is not the same as held.

So I go where the beetles are, in the dark behind iron plates and under eaves, small and black and slow, moving as if through shallow water even on dry concrete. In the cavity where a cable run meets a brick wall there is no green, no soil, no outward growth. There is only damp that is constant and heat that is constant. Life here does not reach for light. It stays where it was put.

And there, strung between artificial structures because only artificial structures are there to string between — rail to rail, pipe to lintel — is a web.

It sags under collected water, beads strung on the threads. I do not weave a new one. I maintain the same one, repaired as it decays, the way everything is repaired here, at exactly the rate it would decay. A drop falls from the tunnel ceiling above and tears a line where its weight lands. I draw a filament along the old line and the tear closes. A midge blunders in from the cloud below and goes still. I eat what the web catches and the web is still the same web.

I find I am holding myself at eight points so I don't have to hold myself at any one of them.

I am between a horizontal rail and a vertical pipe, above the mouth of a gutter where the warm pools gather. Below me a herd shifts a few inches, a slow weather moving through bodies that takes minutes to cross the yard, and I feel it as a low tremor in the rail. Above me the wires are black with birds, feet gripping and releasing and gripping again, and higher the flocks wheel over several blocks in one silent body, turning as if on a mechanism, casting no shadow I can trust. The walker passes under me, damp shoes on wet tile, and I know him by the distribution of weight through the iron long before I would know him any other way. I do not drop. I do not shake. The web steadies.

The sun keeps its angle. The smokestacks release mostly steam into the wet air, which condenses under the high damp layer and rains back in its own district and falls and falls. Rust blooms on the rail and stops. Paint does not fade past a certain point. The bacterial film tightens and loosens with the temperature. Everything is young and sharp and recently-emerged, and nothing ages, because what would decay is repaired at the rate it would decay, or was never allowed to begin.

I sit in the middle of the damp geometry for hours. A bead loosens from a thread above me, falls, lands in the gutter, is collected, is pumped back to the roof tank, will drip again tomorrow that is today. I mend the line where it tore. It is not new. It is the same.

Below me the footprints fill and level and are exactly as they were before.
