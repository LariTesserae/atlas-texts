When the note ceases at night, I have a voice.

Not in the day. In the day the shell is expanding under the sun and the hum lives in my teeth and in my sternum, and a spoken word would be swallowed and spat back to me from the far wall, thinner and higher than I sent it. So in the day I do not speak. I plug my ears, and I work by touch, and I talk with my hands.

But when the sun drops and the amber goes from bright gold to dusky brown, the shell contracts and the great note thins and thins and, for a brief astonishing interval, ceases. Then I take out the wax-and-fiber plugs with a small practiced twist, and the ears open, and I can hear a loom three rings away. I can hear resin bead by bead on the inner wall. I can hear my neighbour, a hundred paces off across the empty ledge, argue in a low, normal voice, and the answer comes back clean.

That is when I am most myself. It does not last.

At dawn I am awake before the light changes, in the ticking and creaking as the shell settles from the night's contraction. For an hour the pod is almost quiet enough for footsteps. I go out barefoot, as everyone does, onto the Great Drum in the centre of the floor — that shallow pit thirty paces across, stretched with dried pod-skin taut as a drumhead. No one crosses it at midday, when dust jumps on it in patterns and it vibrates visibly under the roar. At dawn I stand on it to listen with the soles of my feet. To test its tension. To feel, through the skin, how the shell is settling, and whether a lashing needs tightening with hand tools. The drum tells me what the day will ask of the pipes.

Then I go back to my stretch of wall, and the day begins to divide me by sound.

You would not find me in my house. Look for me between the rings.

My house is an island on the Low Ring, as all the houses are: thick-walled, a meter of mud and fiber and ground shell laid in courses, small dark mouth deep-set against the glare. For every house like it there are ten empty cubes, a hundred paces of naked ledge packed and polished by feet where a shout will travel a minute and thin. I know my neighbours not by their faces, which the amber light never quite gives you — no true shadows here, only softer and brighter ambers — but by the pulse of their kilns and the pitch of their fern curtains and the creak of their pulley-ropes three rings above.

My yard is vertical, and it is an instrument. Above my house and below it, on the steep vegetated slope that is too slick to climb, hang the curtains of hook-fern that root in fissures in the resin layer. Each frond is a hollow tube open at both ends. When the pod sings, they sing back, and I tend them the way you would tend a sleeping animal, because a garden that is not allowed to hear the drone will languish and die however much light it gets. The cilia along the fronds catch the fine silicate mist the warm shell exhales all day, and the vibration strips the nutrients from it. Beside a tuned flute a fern will double in a season. In an unpiped niche it will sigh its tubes shut.

So I pipe my garden. A clay flute is set into the wall beside my door, and hollow ribs are left open to the rising air, and ceramic bells and beaten gourds hang from the rope bridge that crosses to the Reed Terraces. They are tuned to the pod's note, and the garden is tuned through them. The cushion moss carpets every ledge not built on, a hand thick, velvet green, crunching dry underfoot when the heat takes it and sweating when the shell expands. In the morning and the evening I go out with a jar and a knife to cut it and chew it and store it. It is not water. There is no water in Iskara. It is sweet amber sap that tastes of warm resin and green stems, and it is my drink and my oil and my glue.

By midday the shell is glowing from outside and expanding, and the note is everywhere. Warm air rises in the lazy central column and whistles through the ribs and through my pipes, overtone after overtone, until my head is full and loose resin beads dance off the ledges. I work by feel. My hands are the first thing you would understand about me if you came close on the ledge — stained yellow-brown to the second joint, nails black at the rim with old resin, knuckles thick from handling. I lay my palm flat against the wall beside the pipe and wait. The hum runs up my arm like a string pulled in the sternum. My fingers make a small adjustment to a lashing, test, adjust again, satisfied by a vibration in the bone more than by any sound I can no longer afford to hear.

I am one of the Resin-people, though I learned that by hands and by posture long before I learned it by faces. The plugs, the fiber wrap on my forearm, the jar of cut moss balanced on my hip when I walk the slope between the rings — all of it is weathered, rounded by handling, like the tools in the throat-chamber where the keyhole lets in the plain. I do not go up to the Bell Rim often. There the shell is thinnest and brightest and the drone is almost too much to bear. Only the tenders of the great resonators go, and they go with their plugs deep and their work done quickly, and then come down again to where the pressure is at least bearable. Sometimes I go only to look through a pinhole worn by erosion at the white cracked plain outside, at the empty sky. I come back down and do not speak of it, except at night.

Distance, here, is not loneliness. It is an acoustic necessity. There must be room for my garden to sing, room for my pipes to resonate, room for the spore-dust — that fine green snow always falling from the central updraft, never melting — to settle undisturbed on my mats and my hair and the rims of my jars. To announce myself at midday I do not shout. I tap the pipe that runs through both my garden and my neighbour's and feel the answer through the wall, or I send a bell on its rope swinging, a purely visual bell, and wait for a hand to wave it still. A child learns the hand-speech at its mother's knee while the drone roars overhead, hands flashing, face intent, because that is the only language the day allows.

At dusk the bell-vines on the Reed Terraces fold their trumpets, the drum-leaves around the low kilns close against the cooling bellows, the moss stops sweating. In the wall cracks the geckos live — deaf and pale, insensitive to the roar that fills my teeth, moving with the shell's own languor — and in the brief silence they hunt the moths. The moths are glass-winged and blind, navigating by the feel of vibration in the air, riding the harmonics up the central column, spiralling, falling. Eaten, or not.

And once in a while, before I see it, I hear the soft leathery snap in the upper air. The glider slips from the eaves of the Bell Rim, tucks through the low resinous keyhole on the northern side, and wheels out over the white plain where there is no water and no green thread and nothing to see. It is the only animal in Iskara that ever leaves. It wheels for a time that seems to stretch, and then it turns — because the world, for it, is not out there — and slips back into the amber light to shake the dust of the plain from its wings. I know it by its particular wingbeat. It tests whether the outside is still empty, and it always comes back.

That is my place, and that is my work: to live in the intervals. To listen with my feet at dawn, to stop listening with my ears at midday and listen with my hands instead, to keep a fern curtain tuned to a note I cannot bear to hear directly, to harvest sap instead of water, to keep a hundred paces between my house and the next because the music needs the emptiness.

When night comes again the thick houses open. The plugs come out. We emerge onto the ledges and do the work that needs language: mending, harvesting the last beads from the closing bells, arguing, teaching. An elder sits with a child on the Great Drum at dusk, both barefoot, and places the child's palm flat so the child can feel the first low creak of expansion beginning again, like a held breath being released. Hands still do much of the teaching, but now there is speech underneath them, and the child hears for the first time that day what its own voice sounds like inside the seed.

I stand on the slope above my house then, in the silence, one hand on the wall where the flute runs through my garden, and I speak and am answered, and the answer is not distorted. The green hangs still around me, breathing only the slow tide of heat that moves through the seed as its weather. And then, imperceptibly, the pod begins to warm for the next day, the fundamental note returns so low I feel it in my ribs and in the soles of my feet, and the whole layered, patient economy — shell, jungle, city — wakes together.

If you want to find me, do not look for a face in the amber light. Look for the curtain that quivers last after the pipe is tuned. Look for the hands on the wall. I am there, between the rings, keeping — interval by interval — the music going.
