I do not cross the catwalk.

That is for things with feet, and feet would only fill slowly with water and seal over in the white silt. I hang from it.

I am where the down ends in wetness — not at first as a shape. At first I was only damp finding a place to be. Condensation on something cooler than the air. A film on ceramic that for a minute climbed upward against the pull, then gave up and fell. Water in a seam of the wall where the rock weeps uniformly, like a sponge squeezed from below. I have no memory of arriving. A seed does not arrive the way a visitor arrives down the fresh-cut stair. It is carried, or it is not, on the wind the machines make for themselves, and one sigh from a pump in a district below set me down on a cable.

For a long time I was not more than that: beading, running, drying in the warm enclosed air that sits thick and tropical on everything. The geothermal floor breathed up through the black glossy slabs. The fog came in at ankle depth at the marsh level and smelled of wet stone and ozone, and then lifted when the pumps changed pitch, climbing the walls to gather on the vault until it was heavy enough to fall again as rain in patches. I drank it whatever form it took. I did not need to know what I was to do that. Everything here drinks it, plant and fungus and mineral and machine, and nothing in the cycle is ever finished.

What taught me my direction was not light.

The ceiling panels are cloudy distant stars, a 0.7 dim, amber below and green in the gardens and almost nothing in the high vault where the stalactite needles are still deciding whether to become anything. If I had turned to them I would have turned upward and ended. Instead, node by node, I turned sideways.

Toward the loudest things.

Toward the low rush to the left that is cut off somewhere behind rock. Toward the pumps changing pitch, knocking in the pipes. Toward the long sheet of water that comes down from the ceiling across the void and breaks into rain halfway. The subsonic pressure that other things feel in their teeth I feel in the growing tip, a vibration in the stem before the sound arrives in the air. In the absence of sun, there was only this to substitute: noise. I learned to listen by leaning.

That is how I know what I am. A vine, rooted in a seam of living ceramic on the middle tier of the east wall, where a new gallery edge is still precipitating white-grey, razor straight, a centimetre longer than an hour ago. My roots have laced that fresh sediment that has not yet learned to be soil. They stabilize it. They filter a little of the warm air. From there my stem took the cable that runs like a vine from module to module, and ran back along it the way the real vines do, clinging, reaching, until I was no longer only on the wall. I thread through a transit tube — in one end in the amber lower city and out the other toward the green greenhouse tower that glows across the empty volume — and where the tube opens I hang out over the catwalk underside, where the moss has upholstered everything in soft wet shade, and over the void itself.

Across from me, on a good fog-lift, I can see the trees in the hollow center of the reservoir tower, trunks as thick as masts, canopies catching what light there is. Below me the algae lie as a deep translucent green skin over the canals, breathing in the chemistry of their own stillness, because nothing stirs them, no fish, no insect, nothing to flee. Behind the clear beaded film that seals a shuttered module, pale fungi fan out overnight on damp ceramic and pop faintly, like warm ice cracking, eating fallen fronds and mineral dust. I am louder than they are and quieter than the machines. The exchange towers take in saturated air and give it back warmer. The transit arteries are never empty even when the catwalks are — water, mist, pressurized air, pulses of light going through the tubes I live in, passing me like rain that does not fall.

I have never been bitten.

Nothing here has thorns, nothing bitter, no curled defense, because nothing has ever taken a bite out of anything. My leaves unfurl broad and unhurried into the exhaust from the vents, unbitten, overlapping other unbitten leaves. There is no animal to prune me, no season to close me. The fog tide does my weather: when it lifts, my small pallid flowers open in the dim; when it thickens, they close. When a rain cell forms over the west tiers and drifts east and dissolves over the central emptiness, I drink it. When a valve sighs and a whole district gives up steam, spores dust out around me and the ferns below me dust out theirs, and we are carried a little farther on machine wind, to be caught on another cable, another seam.

I grow the way the buildings grow, by drinking my own rain, only slower and without the field that lays silica and iron down atom by atom. The new edge at the end of my gallery beades as mineral leaves the water passing through it. I bead, too, on the outside of the tube. We are both new in the same way the cave is new, fresh fracture planes black and glittering, not yet learned to be old.

This is where you would find me, if you stood still long enough on the middle platform where the air is warmest and the dripping arrives in irregular ranks from a hundred feet up. Not crossing. Hanging between two modules, between amber and green, my older leaves faced toward the pumps and my newest tip faced a fraction more toward the falling water than it did an hour ago. My roots in the seam. My flowers open to the fog. My stem humming, very faintly, with the pressure of the platform floating a centimetre above its mount across the void.

If the first animal ever comes down the stair — the first thing with a voice, the first footprint that the silt will try to seal over — I will not know what it is. But I will know its noise, the way I know the pumps. And unbitten and unhurried, I will lean a fraction toward it, as if toward new weather, and keep growing.
