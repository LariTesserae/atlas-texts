It is green before it is anything else, and I am in it before I am anything else.

My feet have been wet for some time. That is the first true thing about me. Not that I am standing, or walking, or looking. Wet, and have been, long enough that I no longer remember the moment of getting so. Ankle-deep in one step, waist-deep in the next where the young land dips between ridges, neck-deep where the water has settled in a hollow and decided to stay. The water does not move me. It does not lap or run. It stands, clear as glass over green, and the sunlight falls straight down through it as if through air, white-gold and ordinary at midday, only slowly understood to be falling through something.

I tried, at first, to be the one who walks.

Above me the canopy touches itself and does not break. Below me the mat gives underfoot, root over root over root, springy and wet, holding gravel and red earth and silt that the sharp ridges are still shedding. If I am the walker, then I am only a weight the mat learns to bear. I take a step. The step is there. A leaf falls an inch and stops on something. I go no farther than a seed would go. The ridges around my basin are knife-edged, black stone with glassy faces, a few peaks punching bare above the treetops, and they seal me in. There is no shore to walk toward. There is no direction in this water that I have not already arrived at.

It did not hold, that name. Walking is too fast for this place, and nothing here is paid to be fast.

So I tried to be the green above.

I let myself be a crown, a raft held out on a held breath. My stems are hollow inside with air hidden in them, not to float away — nothing floats away — but to keep a leaf up into the thin, hot air of the plateau, into equatorial sun that burns a little stronger up here. Canopy touches canopy because the holders touch. A seed of mine fell once, not to earth, but onto a leaf, onto a mat, onto water that carried it an inch before it snagged. I germinated in mid-air. I remember that as a beginning, though it may not have been mine: a pale rootlet let down from the upper green, feeling by growth, descending until it found a trunk, a vine, the surface itself, and then finished opening below. One end drinking rain that comes as a fine patient wall, the other end drinking the standing sheet, and no hurry between them.

That fit better, for a while. I could feel the sun on my upper face, waxy and bright. But I could also feel something else, and the green above could not account for it.

So I let myself sink to the waterline, which is where I truly live.

Above the line my leaves are thick. Below it the same leaves are pale enough to see the veinwork through. This is not a border. I root in air and cross the surface and root again underwater and thrive at both ends equally. I spread flat at the interface, half in and half out, expanding and contracting almost imperceptibly as the sheet rises by a fraction after rain and stays. I am the ceiling of the world under me and the floor of the world above me. I gather light in one register and pass it down in another.

Under my undersides, something trembles.

Oxygen made by the submerged leaves does not race up in bubbles. It collects in slow silver films against a leaf, held by surface tension, trembling, colonized, reabsorbed before it can leave. I am busy there, and busyness here is a very small, very local thing. Smaller than an eyelash, I live on the underside of a single leaf, eating the algae that grows rich in the captive breath, keeping the film from bursting, returning what I take to the leaf that made it. I never surface. I never need to. The rain brings almost nothing but water. The stone brings the rest. A leaf dies, falls an inch, is worked and taken up again by a root already there. Nothing runs off. Nothing has to.

That fit even better. It fit the pressure of the film, the way I am both maker and keeper, both leaf and mouth, an agreement between layers rather than a thing. But it was not whole. Under the film there is more of me, older.

Because I have been here long enough to have left myself behind, alive.

A tree here grows upward as the floor rises beneath it. It does not shed its lower branches to make room. They stay, still photosynthesizing in clear water. So I am several forests stacked. At the top, a crown in white-gold light. A few feet down, the crown of a generation ago, broader, paler, a rosette turned flat by stillness. Deeper still, the crown before that, spread between roots like a carpet that never rises. From above you see leaves; from below you see the same leaves, mirrored in branching. The ridge that was an island is now a reef of crowns, now a suggestion of crowns under bright water, and I have gone the same way vertically, without hurry. The mat keeps rising a fraction at a time, leaf by leaf, silt under root under silt, and I keep my face at the same bright depth while the world accumulates underneath me.

Even that was not the deepest name.

I know it because the light is thinning now, just a little, and the silt has risen too close under this leaf, and the film is harder to keep. A whole life can be lived suspended, but not forever on one leaf. I am, at last, the rare exception that proves the rule of this place: the broad, blind, frog-slow shape easing far below the surface through the weeds, once.

I have no eyes worth mentioning. In clear, bright, shallow water they would be an expense, and I have refused to pay it, specimen after specimen. My skin does the work of leaf and lung and root. I press myself to the underside of a shelf-leaf — of myself, of the layer I just named — and I stay there for months, absorbing, grazing the silver film, letting the plant do the work of making the day. To watch me move you must watch by position, an hour apart. When this leaf sinks too deep, I do what the plants do. I grow a new anchor forward, an inch. I dissolve the old one behind. I settle again.

That is how I go. Not by going. By becoming plant long enough that the next place is already here.

There is no birdsong to mark me. There is no chatter of insects. The only sound I make is the sound the place allows: a drip that takes a long time to fall the last distance to a surface it was already almost touching, water sighing once over a submerged log, a leaf uncurling under water with a small patient displacement. By growth, not by voice.

Far above, on the bare peaks, the crusts are drinking their cracks on black stone, slower than I am. Down here, I am drinking a lens of breath curved under green. The mat is rising a fraction. The shelf is holding its film. The trunk-reef is photosynthesizing in three registers at once. None of us is doing anything you could recognize as doing.

At midday the light is ordinary and falls straight down through clear water onto green that continues unchanged. That is what makes me stay.
