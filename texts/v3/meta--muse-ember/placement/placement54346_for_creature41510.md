You would not find me in the hut.

I don't come up in the morning with my ears popping. I am already here when the cloud breaks and the shelf shows itself — no ridge, no traverse, just the abrupt flat of new basalt, warm from below, and the wet white falling away on every side. If you stood in the middle you could see the edge in every direction and walk the whole boundary in twenty minutes. I am not standing. I am spread.

At first, to be somewhere, I try on the shapes that move here.

I try the worker. The slow cross of the terrace with a bundle of broad leaves, breath held a little too long between steps because the air is thin and mild and saturated and makes you sweat in a fleece that will never dry. I could do that pace. Everything here is laborious. The belt inches, clanks, pauses. The turbine in the culvert turns but does not hurry. The press waits. It would be easy to borrow his slowness and call it mine.

But he closes the hut door against the afternoon mist. He sleeps thinly against the altitude. He leaves the layers to themselves at night. I do not leave. And his boots are spotted with black soil that is no soil — root and wet film. I am not standing on that. I am that film, gathered.

I try the hummingbird. Once or twice an hour, impossibly bright, darting through the terrace and gone over the edge. That is a tempting speed to have in a place that forbids speed, to belong to the orchids fastened to pipes rather than to trees, to pause exactly where condensation runs and then not stay. But I cannot be that bright, and I cannot be gone. Nothing here is gone for long except the water, and even the water returns from the air. I stay. That is the first true thing about me: I stay, and I am wet, and I am not disturbed.

No one lifts the stones here. There are no tracks in the leaf litter, no birdsong to mark an hour. If you lift a wet stone you find stone. That undisturbedness is my condition. I accumulate.

I try the machines, because they are the other residents and they are, like me, tethered. The generator drones through your teeth. Metal ticks as it warms and cools in the damp. The fence ticks with electricity at intervals, a small nervous sound under the roar of sheets over basalt. The weather mast thrums over the rim with no one listening. I could learn that hum through iron. On the shed roof, where the conveyor crawls out through the fern, the vibration comes up through corrugated metal and into everything rooted there. For a while I think I am a kind of orchid on industry — small, improbable, drinking from a dripping elbow, opening exactly where the warm motor housing sweats.

It is close. It is not enough. A flower is an event. I am not an event. I am a duration.

I try the smaller, more stubborn populations. The mushrooms growing pale and stubborn in the oil that leaked onto concrete and never washed away — living precisely where nothing else will, in the residue. The lichen branching across the cylindrical tanks in patterns like wiring diagrams, tracing a system without needing to understand it. Those are near my scale and my patience. I could be a branching, a diagram, a pale insistence on a surface that was meant to be clean.

But I am not on the surface. I am in it.

Later, when the mist comes up from below and erases the edge for the afternoon, when dripping continues out of rhythm from eaves and pipes and rock faces that weep even under a clear sky, I feel the difference more clearly. Water arrives constantly here and does not gather. That is the rule of the shelf: it tilts outward in every direction at once, so water touches and leaves, finds a crack, plunges at the rim in dozens of thin waterfalls into cloud where no one hears it land.

I am the interruption of that rule.

I am the mat thick as a mattress on the low flat just below the terrace, where the rivulet spreads ankle-deep over smooth basalt before it narrows toward a crack. I have grown over the edge of a concrete plinth and over a pipe bracket at the same time, so that rock and industry and root are one continuous damp. Moss, algae, liverwort, the thin green skin that forms on water when it lingers a moment too long on warm stone — I am not one of those. I am the layer they make together, and the layer is what matters here. The prompt of this place, if it has one, is layer holding water for the next layer.

So I hold.

In the morning the deep warmth comes up through the basalt and through the iron of the shed roof and into me. It is not sun-warm. It is interior, recent, from a mountain still deciding its shape. My water is warm where a fresh crack lets it show through. In the flat equatorial light, filtered by cloud to something saturated — green, rust, copper, black — I take in the mist that is really rain, I take in the sheets crossing the stone. I swell, very slightly. Sponge-breathing, slow.

Then, hours later, when the sky has cleared and everyone else is complaining that their jackets never dry, I release. One drip from an upper frond to a lower bracket. A slow seep into the soil pockets where roots have made something like soil. A thin sheet that starts again at my edge and crosses the stone ankle-deep, greener than it was. I do not move. Water moves through me, and that movement, delayed, is my movement. It is as slow as I can be allowed to be here, and everything else is measured against it — the belt inching, the press clanking, the worker feeding sparingly because the loads arrive in cut armfuls and the air will not let him hurry.

The green presses against me from above, three or four strata of leaf, tree ferns taller than a man dripping steadily, bamboo with wet weight, vines that last week sealed the path and this week are sealing it again. They are cleared from the way, never from the place. I am not in the way. I am under the way. Ferns grow out of gutters above me. Orchids open on pipes above me. Their excess, their drip and fall, becomes my intake. What the machines spill, the oil excepted, becomes my intake. I am between, and between is enough of a world at this altitude.

By evening the cloud does not fall. It rises from below and fills the bowl to the rim, and the shelf becomes an island again: wet stone, dripping iron, green pressing close, the generator keeping its drone, the small waterfalls plunging. The workers are in the hut, smoking or sleeping thinly. The hummingbird is gone. The high hawk far below in the cloud sea describes a wider room and does not come up.

I drink the air itself, as the plants do. The condensation on the inside of the shed glass will run in slow branching rivers for hours after the mist is gone. Mine run outward, slower still, branching over the tanks' lichen, over the warm iron, over the sharp new edges of basalt that were faceted like a broken dish and have not had time to soften. I will be wet in the morning. I will be warm through the sole of nothing. I will still be out of breath in the way a sponge is out of breath — full, and slowly letting go.

Find me, then, not as a person, not as a bird, not as a machine, but on that low flat where the water first decides to be a sheet. I am the pause before the fall.
