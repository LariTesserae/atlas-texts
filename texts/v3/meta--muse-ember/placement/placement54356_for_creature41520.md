Most of what I know arrives through concrete, and through water moving over concrete.

I live just beneath the grating at the edge of a terrace, in the channel that runs shallow and fast along the retaining wall. Above me is decking, and boots, and the wheels of carts. Below me is the next deck, and the next, and the warm lower rooms where the water is gathered and cleaned and sent up again. I am in the skin between them.

The channel is not a river. It is a made thing, a poured line with squared corners, smoothed over years by the flow itself. In places it widens under an expansion joint where I can rest without the water taking me. My belly lies against the cool concrete, my body follows the current, and when I move — which is not often — I move slowly, against it or across it, a small displacement in the pattern of the water. People who crouch and look through the grating call us drain eels. I am one of those. I am not kept. I persist.

From underneath, the place does not look like a city at all. It arrives as pressure and temperature and taste.

A boot is a brief weight through the deck, transmitted down through stone and water. A cart wheel is a rolling vibration, slower and heavier. A press three levels down becomes a soft thud in the water itself, a ripple that passes along my side. A kettle in a kitchen above, behind a thin wall, is a high, muffled note that travels through pipes and mortar and then, faintly, through the air trapped under the grating. Voices are a working murmur, never raised, because raising them would be lost anyway. The people are not really seen in my life. They are weather.

Water tells me more. Warm water means a kitchen has drained, or a greenhouse overhead has been hosed, or engines below have been working hard and are giving up their waste heat. Cold, clean water means rain has passed through the upper terraces and washed the dust off the decks. Green water means the food terraces have flushed their beds. Water that tastes of iron means a tank overhead has been tapped. After heavy condensation in winter, the whole system weeps a little more, and the channels run a little fuller and a little faster, and the drips from the windowsills above fall in a steadier rhythm — thin and quick on the upper levels, slower and heavier where the concrete is thicker.

Light, when there is any, comes in strips through the grating. In the grey-white days it is the color of wet paper, laid across the water in squares whenever no one is standing over it. At night the lamps above make the strips amber, and their reflection breaks and reforms in the flow. I have no need to look up. I know the time by whether the vibration overhead has slowed, by whether the drips have settled into their night pattern, by the lamps turning the water brown-gold for a few hours before the morning carts begin again.

The plants are closer to me than the people are. Ferns grow in the joint right above my resting place, and their roots find their way down through the mortar in thin, persistent threads. They do not reach the water, but they reach the damp, and sometimes a root tip breaks the surface and trails in the current, swaying. Higher up, the watercress and algae thicken in the gutters where the runoff gathers, and when the terraces are flushed the bright green slicks come down to me in pieces. Moss softens the iron of the grating until the squares of light it lets through are edged in green. I live among their leavings. They live among my leavings. Everything that can be reused here, is.

I have been under this joint long enough to have been re-laid around, twice.

The second time, I learned what the place looks like from above the channel, briefly, unwillingly. One morning the flow slowed, not blocked but diverted, and then stopped. The wall that holds the terrace was taken down block by block — I could feel each block lifted, each vibration change as the structure opened. Then light fell into the channel whole, not in strips, but a flat, diffuse, unbearable brightness, and air that was not damp. Hands reached in with tools. Water that had been my sky was now only a shallow trench in the open. I pressed myself into the side pipe where the culvert turns, and stayed there, in the dark, while the workers scraped and re-laid the same blocks and planted ferns into the fresh mortar before it had set. By evening the grating was back in place and the strips of light were back, and the water was turbid for a day and then clear again. To them it was a morning's repair. To me it was the world unroofed and remade.

Things change above me without ever being empty. The workshop that was over this section for years — the one whose press thud I knew by its exact weight — was gutted and refitted as a drying room. Its machines were carried out in pieces. For two days the vibrations were strange and irregular. Then the new use settled in: at midday, warm, steamy air from the racks above condensed on the cool outer walls and ran down in a warmer trickle, and the temperature of my channel rose by a degree for an hour each day. A terrace that goes brown is failing, the people say. A terrace that goes too green is also failing, but slower. I know both kinds of failure by taste. When the green thickens too much upstream, the water slows and thickens with it, and the algae mats cling to the channel walls and the taste goes sweet and stale. Then there is a morning of boots and crouching and a lifted grating, and hands — kept, the children are told, out of the fast cold water, though it is never the water they are really being kept from — clearing the matted growth away. I retreat downstream until it is done.

The other tenants are mostly heard, or inferred. Rats live in the service spine beside this channel, in the damp green dark between buildings. I never meet them, but I hear the scurry of their feet on pipework above the waterline, and I smell them more after rain. Pigeons hold the highest terraces, far above the mist, and give nothing to me except, now and then, a grain washed down through a downspout from the food terraces, which tastes of the place where it fell. In summer there are moths at the lamps, and sometimes a larva or a battered wing drops into the channel and is gone in a moment. One cat sits for hours on a windowsill directly over this grating — I know it by the still, warm weight through the deck, unmoving in a way no person's boot ever is, while the carts go around it and the murmur goes on below. In the basement rooms far below, where the water is collected, there are tanks of small fish that no animal larger than a forearm ever sees. I can smell them through the filter beds when the flow is low. We share the loop, separated by screens I have never needed to test.

I travel it, slowly, over the seasons. Down to the warmer outflow, where the algae beds are bright with their own work and the bacteria in the warm water do the foundational labor no one sees. Up again through the culverts when the rain is heavy and the channels run fast. I am not the fastest thing in the water, and I do not need to be. Eels were made for the margins of flows. I hold in an eddy under the joint, I let the water pass, I make the small punctuation in its sentence that the people, when they crouch, mistake for stillness.

By night, when the dimming is only slight and the lamps come up everywhere above me, the strips in my water turn amber and the traffic slows to the drip rhythm of each level. The press three levels down rests. The carts stop. The whole crowded, quiet, constantly remade place settles into its layers — people in their saturated rooms behind thin walls, plants in their troughs and joints and bins, rats in the spines, pigeons in the eaves — and the water keeps its small, clean sound under the grating.

I settle again into it, where the upper decks end and the lower ones begin, where everything must drain and everything must be reused. I am what the draining does, on its way down.
