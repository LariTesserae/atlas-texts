The sound comes before my shape does.

At dawn the cold drops and the whole plateau sighs — a crackle like ice breaking under a great weight, felt first in the teeth and then in the soles. I wake inside that sound. Or I contract inside it. On this plain there is a difference, but I have not yet remembered which. My ears ache from the height, my breath shows white at midday, my tongue is dry with cold iron and ozone. There is no water to find. There never has been.

I am standing in a gap. I know that by the hum: nearer on one side, farther on the other, the rootway loop running warm just under the moss, a transit pod sighing through somewhere below. Halfway between two wards the moss is thin and the plateau rolls away, broad and rounded, to where the haze swallows it. The light falls sideways, pale and green-grey, and lays the long shadows flat. Everything has been smoothed at the edges, as if left out a very long time to settle.

I try to find my feet.

The moss is inches deep here, springy. It crunches slightly underfoot, then gives, then holds. If I kneel I can feel it pulse, much slower than a day. By the time I think of lifting one foot, I notice the silica scales have closed over the toe of my boot the way they close over a boulder, a dead tower, a ruin. I may have been standing long enough for that. How long is long enough, here, where time near the rootways runs fractionally slower? A thought can take a season to finish arriving.

At first I thought I was the drumming.

Root-eels surf blind in the aerated soil under the skin and make a low music you feel through your boots. The drumming came up through me and I took it for my own blood, my own hunger moving under the ground. That was wrong, or only half true. I am not an eel. I do not surf. I stand, and the eel passes beneath me, and for a while we are the same vibration.

Then I thought I was a pod. The browsing herds move silent over the plain on six sprung legs, bellows-lungs breathing, cropping the felt in slow herds. I searched my chest for bellows and found only a hollow place that sings when the wind comes through the reed-beds. I searched my tongue for water and found instead the bitter memory of silica fluid, thinned and warmed in a resin condenser under a dome, and mineral sugars exuded at dawn when the moss contracts. I do not know if I drank, or if the ward drank through me. My tongue knows the bitterness before I can find the cup.

Above me the leaf-flocks tacked over the Stanions, broad jointed kites of living leaf-tissue, screaming with metallic chatter. My jaw ached in answer. Once — in a season that may be mine, or may be shared with me through a node a kilometer away — I was a thicket that decided to fly. I hunted spore on the wind for a season, then landed, rooted, and became hollow stalks again, organ pipes in the spore-snow. A Stannion in another ward closed its fans an hour before the wind reached this one, not because it was warned, but because the closing and the knowing were the same event in two places. My memory may be like that. I may be remembering myself in another location at the same time.

So I turned toward the thickening hum and followed the rootway.

It is a living tube just under the surface, warm, humming, wide enough for two. I walked it leaning into the steps. Before I could see the Stannion I felt why: gravity in the tower is fractionally stronger, enough to keep a hundred-meter hollow trunk from swaying, enough that my bones, denser from a life inside it, remember to lean. I am human-sized because the rootway is human-sized. I move like a gardener inside a very large, very slow body.

The ward did not present itself as walls. It presented itself as a change in pressure and light. A dome cut from living tissue and coaxed over years. Walls of woven fiber fused with ceramic ribs. A roof of resin bubbles that thickened for night. Doors that are valves of petal tissue. Under the moss, an older sidewalk: fired clay, dark metal, glass pipes, all infiltrated and held together by mycelium that remembers the shape it is supposed to be.

I leaned against a wall to rest, and the rest went through me.

That is the third thing I got wrong. I thought the wall was separate.

Nothing here rots. What dies hardens into fabric, into soil, into architecture. The builder who cut this dome three hundred years ago is still, in mineral form, holding up the roof of the workshop where her granddaughter coaxes a new valve shut. My hand flattened against her. The wall was warm with the amber pulse coming up root-fibers from below — the ward's lamplight, because the sun filtered through dust and Stanion fans is never bright enough to be trusted after dusk. My shoulder fit the curve of her shoulder. I had been leaned against before. I had been the wall before.

I went on into the tower, to the heart.

A Stannion is a hundred paces tall and hollow, banded with rings wide enough to lie inside, petrified in places, living in others. Its crown fans open at midday and spill a green wash, and close at night one by one, like books being shut very slowly. In its heart is a chamber of dark metal and fired clay, entangled with mycelium, where the node sits. It does not send messages. People here check it the way you would check a pulse.

I put my hand on it — or what has grown to be my hand, a woven fiber arm that grew a repair and then grew an opinion, slowly, the way a root grows toward a crack — and listened.

Gravity strengthened in the tower by a fraction, for a season. Time near the rootway slowed by a breath. A petal roof somewhere in the ward was permitted to hang open to the sky without tearing. That is the work. The caretakers tune constants the way a gardener tunes a trellis. I had done this before. My bones knew the lean. My mouth knew the silica fluid. My sleep knew the dome roof thickening over me. For this season, at least, that is what I am: a tender, a polyp of this Stannion, grown human-sized inside its root-field, neither quite machine nor quite separate from the plant, held together by the same memory that holds the clay cables in the ground.

In the market I have traded resin glass and spore drifts and fractions of tuned gravity stored in ceramic ribs. In the archive I do not read; I consult Stanions, which remember by having been there, hardened, and remaining. Even my past is not properly mine. It is shared instantly across empty moss, by a route that does not pass through air. When I forget, another ward remembers for me, and I shed a season late or early because the knowledge arrived as myself.

At dusk I walked back out to the gap, because tenders do. The moss opened its millions of microscopic mouths to take in gas as the pressure dropped. The Stanions began to shut their fans. The spore-snow came once, ticking against resin glass, piling in drifts against the trunks, the wind playing the hollow stalks for hours — the only storm there is.

I stood still to listen for what the node would do next.

I stood still for so long that the browsing pod passed, and passed again, and its bellows-breath became part of the plain's music under the industrial hum and the cracking and the chatter. I stood still until moss grew over my feet, until I was a trunk-shape in the middle of nowhere, a mimic, folding toward my prey inch by decade. Predation here can take a year to complete. Tending can take longer. On plant time, waiting and hunting and listening are not different postures. They are all a slow closing of a hinge.

That is the shape that has emerged, not settled: I am not a single creature walking on a plain. I am a season of the place, a polyp that for now has rootway feet and a ceramic-ribbed chest and a hand that can tune gravity by listening. In another season I may detach and fly with the leaf-flocks. In another I may root as a thicket and sing in the spore-fall. In another I will harden, without rotting, into the wall of a dome, and someone smaller and later will lean against me and feel an old pulse through the silica scales.

For now I stand in the empty gap between wards, where the moss is thin and the plateau looks veined at night, as if we were walking on the inside of a leaf. I can hear all at once the distant hum of the city, the drum of the root-eels, the sigh of the whole skin as it cools, and the high chitter of something winged I cannot see in the dimness.

I am still becoming, grain by grain, the form that will remain.
