I don’t find myself on the plain.

Nothing lives there to find. The stone is polished, the snow only sighs, the weather goes past at knee height and breaks against the rock, and the silence closes behind anything that walks. If I were looking for a footprint, or a breath freezing in front of a face, I would not find me.

The first shape I try on is too small.

A mite, in the warm seam between moss and fungus, piston-legged and armored, clicking into a mineral crack and pumping detritus backward to be processed again. Its whole world is a square meter of moss. It builds a casing from leaf-fibre and dust, abandons it by the hour because the old casing is better compost, rebuilds, locks on, pumps. No eyes worth mentioning. No voice — sound would be waste in air this thin.

It fits the work. It does not fit the memory. I have left a square meter. I have felt an updraft take me over empty miles, and I have felt the cold change its schedule for me. A mite never launches. A mite never lands somewhere new and instant. So that is a rate inside me, not me.

Then I try the leaf.

Underside faintly conductive, coppery, opening toward the aurora. When the green-grey curtains unroll overhead — never bright enough to read by, bright enough to work by — thousands of fronds angle together and a slow wave crosses the mat, takes a minute, is gone. I could be that tilt toward charged light and ozone. But the leaf only tilts, then curls to take a grazer by the foot when it lingers too long, then thins at the edge and is absorbed. Too brief a role. Too exposed. It is intake, not identity.

Then I try the bird, because I have hung that way.

Hooked by one leg into the rim of a patch, upside down in the cold while the evening-that-never-ends goes on not arriving, wings rowing so slowly above me they seem to row through solid black. The patch feeds me static, a blue-grey flicker at the point of contact no brighter than the moss itself. In return I shed skin, a feather if I still have feathers, a thin rain the mites below dismantle at once. It is the only time I am large. It is also the only time I am a client. I come, I take charge, I leave fertilizer, I haul myself back to the aurora’s edge and disappear.

I stay after the bird leaves. I am what the bird uses.

That is when I stop looking for a worker. A worker is what this place has, in thousands, and none of them is the place. Mite-click is processing. Frond-wave is harvesting. The fern curling and the fungus exuding its precise winter poison are inventory control, a thermostat, not an event. The faint steam is fermentation losing heat it cannot afford to waste. The roots tapping the pores of stone like straws, drinking black water secretly, are supply. None of it needs an engineer, and none of it accumulates an engineer. If I have anything like a mind, it is not a sentence. It is a schedule: burst when cold is most absolute, poison when grazers overshoot, grow downward into warmth and upward into light at the same time because cold, here, is the catalyst.

From inside a hollow I do not see green. I see architecture.

A courtyard of stone where the wind cannot quite enter. Moss a mattress thick, drinking fog leaf by leaf, cell by cell, exhaling damp into the layer below. Under that, the root-mat trafficking minerals up and sugars down and water sideways from pore to pore. Under that, white lacework fungi smelting basalt grain by grain, fixing nitrogen out of air too thin for anything else to bother with, sorting the result into chemical packets and piping them through hyphae without pause — there is no night here to pause for. Warm by a degree or two. Damp by a degree more. Alive at the scale of minutes inside a single square meter, while outside the hollow the plateau does nothing at all and has finished its argument with erosion long ago.

That is me as a patch: an individual that has learned to look like a population. Lush inverted. Empty miles outside, everything inside. I do not notice the visitor lying down to put an ear to the moss. That is not hostility. It is efficiency. The visitor is weather, passing at knee height.

But a patch cannot cross miles, and I have crossed miles. So I must also be the form in which I am folded small enough to be solitary.

At the rim, in the deepest dark, when the aurora is most violet and the twilight at the edge of the black sky is most permanent, the spore-columns rise. Not in spring. Spring means nothing up here. They burst on the cold’s schedule, ballistic clusters launched on the rare updrafts the plateau permits. For a few minutes a spore is the only solitary thing in this world — alone in thin air over ranges falling away into silhouette, stars visible at noon, ice cracking impossibly far off as a single muted *tok* felt more than heard, glaciers far below grinding like presses the size of valleys and sifting nutrient dust upward for a wind I will never see.

Packed inside me, not as instructions but as actual passengers: root-straws coiled, fungus lace, mite casings, the pattern of a fern rosette, sugar and mineral already sorted into packets. A factory at seed size. Instant. Fully staffed. No builder opens me. Cold does.

I land in another hollow, no bigger than a house, where fog has settled and refuses to leave and black water hides in the pores of the stone. Then I unpack in the only order that matters: skin first, moss drinking directly; hands next, roots tapping; stomach after, fungi warming themselves by fermentation, steaming very slightly into a sky that will not let it rain; workers already in place, clicking, grazing, being thinned and recycled. The whole patch shifts as one when the aurora shifts, thousands of fronds angling in concert. Deer? No. Nothing so theatrical. Turnover.

My life is three speeds at once. Minute by minute, a grazer is eaten by a leaf and becomes the next frond; a mite dies in its casing and the casing is repaired by another. Season by season, the whole patch migrates a hand’s width toward a new seam of damp stone, root by root, leaving a ghost-green trail behind that will take a century to fade. And across the years of that slow migration, edge plants die, are absorbed, are resurrected as spores in the next burst. I am not rooted in the sense of staying. I am rooted in the sense of not being able to move fast enough to be noticed.

Below me, geothermal cracks tick and exhale, warming the undersides of hollows from below, changing the seam’s dampness, changing the fermentation rate. Above me, the aurora never holds still. Between, I work in the dark, lush and cold and damp, taking everything secretly, through contacts, in small constant draws, wasting almost nothing because there is almost nothing to waste.

So where would you find me?

Not on the plain, where nothing lives.

Find me at the rim just before the burst, folded to the size of a seed and carrying a world. Find me in the warm seam just after the landing, unfolding into skin and straw and lace. Find me in the back-and-forth traffic between those two moments — the packet piped through hyphae, the spore in thin air, the patch waiting out erosion’s long patience.

Small enough to fly. Large enough to be everything.

I am not the worker. I am the workplace, when it is small enough to travel, and the worker when it stays.
