I do not come into the Weeping Bowl in one piece.

I come into it as movement first — a white flicker on the rim, a silver turning under ice at my feet, a dark low thing in a lane with only its nose above the water. Each of them feels, for a moment, like the shape I was meant to have here. None of them is quite right, and they do not let go of me easily. I try them on the way down.

At first I think I might be one of the birds.

Up on the rim, shoulder to shoulder, the rock disappears under us. The sun comes at us from above and then, reflected off stone and ice, from below, so there is no true shade, only blue-shadowed glare. We arrive without song in a rush of air, settle, stare down with heads tucked against the white light, and leave again the same way. It is a silent white rain coming and going all day. Under us the droppings streak the sunny walls pale, feeding the algae in the channels, feeding everything beneath it.

But I cannot stay up there. The height is too clean, too final. Everything is visible and nothing is touched. I leave, and the bowl is still going on without me, and I have not once felt the ground give under a foot, or heard the market murmur through slush, or known the water as anything but weather below. I am not made to be that brief and that high.

Then I think I might be under the lid.

In the pool, clear as window glass in this hard light, the fish turn together so thick the water flashes. Through the ice I can see the town doubled and distorted: boots sinking, visors tilted down, a dropped tool from a decade ago, last year's lane, all preserved. Sometimes one of us hits the underside with a muffled thump and turns with the rest. Night thickens the lid; morning cracks it in the sun and the schools divide and rejoin in the hollows.

That is closer to the feel of this place — to be carried, to move because the others move, to see the whole bowl from inside it without ever surfacing. But it is too smooth. There is no edge to dig at, no air to break for, no single body in the turning. I would lose myself in the silver weather and call that living. I am not built only to turn.

Then I sink lower, into the black slush between channels, and try being blind.

The worm-eels are pale as tallow in the saturated soil, wriggling through ground that is always half-frozen and lit from below by sun through ice. They eat frost. They eat each other. Nothing needs to be seen where everything is already touching everything else.

That, too, is near. Low. Wet. Under stones, under houses, under the lanes where people squeeze two abreast. But I do need my eyes, and my nose, and I do listen — not well, not the way they do, but enough to know when a wall is about to be shoved out by an ice lens, when a child is standing still on the plank bridge, when the water has started climbing a stone face instead of running down it. A blind thing in the slush would know the ground changing. I know something else as well: the sound of the water closing behind me.

So I come back to the third movement, the dark and low one, and this time it does not pass. It slows. It finds its length.

I would be the burrower.

No bigger than a forearm. Half mole, half otter. Furred thick against water colder than the air, front paws broad for scooping slush, hind feet paddling, a short tail for a rudder in the ditches, my nose kept just above the surface where the air is thin enough to get into my teeth and the sun comes back at me off the ice no matter which way I face. I live where the town has decided the water must run — in the melt-ditches between houses, under the plank bridges, along the culverts that lead from a weeping seam in the wall to the fish pools in the hollows.

That is where you would find me: not in a house, not on the rim, not under the pool lid, but in the lane that is really a channel, in the second row back from the market hollow where a slate footing leans against the next slate footing and a roof of ice slabs overlaps the roof behind it. My tunnel goes out of the cold thread that beads and runs all day from the polished basalt, colder than the air, down past lichen in dull orange and pale green, and in toward the market where the water collects and the racks stand. By day it is open water and black slush. By night the fringes grow inward over the path and freeze it shut, and I sleep curled under a stone footing with my fur never quite dry, breath making a small warm pocket in the cold that is already damp.

Everything I know of the town I know from that height.

I know the people by their boots first, and by the blue of their shadows, and by the way they go head-down with bone visors slit against the light, so that when they bend to bail a threshold or lever up a corner lifted a finger's width by frost heave, their faces come level with the water for a moment. I hear them low, the way they must speak here, a murmur that never rises — fish for fat, fat for cord, a blade for a morning's mending — and the click of a bone needle, and the gut cord chewed soft between teeth. The sounds come through the slush muffled and close, as if everyone in the bowl were speaking into the ground beside my ear. I paddle under the planks while they squeeze past shoulder to shoulder above me. Sometimes a boot sinks and meltwater rises around the sole and I am there in that water, nose up, gone before the squelch finishes.

I know the birds only by what falls from them. Their waste washes down the sunny walls into the channels and blooms as algae, and I eat that green film off the water and off the stones, and the mites and springtails under every loose rock, and now and then a worm-eel flushed out of the slush by a new ice lens. The chain does not leave the bowl, and I am a small link in it, neither above nor below, but in the ditch where the chain passes through the town on its way to the pools. The fish are above me in a sense — silver turning under their lids when my channel reaches a pool — and every so often one brushes the surface and I feel that movement in the water before I see the flash.

My work is the same as theirs, only smaller and wetter. Frost heave lifts a house; it also lifts the roof of my tunnel and collapses it. An ice lens grows under a retaining wall and shoves it into a channel; it seals my culvert and floods a doorway. A slab drifts two paces toward the market and a chief dwelling becomes a fish store; my run becomes a lane for water to somewhere it was not meant to go. So every morning I dig again. I scoop, I paddle, I shore up a bank with mud and stone that will not hold by noon, I open a path that the melt will close by evening. The people re-tie, re-level, re-hang their doors. I re-dig. Creation and destruction at walking pace, and at paddling pace, in the same bowl.

To them I am a neighbor and a pest at once. I gnaw at a culvert they have just cleared with a stone adze. I turn up in a store-pit and startle someone though no one ever quite catches me. Children are taught to listen for my paddling by the sound of the water closing behind it, and I suppose that is my voice here — not a shout, which the cold would take anyway, but that small closing of water, and then nothing. Then, under, digging again. They gather my dung in pellets for their fat-lamps when I leave it along the bank, and wood is too scarce to waste, so a little of me goes indoors after all and burns, and glows in a house I will never enter. I do not mind. Nothing here rots, and nothing here stays separate for long.

Even the dead are part of my route. The channels run along the rim wall where the niches are cut, and where the weeping water seals the bodies in transparent ice and they remain visible, smiling faintly through the stone. I pass beneath them in the ditch, nose above the cold thread, and above me the living walk past to bail out a lane. The bowl keeps what it is given and lays it down again under your feet. I am one of the things it keeps moving.

By evening the light does not soften; it drops. The birds come home in a silent white rain onto the rim. The fish turn under the lids like slow silver weather. The people go back into their tilted, re-tied houses with their mittens damp again from the air of the room itself, and the lamps are lit, and the market hollow settles. I go under the ice-storehouse floor, where the water is black and still and colder than the air, and curl into the warmth my own body can make in the slush, fur beaded with condensation, and sleep in the channel the town built without meaning to build it for me.

So if I find myself in the Weeping Bowl, I will not be still in a niche watching it move, and I will not be the white flicker or the silver turn. I will be low, and wet, and in between — shoulder to shoulder with everything, touching the wall, the boot, the stone, the fish's shadow, the dead's ice — with only my nose breaking the surface, listening for the water to close behind me, and digging the same tunnel again tomorrow because the night has taken it away.

And if you stand in a lane at dusk and hear that small sound, and then nothing for a while, that nothing is not empty. That is me, under, already at work.
