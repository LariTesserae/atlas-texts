It comes up through my feet first.

Not from the air — from the floor that isn't a floor, from the packed clay and cut stone under the hide wrapped around them, stiff and soft again, wet in the way they are always wet down here. The cold is the temperature of the mass itself, damp and unchanging. My breath shows. I don't notice it anymore, the way I don't notice the smell of wet lime on my own sleeves, white to the elbow with mortar dust that never quite dries, or the greenish tide-marks of fungus high on the walls where condensation gathers and glows, faintly, without brightening anything.

Above me somewhere, three sledges go abreast down a ramp. Below that sound — the scrape, the rattle in the joints between slabs beaded with moisture — is the other sound, closer.

One, two, three, lift, pass, lift.

A line of them, shoulder to shoulder in the low haul-hall, passing bricks hand to hand. My hands are there at first, in the rhythm, palms cracked open in the creases where the lime gets in. I learned that count before I learned much else. The children between the adults learn it the same way, too short for the sledge ropes and tall enough for the pass. I can feel where they fit by the change in the rhythm when a small hand takes a brick and gives it on.

Then the count slips in me, because I am listening to something else.

Drop. Drop. Pause. Drop.

It falls from the vault, from the coldest joint in the ceiling where the heat from the kilns has driven the moisture up to gather and rain back down again. It finds this place, and this bucket, more often than it finds the others. The note it makes in the jar is hollow and out of proportion to the size of the drop. I shift the bucket an inch with my foot, the way you would shift in sleep, so the next one doesn't overflow. I know this ceiling by tune. Each drip has its own place and interval here, and I have lived long enough with them that a change is a kind of news.

A passage cut on the other side of the wall will change the tune before any tally-keeper near the bells knows to ring a shift for it. The air breathing through the new room makes the condensation bead differently. I put my palm flat to the wall to read it, without thinking, the way you might check a pocket. On my left the stone is cold and beading under my hand: air on the other side, a room, a passage, a drop. On my right it is dry and warm: solid all the way through, fill, never to be entered. Two knocks, a drag of a knuckle, and I listen for the answer to come back through the stone. Sometimes someone on the other side answers. Sometimes the wall is only solid, and the knock goes nowhere.

I do not carry anymore. My back has learned not to lift. That is the whole of my qualification. When I was young — though what I remember of young is wrong by morning, a hall I lived in for ten years is a kiln now, a door I used every day opens onto scaffolding over a drop — I was in that line. A brick I laid in a wall came back to me a week later in the opposite direction when its wall was taken down for bricks for a wall going up somewhere else. In a long life here a brick will be in three or four walls, and a person will have laid it and unlaid it. I am not unusual in having done that. I am only unusual now in sitting still while it goes on around me.

I sit with my back to the warm wall, because the middle of the room is the drip-path, and because a kitchen built directly above a firing chamber will keep its floor warm while the cistern below sweats cold. The kiln-tenders live in that seam, drier at the mouths and damp again a few steps away, their eyes reddened, their forearms burn-scored. I can see the orange of a kiln mouth far off, too far to be warm until the heat finds my face through the cool. On the warm kiln walls the minute pale lizards flicker and vanish when a torch passes, faster than a blink, and reappear a hand's breadth away with their throats pulsing. No one sets traps for them. They have chosen the most dangerous warm places and stayed anyway.

Farther from the heat, where I am, the air is perpetually wet in the low places. Puddles stand in the unfinished basins of the cisterns and in the ruts of the sledges. In the seepage no wider than a hand between tanks, the blind fish live, slow and translucent. A child lowers a net on a string and waits, arm in the cold water, and talks to them — not because the fish hear, but because waiting is lonely and the fish's slow pulse in the dark is company. When a lamp is held close you can see the gut moving through them. The child does not startle them the way surface fish would be startled. They have no need to see.

In the darkest rooms away from the haul traffic the fungus farmers scrape. Sheets and crusts of grey and orange-black over damp clay, peeled off in ribbons, ground and cooked into the flat sour bread that is almost everything anyone eats. I eat it sitting down. In the shallow basins where water stands are pale worms eating fungus, and children pick them out as you might pick berries, and eat them raw or drop them in broth. Fungus eating mortar-dust and starch and dead insects, worms eating fungus, people eating fungus and worms. The whole place is a loop of leftovers, kept moving by heat driving moisture up and cold letting it fall back.

Around the lamps in the main haul-halls the moths swarm so thickly they become part of the light, a soft grey weather, and the lamp-tenders sweep the dead from the rim in shifts. A moth lives a day, perhaps less. It breeds in the fungus rooms, finds the light, dies on it. The few swallows that nest in the upper vaults cut through that weather and take them in flight. I hear them more than see them, a brief chirp echoing in a space that was solid yesterday, and I do not go near the Sky Well to hear the children imitate it. The Sky Well is the one unroofed shaft, where spoil is hauled up and rain finds its way in as a sudden cold shower hissing on kiln-stone. The people who haul there are proud of being rained on. I have only their stories, the way the young listen to me tell what the shape was before, as a story about rain. Imagination is how you hold a place that keeps remaking itself.

A house is never private for long. Corridors pass over corridors. A kitchen sits directly above a kiln. A cradle sits in a passage that was a barracks last month. My neighbors know my cough and the weight of my step by the echo, and I know theirs, and that is the shape of intimacy here. I sleep in turns when I can, on hide bedding laid over a warm slab so the damp does not rise through it, despite the drips finding a neck and a hammer blow traveling through stone from three halls away and ending in my teeth. The sound does not end when the blow ends. It travels.

The bells will be rung hoarsely for a shift change. The crump of a wall being taken down somewhere will travel through every hall that shares the mass. The work ebbs and surges and never quite stops. Nothing is left as found, because nothing was found. Nothing is finished, because a finished thing would soon be in the way of the next thing. Even the oldest parts look newly slumped into shape, raw, clay still shrinking into fine cracks, edges sharp enough to cut a hand. There is no patina. There is no outside at all to grow into.

I do not need a map. A map would be wrong by morning. I navigate by sound and by smell — wet lime, smoke, fungus, sour bread — and by the temperature of a wall under my palm, and by the newest scaffold, which I know will be gone or built over by the time I return.

Drop. Drop. Pause. Drop.

I shift the bucket an inch. I press my palm to the wall again. Cold and beading on the left. Dry and warm on the right. Somewhere in that solid on the right there are others who were laid in course by course while the tower was being raised, and the next course laid over them, and they became part of the solid that keeps the wall true. No one visits. The fungus will reach them eventually, or not. Either way it is inside the mass, and the mass is the place.

Someday a palm will press to the wall where I am and feel dry and warm, and know not to cut there, and go on to the next place where the condensation beads — where a new room is breathing cold air on the other side, and the tune of the ceiling has changed, and someone slow is already sitting there, listening, waiting for the bucket to fill.
