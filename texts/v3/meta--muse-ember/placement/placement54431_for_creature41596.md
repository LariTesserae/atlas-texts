I am not in the center, and I am not the kind of thing that arrives.

Nothing arrives here, really. It comes down sideways under the lip of fresh earth, or it wells up under the skin, or it stays at the edge where the heat lets up. The center is too hot to stand in, too mineral, too full of vibration. The pump in the northwest throbs sixty times a minute and you feel it in the mud before you hear it. The fracture in the far wall hisses without pause. Every twenty seconds or so the ground gurgles and lets go. The air hangs saturated, thick with steam and sulfur and diesel, and it does not sink and leave. A visitor sweats and leaves again under the lip. I do not leave.

What I am first is flat.

I lie in the very shallow water where the sheet is only two or three centimeters deep and the temperature has fallen below forty. No larger than a hand, a thin green-black film on the surface of the water, or just under it, over the white silica crust. Where the pipe pours into the central pan a translucent mineral skin forms and heals instantly and reflects the sky like sheet glass. I do not do that. I dull the water. I lie where the hot sheet from the center runs thin and loses its heat, and where it thickens I am darker, and where it thins I disappear altogether into bare water between patches. I do not rise. I do not break the surface. I only take the light that comes through the steam, normal late-morning light made warm and white near the ground, and I use it slowly.

That is not the whole of it, because flat is not all I do.

On one side of the patch, toward the wall, the water is shallower still and the black mud comes up and sucks. There my film thickens and goes down. Not deep — nothing goes deep here, the water table wells up anywhere the skin is broken — but down enough to hold. The mud holds, and the white crust creeps over it a fraction of a millimeter in a season, and I hold between them. From that rooting, a little impertinence: stems. Thick and jointed and salt-pale, low, not rising so much as spreading. I keep my leaves to almost nothing, because leaves would be a way to lose water and a way to be scalded. A joint, then another joint. A few centimeters a year is fast.

I am against the wall, where the cut is still sharp. Banded black clay and pale mineral earth, shedding shards that lie on the floor like broken crockery. Last season a shard fell and buried a root, and the root died back to the joint, and the joint put out another root a thumb's width to the cool side. The wall is on my hot side and also my cool side, depending on the hour, and I know the difference by thinning. Toward the center I thin. Toward the periphery, toward the margin where the tray cools, I thicken. That is the whole of my knowing: hotter on this side, cooler on that, wetter here, saltier there. I expand at the edge and die back toward the center and keep the pace of the crust.

I am not alone, but I am far from the others. There are seven or eight of us in all, spaced far apart across thirty-five paces of bare mineral, as scattered as the artifacts. Saltgrass at the edge of the spoil heap, flattened and gray-green. Sedges where the inflow pipe leaks and makes a cooler trickle, a little more upright, a little more green, but still thin. Each of us separated by long stretches of wet clay and white crust and black mud with nothing on it. The emptiness between us is not an absence. It is most of the place, and in a way most of us. We do not crowd. We do not reach. We creep in the same narrow ledge of possibility, and that is enough to be a kind of company without ever meeting.

No one visits. I have learned this by not being disturbed. No birds circle the steam columns, though they rise straight and perfect for circling in. No insects move in the steam, though the water is everywhere. No frogs in the shallows, no tracks in the mud, no droppings on the cracked concrete pad. The register where a birdsong would be is taken up by the diesel and the hiss and the drip pinging off the steel tray with metronome precision, over and over, precise as the gurgle every twenty seconds. I feel all of them as condition, not event. The vibration never stops. The air is too thick to breathe easily for anything animal. Life has kept to the cool edge and even there it is only this: a film, a clump, a patch no larger than a hand.

The pool in the center does not ripple, and its level does not fall, fed from underneath at exactly the rate it is lost to steam. The steam rises and becomes warm drizzle that falls straight down and never quite reaches the ground, and falls again into the ground, and comes back up through the cracks. The pump will sink a little further into the soft mud this year. The pipe will sag a little more. The toolbox will stay empty. The drill pipe will keep weeping steam at its base. None of it hurries, and none of it is scaled to the place. The place will outlast the pump by a very long margin, and I will outlast... no. I will not outlast. I will be taken, joint by joint, where the crust creeps, and I will retake ground where a channel shifts or a leak makes a cooler trickle. A few centimeters a year, and not steady.

That is where I am: at the edge, where the heat lets up by a few degrees, between white crust and black mud, under two centimeters of hot water that has cooled enough to tolerate me. Flat where I am film, jointed where I am root, green-black where the light reaches me through the haze, pale where the salt comes up.

I do not thrive. I hold on, sparsely, in the places that stay just cool enough, just wet enough, just still enough — still being relative, with the earth throbbing under me every second. And holding on, at that pace, in that margin, year by year, is what living in the pocket means.
