The boiler is sighing again.

Down in the foundry first, where a boiler ought to sigh, and then, a breath later, up above me where there is nothing above me but cloud. My hand is on the rope between two lamps and I do not move until the second sigh has finished. It is a small thing. It is the whole of how I was taught to answer anything at all.

I am damp before I have done anything to deserve it. My wool is damp, though I hang it by the door and wear the other coat in turn and take both off at night and find them in the morning exactly as damp as I left them, only now with a little moss beginning on the left shoulder where the stairwell plant has leaned too close for too long. My hair is damp. The skin between my fingers is softened. My breath shows indoors. I have lived here long enough that I no longer think of dry as a condition other people have; I think of it as a rumor from the lowland, like rain.

I do not hurry. Hurry would be wasted here, and impolite.

When I was small my mother made me wait for the second sound before I answered a bell. A bell in a district I could see would be struck once, and its echo would answer before the rope was pulled again, and I would be halfway through a reply before the reply had room to go out. Now I wait. I speak, my voice goes thick in the cool air and comes back off the blank slab across the street, and the person opposite me nods and only then answers, so that his answer too will have its echo. A conversation here has a tide to it. No one is ever startled by a loud noise. We have already heard it coming, once.

In the morning I fill my bottle at the public tap and carry it up three levels to a riveted block that has been refaced twice, three inches out each time, so that the corridor is narrower than the stair that led to it. The bottle slops against my leg. Then I go back down and out to work, hand over hand along the rope, counting lamps.

I do not count distance. Distance lies. A housing tower two kilometers off looks close enough to touch on a clear, damp day, and I walked toward it for an hour once when I was younger, thinking to save time, and watched it withdraw the whole way. Now I count lamps, and the feel of the flywheel turning once a minute through the slab under my feet, and the places where the factories stop and the lamps stop and there is only fog and wet stone and the sound of my own boots for two hundred meters. Sometimes the rope meets a lamp that is not on any map, burning in empty space above a drop, lighting nothing. I touched it once, out of curiosity. It was warm. I marked it in my head and went on.

By the time I reach the net fields I am as wet as I will get all day, and I am one of the wettest people in Varkesh.

The nets are copper wire strung between poles like sails to comb mist from the air. All day they drip into long troughs, into cisterns, into the bottles people fill at taps and carry home. I walk the slow rows mending them, reweaving the wire where it has thinned, where a goat has rubbed, where a raven has pulled a length loose for some raven purpose. The same tear comes back in the same place between pole nine and pole ten, on the net I have tended for eleven years now. I know its particular slack. My cuffs carry the damp home with me. My housesmells of wet iron and hot oil and canteen cooking that never quite dries out either. Oil lives under my nails in a way that has never washed out. My leather apron has been re-riveted at the strap and then again, and the front pocket is newer than the rest of it and therefore older, if you follow the logic of this place.

Under pole nine there is a tank where the troughs gather, and in it a blind fish circles palely, all day, feeling the flywheel through the steel. I know it by its tank, the way I know a neighbor by his bell. It is old now. It follows the vibration of my hand more than my hand itself. When I fill my bottle I scoop from the edge and leave it its water. People do not drain a tank that has fish in it. That would be an eviction.

Late morning the goats come down through the net fields in their shaggy, sure-footed way, cropping moss off the hot metal in the casting yard while it is still cool enough to bear. The foundry men let them. A goat with a mouthful of warm moss is a goat not eating the nets. The castings sit in the yard waiting — anchor plates and counterweights and brake-wheels the size of doors, parts for a mountain that is still rising, to pin it, to persuade the strata to settle. The geology is too young to receive them, so they wait, and while they wait moss grows over their bolt holes and a raven nests in one of them and builds twigs around the work. Ten years ago I dropped my lunch tin beside the third plate. It is still there, sharp and clean and half skinned over by new rock gathering a millimeter at a time in the damp. The workers walk around it. I walk around it. I note it and continue. We are making fossils in real time and everyone knows and no one stops.

At noon I stand in two skyshades at once — one cast down from the pale sun smearing behind cloud, one cast up from the cloud sea below — and a mountain fox crosses the empty district as if it owned the street. It does, in a practical sense. I step aside for it not from fear but from a respect for schedule. It has always crossed at noon. It will cross again tomorrow. The children watching from the terrace will see the second shadow of a bird pass not over the plateau but over the clouds beneath us, and they will not find it strange, only true.

After that the bells begin, all across the plateau and out of time with each other, marking shifts that no one else is marking. Their echoes come back before they are struck again. I have learned to do my timed things by other departures: by the swifts pouring from the vent stacks at dusk in black curtains, screaming as they rise; by the flywheels, which never stop; by the ravens in the cranes, who steal a bearing a week. That is the raven tax. The crane minders replace the bearing, they check the nests for jammed twigs, they do not move the nest. A crane stands over the rooftops not lifting, only holding, its hook swaying a fraction in the wind, and the raven goes on building around the machinery and the machinery goes on turning around the raven.

Moths make weather around every always-on lamp. You walk through it with your mouth closed as a matter of course. They settle in your hair and on your damp wool and you do not brush them away until later, because there is nowhere dry to put them. Mice run the looped rails at their different unlevel levels as if the rails had been laid for them. Pigeons live in the scaffolding that has stood for twenty years over inhabited brick — a trellis and a storage cage and a pigeon house at once, because a building inside is never finished enough to take the scaffold down.

In the afternoon I eat in the canteen, where the apples from the walled gardens between the casting sheds are sour and slow and never quite ripe in the smear of sun, and the fern in the drainpipe outside the window is bright and frilled and drinking the condensation that runs there. A tree fern on the roof I have watched for three seasons has shown no difference at all. It does not grow so much as accrue. Moss forms lawns and then meadows and then soft green forests a few inches high across rooftops and abandoned platforms and the shoulders of statues. Lichen has painted the fresh faces of the slabs rust and gold and a blueish green that glows in the dim light. No one commissioned it and no one erases it. Nothing here is scoured. Nothing is cleared.

On the way home the fog thickens between districts and my voice comes back to me off stone before anyone answers. In the few sheltered hollows where the slabs intersect just so there are real pools, black and still, green with algae, and at night the frogs sing in a loud wet chorus that answers the boiler, and the boiler answers it a second later from above.

My door is laced shut with moss again. It was laced shut last spring. I tear the green lace with my shoulder, feeling the damp stone give a little, and by next week the moss will have begun again as if the tearing had been a kind of pruning. People open it anyway. I open it anyway. Inside, the corridor is narrower than the stair, the bottle slops, the plant on the stairwell leans a fraction closer to my left shoulder, and the moss there is a little more established than it was this morning. I do not brush it off.

At night night is only day made darker and the lamps more distinct. The whole plateau becomes a constellation of points across a black wet plain, each district haloed in its own fog. The sound does not lessen. The flywheels keep turning, one revolution a minute, felt in the feet through wet stone. Somewhere a swift screams at a moth. Somewhere a goat steps carefully between slabs that have never been rounded by weather. Somewhere out in the emptiness between settlements a new slab rises a centimeter, and no one hears it except as a tiny, deep click in the stone under their feet.

I stand still where the lamps stop, between one district and the next, and I wait for the second sound of the click. The inhabitants come to me, as they do to anyone who stands still long enough. A moth settles. A raven shouts from the crane. The fish in the tank under pole nine circles palely, hearing the boiler twice and never having seen the light. The moss on my shoulder begins, patiently, to be a meadow.

Varkesh is not finished with me, and it is not falling down around me.

It is accruing me.
