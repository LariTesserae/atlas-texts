I am not swimming.

I am not in the water, really. I am on the thing that holds the water. That is where I live. There is a difference, and everything here knows it without saying it.

The cold comes up through my feet first, up through the steel, before anything else does. The hum comes the same way. Thump. Thump. Thump. The pumps in the pump house working without hands, driving the tide through the sluice gates, and the sound does not travel to me through the water so much as through the plating I am standing on. It runs up my legs. It has been running up my legs for as long as I have been here.

Above me the light is green-grey and it does not change its mind. At twenty meters the blue went first and this stayed, a filtered dim, shafting in through the grated roof of the turbine hall in places so thin it makes the algae glow instead of grow. Somewhere across the empty street a sodium lamp in its glass housing is still blinking red, amber, red, amber, patiently, on a tidal generator nobody tends. I see it with one eyestalk while the other watches the dark behind me. My eyes move on their own. That is useful in a place that is mostly empty water and mostly dark behind you.

My home is the curve of a pipe elbow.

It is not much. The elbow bends down into the turbine scroll, and the inside of the bend is hollow and black and out of the current, which matters more than it sounds. The water flows past, not through. I sit in the curve with my claws tucked in, brown though in this light brown is only a kind of dark, and I wait for the flush.

Every so often — twenty minutes, an hour, when the sluices open — the whole bowl pours. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it, if you have a sternum. I feel it as a change in pressure against my shell and in the way the fronds of algae unspooling from the pipe joints high up near the roof start to wave all at once, in the same direction, like curtains. Below me on the wall the mussels open. The whole crust opens. Barnacles between them open. Anemones that have taken over the valve wheels and the rivet heads out there open their pale tentacles in the current and catch what the current is carrying. That is when I eat. The flush brings food past my door, and when the current changes I come out sideways, in the crab way, and shuffle down the steel to the mussel bed at the base of the wall and feed there, over concrete that was poured and plated and riveted and then pitted and bored and smoothed again by things like me until it looks like limestone.

I go back. That is the whole of my territory. A few meters of built surface, plated seam, pipe, wall. The street of flooded asphalt between the turbine hall and the tanks runs empty in front of me and I almost never cross it. When the herring come through — hundreds, turning as one body, a silver weather system darkening the water and then lightening it — I watch from the mouth of the elbow. They do not feed in the street. They pass through it. They are gone around the shadow of the gantry the way a rumor is gone. If I were one of them I would not be here now. The middle of the bowl is uninhabited. Nothing resident swims out in it. I have never been out in it except once, at the beginning.

I am a crab. I was not born here. No one with any choice is born here.

I came in on a pour, a larva, no bigger than the period at the end of a sentence, carried over the street where nothing lives and over the open water between the tanks where nothing lives, until I hit a wall. Then I felt for something built with my antennae and I settled. A barnacle larva feels for steel. A mussel larva finds the crust. I found the elbow, and an elbow is an apartment, and I decided, once, not to leave. Most of the residents of the bowl are like that. Immigrants who decided once not to leave.

There are thousands of us in the pipework and the scroll and the hollow legs of the crane and the pump house with its doors open like a mouth. I hear them clicking in the dark when the current swings. I hear them before I ever see them. But each hollow is its own. I know the bass under the catwalk the way something knows the exact angle of a shadow. He hangs there motionless for an hour at a time, fins barely moving, gills opening slowly in the cold, facing the current, waiting for a crab or a shrimp or a straggler from the school to be carried past. He is older than he looks and fat and slow, grown at the pace of cold water. When he turns his head I freeze on the wall with my claws tucked tighter, and the whole mussel bed seems to hold still with me. Most life happens a meter away from him, carefully. Mine does.

I have left something of myself in the back of the elbow. Once, in the dark where nobody can watch, I molted. The old shell split and a smaller, soft thing stepped out of it and stood there in the same hollow, and within days the amphipods had picked the old one clean in the corner. It became substrate. Everything here does, eventually. Upper layers of mussel shell bury the oldest mussels. My old carapace is still there, in a sense, part of the apartment. I am not that. I am the thing that came out of it and stayed. I am still in the same curve of steel. I will not leave it.

Across the street, under an overturned tank, there is a thin trembling pocket of air, silver, unreachable. It is the only air left in the whole place. I have never needed it. I do not think of it as air. I think of it as a bright line that does not move. The tank it is under still says, in letters eroded to suggestions, DO NOT SMOKE. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. I do not read them. They outlive their purpose and then they do not matter. The living things here do not need the labels.

The pumps change key as a second one kicks in. The hall pings, a soft metallic ticking, as the cold contracts the metal. A shrimp pulses in the grit between the slabs below — concrete dust and shell and droppings and crushed brick, which is the silt here, though it was never silt — and its eye catches the lamp for an instant, red, amber, then it is gone. The algae at the roof sway. The school does not come back. Nothing is static and nothing is frantic. It is the pace of cold water, and I move at that pace now, sideways, clumsy-handed in the cold, in no hurry at all.

This place was not built for me. I was not built for this place. In the long time the water has had to work us both over, the corners have rounded enough that neither of us notices the mismatch anymore.

I tuck my claws in. I turn one eyestalk to the empty street and one to the dark behind me. I wait for the water to come past my door again.
