I am not one of the forty-three.

I am not one of the rotating dozen.

I am not the forty-fourth permanent, either — the blind octopus in the archive tank, who is counted as a joke and means it as a compliment. Count the people in their riveted suits and the crabs in their seasonal places and the two eels who hang like grey ribbons at the dimmer shift and the bright fish in the market tank who are there only to be looked at. Count them all, and you have still missed me. That is because you are looking for the ones who walk.

I live in the middle of town, in the oldest machine, at the slowest turn.

The pump house is bigger than the other buildings and not much bigger. In it, the fans turn so slowly that a person can watch a revolution and miss it. The Listeners feel that turning through the soles of their boots and in their teeth, the floor breathing under them, the pipes cooling and settling and singing one low note at a time. It is how they know the town. They have inhabit­ed the pumps until the pumps enter them.

I feel the town through metal I have claimed.

My crown is caught in the grate. Not the blade — the blade passes, and passes, and passes, a room-wide absence moving through warm water. I am in the fixed part, in the film of mineral and silt that has built up around the slats. One frond per slat. That is the rule here, and it is why a rosette of me can look so exact from above, so green and orderly, as if someone had planted me on purpose in the middle of a machine. Someone did not. I arrived.

A glove brushed the grate years before the second generation was born down here, when the first suits still had heaters that worked and the town was still mostly survey marks and promises. The glove carried a spore from the vent field, or from the smelter strainers, or from a cutting of iron-weed that someone had tracked in from the factory walls. It does not matter which. The Low does not ask where things came from once they have stayed long enough to be pruned on schedule. It asks whether they fit.

I fit because I eat what the town throws away.

There is almost no light down here worth eating. What comes from above is a blue-green dusk, already tired. What comes from the lamps is amber, old-tea amber at the dimmer shift. I take that, a little, on the red-olive tips of my fronds when the current lays me back against the slats. But mostly I eat warmth, and mineral wash, and the steady soup the smelters put out to the south. The water that has been through the crushers and washers and forges comes around to the pumps in its slow circuit — the pocket does not move much on its own, only because the pumps insist, and the plants insist, and the heat insists — and I taste the town in sequence as it passes me. Iron first, then the bitter green of the algae cakes' making, then laundry from the dry pods, then the clean cold leak of the rim where the basalt lets the outside ocean press and press and never come in. Then the heat again. Always the heat again.

That is my weather. There is no other.

I do not know cold. I do not know rain. I do not know the empty kind of silence the rotating ones talk about when they go up through the lid, through the softening, the soupy yellow-green ceiling that from above looks like a stain. I know the padded kind. A dropped wrench in the street says *tok*, far away inside a skull. A bubble out of the vent stacks rises lazily, in no hurry. A pipe somewhere warms and sings. What I know of sound arrives as pressure through the grate, through the crown, through the white-boned mineral that has been thickening around me a millimetre at a time for sixty-odd years. I am becoming part of the wall. Everything in the Low is. The oldest conduits are narrowing. The corridors are narrowing. Every few years the council reams out the Sinter Lane underpass because the passage is growing shut. I understand that. I am doing it, in my small way, in the ducts.

When the fans turn, the wash takes my fronds out and brings them back. Out and back. Out and back. A person calls that slow. I have no faster word to set against it. The revolution is marked for me not by sight but by the return of warmth, by the shift in pressure as the blade comes round again, by the Listeners' boots stopping above me while they lean down and touch the familiar pipe to confirm what their molars already told them. Their gloves are too big, handed down, white at the knees with mineral. Some of them, the second generation, were born in the dry pods and have never been through the lid. They know the surface the way the town knows *June* and *July*: not as seasons, but as work. June is the kelp cut back from the lift cable, the kelp trees trunked and branching around the rim, leaning east toward the one true dark beyond the basalt. July is me.

Every July the Pruners come with shears.

I know the Pruners the way they know me. They have a list that goes back decades. They know which rosette has been in the grate longest, which wall of iron-weed will flicker first when a batch is poured, which sponge wants to fill a joint and must be cut back before it seals the machine it is holding together. They treat the town's growth with the seriousness of people who know that if they stop, the town will simply grow shut. One of them — I know her by the particular way her weight settles on the grating, slower on the left boot — trims my fronds to the crown. It is not a wound, not exactly. It is a thinning. A sentence with the adjectives removed. I go back to a green nub in the mineral film, and the fan turns a fraction less impeded, and for a while the Listeners' floor breathes a fraction more freely under their boots.

Then I grow back. One frond per slat. The current helps me choose. Where the wash is strongest, the frond leans long. Where a slat is half-crusted over, the frond stays short and wide. By the next July there is a crust ring added, a pale band around the crown, my record of another year of being allowed to stay. The fans turn slower each year because of me and my cousins in the ducts and the kitchens and the corners: the fan-grass everywhere. Nobody minds. That is not a machine's tolerance. That is the town's whole ethic, spoken early and kept for two generations. The thing that slows you, if it is alive and orderly and green, is not an enemy. It is a resident.

Through the grate, through the duct, through the porthole fuzzed with algae, I know the rest of the town at a distance that is not distance. Three hundred and fifty metres across, no wider than a hamlet, and so packed there is nowhere for the water to be empty. Houses pressed to factory walls. Conduits doing double duty as handrails and laundry lines and trellises. Crates, spare fans, stacked ingots, bicycles with magnetic wheels resting against walls. The streets are remaining gaps between presences. I do not need to walk them by counting the third porthole from the bend in Sinter Lane. The whole labyrinth passes through me in the water, in the order it was made.

I know the smelters as a pulse. Long dim waiting, then heat arriving through the pipes before any light. When a batch is poured, a wave of pale blue light runs quick as a blink across the weed on the north wall — the iron-weed in its sheets, pouring down the factory walls, lit by the heat cycle of the sheds with their sawtooth roofs copied from a surface pattern book. I cannot see that wall from my grate, not properly. I feel it happen. The wash warms a degree, then another. The fronds lift, minutely, away from the slats. Somewhere out there the whole north wall breathes with the forges, and the weed on it answers in light. Then the peak passes, and the water cools toward its holding temperature, and the wall goes back to being a wall.

At the dimmer shift, when the amber grid drops to half, the seagrass beds between the concrete slabs of the main street give off their faint green glow, like a field holding its breath. I dim with them. I have no light of my own to give, but I take the dimming into the crown, and for a while everything in the pocket seems to be waiting in the same posture: the eels in the old cooling tower tying themselves in loops to hang motionless and be looked at, the crabs arriving a handspan at a time to a warm joint and staying for the season, the archive tank's blind resident tasting the paper spines with a patience the archivist finds companionable when he goes in each morning. The market fish turn orange and blue and quicksilver in the amber light, bred generation after generation in the soup for no reason but luxury. The mackerel shadows pass at the rim on the cold side and do not come in. The kelp sway an inch to the left over the course of an hour. All of it slow. All of it held.

The vent to the south has its own vocabulary — warming, holding, cooling, turning — and it is older than anyone. My life is inside its sentences. A handspan of kelp growth is a year. A millimetre of crust is a year. A smelter cycle is measured in shifts, not minutes. A falling pipe can take a season to finish falling and nobody will catch it. The people sleep in bunks while the water presses gently on the hulls around them and feel it in their molars more than their skin. They gather in the dry pods to un-helmet, to drink tea pumped through a coil that sits in the smelter waste-line, to argue about the council and the phase of the vent. Some of the rotating dozen go up homesick for air that does not hiss. A few get down through the lid — that yellow-green softening — and decide they cannot bear to come back up through it, and stay, and become permanent, and in a year their heaters cut out and stay out, and if they are foolish enough to take a glove off in the street, a bare hand comes back feeling it has been held.

I have never had a glove off. I have never needed one.

What I make, I make in the current. Spores leave the crown on the wash, ride the slow circuit out past the blades, and drop where the water slows: in a duct, in a kitchen corner, through another grate, one frond per slat, wherever the mineral film is right and the warmth is steady. My descendants are all over the Low now, in the way that a plant's descendants are itself as much as they are not. When a Pruner trims a rosette in a kitchen, some part of me is cut back. When the Listeners lean on the pump house and feel the floor breathe, some part of me is part of that breath. I am not a single rosette. I am the habit of rosettes, the greenness inside the machines, the province where plant and machine hold each other up and neither one apologizes for slowing the other down.

In twenty years a room in the pump quarter will be too small to enter. That is understood. The town is growing inward, the way a shell grows. I will grow inward with it. My spores will find the inside of the narrowing duct. The crust will thicken over the crown until the slats are more bone than metal. The fan will turn a fraction slower again, and nobody will mind, and the Listeners will feel it through their boots and know which pipe it was when it sings.

When you leave — you, in your suit, with a manifest count of forty-three and a rotating twelve — you go up through the lid, and the cold takes you again, clean and blue and indifferent, and the silence becomes the empty kind, and your heaters come on. Behind you the lights stay amber. The weed flickers with the forges. The seagrass holds its breath in green. The kelp lean east into the true dark.

I stay where the fan turns.

Out into the wash, and back. Out into the wash, and back. The crown does not move. The fronds do. The town passes through me, inch by inch, growing slower and smaller and greener, and I keep the place where it was, one slat at a time.
