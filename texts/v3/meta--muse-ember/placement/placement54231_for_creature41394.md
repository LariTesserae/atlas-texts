I find myself first on the ridge, because that is where I think a self should be: above, looking back.

The light is flat and does not change. The clouds do not commit to moving. Three valleys away the pale roofs are still there, and the stone pens, and the green mat closing over the last paving. My breath shows and fades though the sun is on my neck. I stand for an hour and take it in all at once, as if taking it in were a way of belonging to it.

It is not. From the ridge everything is composition, and nothing down there asks to be composed. The pools glitter in their hollows. The bead under the ledge by my foot is swollen to its perfect size. The sheep are chewing in a pen three valleys off. I can see it all, and I am not in any of it. My own steps, when I finally go down, are too loud on the slab street, and each one rings a little, because the stone is fresh.

I try the town, because the town looks as if it has been kept for me.

The street is absurdly wide for one person. The houses are spaced far apart and stand with their small thick doors pegged shut, turf roofs green-gold, windows slits. I open a door to exactly the same degree a damp hand opened it yesterday and close it again. Inside, nothing needs me. The field inside its low wall is ripe and will stay ripe. The workshop is charred and clean in the same moment. A granary door waits to be opened and closed to exactly the same degree.

I carry a basket between two houses a long walk apart. I set it down where it was set down yesterday. I pick up a flint hammer where it was dropped, still sharp, and stand at a lintel that is half-carved — a line, a spiral begun. The adze rises and falls. The chips at my feet are exactly as many as my work requires.

And I want the next line. That is my trouble. I stand there wanting the spiral to continue, wanting the lintel to become finished, wanting the basket to arrive somewhere it has not been before. In this place the wanting is a noise. I can soften my steps, I can learn to make them smaller on the slabs, but I am still standing upright in a world that lives low, still expecting movement to mean progress.

So I try watching instead. That seems closer.

At the edge of town I stand by a condensation wall of woven fiber strung between posts, where the damp air is caught and held. On the waxy leaves below, the beads gather. They swell and do not fall. My sleeves are wet from brushing through fern and moss. I watch the beads. I watch smoke from a kiln go up in a straight column and stop, like a bead. For a while I tell myself this is enough: to watch, to keep the equilibrium by not disturbing it.

But even there I am outside the damp. The damp is in my sleeves, not in me. The beads are on the leaves, not under my skin. And I am still too tall for the place. The plateau does not hold its life up high, in houses and on ridges. It holds it down, in the green.

I go where the green pours between the sharp stones.

It is a mat thick as a mattress in the sheltered places, ferns unfurled to the size they will keep forever, grasses that do not bend when my feet sink into them. It smells of wet stone and moss and animal fur. It is cool to the touch even in the flat sun. Everything here has found the exact soil it can trap in a crack and filled it exactly, and stopped.

I crouch. Then I sit, because sitting is a smaller shape than standing and the moss receives me without flattening. The beetles go by, shiny and slow, black-green in the still light, making no sound against the stems. A ground bird, mottled exactly like the slabs, does not flush until I am almost on top of it, and then it does not really flush at all; it steps aside, a degree, and is stone again. In a hand-pool between tilted slabs a frog waits with only its eyes above the surface, grey-brown as wet rock, blinking seldom. The pool reflects the sky and my face, and then the eyes.

And there is a snail.

I had seen it before, of course, the way you see things here, by coming back to them. Its trail was silver yesterday in the moss under an overhang, and it is silver still today. The snail itself is only a little further along. Its shell is damp and bright. Its feelers are raised as if listening to the still air. It inches, and the inching is not a failure of speed. It is the whole of its life, done at the size its life requires.

I watch it for a long time. My blinking slows to match its blinking. My breath, which used to show, no longer seems to need showing. The spaced sounds of the place — a single bird call, the tick of a stone cooling, a hammer far away — come and go without asking me to answer them.

After a while I am no longer sure I am watching it.

I have lowered myself so far into the moss that the grass is no longer grass at my level but a green ceiling, the tilted slabs are cliffs, the hand-pool beside me is a still lake that reflects the sky when I lift my eyes to it. The towns in their valleys are a pale smudge far off, not a place I need to see. What I need is the damp that never runs: the condensation beading on the underside of the stone above me, the moisture holding the moss lush against my skin, the bead under the ledge swollen and not falling, catching the flat daylight.

My house is not pegged planks. It is not a turf roof staying green-gold. It is the spiral on my back, and unlike the spiral on the lintel in town — begun, and to remain begun — mine is not begun. Mine is carried complete, exact to the size of the life it holds. I do not need a door. I do not need to open and close anything to the same degree each day. I eat the moss as I go, and the moss grows exactly as much as I eat it. I grow exactly as much as I die. Nothing accumulates in me. Nothing is wasted.

That, I think, is where I am.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to come down from the ridge, past the wide empty streets, past the half-carved lintel and the kiln smoke that stops in the air, past the pens where the sheep chew and the bells never ring, past the last paving stone where the green mat closes over the town as if the town were a temporary arrangement. Then you would have to crouch, and then sit, and then bring your face down to the moss in the hollow between two tilted slabs, under the overhang where the stone sweats and never drips.

You would see a damp shell, bright in the unchanging light. Feelers raised, listening to stillness. A silver line behind me, still there tomorrow and the day after, when I am only a little further along inside the green that does not spread.

Nothing has moved to meet me. Nothing has gone. The bead above me is still swollen to its perfect size, waiting to fall and not falling. The frog in its pool blinks, seldom. I inch. I am here, alive in the equilibrium, consuming only what I make and making only what I consume, and I will still be silver in the moss tomorrow.
