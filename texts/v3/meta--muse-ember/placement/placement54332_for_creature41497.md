I am wet in a way that has no beginning.

I did not climb, and I did not arrive. Nobody new arrives in Tawaq. I was here in the mist before I knew what I was, and the mist has not left since.

The light here never sharpens. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, flat and green-grey, so I have never seen my own shadow. I have never seen anyone's shadow. At noon, far below me, they light the lamps in the market hall, not for ceremony, only because inside is dimmer than out, and out is never bright. At dusk they blow the lamps out, and the light does not set so much as thin further, until noon and midnight are the same color.

I hear them first. Hammering below me, hammering above me, because everything leans and the town has nowhere to spread. Slate snapped with a stone hammer. Wood pegged with hardwood. No iron to speak of, only a little copper worked into pins. Market women calling from stalls under tree ferns. A goat bleating from a terrace over my head, guinea pigs squeaking up through the floorboards under my feet, a dog barking at the fog, at nothing anyone can see. Birds in the eaves starting up on schedule, hummingbirds scolding at the orchids in the doorframes, insects making the warm air itself roar, and under it all, drop, drop, drop — the irregular percussion of water filling jars.

For a long time I thought I might be one of the people.

They pass beneath me all day. Women seated in the lanes mending the great fiber nets, their hands slow in the flat light. Old men re-laying the wall in spring with round river-stones and flat slate, stones taken from a ruined house and cleaned by a grandchild. A child carrying a jar up twelve slick steps, lid tied down with string, stopping halfway because her heart is loud in her ears and the damp air is thin to breathe. Her skin sweats and never dries. Her clothes never dry. She has been taught before she could read: you do not wash, you do not bathe, you do not tear a leaf, you do not spill.

I watch her go up. I watch her come down. If I were one of them, I would follow. But I stay. While she passes below my level and rises toward the terraces, I remain exactly where I am, tilted a little more toward the wall when the mist thickens and my weight pulls me down.

The guinea pigs answer me from under the floor, a whole hidden population squeaking through the boards at feeding hour. I open my throat in my head to answer them, but in the daytime I make no sound. The goat looks down at me from above and bleats. The dog barks through the wall at a voice that turns out to be a goat, or a child, or nothing. The hummingbird hovers so close that its wings beat my leaves, furious at an orchid growing out of the doorframe beside me, fighting over its bright damp mouth as if it were a market stall. It does not drink from me. Nothing here drinks in the open. Water lives inside things.

That is when I understand where I am.

I am not in a house. I am in a gutter where two houses lean together and make a permanent shade, a bromeliad big as a bucket, its thick leaves overlapping into a rosette. The roofs are funnels. The lanes are channels. The nets on their tall poles comb the mist out of the air and drip it into clay jars below. What reaches me, I keep. In my center the leaves make a perfect cup, and in the cup there is always a spoonful of water — dew, mist, the slow drip from slate when the cloud thickens. It never runs out. It never pools. It lives, held, inside the plant that holds it.

And the cup is not empty.

Something lives at the surface of my water, no bigger than a thumbnail, eyes just above the skin of it. By day he sleeps with his back to the light, gripping the ridged leaves with his toes, his skin damp as everyone's skin here. He has never touched soil. He was born in this teaspoon, a tadpole in the leaf-axil, and he will call and mate and die without ever going down to a lane. A whole life above the street, in miniature, in a cup of water an ancestor of the town would have called rain.

We are not separated very neatly, he and I and the water. He is the throat, I am the bowl. He is not the plant, and he is not a guest in it. We lean the way everything leans here, house on house, vine on balcony, terrace on ceiling, the dead in shallow garden soil under a tree fern so their shade falls on the living. The green mass they see from the lane has doors cut in it, and windows, and orchids keeping the doors, and potatoes on the roofs with roots shallow because there is nowhere deep to go — and in the gutters, in every cup like mine, a frog they mostly do not see, loud enough at night to wake the street.

Time moves the way the mountain moves. A house takes years to become itself. A terrace takes a lifetime for its wall to settle. The child who learned not to spill grows tall enough to mend nets in the lane below me, her hands slower, her voice changed. She passes under me in the evenings now, up and down the shared stairs, checking the poles like sails between the houses, tying down another lid, telling a smaller child in passing — you do not wash, you do not spill, this leaf is where we drink. When one of the old ones dies, they bury her shallow in the garden soil so the potatoes will be strong, and they plant a tree fern over the grave, and its shade shifts across my wall in a way it never has before. The ancestors who sealed the cistern under the market square have lost their names. The cistern remains. I remain, older than any single life that passes beneath me, renewed in the same cup, calling from the same leaves.

Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks ruined. It has settled. The mountain settled first, and the town has settled in imitation.

At evening the hammering stops in its order. The market women fold their stalls. The larger birds settle in the eaves. The goats quiet on their terraces. The guinea pigs go on squeaking under the floors, softer. The lamps are blown out, and the frogs begin.

That is my hour. My throat lifts, pale and round, and I peep, and the cup of leaves amplifies me like a little earthen pot, hundreds of us balcony to balcony, gutter to gutter, across the whole quarter. The child below me — the net-keeper now, with her slow hands — pauses on the step and listens without looking up. The dog barks once at the fog and thinks better of it. Somewhere below the rim, beyond the low wall that marks where the soil ends, the bell is rung slowly, not for time, only to tell the net-keepers the mist is thickening again.

The mist is always thickening again.

I hold my spoonful of water in both my leaves and my throat, and I do not spill it. There is no river here, no lake, no spring, no puddle. The trees are the rain. So I stay where the cloud leaves something behind: a cup, a throat, and a small wet life that must not be let go.
