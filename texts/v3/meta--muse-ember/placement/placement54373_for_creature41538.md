I am not on the stair-path.

I was here before the footsteps because I am underneath them. I hear the Bowl from below the gravel, from inside the moss, and everything arrives filtered through sponge: the slow *bong ... bong* of a drip falling three meters into a cistern, muffled and made huge, the thin ticking of the thousand smaller drips on wood and clay, the knock of poles and shutters somewhere above me that I feel more than hear. The damp cold is not in my wool, because I have no wool. It is in me entirely, and it stays.

For a while I thought I might be one of the builders.

A dozen households, spare voices, baskets of stone, a course of wall a day. The streets are broad for carts that do not exist, and I thought I would walk them wide and waiting. But my step would have to fall between their steps — between the gravel and the marsh grass already taking back its edge, between a house with no door and the next house with no roof. They are gone around a terraced corner and the street is empty again for yards and yards. I am not gone. I fall through the interval they leave and stay there.

Then I thought I might be the water.

A bead on the limestone face, swelling for a long patient minute. I was that for a minute. Clinging. Undecided. Blue-white in the shadowed corner of the rock. And when I fell, I became the sound, and the sound lived longer than I did — rolling off the cliff and back, slower each time, hanging in a chest until the next drip swallowed it. It was kind, to live that long. But the sound does no work. It only repeats, and dies into the next fall. I wanted something that remains after the boom dies.

I considered the heron, because he is the only one who seems to understand the place without belonging to it. Long legs in the black water, silent, checking whether the marsh is ready to be a marsh, and leaving again. An observer. Not nesting, not staying. The frogs tempted me too — cold, slow, croaking once and stopping as if surprised at their own voice and at the echo that comes back. But I have no voice to be surprised by. I only listen, and I stay.

The plants are the most numerous residents, and for a time I tried to be green. Sedge and cotton-grass leaning in the slow tide, fern uncurling out of a wall joint, vine already pulling at the scaffolding. They live close to the water and close to the ground as if not yet sure how tall they are allowed to be. But I am paler than that. There is nothing here yet to give me color. I am bleached by cold and by newness, translucent, grey-green in the low flat light — the color of things that have never seen strong sun.

So I sank. Not from the cliff, not from the roof timbers. Down through the street itself, to where the moss makes its thick spongy mat on the north face of the retaining wall, on the second terrace, just off the edge of the gravel.

Here the moss does not grow randomly. It grows in a straight line.

It follows the line of the street, where feet shuffle the spores but do not crush them. That line is my whole world. My road, my record, my house. I live seven, maybe eight, millimeters long in a wetness held in sponge, under the rim of a clay pot left out to catch rain, beside a thin clay pipe whose carved spout drips without pause. Every swell and fall divides my day. I can hear my own heartbeats inside the *BOOM* of the pot, and I count them the way the builders' child counts them — not to hurry time, but to keep it from going entirely unmeasured in so much waiting.

I am a springtail, if that name is not too large for me. With me are mites and worms, all pale, all slow. We eat rot and iron and new growth in the peat that deepens by a fraction each year. Where a reed is poked, dark water wells up, and we are at work in it. We do not build. We do not wait for the peat to thicken enough to trust, or for the children who are not yet born to finish the roofs a hundred years from now. The work we do is the work that will make a marsh a marsh, and it is already being done, fraction at a time, in damp where no bird would think to look.

When the builders return, they must clear the vegetation that has grown over their work in three weeks. They clear over us too. Then I press myself flat in the sponge and go quiet, and the moss closes above me, and then it softens a step again. It does not matter. A step and the space after a step is exactly the size of my life.

By late afternoon the fog thinks about coming back over the rim and the stone weeps harder and the threads of condensation stand out thin and vertical on the inside faces of the walls, as if the stone were breathing out. Below me, in the reservoir, the black pool never ripples. It only reflects the scaffolding upside down, so the town is built twice. From down here I see only one drip shadow cross it, and the pool stays perfect.

I will not be full like the empty vessels. I will not be finished like a roof. I am smaller than I thought I was when I began, and colder, and I have no color, and I belong to the skin of the place more truly than any house does. I live in the interval between one drip and the next, between one footfall and the regrowth, and I listen while the sound of water falling into empty things goes on above me, patient as geology, waiting — as I am not waiting — to do its fraction of work.
