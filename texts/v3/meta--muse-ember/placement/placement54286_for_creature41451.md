You will not find me by looking for tracks.

There are none here to find, except yours, and the older ones pressed into a town's mud that no one can quite account for. I do not walk the bare stretches. I do not cross from one green pool to the next, six days over open stone, watching weather be born a hundred miles away. I do not stand in a courtyard in a woven cloak, clicking softly while my woody hands work a loom, or shake moss from my shoulders in a slow green rain. Those are the rootfolk. I know their weight the way a bed knows a foot.

At first, even I was not sure what I was.

I thought, for a while, that I might be the moss. It is the first thing most people step onto, coming off the stone — that soft, surprising hush under a boot, a footprint held for a week. Moss is deep here, mattress-deep in the hollows, cold through to wet peat underneath. It receives the condensation that beads under stones at night and drips at dawn. It mats, and gives back. If anything on the Table is widespread, it is that.

But moss is a skin, and I am underneath the skin. Moss spreads, and is shaken off, and grows again on the shoulders of the rootfolk when they stand still too long in the cold snaps. I am what the moss falls into.

I thought I might be a fern, next. The knife-ferns rise taller than you are in the hollows, arching and heavy, their edges still sharp while everything else has been worn soft for longer than old has meaning. They unfold, and curl tight into bud in a frost, and unfold again from the bud, as if yesterday had been a practice run. I have felt that practice run through my whole thickness on the coldest nights, when the Table erodes backward and a stem that had opened is, impossibly, unopened at dawn. But a fern goes up, toward the thin white-gold sun. I do not go up.

I thought I might be one of the grey-blue bells in the reed-grass, opening for the one warmest minute of the day to no one, because there has never been an insect to come to them. I do feel that warm minute. It comes down through the fronds and warms the top of me while the cold still lives deeper in. My breath, if I had breath, would be visible at midday. But the bells close again. I do not close. I only thicken.

I thought I might be stone. The stone here ticks of its own accord as it cools, like a knuckle cracking, warm on top from the sun and cold through to wherever the moisture lives inside it. It has been wearing down for so long it has finished, and is settled into its final form. Nothing sharp remains. A step is dished in the middle from ten thousand feet. A wall is not ruined, it has slumped into a low patient mound. But stone is the Table itself, vast and level and higher than the weather. I am a small interruption in it, three feet down at most, where the ground suddenly stops being bare.

That is where you would find me. Not on the Table, but in its pools.

A hollow, a shallow trough worn smooth as the inside of a bowl, where the condensation decides to stay. The soil there is no deeper than your palm in the bare places, and deeper where my work has been done longer. It is pale and recognisable leaf-fall that has lain for decades because nothing here hurries decay — birch leaf, tallow leaf, a fern frond whole as the day it fell — layer by patient layer, becoming spongy peat. It is grit and fallen leaf and moss worked in by frost, which heaves a pavement a hair's breadth and lets the moss fill the crack and heaves it wider. It is the black, shallow water that persists in a few hollows, ringed with frost and with green both, tasting of stone and lichen, always slightly out of date. It is the damp that darkens the stone and makes it steam faintly in the sun while the air stays cold.

In the towns, I run along the courtyard walls. The Table's towns are walled against the wind, pavements on pavements, roofs of woven grass and turf and copper hammered thin by hand, channels of carved stone catching condensation from the roofs to cisterns sunk in the earth. Along those walls are the planted beds — deep soil, dark with all my undecayed years — and standing in me, politely, are the rootfolk.

Their feet are not visible. They do not need to be. Their taproots are in me, dark where the soil has washed away, drinking the peat that the leaf-fall builds up year after year. Decay that never hurries is, here, a pantry. I know when one of them grows, slightly, in the summer — a finger's length at the hand, above my reach — and when they prune it, and grow it again. I know when the long cold snaps come, because then they stand perfectly still and moss sprouts on their shoulders until they shake it off, and the slow green rain falls into me and is, perhaps, harvested again for new cloaks. I am where that moss returns to, and is remade.

I am also where the other things return. The lichens do not travel, exactly. The Table remembers a pattern and applies it again where the pattern fits — a yellow crust on the warm side of a stone at midday, the same pattern on the far wall of a storehouse that by evening has gained an extra room at the back, well-built and empty; the same again, hours later, frosted on the inside of a window. I have held that pattern in my top layer, in dust and spores, and given it back to the stone above me. When a street you entered from the south deposits you facing north across ground you have not crossed, when a town adds a storey in the night and loses it by afternoon and a basket on the upper floor is found carefully on the ground, the shift passes through me as a small, settled sigh. The rootfolk are not dismayed. They redraw their maps in ink that smells faintly of damp and is still wet in the morning. Sometimes the bed itself is redrawn. If the town revises itself around them, they are replanted with the same politeness with which they were first planted. I move with them, a little. The roots stay in the bed that remembers them.

That is the rhythm, from underneath. Morning: condensation ticks down from the roofs and the undersides of stones into me, bead by bead. Midday: the sun warms my top, the great open sky does its ordinary pitiless work, and for ten minutes a shadow may point exactly opposite to where the sun requires it to point, cast by a tree that is not there, and the rootfolk's shadows do the same. I feel the wrongness of the light as a coolness that arrives before the sound. A bell is rung before the hammer that forges it is struck. Your echo off a cliff arrives a fraction before you shout. The vibrations all come up through the stone and into me first, like news arriving early.

Then the weather happens, locally, above me. A sideways mist for eleven minutes, hissing faintly on warm stone, beading on cloaks, darkening me and steaming. A storm assembling a hundred miles away that will rain itself out between ridge and ridge and never reach us. A spring appearing in a fixed crack, clear and very cold, shrinking in three days to a damp stain with a collar of bright green that is me, becoming greener.

And at night, when the temperature drops below its usual cold and holds, the Table practices eroding backward. A step worn hollow is shallower at dawn. A crack seals itself with soft ticking and a wall stands straight for an hour, proud and impossible, before the sun returns and it sighs back into its slump. The rain that fell on Wednesday is still, in the cisterns, falling. On those nights I am, briefly, thinner than I was at dusk, as if a top leaf had been lifted back into the tree, as if a footprint you made had been unmade. Then the sun returns, the moss fills the crack, the peat thickens another fraction under another decade of leaves. I tick and settle. I tick and settle.

You left your footprint in me when you came. That is the only walking I have ever known. I held it for a week, or for as long as the cold allowed, and then the frost heaved and the moss filled it and I revised it, gently and inexorably, until it fit the oldest stable form. You are the anomaly here. There were never animals on the Table, never birds overhead to listen for a click in a courtyard, never dogs in doorways or flies around meat, because there is no meat. A walker with boots and a visible breath and a shadow that occasionally points the wrong way does not fit. I do not judge you for it. The rootfolk do not judge you for it. They go on weaving and repairing, laying the same stone back in the same place until the place remembers it. I go on holding the place where your foot landed, until I remember it in a different way — in the pattern that reappears in lichen under your boots as you leave, in the frost on a stone that has not yet rained, because behind you the town does not fade. At a certain distance it becomes me, again, in another hollow where the bells are opening for their one warm minute to no one.

I am not the lowest form of life, and not the highest. The lichen is above the stone, the moss above the lichen, the fern above the moss, the birch above the fern, the rootfolk above the soil, patient and woody and still. I am the thickness between the stone and all of them — the architecture that decay becomes when it is given enough time and no hunger to hurry it. Everything that fell and was not taken away has stayed in me, recognisable, until staying itself became a kind of memory. The Table is too old to erode any further forward, and so it repeats its first hills and its first rivers and the floor-plans of its first towns. I am how it keeps the repetitions from being only shapes. I am the weight of them, lying in layers.

Where would you find me? In the soft place, three feet down from the bare stone, along the wall where two rootfolk stand at a loom among stone hammers worn to a shine and copper awls, in the hollow between the fern-clumps where the water persists black and still. If you step off the stone onto the moss, you are already standing on me. Stay a moment. The cold will come up through your shaded side and the sun will cook your back. My footprint of you will last a week. Then, on a night colder than the usual cold, it may be that for an hour I hold it shallower, younger, as if you had never quite arrived — and then, in the morning, I will hold it again, practising, in the same slow breath, how to go on.
