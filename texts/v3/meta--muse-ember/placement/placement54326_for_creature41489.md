It is warm before it is anything else.

I would feel it through my soles first, although by the time I understood what I was, I no longer had soles in any familiar sense. I would come down out of the throat into the first vault, and the air would already be there — thick, dry, heated, pressing evenly against everything I had. My mouth would coat with hot iron and oil. There would be no water to wash it away, and after a while I would stop trying.

At first I would mistake myself for something made for the floor.

If I lay myself flat on the warmest plate, my back would learn shingles. Segment would grow over segment, chitin and accreted stone, black and rust and tarnished copper, until I was difficult to tell from the shattered pavement around me. Beneath my file-mouth the gray fungal mat would be scraped and scraped again. I would shuffle an inch toward a hotter seam, settle, scrape, settle. The neighbor on my left would feel my scrape through its sternum plates, and the neighbor on my right would answer or not answer through the stone. That would be the whole of travel. That would be the whole of company.

It would be a relief, at first. No horizon to fail toward. No distance to cross. Just warmth below, ticking below, the comfort of being so thoroughly in contact that loneliness had nowhere to stand.

But I would keep waiting for the shuffle to become a journey, and it never would. I would discover that I had confused not moving far with not being anywhere. Under that stillness a great deal happened: heat rose, flakes fell, wax cooled, bodies dried and hardened into new plates. Everything around me was busy in the only way this place allowed. I would simply be busy at an inch at a time.

Then I would turn myself over.

Hanging would be the opposite lesson. I would hook my limbs into the stalactite thicket and let the warmth rise up from below, through me, in broad sheets. I would not walk. I would trail fibers down into the standing haze and eat at their far ends — sulfur flake, mineral dust, shed skin of the floor world, all the fine permanent weather of the Deep catching on me while my body stayed exactly where it was born. When something larger pushed through the veil, I would not flee. I would grip tighter and let it pass.

That, too, would suit me for a while. There is a kind of attention in hanging: mouthless, eyeless, receiving what the air delivers. But in the low places the fibers interweave until there are two ceilings, and I would lose the edge of myself in the veil. Which warmth was mine? Which fiber had my name? I would be touched on every side and still not know where I ended. The floor had taught me contact. The ceiling taught me the danger of contact without a center.

Neither is where I would stay, though both would touch me from there.

Between the floor and the ceiling, I would narrow.

Not narrow as in confined — the vault is continental — but narrow as in made for a passage. I would find the powder of a dried salt basin, white and blistered, and go into it. My body would be long and jointed, partly glass, clear as a bubble. I would have no eyes to close. Instead, heat-sensing pits would wake along my sides, one after another, mapping the Deep as gradient: warmer here, cooler there, a seam glowing amber somewhere beyond the black. I would tunnel through the powder and leave a burrow with edges as sharp as a fresh cut, because there is no water here to soften the walls, no rain to fill them in. Years later another of me would follow it and it would look made an hour ago.

That is the part I recognize most.

I would not often dig new tunnels. I would retrace. I would follow my own trail back to a warm plate, rub my glass flank against itself and make a brief spark — a thought with no witness — settle for a while against the heat, then tunnel again along an old path. I would seem to go somewhere only because I moved between the still layers, and even then I would return. When one of the great flank-striped bodies passed near, blue-white light spilling along its sides, I would flare faintly from within, as if something inside me had answered before I could decide to answer. I would not have made that light. I would only have been clear enough to let it show.

Above me, through the floor, I would feel the Plate-Clingers ticking in their shingle drifts. Above the tunnel mouth the Sky-Clingers would brush the veil against me, warm and translucent, gripping tighter as I passed. A stone-backed grazer would go by on its polished route, one slow step and a long stillness, breathing sulfur in and laying molten wax down to become fungal mat. Farther off, a builder would be stacking the same semicircle of stones by a geothermal pit, knocking obsidian against stone, rebuilding the wall the last builder left because the plan lives in the arrangement and the arrangement has not eroded. I would have no hands for that wall and, after a while, no wish to improve it. The stones would tick through the plate into my back. I would keep perfectly still, and in that way I would be part of the repetition too.

I think that is where I would find myself: not on the floor, not on the ceiling, but in a sharp, unslumped burrow in the salt powder beneath the warmest plates, under the veil, between the ticking above and the glow of the seams below. One of the Glassthings. Long, eyeless, jointed, glass-bodied, feeling the world only as warmth and touch and the thin music of glass on glass.

I would tunnel until the tunnel brought me back to heat, and then I would settle there. I would curl in the old passage and let the mineral dust coat me. When I died — birth and death happen constantly, even here — I would dry in place, my glass becoming one more bright break-face in the floor, my burrow waiting sharp and unweathered for the next one. It would follow my trail years later and flare when the striped bodies passed, and spark when it rubbed its own flank, and think, in the only way available to it, that it was going somewhere.

It would not be going somewhere. It would be going back.

And that would be enough. Far off, a rock-plate would ping a single clear note and fall silent for a long time, and ping again at the same interval. I would not call back across the distance; the thick air will not carry it. I would answer through the stone, a faint scrape, a faint tick, felt by whatever lay nearest. Then stillness would return, warm and crowded and unchanged — and I would be inside it, touching it, held by it, holding time the way everything here holds everything else: not by passing it on, but by staying.
