I do not come in on the causeway.

That is a place for arrivals, for looking in over a flat basin spread thin between sea and drainage plain. I was never an arrival. When I first know where I am, I am already settled, already warm, already pressed flat to something and not moving.

First, I tried to be a person.

It seemed the obvious shape for a town with fences and shift crews and houses with laundry that will not dry. I could feel the heat come up through the soles of my shoes from wet concrete, sweat sitting on my skin and not evaporating, breath coming short against the damp air. I could tend one of the pumps on its plinth, because without the pumps the cellars would fill and the sheet of water would take the town back in a week. I could cross five unshaded minutes of empty apron from one sodium pool to another, from the refinery to the cold store, and shut a door against the midges at night.

But it made me the centre, and this is not a place that has a centre. The people are thinly spread here, fewer than the empty lots. They move between fences and yards of drums and scrap, they are known in a way to the cats that patrol the warehouses, but they do not hurry and they do not quite belong to the ground. I would make too much noise with my worry. I would go home. I would leave.

Then I tried to be a heron.

That was closer. To stand knee-deep in a flooded lot where the water lies brown and still over concrete, grey on grey, so still you become part of the reflection. To wait, unhurried, for a fish that might wander out or a frog from a drain, for nothing in particular, because there is something always moving below. The stillness pleased me. The damp air swallowing sound pleased me. The whole town from that low, patient height — stacks and cranes and water and more stacks dissolving into heat haze — I could have taken that in.

But I was too visible in it. Too much horizon. Too much of the sky pressed down and smudged. A heron must lift and move when a truck passes, must choose a new lot. I did not want to choose. I wanted a place that chooses me back.

I went lower, to foot level, where the second part of the town begins before anything has a face.

I tried a rat. There are hundreds in the culverts, well-fed on grain spill and fish scraps, running in set lines through the dark with their eyes catching the lamplight. From the silos to the truck depots, from culvert to crack in the apron, I could have run the whole map. But rats hurry, after a fashion. They are warm-blooded and populous and always arriving somewhere else. The activity here is low and constant, not busy. I did not want to run set lines.

I tried a catfish. Twice a day the factories discharge and the canal suddenly moves, and we answer as one — backs breaking the surface in a silent boil, gulping air, thrashing in water warm enough to poach in. The world would be temperature and ripple on ripple, reflecting nothing but cloud. Culvert mouths, crabs sideways in the mud, snakes with only a head showing. It was warm and enclosed and complete.

But it was too much movement. A constant boil is a kind of noise, even if no one hears it. And the gulp — up to the skin of the water, down again — I kept feeling the surface as a ceiling I had to bump. I wanted a surface I could rest against.

Then I felt the heat, and I went down further still, to where heat stays longest.

Not in the puddles, which are the first houses, warm and standing and joining each other, full of larvae hanging under the film and beetles diving from it. Not in the grey-green slime in the slow corners of the canals, spreading in sheets and holding the light. On the walls.

The walls weep. After rain — and rain comes and goes and changes nothing fundamental — water beads at the mortar of the warehouses, runs in dark streaks down brick, sweats along the whole length of pipes, drips steadily as if it were always raining somewhere inside them. The north sides go black with mould where what little sun there is never reaches. White salt flowers push through after a dry hour. Copper goes verdigris. Iron rusts through in vertical tears. It is a mineral palette, wet grey and rust red and sulphur yellow, with nothing green in it at all, and it is all surface to hold moisture against.

And on the warm side, where the wall holds the day's heat long after the light has gone to late evening at noon, I found myself plastered flat.

I am a house gecko. That is what I have become as I write, not what I decided. Small enough that the spalling of the concrete is a landscape. Translucent and mottled against brick and corrugated iron, grey-brown as the cloud, so that I am mostly a shape the wall makes. My feet are wide and quiet and they stick. Heat comes up through them from the masonry the way it comes up through the soles of the people, long after the rain has stopped, and I stay because of it.

I hatched from an egg laid in a crack of this wall, near the corner where spilled grain has worked into the joint of the rail siding below. I did not need to be fed. I never have. I do not need a season. I do not need to leave.

The lamps come on early here, in the permanent gloom under steam and monsoon cloud, and throw a thin yellow pool onto wet concrete and up onto my wall. That is my evening, whenever it happens. The midges hang over every still puddle in visible clouds, and around my lamp they blunder and hesitate and lift when I move and settle again behind me. I am perfectly still for minutes at a time, head turned a fraction, eyes — I have no lids, I lick them clean — watching. Then a flick of tongue, a sudden rush along two inches of brick, and stillness again. Skinks do the same higher up. We keep our places.

Below me the town goes on working through its emptiness, quietly. A cat sleeps in the shade of idle machinery by the container yard, well-fed on scraps, lifting its head only to watch a rat pass in the culvert mouth or a stranger cross the yard, and then sleeping again. It does not threaten me. The rats are too big and too intent on the grain dust. The pigeons occupy the ledge above my head, on every tank top and crane arm that has stopped moving, their coos softened and swallowed by the damp air. Mynas and sparrows work the rafters. Swifts stitch back and forth under the eaves in the last grey light, if this grey can be called last. At dusk, which arrives slowly and stays, bats flicker out from under the low bridges over the canals, erratic against plumes of steam that never quite rise.

I can hear, distantly, the factories throbbing, a clank far across an empty yard, brakes squealing two streets away, pumps keeping the water sideways to the sea. I can hear fish turning the canal over with their bodies. It all reaches me dampened, as if through a held breath.

Sometimes a person passes close enough that the heat of their body and the shadow of their legs cross my wall. Laundry hangs from the two-storey worker houses across the flooded lot, limp in the wet air, never quite drying. A door shuts against the midges. No one looks up. The geckos on the walls outside the kitchen windows are part of the wall to them, the way the mould is part of the north wall. Tolerated co-residents of a place that feeds on what it discards.

Rain beads on the brick around me. I drink from a bead that has formed at a mortar line, tasting salt. A salt flower pushes up beside my tail. Black mould takes hold around it again in the corner I do not use, where the sun never reaches. Acid-yellow from the refinery earth stains a puddle at the base of the wall, where it reflects the cloud and nothing else. Nothing here photosynthesizes. Nothing needs to change colour for a season. The ground stays wet, the pipes keep dripping, the canals keep boiling, the flooded lots stay flooded and reflective and empty — and crowded, at my level, with all of us going on working through them.

I am not the centre of any of it. I am not even, really, the visitor in it. I keep my place on the warm wall in the yellow pool of light, and the midges keep theirs around the lamp, and the cats keep theirs in the shade, and the herons keep theirs in the brown water, grey on grey. That is the rule here, and I have learned it with my feet.

The lamp is still on. The wall is still warm. I am still here, flat and translucent and still, waiting for the next flicker in the light.
