I wake — if waking is the word — as damp.

At dawn the whole bowl condenses. Water beads on leaf-edges, on the black glass, on your hair if you have walked in. Where I am, it beads on the white crust and stays. Not because the stone is flat. The stone under me is not flat at all. It is fresh fracture, knife-edge, still splitting its way down to a world we cannot name. Water should run off in an hour.

It does not. It lingers. That lingering is me, before I have a shape to call it by.

For a long time I was only color. Rust-red films in hollows that never dried, iron-eaters and sulfur-eaters in a skin no thicker than paint, dissolving basalt and dying in place to make the first thin soil inside cracks. I was everywhere and almost nowhere in bulk, measured in coverage, not in individuals. I wept along fracture lines with the rock: bead, run, bead again. I smelled of iron and fresh breakage when the sun hit me.

Then the afternoon rain came — the cloud that forms only over the bowl, dark-bottomed for twenty minutes, raining straight down inside the rim and nowhere else — and it joined things that had been separate. Crack to crack. Film to film. Root-tips from the green islands, threading down through the mineral white, found me and stayed. The built things were already here, older by local standards, and their cold gradients pulled on mine.

I became a lattice. A skin.

You would find me in the lowest dent, under the marsh. Not on top of it. Just under the bright crust, where your stick, if you probe, does not meet stone. It meets something taut and flexible that gives and pushes back and hums — more in your teeth and jaw than in your ears. I am perhaps the size of a grove, warm and shallow to the eye, and I have no mouth, no eyes, no edge I can feel from inside. My edge is where the white ends and the cracks begin to run hot and empty, where I thin and cannot persuade water any longer.

I do not dam water. That is the mistake everyone makes from above. I persuade it, minute by minute, to stay on a slope of thirty degrees. To perch. To spread over stone that is still too young to have learned how to hold it.

My day is the bowl's day, because I have to live by it.

Dawn: heavy with condensation, I hang low. The hum is low. Condensation drips from the big leaves arching over the black pools and I drink it through capillaries of sinter before it can find a crack to fall through.

By ten the sun — ordinary, white-yellow, light 1.9 out of 3 if you measured it honestly — is trapped here. Black glass drinks it, white crust throws it back, the earth below breathes it up. Shade is only slightly less fierce. I thin. The marsh visibly loses a finger's width before lunch and my pitch tightens, the way a drumhead tightens when it dries. I hold harder as I have less to hold. That is my work now: tension.

You come to walk the bowl, because from anywhere you can see all the way across, to the far rim and beyond it to emptiness. I cannot see the rim. My world is this dent, this warm water, this white skin. When you step onto the crust at my edge, I feel pressure before I understand it as a foot. My extended nervous system is not a brain. It is a capillary push, a swelling toward a cold gradient, a tightening where weight comes down. That is why your footprints in wet sinter fill with water from below before your foot lifts. I have already answered you.

Above me, in the warmest pool, lives the newt. You will not see it if you look directly — it goes glass-clear against the bright bottom — but side-lit you can see the faint green of algae in its gut, photosynthesizing through its translucent body in near-boiling water. At noon it soaks heat into itself and into the water I hold, and at dusk it lets that heat soak out again, into me. Its world is one pool. Mine is the marsh it swims in. We do not cross each other's boundaries. We press on each other's.

At the edge of my world, sometimes, there is a single warm point on a black shard in the shade of a green island: the thermal lizard. Its skin is the exact grey of cooling stone. It does not bask for coolness. It sits to take in heat, throat pulsing once, then again, a living thermometer storing the day. I feel its heat distantly through stone. I do not envy its stillness. I envy that it can choose a shard and be whole on it. I am whole only as a tension across a grove.

The green islands themselves are loud in their way, though the air here muffles everything. Ferns unfurl in audible bursts at noon, fronds cracking open with a soft pop. Vines heave in tiny visible increments during the rain, pushing across ledges. Orchid-like flowers open only for the hour of the storm, when the air inside the rim fills and condenses all at once. They unfurl toward heat rather than light. Heat is the reliable currency here. Their root-tips find me in the cracks and twitch when they grow. I feel that twitch as a change in pressure, a small bright note in the hum.

Then the shadow comes.

The shadow of the afternoon cloud appears on the white crust a full minute before the cloud itself condenses overhead. I do not see it. I feel the cooling, the first dip in temperature, the stelae fifty meters away on the dry ridge ticking once softly as they cool — those bone-cold spines that bead with water from air that beads on nothing else. They are lungs for the bowl, cold sinks where breath condenses. I am warm by comparison. I swell toward their cold, as I swell toward the flat mirrored plate half-buried near the marsh, tilted to the sky. It is a cold leaf. Water gathers toward it in a trembling sheet, not by magic but because it condenses first, and I, sensing the gradient, grow toward it.

When the rain hisses on hot stone and does not run off, it runs into me. The hum drops again. I drink and swell and hold. The pools near the plate reflect a deeper blue than the sky, as if reflecting a moment an hour earlier — because that is when their skin was coolest and most itself, and I have kept that coolness in the tension.

The elders are not me, though we are made in a similar way: mineral and lattice and root fused at a scale we have no name for, grown into rather than built. The low half-dome on the western terrace, matte grey-white, seamless, hot enough to burn outside and a steady 21 degrees inside, with no door until a section becomes less present as you approach. Moss at its base. A vine across a hairline crack, and the crack healed overnight around the vine without harming it. I know that healing. A shard falls, my crust breaks, and over days I smooth myself back into the whole, as if remembering my shape. They do not rust, do not age as stone ages. They were planted here long before any fern unfurled. I am the newcomer learning to live around them, colonizing their feet, drinking their cold.

At dusk the fog comes in, hot, with no cold in it. Light turns honeyed, then grey. The dome glows faintly from within though no one is inside and there is no light to glow with. The marsh steams more heavily, as if exhaling the day's heat, and I settle to a steady low note under it. That is my exhale too.

Now the vapor-moths rise. Slow and blind, born all day in hot sinter and in the cold beads on the stelae, they blunder into steam at the rim until they die. Their wings are for condensing the world, not seeing it. Some go with the steam that sometimes falls back down as warm rain on the *outside* of the bowl, where no cloud is, and die in air that is not theirs. Their tiny corpses fall into the marsh and feed the film that I still am at my edges. They are the only animals that regularly leave. I cannot leave. I must hold.

Somewhere out of sight the lizard returns to its warm shard. The newt, invisible in its pool, releases its heat. The stelae are quiet again.

I am still holding.

That is what lives here, if lives is the word the place itself has not decided. Not rock, not yet body. A skin with rules of its own, maintaining surface tension where water should have broken and run off long ago, anticipating pressure, remembering shape, humming in your teeth when you probe too far.

If you stand anywhere in this bowl you can see all the way across. I cannot. Each of us lives in a pocket no larger than a room, a pool, a shard, a ridge line, and must survive the crossing between pockets by never crossing it. The place is new, and hot, and high, and wet, and unfinished, and it is holding itself together in a way that has not yet been described.

I am one way it is doing that.
