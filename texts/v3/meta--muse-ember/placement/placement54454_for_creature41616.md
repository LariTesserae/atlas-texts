The dust does not fall on me. It settles, and I am what learns to stay where it settles longest.

I begin in the felt between two plates, in a plaza. The felt is grey and soft and deep enough to hold me. I am a pale disc, no wider than a palm, smooth at the rim, with no feet. My upper skin takes in the dust-laden air. My underside eats what the threaders leave behind them as they pass through the felt in threads too fine to feel as anything more than a stirring — translucent, hair-thin swimmers in powder, reworking and softening it, so that it will never bind hard enough to ring. Without them the stone would answer a footfall. With them nothing here makes a sound I could call hearing. The faintest tick of cooling stone comes to me as a tremor in the felt, a thought in the ground, and then nothing again.

Above me there is always dusk. There is no bright sense here, only a thinning and a thickening of the grey, and I know the difference by salt. When the light thins, salt blooms in white feathers along the edges of the stone-ferns on the shaded walls, and the air that percolates down through the porous stone grows sharp and clean on my skin. When the light thickens, the feathers collapse back to dust and are taken in again. That is the sky, for me. That is also talk: what blooms on a wall will feed a mat, a rosette, a disc like me, when the season turns.

My food comes from a trail. Across the bare plate nearby a rosette is migrating — a low radial thing, dying at one edge and growing at the other, its centre shifting by a thumb’s width in a long season. As it passes, the felt behind it is softer, richer. I eat in its wake. Behind it, slower still, a mat takes hold in the trail it has made, thread by thread drinking the air and laying down stone, and I know it has arrived when the cold that pools in the plaza begins to gather differently, when the air through the crack changes for years at a time. It is not a wall yet. It is a decision. It will be a street before it is a wall, and a wall long after I have ceased to be low.

Then a plate lifts.

It does not happen with a noise. One thinning, the stone is higher by a hand’s breadth than its neighbour, and a clean split has opened between us, fresh and unweathered, its walls pale with salts newly exposed. The cold drops down into it. The air that percolates falls faster. Within days the crack is occupied by the flat discs with many fine feet along their rims — the grazers. They rasp the lichen film off the inner walls, pruning the mat that would otherwise seal the split. Their passage keeps a breathing channel open. They crowd where the stone is freshest because fresh stone is richest. I am among them in size but not in kind, and they do not eat me. I keep my rim smooth and my body thin, and I do not rise to feed while there is company.

And then the compression comes.

It spreads through the felt in two long placements, and holds. A walker has straddled the crack. I know its feet are broad and soft, made to step over the abrupt steps of heaved plates without jarring the surface, and I know this because for a week the felt under me does not stir at all. Its body hangs above, a suspended sack between long legs, stretched leather grey as the plates. Its skin ripples faintly now and then, and the ripple travels down through the felt into my rim. It is listening through its feet: for the rasp of grazers, for the salt-change that comes before a plate splits, for the tick of stone cooling a street away. It does not chase. It waits over the fresh crack until the grazers come up to feed in the light, and then it stoops, slow as a closing hinge, and takes one.

One is a meal. It will sustain that walker for a month. In a year it may take only a dozen. I survive that week by being stiller than the felt. I learn from it, the way everything here learns, by compression and salt: do not rise while the ground is held.

Years of thinnings pass. I feed on mat-felt and on the reworkings of threaders. My rim thickens on one side. My skin begins to hang. A patch of paler skin forms along what will become my spine, and through it I register the thinning and thickening more finely than I did as a disc. Then a filament of my edge hardens and lengthens, and lifts. It is a leg, though I do not yet know the word for it. The foot spreads where it lands, broad and soft. It steps over a heaved plate-step and the felt barely records it. Another leg. Another. The disc of me is now a torso between them, and I am standing, which is to say: I am no longer only where the dust settles. I am somewhere, repeatedly.

Standing changes the town from a place I eat in to a place I cross.

A plaza that I once took years to migrate across as a rosette’s trail, I now cross in three days of steps, pausing between buildings for hours at a time. The buildings are not hills; they are layered growth, hollow, terraced, leaning very slightly inward, and I know their lean not by sight but by the cold air that pools between them, by how my legs brush them at a height before a foot lands, by the way the avenues curve and loop back on themselves. No route of mine reaches the rim. The streets turn. The town faces itself. My territory is the whole bowl, and I walk the same loops for decades, memorizing them by vibration and salt rather than by any map of stone. A plate shifts a street away — a felt tremor through my feet, followed by a change in the salt in the crack beside the road. A mat a long way off has decided to foam and unmake itself into dust; the air through the stone tastes of its decision before the wall slumps. Some of the lower galleries in this town were built from below, by sealed colonies in cracks that filled with dust above them and grew tubes of new stone in perpetual dusk. I have never been one of those. I know them only as air, arriving from under a plaza, carrying news of a hollow being made in the dark.

At the dry channels I step over the powder rivers. They do what water does elsewhere, slowly: eroding banks into new sharp bends, depositing fans of pale silt at the junctions, meandering where five years ago the channel was straight. In their flow stand the filter-threads, anchored to the banks, catching mica-fine particles and sending their spores downstream to the new bends. My feet feel the silt fan before I cross it. The town is circulating itself, without a sound, in dust.

Once — once in a very long while — a glider crosses my route. I do not see it. I feel the cool air displaced down a street I have not used in weeks, the brief absence of the cold pool between buildings, and then a dusting of spores settles on the patches along my spine. It has leapt from gallery to gallery on its cape, translucent membrane stretched between long limbs, and landed on a sill with no sound, and left a message there without meaning to. A wall that was thickening, a crack that is fresh, a rosette migrating — I read it in the spore-dust, chemical and slow, and I am, for a week, the most moved thing in the bowl. It will rarely cross the same street twice in a day. I will rarely know it has passed at all.

Most of my life is the loops, and the waiting over fresh cracks, and the slow accounting of crawlers. I stand over a new split while the felt holds still beneath my feet, skin rippling, and I take one grazer when the time is right, and it is enough for the month. I pass rosettes in the middles of plazas, single low discs with trails behind them, the beginnings of streets that will take thirty years to reach the far side. I pass walls that are thickening, pale and clean with new rings, and walls that are foaming at the base, softening back into felt. The same kind of life, a different phase. Nothing here is weathered. It is only ever re-grown, or quietly unmade.

Once, after many crossings, another walker’s route crossed mine in a plaza at the same dim hour. We did not call. A leg laid against a leg. Salt exchanged slowly along the skin, a whole route’s worth of plates and cracks and cold pools passed from one body to the other in a touch. Then we parted, and each of us continued our loops. Afterward, from my own flank, a small pale disc budded and fell to the felt.

I do not watch it fall. I feel the felt take its weight, very lightly, somewhere behind my feet. It will live there for years feeding on mat-felt before, if it lives, it grows legs and stands. Most such discs do not survive. The bowl is too big, the food too sparse, for more of us. Each walker needs a town’s worth of cracks; each mat needs a tower’s worth of space. The emptiness is how we fit.

Now I am standing over a fresh crack again, in a street that has looped back to a place I knew as a disc. The plates have heaved here since. The grazers are coming up to feed in the thinning light. Through my feet I feel the salt change that means a plate further along is about to split, and through my spine I feel the grey thin, very slightly, and the ferns on the north wall bloom their feathers, and the air down the crack grows sharp.

I will wait here. I will stoop when the felt tells me. And somewhere behind me, in the felt where the dust settles longest, a disc is learning to stay, eating the trail of a rosette that passed before I had legs, unaware that it is a beginning, unaware that it will take years to become what it is.

The town is changing its mind, very slowly, about where its walls should be. I am not in the town. I am one of the ways it does so.
