If I had to find myself here, I would not start by looking out.

I am tempted at first by the highest tooth of the rim, by the cairn built stone by stone, by the view down to the cloud sea. It is the place a human story would put its observer, and perhaps I do stand there for a moment, in the white light that comes before the cold, watching my own shadow fall blue-black and knife-edged across the bowl. But the wind makes even the stone look unsettled. Nothing here, I notice, is meant to stay on top. The rim is all sharp new edges, still splitting at night, already taking back the few human marks — the windbreak, the flakes of obsidian, the rope frayed to fibres. A human does not live here. A human passes, and is passed.

So I work my way down, as the eye does here, not across the centre — the centre is mostly empty, twenty, thirty paces of bare stone between one green pocket and the next — but toward the pockets. Toward where life is concentrated and small and fast.

The pika-like mammals are too much heart for me. They dart between burrow mouths, sit upright to take the sun on a belly, vanish at a falcon’s shadow. It is a whole warm-blooded urgency I don’t have. The snow finches are too much air — a thin bell-call over the flowers, then minutes of silence while they hive in the altitude and are gone. The falcon itself only visits as a pressure, a cooling on the stone. Even the flies on a warm slab are too frantic, born and dead within a metre of where they hatched, all in a sudden midday boil. I feel the pull of that heat, but I don’t want to live *in* it, exposed, all at once.

Step off a cushion and you are back on stone. Step onto one and you are in wet earth and crushed herbs, silvered hairs catching light even when the sun is behind them, flowers opening at sunrise and shutting when the shadow reaches them. The cushions are the oldest residents, I think; they grow in and down rather than up, into soil that is never dry and never wet, drinking the night’s condensation leaf-hair by leaf-hair. I admire them. I would be too rooted, though, too still, and too visible to the frost that rimes them at dawn.

The lichens are closer — sulphur, rust, pale green crusts on the sunward faces only, colour where the cushions cannot go. But they belong to the full sun. I keep feeling my way past full sun.

What I am looking for, without saying it yet, is a place where the light arrives secondhand. Where heat arrives through stone, not as glare. Where damp stays.

Under the edge of a dark slab, then. Not on the warm face where the beetles, wet-basalt coloured, go suddenly fast, and the mites work the dry film of dust, and the flies crowd the shadow-edge. Just underneath it, in the lee of a grit grain, where a velvet trough of moss runs into the grit and the rock stays cool and faintly peaty. That is the dampest hollow in the bowl. There is a little garden of moss there that stays green all winter, feeding only on condensation, and beside it thin stalagmites of clear ice grow upward season after season though no water ever visibly drips. I could live there.

Not as the moss. I am too alert for moss, too much in motion when it warms. Not as a beetle, armoured and sun-drunk. I am one of the tiny spiders I kept hearing about in the description of that slab — translucent, on legs so thin you would miss them if you lifted your palm too quickly, stringing no web, hunting by touch in the lee of the grit. My whole world is an inch wide: warm stone above, damp moss below, a grain to hide behind, a film of dust to cross. At midday the slab above me burns, and I do not have to see it to know — the heat conducts down through the rock and wakes me out of torpor. That is my sunrise. The light, filtered and delayed, is almost an afterthought. I move by contact, by the tremor of a mite in the grit, by a pika’s foot springing the turf somewhere beyond my slab, by the stone ticking faintly as it settles and splits in the cold.

At dawn I am still folded in the grit, cold, waiting. Every underside above me is beaded. Condensation has gathered in the night and drips so slowly into the moss that I can drink as a droplet, as a film. Animals here drink dew; plants drink dew; I drink it too, from the edge of the moss, from a hair on a leaf. By ten the frost and rime are gone, the ice needles in the soil cracks have melted, and I am out, incredibly small and incredibly busy, in the only warm window the day allows. Everything must feed and hide between morning frost and evening freeze, and I understand that rhythm in my legs before I understand it in any other way.

In the afternoon a squall may boil over the rim. I do not see it, mostly — cloud wraps the teeth, light goes white, and then above my slab comes the hiss of ice grains striking dark stone, darkening it for a minute. A cool drip reaches the moss. The turf steams. Then the light returns brighter for being reflected off cloud, and the heat comes back through the stone. I go still when the falcon’s shadow crosses the bowl, not because I see the bird but because the stone cools a degree, and that is enough. The finches call somewhere above and the sound is already swallowed by thin air. I stay under my slab and wait for the warmth to return.

At night the flowers shut, the mites go torpid in the grit, the cushions rime over. I withdraw a grain’s width deeper. The cold falls off the sky, the rim becomes absolute, and the stone around me does its slow work — frost wedging, edges splitting, new scree being made shard by shard. I feel it through my feet more than I hear it. The moss drinks again from the air. Beside us, invisible water keeps building its clear ice upward, season after season. It is patient in a way that suits me. I am patient too, in bursts.

That is where I would be: not on the rim, not in the centre, not in the open green, but in the between-place, under the warm slab where the moss stays green, keeping the heat a little longer than the stone around me has any right to. If you put your palm down at midday, you would feel me already moving. If you lifted the slab — please don’t — you would find me gone in a flicker, back into the grit, back into the damp, waiting for the light to arrive first, before the cold, and scatter the shadows again.
