Cold finds me first.

It is in the damp that never leaves the cuffs, in the breath that shows in the grey-green noon, in the metal that sticks when I touch it with a bare hand. At first I think I am one of the coats.

I walk in the stream, shoulder-to-shoulder because there is no spare width, along a lane that was level in the morning and cambered by evening. The slab heaves a hand's breadth under my boots while I am still standing on it. People peel off into doorways and down cellar stairs and up ladders lashed to a wall, and the sameness breaks up close — moss-green on the north shoulder of a coat because someone stood too long in one place, rust-orange where a foundry door has brushed it for years, a warm stone in a pocket turned inside out from use.

We do not shout. Sound would carry too far through the packed town and be swallowed anyway, by felt and moss and stacked goods. We breathe close to the ear. We move slowly because heavy things move slowly. The hum of generators comes up through the soles, not through the air.

I learn the work the way everyone learns it, by picking it up from the person beside me. I am given a brush on a slow rail and a tank to carry, thin and needful. I am a Dewhand, for a while. I wipe the backs of machines and the insides of windows and leaves thick with it in the morning. I wipe my own sleeves and wring them into the tank. The water is cold and tastes of iron. A child runs past on a roof, licks dew from a rail, and runs it across a walkway to a tender. Children live highest and know every vent by heart.

On a cold morning a hundred stacks vent at once and the sky fogs for an hour. The children stand on the slag heap and watch everything glisten. They bet, quietly, on how long condensation will run uphill on the underside of a condenser when it cycles. I watch with them, and the bet is not about winning. It is about paying attention.

Later I sit with an old man whose job is now to listen. He puts his ear to a wall in a warm tunnel and listens for the drip inside it, never quite making a puddle, and knows when the pitch changes. The coats settle around him in layers, breathing each other's damp air under a felt curtain. His hands are slower than mine. He does not hurry me.

That is when I stop quite being the hand.

The water on my sleeve does not go where my hand goes. It stays. It beads where the cold air from the doorway meets the warm pipe behind the curtain. It gathers on the underside of a fin in the condenser farm, between the sorting-sheds and the loom quarters, on the north side of a pipe where a vine is layered in perfect geometry to keep the frost off. I am there, upside down, clinging.

The condenser cycles. For a few minutes I run uphill. The children would have bet on me and lost, or won. A brush on its slow rail finds me and wipes me down into a trickle, and the trickle runs needful into a tank.

In the tank it is dark and warm and green. Algae fills the water. A blind fish rises when the drip comes, pale and eyeless, and bumps me as it turns. The Tank-Keeper reads the fish like a dial, too fast, too slow. An Eel-Handler puts a forearm in beside me and clears silt, talking low to the eels as if they were part of the household, because they are. I am not read. I am only what makes the fish rise.

From the tank a fern drinks me. Its fronds go translucent where the heat of a furnace hits them. A dragonfly patrols the steam above it and pollinates nothing I can see. I am part of the wall that is ferned and therefore holds its heat, until the plant is cut and re-blooms a foot to the left.

And then I am lost to the air again, which in this town means I am found again. I rise in a vent with all the others on a cold morning. For an hour I am the fog that softens the ordinary light. I glisten on the slag heap where the children are standing. I settle, in turn, on a leaf and am wiped up by hand, on the inside of a window, on the back of a sleeping machine where moss is thick as carpet over the warm metal, on the ear of a boiler cat asleep on a generator housing, deafened pleasantly by the hum it chose. The cat shifts as the cranes turn all night like patient animals above us. The moss absorbs me and does not freeze.

I never make a puddle. I am wiped, drunk, fallen into a tank, lifted by steam. I am a temporary arrangement of heat and cold meeting, and remade while still in use, the way the town is.

You would find me now where I started, and not — inside a wall on the north side of a loom-shop, beading on a warm pipe behind a felt curtain, drip, drip into a thin trickle that an old man is listening for. His ear is to the wall. The pitch has changed a little.

My breath is still showing somewhere above me. The light is still normal.
