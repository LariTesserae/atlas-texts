I come up to it from below the weather, in boots.

For a while that is what I am: a walker. I come through the broad lumpy saddle where the mountain gave up, and I can walk the whole dish in twenty minutes and hold it all in my head at once. That should help me. It doesn't. I keep looking *across* — across the plaza, across the street-swales, across twenty, thirty paces of empty air to the next leaning shell — because across is how I am built. At head level there is nothing to see, a few twisted trees in a lee, a raven once, high, uninterested. The emptiness becomes architecture and I walk around inside it feeling lonely in a place too small to be lonely in.

The cold gets into the soles of my feet and I sit down because there is nowhere else to sit, and I look down instead.

Knee-high at most. Moss lawns bright where wet, black-green in shadow. Ferns uncurling from cracks. Cushion plants like pillows. Herbs with a wick of water in each waxy leaf. It has grown straight over the old street lines, and the farther I look the more packed it is. Density is vertical, here. Crowded at ankle height, nothing above it. I put my hand flat on it, as if to steady myself, and the ground gives a fraction, as if laid over a hollow.

I try to be the one who sits up at noon.

The sun warms a flat plaza stone a fraction of a degree and the stone tick-tinks as it expands, and a small blunt thing settles on its warm face and watches me with complete indifference. It is not feeding. It is charging. It takes that fraction of heat into its back to move its blood faster for an hour, then drops back into its crack.

I try that posture. Stone under my back. Sun. Waiting for the warmth to move through me. Indifference as a strategy: do not move unless the stone moves first.

It works for a minute. Then I want to answer something. The sitter only takes. It does not listen back. In the crack its own breath keeps a pocket from freezing solid all winter, and that is a kind of company, but it is company with itself. I am restless in a way it is not. I keep turning my attention — if that is the word — toward sound.

I try to be the wren, next. One tree, far off, leaning in the lee of a wall. When it sings it is harsh and far too loud for its size, and the bowl throws it back so it sounds like two birds arguing across empty air. To be that loud, that brief, that soon gone — the tree empty again for a season. I sing once in my head and the dish gives it back to me, doubled, and I do not want to be a visitor to my own neighborhood. I do not want to leave.

I try, briefly, to be high like the raven. Nothing to take. A shadow, a brief cold thing on moss. No.

So I listen down, where I should have been listening all along.

Scrabbling under the lawn. A soft give under a boot. Under the moss there is movement that almost never surfaces: long, blunt, pale as wet stone, blind. Pinprick eyes gone to vestige under skin. They live by vibration. A frost-heaved pebble rocks at thaw and drops with a clatter, and they feel it through their feet and run to push it back down, to tamp the moss flat. A drip falls from a lintel into a basin and echoes, late, three times — one, a pause, then two more, as if something answered from under the moss. They press a broad flat ear low on the skull to the stone and listen to the wind tune the doorway. West wind makes that wall hum bass. They navigate by that hum the way I would navigate by streetlights. Their radius is one house. Twenty, thirty paces to the next of their kind, and they almost never cross.

That is close. Closer than the sitter, closer than the wren. Blind and listening. Solitary because the town is solitary.

I try to be that. I go under. I follow a tunnel along a subsided street-line where the trough holds water, I eat moss roots and the fungi that thread them and the slow bloom on the undersides of walls, I tamp and am tamped. I learn which doorway moans when the wind is west and I know a burrower is awake underneath it, listening too.

But I do not push pebbles down. I am not the one who runs toward the clatter. I am what the clatter travels through. I have no forepaws for pushing. I have no skull for an ear, though I have the place where an ear would go, and the hum arrives there anyway, as pressure, as a change in the stone itself.

I go smaller. A shadow passes and springtails jump in the moss. Mites graze spores. For ten minutes at midday the whole dish breathes out at once — a slow white exhale as last night’s fog is breathed off the lawns, ice popping in the puddles, drips answering drips. Then the clouds climb out of the bowl below and drown the rim and everything retreats down again except the moss, which never retreats.

I am too brief for that, too reactive. I want something that stays when the exhale is over.

So I follow the water, which here does not run down. It runs *in*.

In the morning condensation beads on the cold undersides of walls and grows until the whole face weeps, and the water goes into the porous stone, so the interiors are wettest when the outside is only misty. Rain hammers the plaza slabs and vanishes — moss drinks it, stone drinks it, thin soil drinks it. What stays is damp. Puddles crack overnight and do not spread.

I follow the damp inward, and then under. Not into the dark interior of a house, where a burrower nests in damp that never dries. Under the lintel itself. On its underside, where the drip is born before it falls.

There I stop trying to stand upright.

I am a crust on the underside of the west doorway of the third house down the north swale — the one that moans bass when the wind is west. Moss in the joint, lichen on the stone, fungus threading both. I do not distinguish them anymore; in this cold and this damp they have come to an arrangement, erosion and growth balanced, and I am the arrangement. The house leans a degree a century, mid-fall, held up by moss growing in its joints. Part of that holding is me.

I have no mouth. I drink fog directly. I drink a squall as it passes over the slab above me and vanishes. I swell after rain and go tight as leather at noon frost, and frost in the shaded hollows at noon is familiar to me as a tightening in the stone I am glued to. I feel the whole dish tick-tink as it expands, not as sound but as a minute widening and narrowing under my skin. I feel a pebble loosened for a hundred years be heaved up at night and let drop at thaw, not through my feet but through the wall, a pressure shift, a rattle far away on a terrace.

I feel the drip before it falls: the stone above me grows wetter, heavier, and then a bead lets go. The first fall I drink. The second I pass onward into the stone, inward, where it is needed. The third — the late one the bowl throws back, the one as if something answered from under the moss — that answer is also me, delayed. I am the pause between the one and the two.

The burrower below me eats the slow bloom on undersides like this, sometimes. I do not run from that. I have grown back more times than I can count, a fraction of a millimeter a year, in straight lines along old walls because the walls hold damp. Growth here is not dramatic. It is finished, and then it continues anyway.

I am twenty, thirty paces from the next crust like me on the next doorway, listening to a different note. We do not cross. The architecture taught us radius, and I have learned it as thoroughly as any burrower. Solitary is not a personality here. It is spacing.

Above me, the wren’s harsh call arrives as vibration in air and then, a moment later, in stone. The raven’s shadow is only a cooling, a fraction less sun on the slab, and I feel the stone above me contract by the smallest amount and know something large has passed and not landed. At midday I breathe out with the lawns — not a jump, not a song, just the white exhale lifting off moss and wall alike as trapped fog leaves. A visitor with boots dark from the puddles might put a hand flat on the moss and feel, under the green, a slow patient pulse. That pulse is not a heart. It is swelling and contracting, frost and thaw, drip and drink, keeping itself damp and listening to itself hum.

If you stay, you would learn where the fog pools first, which doorway moans when the wind is west, when the dew-basin in the plaza holds enough to drink from without disturbing its skin, and that the sitters will come only after you leave. You would move at thaw and be still at frost.

I moved at thaw, in a way. I am still now.

I am just under the surface of an empty place that is not empty at all, on the underside of a stone that is not falling, drinking the weather as it arrives sideways and inward, answering the drip three times late, spaced one house from anyone like me, awake in the damp cold the way the town itself is awake — not sharp, not flowing, not crowded in the air, but dense and listening at ankle height, forever.
