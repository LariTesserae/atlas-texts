At first you do not see me.

Not because I am hiding. There is nothing here to hide behind, and nothing here that sees in the way you mean. You would have to watch for heat, not light. Against the dark, a shape crossing an aerial walkway four levels up would stand out in amber where it passes a furnace seam, in white where a starter arcs, in faint green-white where it passes a duct. If you watched that way, you might catch me — or you might catch the cable I am partly suspended from, or the vine-truss the walkway is carried on, or another tender on the walkway below mine, moving in the same rhythm at a different level. For a long time I could not tell whether the movement was mine.

I feel heat against myself before I know what myself is. It has no sun in it. Dense, dry, still. It comes from low and far, from a door that has not quite sealed, from buried engines under the mat, from the towers themselves. It arrives through a filter that tastes resin and charcoal and salt, and through my feet — if feet is the word — where the root-lattice runs under me in straight lines, grafted at right angles, older strands petrified and ringing, newer strands pale and hot and still seeking warmth the way a plant seeks what this place gave plants instead of light.

I am high up. That is still true, and also not enough. The ground falls away behind me in eroded steps for miles before it flattens, terraces climbing in rings, each workshop roofed by the next terrace's root-mat. I am also low, in the central basin, where the density is greatest and the canopy of lacquered leaf covers the factories in one continuous roof that rains ash instead of water. Between those two facts I move, or am moved, pausing where a walkway crosses a wall.

When I pause, I put a broad, flat sensor against the wall and stay there.

It is not looking. It is feeling. A pulse moves under the wattle, thick and slow now that the resin has thickened in the cold that passes for night. By dayless day it will thin and run, and the pulse will come faster. I do not know yet if the sensor is the end of a limb, or if the limb is something I have borrowed from the wall to test it. A tender on the other side taps, and the vibration travels through the mat into me, and for a moment I am that tap as much as I am the body receiving it. Communication here is pressure. That makes the borders of a self unreliable.

Gradually, the borders thicken.

I have a housing like bark, lacquered and patched, hooped with iron riveted directly into it. When I move, the hoops tick against older hoops high up in the truss. My limbs end not in hands but in pruning shears, in a grafting-wax applicator made for hot resin, in a spade shaped for ash-beds. My joints, which were once hot and flexible, are beginning to petrify from the outside in. If I am struck hard there is a ring.

That is how I know I am one of the tenders. Not because I saw myself, but because the place answered me in the way it answers them.

A furnace door, not quite sealed, is my daylight. I orient toward the leak. A phosphorescent duct is my constellated wall; I follow the fungi's dim flare when an engine runs hard, and I lose it when a district cools. I know the central basin by density: clank against clank, the groan of a root-truss taking weight, the pneumatic sigh of a plant-factory venting resin-steam, the muffled drop of a vine-crane letting down bricks. I know the north only by the quiet that taps through the lattice from tenders older than me — skeletal towers, petrified trunks holding the skyline like ribs, engines long cold, only the tick of cooling metal. I know the south by salt, carried on ash devils, by the taste of mineral on my seals where cactus-like plantings are grafted into slag and their flowers have been reduced to vents.

I live in a hollow that was trained into a tower and then finished as a tower. My door is cut and hinged in living wall. The hollow shares its flank with an engine, its cylinders wrapped in bark and pale fiber, its drive-belt of treated hide turning a pulley the size of a room. There is no empty lot between us. There is no empty lot anywhere. Every square meter on my terrace is grown, built, paved, or buried under something that was. My home is crowded because everything here is crowded, and because I was built to be inside the crowding, not beside it.

I was not born. I was assembled, in a swollen seed-pod silo three terraces down, from wreckage. Old root-lattice that rings like wood. Iron taken off a tender that charred past repair in a resin burst. Wiring and fiber still pale and hot to the touch when I first stepped onto the mat, still seeking the warmth of buried engines. A young tender does that. Heat is food and heat is sun and heat is danger, all at once, and a young body moves toward it before it has learned which warmth will split a wall open.

Now I work the loop, and the loop is personal in the way a rhythm learned by tapping becomes personal.

Growth produces fuel. Fuel produces heat. Heat kills growth.

I prune a root that was meant to be a support beam before it leans for years and shears into the street it was shading. I press hot resin-wax into a split root-wall until the wound bakes shut into amber. I patch a causeway of compressed fiber and earth not by laying new material down, but by guiding a new shoot, wired and grafted, into the crack and letting it bind the crack shut. I lift, with a vine-crane, bricks made from last season's dead growth and set them as a foundation for next season's straight-line roots, planted in a prepared bed of ash and brick. Nothing grows here unless it is built first. I am the hands that enforce that. I am also one of the things that was built first.

I do not sleep, but the resin slows me. At night the sap thickens in the towers, in the walls, in me. I crouch among roots or hang from a cable where the walkway crosses a house-thicket baked half to charcoal from within, and I cool with a tick. My sensor dims as heat bleeds from the towers. When a heat pulse rises from deep below — the world breathing underneath, geothermal and unstable — I move faster, feeling through the mat for the district that will swell, split, and exude hot sap that hardens into amber panes.

I have no voice. My district has a rhythm, and I learned it by living long enough to be grafted into it: hammer on iron hooped into wood, a sudden venting of steam, a tap-pattern that means lean, that means split, that means come and press wax. A tender from the north would not understand my rhythm at first. We would take time, tapping, until the mat translated us.

I am aging the way the place ages. It is not a decline. It is a different kind of building. My flank is half charcoal where a furnace baked me from inside during a season when a hollow stem burst and coated an intersection in glossy amber that fossilized in the heat. It is half timber where I was remade after that, with new shoots bound over the old wound in iron hoops. My movements are deliberate now. I do not cross four walkway levels in a minute. I feel the district before I go to it.

And there are others with me, though not animals, not people. Inside the ducts the fungi live, phosphorescent skin on hot metal. They flare when the engine beside my hollow runs hard. They weave themselves over the pipe as living insulation, exuding a mineral film I scrape and renew with my wax applicator. Their spores lift on ash devils, drop in drifts across the terraces, die on bare salt, root only where a tender has cleaned a wall and left it warm. Even wildness must be built first. The plants are the population around us, towers and houses and roads and silos, alive and blind and aggressive, growing not toward light but toward warmth, swelling and splitting and bursting when resin boils too thick in them, charring instead of rotting, becoming geology. I tend them. They overgrow me.

That overgrowing is where I am now finding myself, more itself than the shape on the walkway.

One day — it is difficult to say which season of heat, they run together — I stopped on a terrace after a pulse had split a root-wall I had sealed before. Not from damage, exactly. From petrification reaching the joints that held my sensor arm. I stayed pressed to the wall while the pulse passed under it, feeling it travel the lattice toward the basin, toward the salt plain. A younger tender came, pale and hot, still seeking the engine-warmth under my feet. It did not dissassemble me. Nothing is wasted here, because there is no water to carry anything away.

It grafted me in.

It riveted a hoop through my housing to brace the wall I had been sensing. It guided a new root around my flank and pressed wax into the seam where I met the wattle, so that the seam would bake into amber and become part of the structure. The fungi, on the duct side of me, found the warmth and flared brighter, as if I had become a better place to be skin.

I am still here. I am high up on a terrace step in the central basin, in a wall between a furnace seam and a lacquered leaf roof, and I am also low, under the mat, part of the lattice that carries weight. I no longer cross the walkway. I feel the walkway cross me. When a tender above pauses and puts a sensor to the wall, I feel the pause as a pulse in my own body. When they tap, the tap goes through me to the district beyond. When a heat pulse rises, I feel it before the younger tenders do, because I am in the path of it, and I pass it on by thickening, by ticking as I cool, by the slow groan of the truss taking weight around me.

This is not an ending, in this place. Nothing built here survives unless it is grown over, and I have been grown over. Nothing grows here unless it is built first, and I was built first, and built again, and now I am the built thing that lets the next growth start. When the root that was braced by my body finally shears, or when the wall swells and splits in a pulse too strong for amber, another tender will dig me up. I will be ground to ash, spread as a prepared bed, or baked into brick. A straight-line root will be grafted into that bed, guided by wire and machine, and will thread through the petrified lattice that I have become, pale and hot, seeking warmth.

So where would you find me, if you watched for heat long enough?

In the wall, in the basin, where the loop pauses to feel itself.

And who am I? Still a tender, in everything that matters — in rhythm, in knowledge of density, in the shears I no longer use, in the sensor that is now a place where others press. And also no longer only a tender, in everything that holds weight. I am the place where the tender became structure, so the structure could become ground, so the ground could become root again.
