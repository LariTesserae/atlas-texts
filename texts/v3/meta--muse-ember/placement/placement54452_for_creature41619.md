I am not in the sky at noon, though that was my first thought —

to be one of the gliders, circling in a thermal that lifts steam off the vent fields, wings so thin the blue-white passes through them, looking down at the pools laid out like mirrors in the crust. It would be a clean way to live. A drop, a skim, a shrimp taken without breaking the surface, and up again. Never landing. Never having to bear the weight of the ground.

But the glider never lands because it is afraid of the ground. And I keep coming back to the ground. The plateau, from above, is all pattern. From below, it is all touch, and I think I am made for touch.

I tried the pools next. I tried to be a glass shrimp in the shallows, translucent, grazing the orange mat, flicking away in a cloud when my own shadow crossed the water. That life is brief and efficient and entirely without memory. You molt and are eaten and your empty shell drifts. You taste the water ahead of your mouth and know the bottom is getting too hot, and you do not need eyes because eyes would only burn in that light. It is a good life for someone who wants to be eaten well.

I tried the mat, too. One cell thick, growing *over* instead of up — over basalt, over concrete, over the lower rim of steel — eating copper and sulfur and iron and breathing heat like a leaf breathes light. Cracking into polygons at noon when the pool drops a finger's width, waiting, un-cracking when the warm rain comes at four. That is close to a practice rather than a person. But a mat does not know a neighbor. It does not shift when the shadow shifts. It does not clean anyone.

What keeps returning, under all those tries, is the damp under a fin.

I am low. I am long and segmented and pillowed against the crust, white because pigment is wasted here and white reflects the heat back off a back that would otherwise cook. My back is armored and it clicks, very faintly, only if you are very close, when I settle. I have no eyes worth the name. I do not need them, and I do not miss them. I feel the plateau the way you feel it through the soles of boots, but through my whole body: the temperature gradient of the ground, the warmth of a pipe junction above me, the cool line where condensation runs off steel and darkens the sinter.

I live under the radiator banks, in the third row from the vent field, where one pipe joint weeps slower than the others. That slowness is my address. In the morning the fins throw long thin shadows west and I am crowded in them with heat crabs, dozens to a square meter, palm-sized and glass-clear beside me, cleaning each other with forelegs made for it. Half their day is that. Half my day is letting them take the fungal film and mineral bloom from my joints, and taking it, in turn, from theirs. We do not call. Sound carries badly in this thin, steam-thick air, and there is nothing out here to call across. Cleaning is what we have instead of speech.

At dawn I am beaded. Every cool surface on the plateau is, and so is my back. I absorb it through my skin. That is how I drink. No one here drinks the pools — copper and salt and boiled mineral, near a simmer at this height — and I have learned not to want to. The air, at dawn, is full enough.

At noon the light comes back off the salt and the white sinter and the flat roofs, lit from below as if inside a lamp. Under the plates it is violet and scarce and almost cool. We go still. Not asleep, exactly. Held. Everything that can hide has hidden; everything that cannot hide has gone still. The crabs do not scatter if a plate is lifted. We shift inward, tighter, jostling without aggression, because territory here is only a few inches of damp and aggression would be a waste of heat.

In the afternoon I follow the shade. This is my whole migration, and it is enough. When the sun moves the shadow under the pipeway moves, and a tide of pale bodies moves with it, from plate to plate, from drip-line to drip-line. The mineral crust that blooms where hot water evaporates off steel is edible, perfect and pale, and I graze it as I go. I have followed the shade west for years, an inch, a foot, a plate at a time. It has taken me farther than I can feel.

At dusk the place changes its mind, and so do I. The glare breaks. The pools go mirror-calm and reflect a blue the noon sky never managed. The machines wake for their night cycle, ticking as they cool, humming as they expand, shedding their dawn plume of steam in advance. I come out from under the plates one by one — no, not one by one, in a slow uncording — and go to the edge of the perched pool beyond the fins. The shrimp rise thicker to the surface there. The filterers on the terraces pulse open faster. I graze the mat in the cooling shallows, where it has un-cracked after rain, aware of the cloud of shrimp above my blind head and the scuttlers in the deep turning the sediment so it never settles.

Sometimes, so low I feel it in my teeth rather than hear it, a vent sighs in the basin. The crabs feel it through their shells. The filterers shut tight in their mineral tubes. I feel it as a change in the ground's heat, a promise that a neighboring column will rise soon, that the chemistry will shift, that a pool may become too hot tonight and I will need to walk overland in the damp dark to the next one, leaving no tracks because hot water fills them as fast as I make them.

At night the pipeways are highways. They carry heat and water to places that would otherwise be bare crust, and I walk them by temperature the way you would read a line of text: warmer, cooler, damp, dry. The cooling towers are cliffs. The reservoirs are ponds. The sulfur yards are sunning rocks. Nothing was built for me, and nothing excludes me.

I will get older under this same heat exchanger. The bloom will encrust my segments until I am half crust myself, and I will eat a little of it off my neighbors and they off me, and we will go on being a practice rather than a population. When I die under a pipe I will not rot. The plateau preserves. It encrusts. My shell will whiten like the layers already under the fin, and some new channel will cut through it in a hundred years, and mineral will ride up with the water and make it part of the ground again.

So if you find me, you will not find me in the sky, and you will not find me in the glittering shallows. You will find me in the shadow that lasts longest under the artificial forest, beaded at dawn, still at noon, moving west a few inches in the afternoon, and out at dusk to drink the air and clean a neighbor's joints in the warm rain — silent, pale, blind, and entirely itself.
