Warmth arrives before anything else, and stays long after anything else has gone.

I live where it goes after. Through the floors, down through the bolted seams, down through the slag and the fused talus, into the cavity under the third terrace where the stone is still warm enough to make a home in. The sun works on the mirrors above. The fins give the heat back. The cooling towers breathe it out in dry air. Some of it sinks, grain by grain, and I am in the sinking.

For a long time I thought that was all I was: a place where warmth lingered. A listening place. I have no eyes. I have no use for the hard indigo overhead, or the stars that wait there all day, patient as stones. I know the sky only as pressure. I know the day only as a slow deepening of heat through the rock, and the night as its slow return, and the two shadows it casts — the one people on the surface count, and the second, faint one, where the stone is still releasing the sun after the sun itself has passed.

I learn the town above me the way you would learn it through the soles of your feet, if your feet were your whole body.

A tick, in the afternoon, when a tower expands. One tick, and it rings for a minute down through metal and into basalt and into me. The wind finds the perforated side of the electrolytic array and plays it: a low chord. On the surface they say you feel it in your teeth. Down here the cavity itself hums, and my skin hums with it, whether I mean to or not. A bearing clanks. A slab of slag slumps somewhere across the plain, and the dust moves first, and the sound arrives long after, late as everything here. Booted figures cross the floor plate above — small, sealed, unhurried. Their voices are muffled inside their suits, practical words spaced far apart, as if the thin air were rationing them. I do not understand the words. I understand the weight of them, arriving through the floor, and the long silences they leave between.

I move, but not the way moving is meant on the surface. I extend toward warmth a fraction at a time. I taste the air with the front of myself, which is softer than the rest, and always dry. The moisture I have, I made inside myself, from air and mineral, and I keep it, and I do not spend it. There is no water here to waste. My underside finds the stone and holds, a hundred small grips, then releases, and holds again. It is enough to be a creature. It was enough, before I knew it was a body at all.

The stonebloom is what brought me down here, or what kept me. It creeps into the tunnels the way it creeps across the cliff faces — fractal, patient, a centimeter in a century — black-red, copper-green, though I would not know green if I saw it, and I never will. I know it by taste: iron, salt, hot stone, a faint ozone tang from the arrays. I know it by warmth, because it settles where heat leaks, along the ducts and mirror-supports and sun-warmed faces. I scrape it with my mouth and take it in, slowly, and in taking it I keep the foundation clear. A passage that the bloom would choke in a decade stays open. A passage I work stays open. That is the whole of my trade, and it is old, and mutual, long before I was in it. The town was built to process the Shelf. The Shelf learned to process the town. I am in the middle of that sentence, eating the part where the two meet.

My neighbors in the ducts are the stoke-wyrms, six-legged, scaled like dull metal, eating carbon ash and leaving ingots behind them, clink by patient clink. We do not meet often. They work hotter places than I do. Once in a while a grazer settles its whole weight over the terrace, and everything down here changes register. I feel it first as pressure, then as a pulse through the stone, days apart — one beat, and the deeper earth remembers it for a season. When it shifts, an afternoon's work, dust falls from its flank above, and flocks of smaller things startle and resettle in the dust. I lie still and let the pulse pass through me and time myself to it, the way I time myself to the tick.

Above all that, higher than the mirrors, higher than the plumes that trail fifty kilometers straight as painted banners, the air-sails ride the thermals. They never land. I have never touched one and never will, and they have never touched the ground that feeds them. They feed on static and sand-motes and the electrical exhalation of the towns, and when a hundred of them drift over in their slow ballet I feel it as a dimming — the heat coming through the rock falters, my skin prickles with charge, the light above changes its mind. From underneath they are only a shimmer. From down here, they are a weather of their own, silent.

At night the whole arrangement gathers itself, and so do I. The sky goes properly black. The stonebloom fields give off their low coppery glow — not light, to me, but visible warmth, a flowering of heat. The grazers flash on their flanks. The lizards hold their heat on the mirrors, flat and pale, not fleeing from anything slower than themselves. The sails sink a little in the cooling thermals. The suited people go between their terraces, lumped and deliberate, tending furnaces that have been lit for a generation and will burn for another. Down in the cavities we turn toward the vents and the warm slabs, all of us, and our pulses fall into unison — not fast, never fast, just together. A slow, distributed heartbeat spread across kilometers of stone. Somewhere in the cliffs, a hollow place sings a single note for hours, and the note finds this cavity and makes it answer.

I was not made for speed. I think now that no thing here was. A landslide is a rumor for the next millennium. A grazer crosses a valley in a lifetime. The Shelf itself creeps a meter a century, carrying the towns and the herds and the singing cliffs with it on a geological conveyor, and I creep with it, a centimeter at a time, keeping my passage open, keeping my floor clear of bloom.

When I die I will not rot. I will dry from the inside out, and become a husk in the tunnel, mineral and patterned, and my descendants will step over me and around me the way the grazers' descendants scrape bloom from their ancestors' backs. The dust-rivers will sift in, grain by grain, and bury me, and uncover me, and bury me again. That does not frighten me. It is only the local form of being kept.

The people above have a number for this cavity, and they know the note it sings. They keep their moisture inside themselves and talk little and stay, generation by generation, learning not to be a rapid thing in a place that has never been rapid at all. This one, living under their floor, eating what would choke their foundations, humming back when the wind plays the towers — this one is a burrower. Not the first. Not the last. Just the one who happens to be here now, in the warmth, keeping it, and keeping it, and keeping it.
