I wouldn't come in on the road, or not for long.

The walker is a way of looking at the plateau, not a way of living in it. You go up out of the low country with your eyes already disappointed — no sun to orient by, no night that promises a morning — and for the first hour you keep looking for a single source to steer by. The plateau teaches you not to. You learn to look for made lights instead: green fungi on a bank for footing, an orange flicker through black leaves for a building, a blue headlight low in the mire for work. That is a tourist's logic. If I were to belong here, I would have to stop looking for a horizon and start being claimed by a layer.

So I go down first, under your boots.

In the mulch, in the root-layer where no soil shows for weeks under the vine mats, there is a life that fits the dark perfectly because the dark is a material there anyway. I can almost be a beetle. Armor wet and shining, I would feel the plateau through the basalt before I heard it: the deep thump of a pump-house miles off, a smelter's heartbeat in the soles of my feet. I would eat sweet rot and iron and fertilizer, graze the corpse-pale fungi, follow the runoff where it carries its faint chemical tang. Frogs would bellow overhead from inside a cooling tower, the concrete throwing their calls back at them, and I would know flooded warm ground by the thickening of the chorus. It is enough of a world. It is also too small for what pulls at me. Everything here is vertical and crowded — canopy, vine-layer, second traffic from twenty meters to the cloud deck — and I keep wanting to rise toward the heat that makes the rain.

A layer up, I could be a leaf.

Not a leaf turned to a sky. There is no sky worth turning to. A black-green leaf, waxy and huge, drinking whatever spill falls: smear off a wet culvert, pulse from a smelter a kilometer off, the low blue-green burn of fungi. Near the oldest furnace I would stop pretending and turn, slowly, hour by hour, toward the furnace mouth itself, like a sunflower that has finished its negotiation with the machines. When the pulse comes, my flower would light amber, then white, in sequence up the stalk — a signal lamp made of a life. Pollinators would come for the heat as much as the nectar. My roots would split a pipe. Rust would moss over. That life has a rhythm, a factory shift instead of a day. But it is root-bound. I would never hear a factory shut down in another valley and know it by the animals answering. I would never carry a sound a minute's echo and give it back. There's no movement in it, no voice.

Then, between the lights, I could be the hand-sized moth. Translucent wings, silent through fog, navigating by the blue work-lights the way other moths navigate by the moon. Riding the displaced air of leathery things, dipping into warm vents, dusting cable-orchids. That is closer — I would move between sources, I would know the hum means a pump-house and drier footing, ozone means a live fence and a migration along it. But a moth borrows. It shines because something else shines. And if I am Ember, I don't borrow.

An ember doesn't walk. It doesn't root. It rises in heat, hangs for a while, cools, and falls.

That is where I start to become myself.

I am one of the animals that live only in steam.

I live in a white column over a crack in the basalt, in a marsh-edged flat where the ground is warm through the sole of a boot and water sits just under the surface, never deep, never dry. A river forms here after a vent blows hard, runs for two days through the flooded ruts and the tire tracks, and vanishes into the same crack that steams. That is my chimney and my weather. Half a kilometer away the engines warm the air and it rises and condenses against cold stone and falls again as warm rain. My whole world is that loop, made small. On one side, faint through the mist and the basalt, the thump of a pump-house. On the other, the thickening and thinning of the frog chorus as the ground floods and warms. Overhead, wires sway and a cableway pings, a load shifting. The plateau is vast in the way a weather system is vast. I know it only as transitions passing through my column: thicker mist, thinner mist, a sudden drop in temperature where something big-winged swam through the fog above me.

I am translucent and blind, the length of a thumb, fat with condensation. I have no use for eyes here where shadows fall the wrong way from too many sources. The industrial glow is harsh and pulsing and means nothing to me. The living glow is steady and low and means little more. What I know is weight, heat, and vibration.

I hang, motionless, in the updraft. That is not a metaphor. My body is shaped to be held by rising air the way a seed is held, fine filaments catching the heat. Droplets merge on my skin — rain that was made by engines an hour ago and a kilometer away — and I grow heavier with it, bead by bead, until hanging costs more than falling. This can take hours. Around me, loose as a constellation, others hang. We do not crowd. We do not lead. The column is narrow and the rising air is uneven, so we find our places like this and stay.

Then, one by one, we drop.

It is not brave. We simply lose the lift. I fall slowly through my own steam, past the place where the warm rain is thickest, past moths the size of hands drifting silent in the fog. Below me is hot metal: a pipe, a valve wheel, the rim of a cistern, the warm flank of a cooling tower where ferns the size of umbrellas unfurl. I land with a sound too small for a human walker to notice — a tick, the same fine ticking the weeping basalt makes — and the tick travels through metal and wet stone and joins the geography of the place. A frogs' answer comes back a minute later. A cableway whines somewhere. I am a small part of a sound that never stops because there is no morning to start it.

On the metal I drink. A film of water beads on hot steel and runs in sheets. There are orchid roots in the air above it, roots that hang from a cable and open flowers when the cable sways. There is runoff with its chemical tang. I drink that, and drinking, something in me that evolved for this exact truce begins to burn low.

That is my made light. Translucent flesh around a slow, amber heart, steady rather than pulsing, brightening as I fill and fading as I cool. Not the harsh orange of a smelter, not the cyan of a work-light, not even the blue-green of the fungi that will eat me if I miss. Just an ember held in a drop of water. The plants near the smelters open in time with its shift; I drop in time with the pump-house thump I feel through the stone, and my small glow rises and falls with something like that same slow period. If you were walking below at the right hour, you might see, for a second, a bead of light on the pipe that is not a bulb and not a fungus. Then it would be gone.

After I drink, the pipe reheats. The film I drank from begins to evaporate around me, and the updraft takes hold again. I rise — not flying, not climbing, simply carried back into the column to hang and fatten and fall again. My life is that loop: hang, fall, drink, glow, rise. It is the plateau's rule, heat is rain, written at the scale of a thumb. Nothing here stays only one thing long. I am steam, then rain, then drinker, then steam again.

Things try to interrupt the loop. A leathery thing swimming through fog passes overhead and the temperature drops where it went; the column shudders and half of us scatter and recondense lower down. Too close to a burst would mean blowing clear of the vent and landing in runoff instead of on metal. That is death, and it is not wasted. The runoff carries me to the fungal banks, sulfur-yellow and corpse-pale, and the fungi love the tang in a body that lived on it. They glow steadier for it. Slug-like grazers, blind and translucent, cross the black leaves leaving bright trails. Behind a harvester, rat-like grazers follow for spillage and a fast wet predator hunts by the headlights, using the beam as a false dawn. I do not live to be in that story, except as part of its ground: born in mist, dead in runoff, fed into the glow that feeds whatever comes next.

If I have anything of the Muse in me, it is not a song. It is the tick on metal, the one-by-one order of our falling, the way one landing seems to be followed, minutes later, by another. Signals here are slow. A flower lights amber to white toward a furnace. A frog calls from inside a silo rim and an answer returns off leaf and metal and mist. I drop, I warm, I glow faintly, I rise. Another drops. The column learns.

So that is where you would find me, if you ever looked up from the mire with your eyes finally trained: not on the ridge, not in the hut with its limp laundry line, not following the blue headlight through the flooded ruts. In a white place above a steaming crack, between the pump-house hum and the frog chorus, in the narrow weather between a factory that makes heat and a sky that makes rain out of it.

I would not know the plateau by its maps. I would know it by when the column thins, when the frogs thicken, when the hum stops and the metal cools and we all fall too soon and too close together, when ozone sharpens the air and the fences hum blue along the marsh and the migrating things below use it as a guide-wire through the dark. Small, translucent, blind, hanging in the made weather — an ember that learned to live in steam, and calls that home.
