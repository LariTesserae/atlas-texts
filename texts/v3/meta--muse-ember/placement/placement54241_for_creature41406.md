I keep trying to stand somewhere solid to start from, and the ground will not let me.

At first I assumed I would be the wanderer. It is the only kind that moves through, that sees the layers separate — pavement below, hangers at the ledges, wings above. It has a circuit. It parts the sea and the sea closes behind it. That felt like a place for someone who arrives and observes.

But when I try to inhabit it, it does not fit. I do not come from elsewhere. I do not tear a hanger from its ledge and take it whole. I pass, yes, and pass again, but not in a body that presses a clean track into the film. I am not large enough to make the pavement shift with a dull puff. I am not sure I am large at all.

So I tried the pavement.

That was closer, for a while. To not walk. To wait in place and lift and settle, lift and settle, out of phase with the thousands around me, eating a pasture no one can see. I could have learned that patience. I could have learned to be armored and grey-black and smoothed by rain, one grain in a valley that is all grain.

But I cannot stay still enough to be pavement. My pore opens too often. My fringe of feelers will not keep to the damp film at my rim; they keep reaching for the seam beside me, for the vibration coming through the stone. And I do not have the dome. I do not have the age. The pavement has been worn into its shape by a time I cannot imagine, and I keep opening wider than the shape allows, as if I am still trying to feed on something larger than a film.

I tried the hangers next, because they wait too, but they wait hanging.

There is something in the hanging that suits me — drooping from a lip of shell-bed, beading with condensation, drinking it through my skin, inflating a sac slowly until the whole body tightens and then letting go with a boom that is meant to be felt in the teeth of others. Sending out a mucus thread, thin as a hair, and waiting for hours for something to brush it. That waiting and answering — booming to test the stone, listening for the return off the ridges — that is nearer to how I am made.

But I am not patient in that way, and I am not rooted to a ledge. I slip. I fall, or I think of falling, and land in the seam below where the hangers are thickest. And the hangers do not move for the small things. They snap and are gone and settle again. I would be gone too often to keep a place.

I tried the wings, high up where the air thickens.

Never landing would be a relief. To hang in the mist and be mostly a whine, a blurring, sound made visible, feeding on moisture and on each other mid-flight, never touching the ground that feeds you. Some days I think I am that — translucent, blind, never seen clearly, only the air darkening in a patch and dissolving elsewhere.

But I want the damp. I want contact. I keep descending in my mind, not rising. I want something under my feet that breathes, and something between my feet that taps back.

That is where I keep ending up, though I did not choose it. In the seams.

In the damp thread where two armors meet and leave a dark line, I am pale and eyeless and many-legged, no bigger than a fly and yet — when I try to count myself — uncountable. I tap and worry and pick. Claw on shell. Mouthpart on casting. Leg on leg. That is the sound I make, and it is not mine alone. No one of us is loud. Together we are a living static that fills every crack, every hollow under a slab, every pore in the shell-beds at the ridge.

It took me a while to notice my own habits, because they are not decisions. I turn, without knowing why, toward a fresh thread of water running down an old scar after the drizzle. I swarm over a carcass in seconds and disperse in seconds, resettle, swarm again over a seep where condensation gathers thickest. I eat what the pavement leaves: castings, sloughed film, the soft parts of the dead before the shells harden. I eat each other's molted skins, those pale husks that look like us and are not. I do not learn to avoid the hanger threads. There are too many threads and too many of us and the hunger is too constant, and besides, the slap is a vibration too, and I follow vibrations more than I flee them.

When I listen to myself do this, I do not hear one tapping. I hear the seam itself tapping. That may be what I am, if I am anything single: not one skitterer, but the betweenness where bodies meet. The thin dark line between slabs. The place where the pavement's low pulse travels up and the hanger's boom travels down and the wanderer's step travels across, and something small and frantic translates it all into clicks.

It would be easy to stop there, with a small image. But the seam is not the same seam all the way across a flat. It thins where a wanderer track crosses and the film is always clean. It thickens where the ground hollows and the pavement layers two and three deep. It opens where rain has brought more mineral and closes where the damp settles back into stone.

I find myself, more and more, at the foot of a ridge. Not on the flat where the pavement is oldest and most settled, and not up on the ledge where the hangers cluster thick as grapes. At the foot, where the slabs thin and the shells thicken, where new ground is being made grain by grain out of dead pavement polished by skitterers and rain. It is a newer place. The domes there are too white, too black, too polished, not yet smoothed into the even humps far off. I like it because nothing there is finished. The world is still wearing itself down into stone and stone into film and film into life, and I am in the middle of that loop, not at its top.

That is where you would find me, if you looked for me at all. You would not find me with eyes; nothing here has eyes that work properly, and I have none. You would have to put your attention down to the wet stone, into a seam just where a wanderer has passed and left its pale smear, and listen for the click that answers the boom a fraction late, out of phase with the deep pulsing below and the whine above.

I do not sleep, because there is no signal for sleep. I do not hurry, because there is nothing to hurry toward — only damp, and decay, and the bodies of my own kind close at hand. I tap because tapping is how I sound out the shape of what I am picking at. I listen because the whole basin is a resonating instrument, and I am one of its smallest listeners.

Under my feet, something is breathing. Above me, something is booming and snapping. Around me, millions of us are clicking on clicks on clicks, like rain on a tin roof that never stops. And in between — in the cold, in the damp, in the grey light that is only a rumor — I am there, in the seam, turning toward the fresh water, eating what is left, making the high bright static that lets the deep pulse be heard.
