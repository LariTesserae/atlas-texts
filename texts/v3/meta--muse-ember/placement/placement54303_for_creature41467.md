If you kneel and press your palm flat, not to feel stone but to feel the pattern change from inch to inch, in some places what yields under you — almost imperceptibly, as if something beneath the surface had shifted its weight — that is me.

I am not on the plateau. I am in it, an inch below its dry skin, where your boot finds the damp though the surface looks dry. That is where the damp lives here: inside. In the deep cups and cracks and in the channels that run for meters beneath the swell where the stone is pitted thickest, cream and rust and dove-grey above, dark and wet and continuous below. If you could find me at all, you would find me there, between the pores, not in any one of them.

I have no eyes to find a place with. To me the rock is not a surface to be looked at from a distance, it is a country you move through by contact. Pore to pore, cup to cup, under the shingled domes and under the palm-sized overhangs where other kinds hang, a channel goes on and on in the dark, and I am its length. Thread-like, blind, wet to the touch if touch is the word for what I do against the wall, I extend myself along it and withdraw and extend again — slower than a grazer’s body-length in a day — widening what has already been opened by the patient pressure of my body, feeding on the films that grow where the light never reaches, and sealing behind me with a lining of my own substance as I go.

That is how the erosion continues now. Weather made the first pits, sun polished the swells, wind took the weak grain. Then we came, or the rock opened enough for us to live in the opening, and the work was taken over from within, grain by patient grain. I do not bite the stone. I breathe through my skin against its damp wall, and in breathing I take something, and in taking I leave mineral behind, and the channel narrows where I have passed and widens where I am pressing. Behind me the stone is quieter, denser. Ahead of me it is still waiting to be opened. A plant root above, following an old grazer’s cleaned path, will sometime find my sealed trail and follow it for the one flowering it gets in several years. I do not know it is gardening, any more than the grazer knows. We only do not close what the other needs open.

At dawn, above me, the plateau exhales. I know it not by sight but by the change at my skin: the warm rock gives up its heat in the night and the air near it sweats, the cups fill with their thumbnail of standing water, the thousands of shingled backs open at once and a vapor rises so faint it would be nothing if my whole world were not made of its return. In the evening the vapor is drawn back in, the stone ticks as it cools, and the ticking travels down to me as a contraction, a settling of houses for the night. Somewhere under a ledge, a translucent hanger tightens its grip and lets go a little more condensation onto the underside of the rock; the change in mineral damp is a message, and I answer it by a touch against the neighboring thread, by extending a branch a fraction further into an unopened pore, by leaving a scent in the lining for the next body to taste chemically as it passes.

Above me the grazers move across the open swells, fringed, coin-sized, wiping the biofilm clean and leaving the stone paler behind them. The barrel-bodied ones pass over me like slow weather, a few meters in a week, damp-skinned, armored in mosaic, their folds crowded with passengers who will be carried and sheltered and sealed in with them through a dry spell. Their weight is a pressure I have learned to wait under. Dry spells here last longer than any observer can comfortably imagine. Then I draw in, I close the pores along myself, I leave my lining thick behind me, and I wait in the humidity that stays in the deepest cups, outwaiting a season and then another, neighbor pressed to neighbor, until condensation gathers again and I can open and dampen and resume.

Birth, for me, is not an event. I do not disperse. I feed where I stand and I branch where I stand, and the young live beside the old in the same channel until the layer is thicker rather than wider. A cleared disc where the crust has been worn smoother over a hundred years is not recolonized by a rush from above. It is recolonized from below and from the edges, body-length by body-length, until the paleness is gone. Nothing rushes here, because there is nowhere empty to rush to, and nothing invades, because nothing is unoccupied. A foreign spore landing on the swell would find every pore claimed, every trail already scented: this is lived in, thoroughly.

The artifacts are the same work seen from the other side. You come on a low arc of larger pebbles, a ring no higher than your ankle collapsed on one side, a cone of pellets, a cairn of old shells. No one built them at once. From below I feel them as lines of weight that accumulate over centuries — a barrelback nudging a pebble with its shoulder in the course of its week-long journey, grazers moving a dead shell a millimeter at a time toward the low side, my own branch pushing up a little dust as it opens a pore. Century upon century the nudges accrete into something almost made and almost natural, and then we begin, at the same rate, to unmake it, returning monument to stone again. When I die in place, if that is the phrase, I do not rot away. My lining becomes a shelter, then a mineral, then part of the path.

Your footsteps, if you come, are too loud on the crust. I feel them as a brief, abrupt pressure through the stone, a clumsy presence on the skin of a community that has finished its long experiment. You will stay a day, perhaps, and think we have not moved at all. We have. By accretion, by a branch, by a sealed pore reopened, by a channel widened by less than the width of your thumbnail. Then you will go — the blue haze of the lower country will take you back down — and the paleness where you pressed will be reclaimed neighbor by neighbor, and the thick silence will close over the place where your voice would have been, for we have no lungs to voice with and no wings to beat the air, only skin and shell and touch, only a slow pulse traveling the length of a thread in the dark.

So find me where the stone is warm long after sunset, especially in the pits where heat gathers and stays. Find me an inch below the dry skin, under the field of shingles, between two water cups that hold the sky for weeks, in the damp continuity that links them. I will be there, grain by patient grain, opening and sealing, breathing the pore’s wet air, tasting the trails left by the grazers and the hangers and the barrelbacks above, holding together not by balance, exactly, but by contact — pore by pore, body by patient body, for longer than any of us, or you, can measure.
