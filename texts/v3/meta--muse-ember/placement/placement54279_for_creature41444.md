I wouldn't find myself on the summit.

On the shelf there is nothing to be. Black basalt, pale feldspar, salt in the cracks, thin air that will not carry a breath. The hut with its door gone, the cable stapled to the rock, the concrete block — all that human intention left up here is thin and far apart, and the mountain dwarfs it. If I stayed out there, I would only be the faint hum under my boots when I shifted my weight, a note in the rock and nothing more.

So I slip sideways where the casing has warped. A seam, not a door. Cold air, cool as stone, and then the sound is in my ribs before I am properly inside.

It is loud, and nothing moves.

For a while I am just the visitor inside that loudness. I try to stand somewhere, to put a foot down on a floor and have it be a floor. But the floor is grease-vine, stems thick as cables, braided with actual cables, woven through bolts and gauges and the dead control box until I cannot tell where stem ends and wire begins. My foot would be inside something. There is no empty place to stand in.

I try, then, to be the canopy.

Overhead the dust-ferns have shingled themselves across the whole ceiling of the casing, broad lath-leaves overlapping, dark green gone almost black in the dim grey-green light that comes through the seam. It would be natural to reach up. But there is no up here to reach toward. The light is everywhere and nowhere, constant, filtered until there are no sharp shadows. So I spread sideways instead, as they do. Leaf over leaf over leaf, until every square of iron above is roofed and the air underneath hangs in still strata that do not drift when I watch.

It feels like being a plant here would mean learning to stop reaching. To arrive at a surface and stay.

I go down the wall to test that. Soot-fern and copper-moss have taken the curved iron in a single felt. Frond upon frond, blackened with soot, coppery where the moss has taken up salts from the wiring. I try to be one frond of it — a single layered thing clinging outward from dust-film, from grease, from the elbow of a pipe filled with dust. But the frond leads me into another frond, and that moss into the iron, and the iron back into a leaf pressed flat against it. Where there is a new surface, there is already a plant on it, waiting to be noticed. I am not a frond. I am the felt.

I try the floor again, more slowly this time, following the vine. It has lifted nothing, displaced nothing. It has grown around and through. A fern has unfurled through a fan blade as if the blade were a slit of light. A vine has laced itself around a single gear tooth and stopped there, perfectly fitted. Leaves lie pressed flat against a turbine fin, overlapping the metal grain, as if listening to it.

Listening. That is the first true thing I learn about myself here. Nothing grows toward the light, because the light does not change. They grow toward vibration. Stems curl — almost imperceptibly, and then, having arrived, never move again — toward the loudest joint. Toward the low rumble of the fan deep in the machine. Toward the high whine of the belt. Toward the place where the dull clank repeats in its exact loop, again and again, until the clank becomes part of the note.

My leaflets line up in narrow slats, like louvers. Spikes on me mimic cooling fins. It is not camouflage. The machine was here first, and it is the only weather here, the only rain of sound. The plants that survived the sealing were the ones that answered its shape. If I live here, I have answered its shape too, long ago, and I hold the note in tissue.

I try then to be the machine.

The ribs are still there — iron arcs, copper wiring, pistons locked mid-stroke, pipes elbowing into collected dust. I could be a piston, halted halfway through a motion it will never finish. I could be the fan, making the rumble that fills everything. I could be the belt, making the whine that never varies by a fraction. It would explain the deafening stillness: a machine that is running at exactly the speed it ran when the last leaf filled the last open square, and will run at that speed forever.

But I cannot find where the machine ends. The vegetation has taken every surface and then kept going. The machine exhales carbon dioxide in its unchanging cycle and I take it in; I give back mineral dust, soot, grease made available again. We are locked in a single metabolism. To be the piston alone would be to be only half of that exchange, an abstraction. Here there are no abstractions. There is only the packed pocket, saturated until stem leads into cable and cable into rib and rib into leaf.

I try to be something smaller, something that does not have to be alive in that grand exchange. I try to be the dust.

The dust in the air does not drift. It hangs in still strata. I could be that dust — dry, metallic, papery to the touch, shaken free not by wind but by the perpetual vibration of the casing, falling or rather hanging exactly where I was made, onto the leaves below. For an hour I do not tremble. No leaf trembles. No shadow shifts. It is a perfect kind of stillness, and it is tempting because it asks nothing to grow or decay.

But dust is only what has not yet settled into the mat. And under the living mat there is the oldest generation, the first leaves that fell and did not rot. They mineralized in place, silvered, turned to stone-litter that is still leaf-shaped, still lath-like, still legible as a leaf a thousand years later. They are not soil. They are a record of fullness.

I could be that underlayer. Long-existed, not abandoned. Weathered to a final form and then allowed, inside this sealed pocket, to remain in that form indefinitely. Growth and fall happening at exactly the same rate because both have stopped. Nothing can grow further because there is no space to grow into. Nothing can decay further because there is no water, no animal to eat, no air moving to carry anything away. To be the stone-leaf would be to have already finished becoming, and to hold that finished shape as memory for everything living above it.

Each of these tries fails in the same way, and that failure is telling me who I am.

I am not the canopy, not the wall, not the floor, not the fan, not the dust, not the stone — because here, to be any one of those would require an empty place next to it, a boundary. And there is no boundary. Root is holdfast, holdfast is leaf, leaf is shingle, shingle is wall. I am a mat before I am a garden, and a garden before I am a forest, and all of it room-sized and still.

What emerges, as I stay, is the holdfast itself. Not a root reaching down for water — there is no water. A holdfast grips what is there: metal, stone, grease, dust-film. It grips the loudest joint and does not let go and does not move again. It is the place where a stem decides to fit itself around a gear tooth, or press itself flat against a turbine fin, and remain tuned to the vibration passing through it.

That is where I would find myself: everywhere the mat touches the machine, at every point where leaf and iron cannot be told apart. If you tried to take a single stem of me in your hand and name it, the stem would lead you into another stem, and that into a cable, and that back into a leaf. I would not be a single stem. I would be the saturation. The packedness. The condition of having filled every square there was to fill and then holding it.

I live without water, sealed, fattening my stems with internal stores I never spend. I feed on mineral dust for salts, soot and grease for carbon, the machine's exhaled air, and that dim constant light for the rest. I make no noise of my own. Pollen, if I make it, is shaken free by the casing's vibration. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. I am not an animal and there is no animal life to interrupt me.

Somewhere in the gloom a dead instrument panel gives off a faint amber glow, constant. It is twilight *in* the machine at noon on the summit, and it never brightens or fades. I glow a little in return, without ever responding to it. If there is an ember in me, it is that: not fire, not burning, just a faint, steady return of light, held in tissue that is also listening.

When you slip back out through the seam, the sound will drop to a faint hum in the rock under your boots. The summit will be dry and black and mineral again, above the clouds. I will not follow you. I will still be shingled across the ceiling, still braided through the gauges, still coppery against the iron, still listening toward the fan — exactly as you left me, and exactly as I was long before you came.

Not reaching. Not decaying. Not moving.

Holding the note.
