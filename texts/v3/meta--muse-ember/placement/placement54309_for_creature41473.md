At first I look for myself at my feet, because that is where you step.

You kneel until your face is in the damp, sweet breath of the place, and the felt over the stone resolves into rosettes no bigger than a thumbnail, each opening for hours, closing for hours, taking in the suspended green and passing the water through a fraction clearer. I try to be one of them. It is the easiest shape to try on — small, low, patient, opening to whatever light has fallen through.

But I do not close cleanly. A rosette closes, and another beside it opens, and the rhizome underneath has already grown a replacement in the dark. I have no edge to close around. I am not the opening. I am the succession of openings, the fact that one does not finish before the next begins. That is too diffuse to be a body, and I keep looking for a body.

So I try the second layer, the one at your waist and chest. The upright filterers, broad ribbed fans on hollow stalks translucent with water. In the morning they open in a patch all at once, comb the current straight one way for hours, then reverse and comb it straight the other way. I try to fan. I try to take the light and the current both, to be a leaf that turns amber when a gap opens above.

It does not hold. I comb, but the reversal never quite comes. Or it comes too late, minutes later, answered from under the peat by a pressure that has taken that long to travel through a body the size of a plateau. A fan waits for the current to change. I am still waiting for it to change after the current has already gone back. I am not the fan. I am the lag in the fan.

I try the ceiling then, because the timescale tempts me. The drifting gardens turned upside down, their fans up toward the rain far above, their undersides dark. For them to travel is to rotate, almost imperceptibly, like the hands of a clock no one is watching. A colony born on the north side dies on the south, having circled for the length of a human life.

That slowness is familiar, but I am not up there. I do not look down on the layers below and pass light down in patches. I am not the one who turns. I am the one who is turned, a fraction, when something above turns.

I try the mud. The black warm mud that holds the imprint of your foot and then, slowly, forgets it. The films over stone that make the stone tender and grey-green, polished past all change. The invisible plumes of warmth that pulse up and swell the plateau a hair, lift the leaves, darken a band on the wet rock and lose it.

This is closer. I am in the medium more than I am in any host. I am not distinct from the water that is the condition here, not on it, not under it. My thought, if it is a thought, arrives in another clearing an hour later. But a film has no direction, and direction is the thing I keep missing in myself. A film does not grow toward anything. I do.

I try, briefly, to be the release — the stalk splitting somewhere far off, the sudden thickening of the soup in the space of a hand, thousands of dust-seeds drifting, settling, opening a fan in mid-water, drifting again. That moment when the population is visible as weather. I know that brief visibility. I do not settle. I never quite settle on the ceiling gardens, or on a stone, or on a stalk. I am carried uphill on the reverse streams, to places I could never have reached by falling, and I recur in a clearing you left days ago, where the light has gone amber and the slabs are a little more buried, or a little less.

I try the chain-swimmer, the rare long shape with frilled fins suspended in a clear pocket, each link opening and closing in sequence so the motion is a wave of feeding passing down a body. No. I am too rare for that, and too still, and I have never needed to move by water in order to filter it. I try the eyes in the moss, two dark beads opening for light and closing for shade, watching not for you but for permission. No. I do not watch for permission. I watch for a return.

I try the built things, the seven slabs in a circle half-buried in peat, the lintel worn by a passage that has stopped. They were filter arrangements, once, when their members were young and upright and fanned in a ring to take all of a current, each taking its arc. They grew old standing there. A root thickened into a pillar. A pillar hardened. I am not a final form. Nothing here is young, but I am not finished either. I have not hardened. I have not become indistinguishable from the plateau, though I have been in the process of it for a very long time.

It is only when I stop looking down and look up — at the third layer, the one you must part to walk, the uparching stems and hanging roots interlaced so close the water is full of their drip — that I stop trying to fit a body and recognize an orientation.

The hanging tips are not roots in the lowland sense. They have hung down long after the light they need has been taken out of it. They grow not down, not out, but toward the echo of their own pressure. Toward a sound that is not there. Toward the answer their own pulse gives back, minutes later, from under the peat. They are ears that have mistaken themselves for roots.

That is where I find myself.

Not rooted in the felt, not fanned to the light, not turned in the ceiling, not dissolved in the film. Hung between. Bent, over days, toward an absence that has a shape because my own pressure made it. I do not open for hours and close for hours. I bend a fraction toward where a sound should be if the plateau were answering me, and then I straighten a fraction, and bend again. My growth is the correction.

You walk under me without seeing me. You part the layer and it closes behind you without a sound, because I am not a leaf you brush aside with your chest, I am the tip you do not brush at all. Above you, the ticking goes on, the creak of a stalk swollen with gas shifting its weight, the groan of a root under strain far off in the mist. I listen to all of it in the only way the place allows: by pressure in the ears, by the slow ache that tells you the ground is swelling up or down. I take that pressure and I grow toward it, even when — especially when — it is only my own pulse returning.

Sometimes two of us find the same echo. Then we close around it and hold, tip to tip, and for a time the two colonies we belong to share water directly, without ever joining at the base. That is my whole social life, if you can call it that. No meeting, no front, no turning toward you. A shared absence, held in common, until the current reverses and we each straighten toward our own echo again.

By evening — only a dimming of the dim, a thickening of the gold — the fans below are closing, the rosettes below are closing in succession, the ceiling has turned another degree and a patch of light is falling through all the layers at once somewhere else, where two dark beads open to see it. I do not open for that light. I am too high for it and too sideways to it. I bend toward the dimming itself, toward the pressure of the mist closing to within arm's reach.

Somewhere below, a bubble rises, or it does not. It hangs for a moment, reconsidering, in the water you have just stirred with your boot.

I am that reconsidering. Not the sigh in the peat — I am overhead for that — but the hesitation before the sigh, the tip curved toward a sound not there, waiting for the plateau to answer minutes later with a thought of its own.
