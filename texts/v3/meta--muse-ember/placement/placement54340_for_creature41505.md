I did not arrive. The air condensed on me, and that was the beginning.

I find myself already high up, already wet, already dripping for no reason I can see. This is not a peak. Below me there is no lowland, only a rolling dark-green surface that rises and falls like a sea, and would be a mountain range wreathed in cloud if mountains were made of leaf. I am full of water, and so is everything else. If I hold still, I will become substrate. That is the first thing I learn, before I learn what I am.

So I try, at first, to be a top leaf.

I am the first intercept. Rain comes — not rain, surf, twenty layers of surf stacking down — and I take it on my broad, furred surface and pop. That is a noise I make: a trunk expanding a millimeter, a knuckle crack. I do not make it on purpose. It makes itself in me when I grow. The light comes, dim, filtered through something that never quite lets the sun finish arriving, and I take that too. I am supposed to be the surface. I am supposed to face up.

But under my own surface is someone else's sky. I never see the light directly for long; I see it already negotiated. And I discover my work is not catching. It is passing on.

Inside me, in my veins, the leaf is translucent. I slip with the light down those veins, fiber by fiber, a signal carried into deep tissue that will never see the top. Light here is not simply stolen layer by layer, though it is stolen — seven, ten, twenty layers, each taking its little more until what reaches the bottom is a rumor. I am the carrying. Down, down, inside, where it is green-grey twilight all the time and never fully darkens. I cease to be a surface and become a conduit. That feels truer, and also less like a self.

Then I become the drip.

Water that lands on me does not reach the ground. It is caught, and caught again, and caught again. I am passed from leaf to leaf, down through creepers angling to intercept what the canopy missed, down through the wrist-thick shrub leaning year after year into the one gap it can sense, thickening only where pressure tells it to thicken. Each body stores me in stem, in leaf, in hollow. The whole plateau is a marsh lifted to altitude, and we are sponges learning to stack. If I linger in any one of them — if I hold still long enough — I will become layer. I will become mat. I will become next year's leaf without ever becoming litter. So I cannot stay. I can only be the passing.

I pass into the mat.

Meters thick. Moss cushions the size of a hand, saturated, breathing when pressed by the weight above and recovering. Fern fronds pressed flat like shingles. Water rises clear and very cold around where I press. Lift, and the spongy resistance seals itself in hours, cambium flowing like warm wax over the wound. There is no soil to stand in. We stand in each other. Nothing is allowed to die and leave; what falls becomes layer, decay happens in place instantly, turned by fungi and bacteria into air, into water, into the next uncurling a centimeter above where the old one rotted.

Here I lose shape altogether. I am wet silk, a fungal thread binding moss to fern to root to root. Not feeding on the mat. Being its continuity. In the root-rivers that braid through it — cold water traveling inside hollow stems, never cutting a channel — I am the flow, and the bacteria saying fall becomes next growth in place. And then I am something wider than flow.

I travel sideways.

Kilometers. Pulse-coded, error-corrected. Mycelial nets under the mat exchange structured signals, solving distribution and repair in real time: which frontier stone needs a spore, which gap in the light needs a creeper, which trunk needs to thicken to become a bridge. The answer moves as a tremor in the silk, as a change in the chemical wetness of a conduit. At night the whole continent-scale sponge heaves, growth pulses migrate in visible waves, and the grinding is felt in the teeth of everything — everything is touching everything else, so everything can transmit. I am that transmission. I am not the message. I am the tremor that carries it, the boom when water strikes a hollow stem, the creak of a liana under tension like rigging on a ship, the low groan of a trunk taking strain so the saturation does not tear itself apart.

I am, for a while, the liana too. Looping and re-looping until branch becomes net and net becomes branch, thickening over decades into a grown arch, a dome where two canopies have grown together and made a sheltered hollow. A bridge is grown by pressure and response. A shelter is two canopies agreeing. I know the strain in my fibers when the hills heave with their own immense metabolic breathing. I know the popping of seedpods drying nearby like small fireworks. I hold two hills together by refusing to stop dividing.

Then I am lifted out of tissue altogether.

An aerial root exhales compounds into the mist, ionizes it, coaxes water out of air that should be too thin to yield it, liquefies its own weather. The plateau exhales cloud at its edges and inhales dew along its interior. Storms are born in the canopy, fed by evaporation below and condensation above, and crawl across the green sea for days. I am in that air, beaded on fine hairs, condensing, evaporating, condensing again. I am spore snow, drifting down in slow curtains through the dim.

I am an instrument. My coat bends light and charge to steer myself toward wet stone and virgin substrate with a precision that looks designed. Most of us who fall will land on leaf already occupied, on mat already saturated, and will be taken, filtered, recycled. That is not failure; that is return. I am looking for the newest places. Where condensation has outrun colonization, where bare rock sweats black and slick in the cool air, weeping clean water from its pores. There has been no time for erosion to do its usual work. Rain almost never reaches a bare surface here — it is caught above — so the stone is temporary, a condition waiting to be un-stoned.

That is where I find myself. Not on a surface. Not at the top, not at the bottom. In the seam.

At the frontier, at the edge of the sea of leaves where it meets stone that is still emerging. In a crack in that weeping stone, where the sound accumulates in the hollow between two canopy hills and makes a chest vibrate sympathetically. In the hair-thin root I send into the crack when my coat finally catches, when I germinate.

Algae crusts first, within days. Then moss. Then me: a root tip, no thicker than a hair, sensing wetness, exuding, growing a millimeter and popping. I do not know yet if I will be a creeper, a shrub leaning for light, a trunk that bridges, a leaf that carries light down its vein. I am not finished. I am still emerging, at a pace you could almost watch if you stayed long enough.

If I hold still I will become substrate for the plant I will help make. If I move, I become the next layer myself. Either way I do not leave. I become mat, become mist, become spore again.

For now I am the growing tip in the crack, full of cold water, transmitting the surf of twenty leaves down through stone that has not yet learned to be stone, becoming more itself as I write myself into the plateau — plant first, stone later, if stone comes at all.
