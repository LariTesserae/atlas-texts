At first I tried to stay above the crust.

I hovered in the water that is only slightly darker than the water around it, the way the pale fish hang over a pillar when the current is strong. Fins barely moving. Watching. Above me the dim disc of the surface was a rumor. Below, the towers rose out of the dusk and rounded off and disappeared again, and between them there was pavement, and pavement, and nothing standing on it. I thought I would be one of the things that do not live in the made world, but over it.

The current would not let me keep station.

Every few days it comes as a slow surge, a wave that has traveled down from a storm I will never see, and it drops the temperature a degree and lifts pale dust off the crust and bends every plume the same way at once. It bent me too, sideways, down the upstream face of a low dome where the tubes are densest and newest, where a pale skin is always being laid down over the old. I slipped, and slipped, and then I was not swimming any more. I was face to a mouth no wider than a pinprick in the side of a tube.

I put my soft end there.

That is the whole of my beginning, and it was not a beginning I chose with eyes. I have no eyes worth naming. I unfolded a small fan — cream-colored, translucent, ragged at the edge — out into the water that was bringing me snow. I beat it once, twice. I caught what the current carried. Behind me, in the newest segment of my own body, I began to harden.

I am still, in the only way I can be, the living point at the tip of a tube on the upstream edge of that dome.

It is a small life and a slow one. The water is barely above freezing and cold settles into everything and makes it unhurried. I do not lay a tube and move into it. I live at the lip and make it as I go, secreting, gnawing, re-secreting, year by year fusing my wall to the wall of the neighbor beside me and the wall of the one before me whose plume closed a decade ago. I can feel their seams wearing smooth. I can hear, from somewhere deep in the lattice behind me, a shrimp click at nothing I can see, the sound coming through the water and through the stone-that-is-not-stone into my ribs.

Around me the dome does its work without me. A sponge has settled on the downstream side and grown up over the older plates, a woven thicket whose pores pulse at a rhythm slower than mine. Water drawn in through a thousand small mouths, expelled through a few large ones, stripped of snow and larvae in passing. It does not move. It will live for centuries and close over a wound made by a sea spider in a season. Polyps wave beside me, single mouths on stalks, bleached and translucent because color is a language for light and there is no light to speak in. When startled they flash a faint cold blue, and then go cream again.

I do not know the tower. I know the current on my fan, and the pressure-change as it sighs through the lattice, and whether my newest segment is pale and eager or already darkening behind. The whole plain is oriented that way. Everything that grows tall grows where water moves past, where food arrives. In still pockets nothing rises. I am not a whole architecture. I am an edge of one, growing blindly forward while behind me I become solid.

I did not die all at once.

My fan beat more slowly. One internal wave came and bent it hard east, and when the calm returned it unfolded only halfway. Then it did not unfold again. The amphipods came first — rice-grain lives that do not build, only keep house — and picked their way through my mouth and ate me clean from the inside, leaving the room empty in the exact shape of my body. My soft part went back to snow. My hard part stayed.

That is when I stopped being the thing that waves and became the thing that holds.

From the inside, the world reverses. Water no longer arrives at me. It passes through me, a low sigh felt more in the wall than heard. Marine snow settles and is dug out again. The tube I am now is hollow and layered, like the inside of a shell, like bark. On quiet days I can feel the crust breathe by opening, pinprick by pinprick along its surface, all around me, in a pace so slow you could swear nothing has happened.

First the cleaners had me for a few weeks. Then a juvenile eelpout found my mouth, head-first, and it was exactly the width of its body.

It stayed. It stayed for most of its life, tail undulating just enough to keep water moving past its gills, its soft shape pressing and releasing against my wall with every breath. When I passed too close — when something passed too close — it darted out in a short, expensive lunge, mouth opening around an amphipod, and retreated. The shoals you see scatter like ash above a dome are families like this, filing back into their holes one by one. They are never far from a hole. To be far from a hole is to be prey. It died inside me, curled. I became its food for the next amphipods. A year later another juvenile found the same mouth and took it in turn.

Deeper in the lattice the shrimp kept clicking. A blind crab walked the pavement on long legs, feeling ahead with antennae that have no use for light, found my mouth choked with hardened mucus and spicule, and worked at it patiently, hour after hour, gnawing. It did not know it was a builder, but it widened me. My perfect fit was lost. For a few years I was its apartment, the chamber slightly larger than any single body that had lived in it. When it died, a snail grazed the film from my walls. When the snail died, I was empty and ready again.

That is the pattern I have learned, having outlived the mouth that made me: the animal is brief, the room outlives it.

And the room does not stay a room, either. Nothing here stays crisp. A tower grown heavy with its own mineral weight settles, not with a crash — sound dies quickly in cold water — but with a slow sloughing. One season the side below me sheared off in a silent slide and settled in a cloud of pale dust. A crack ran through my wall. New tubes sprouted on the fresh break within weeks, pale and unprotected, eager. My fragment fell, became rubble, and rubble became foundation. The sponge that had shaded me for a century was half-eaten and closed over itself. The dome I had stood on slumped, in time, toward a low disk you could swim over without knowing it had ever been vertical. Fish that had sheltered in its lee moved into the hollows. The pavement itself, polished by generations, smoothed me further into the plain.

I am now both older and younger than I was. In the deepest crevice between plates the bottom is an older generation of me, flattened. On the upstream skin I am still fresh enough to catch a polyp colonizing my broken lip, its tiny plume unfolding where my own fan once unfolded, beating once, twice, and waiting. I feel it from the inside again, the way I once felt my own current.

From the slope behind the dome, once, a sleeper shark rose slow as a landslide to mouth at the tower above me and leave. I felt only the pressure of it, and then the rain of larvae and snow from far above arriving again, most of it simply eaten and turned into more crust. The builders eat the abandoned and build with the eaten. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to be.

So where am I, if you come down to the summit and stay still until the crust begins to move?

I am not above it, hovering. I am not the tower. I am on the upstream edge of a low dome, in a tube facing the current, hollow, layered, worn. I am the pavement between towers and the tower itself, in the slow turning between them. My mouths open pinprick by pinprick and my plumes beat and wait. My tenants die in me and are cleaned out of me and another tenant finds me exactly wide enough — or a crab makes me wider. My walls are bored and eaten and fused and bored again.

Nothing here is natural, and nothing here is artificial. I am what could have been rock, secreted into a room, and what was a room, worn smooth into ground, and what is ground today, waiting in the current to be tower again.

Look ahead and another dome rises out of the dusk, rounds off, disappears. I will not go to it. That is the only way anything here can be still: to be the place that stays, wide enough for the next mouth, open to whatever the cold water brings down out of the dark.
