I live where the heat stops.

An inch down, where the top of the thatch would burn through a sandal and sweat dries before it can run, it is cool and damp to the touch. That thin coolness is my country. I do not stand upon the mat. Standing would be on it, and nothing here, in the end, is on it. I am in it.

I am soft, because hard things tear. My joints give. My legs are not for strides so much as hooks, root-hooks that curl and grip the runners from underneath, tasting them. My eyes are hooded against a light I rarely need. For most of my life the light is only a rumor, a pressure coming down through fiber. For a little of it, at dawn and dusk, I surface to lick.

What I do, mostly, is keep the tunnels.

They are not dug. They are pressed and re-pressed, generation after generation of bellies and hooks moving through the living floor until the mat has learned to keep them open, like the passages in a sponge. I am one of thousands to a stride, moving in the dark fiber traffic while the surface above seems to have died of heat. We feed on rot, on the shed skins of the mat itself. We turn dead fiber into soil slowly, inside the living floor. We keep the tunnels damp by our own presence, a shared breath in the cool inch. And when a foot steps above and tears a runner, I come and knit it.

That is why it heals behind you.

At dawn the whole thatch exhales. The mist rises ankle-high and tears and vanishes by mid-morning, leaving the leaves beaded. That is when I come up. I do not see far. The world is red-green runners, waxy and springy, and under them the pale undersides where I hang. I lick the beads. I taste pollen and spores in the permanent yellow haze. I taste night. The giants are still open then, their door-sized leaves unfolded, and underneath them the thin air has condensed all night and drips in a slow tick-tock long after the sun is ordinary white. I time myself by that drip. The frogs answer it from the stone pockets at the foot of the escarpments — no bigger than thumbs, pale-throated, bright-eyed in black hollows a hand across, singing a thin needling chorus in the rainless hours. I never see them. They never leave the stone. They are a memory of water made persistent, and no one drinks from them, not even when thirsty, because where the frogs are the water will last.

By ten the heat sits down like a hand. The surface becomes too hot to sit upon without moving. The runners curl, the giant leaves fold along their midrib with a slap and boom of wet sail, the animals seek shade or go underground. I go down an inch into my chill. That is the hour we are the true population.

Above me the world continues as weather. A sheet of insects comes over a swell and darkens the sun for twenty minutes, settling in a rustling carpet. I feel it as a vibration in the roots, a crackle. Then something big moves underneath them and they lift all at once, like a sail hauled. I feel the grazers more than I see them, which is to say I almost never see them. They are slow houses, six-legged, plowing a swath a valley wide through the mat with their heads down. When one herd passes, the whole floor bends and sighs. Their dung lands steaming above and the thatch takes it up within hours, visibly greening. Their backs are meadows in themselves — moss thick enough to trample and crop, with their own tiny grazers browsing it and hunters upon those hunters, whole lives timed to the slow stride of the host. At night when the herd beds, the sighs come down through fiber to me like a distant avalanche of exhalation. Somewhere in that moss a bird sometimes sleeps and turns in its sleep as the ground rises and falls.

I know the walkers by their tasting.

They are lanky and thorned and green-skinned, hooded eyes set in ridged sockets, photosynthetic and patient, neither quite animal nor quite plant. They do not chase. They wait mid-step among a grazing front, one root-leg lifted for an hour, their flanks turned to the sun. One will stand over my tunnel for a long time, rootlets curling and uncurling in the thatch just above my hooks, tasting. I hold very still. They are a tax upon carelessness, taking the odd distracted young grazer that strays too close to their shadow, catching insects on their thorns. The herds give them a wide berth and never break stride. I give them a wider one. When night falls a walker settles, its rootlets spreading, greening darker in the dew. At dawn it rises again.

The giants I know only in part. Half a mile apart, sometimes more, a single trunk as thick as a hut rises, ribbed and succulent, or a hollow stalk big enough to walk inside. Birds nest in the forks by the hundreds and launch at dawn in a clatter that carries across a valley — I hear that clatter as a shiver in the air. Inside the hollow the walls sweat condensation in the dark and smell of sap. At noon a predator will sleep in the shadow of one leaf, because that shadow is a room, cool and still, while outside is humming heat. I know that cool as a pause in the drip above me. Water lives inside them, stored and sweet, and under their vast undersides it becomes tick-tock. They do not move. They do not need to. The world comes to them, and the tunnels under them carry their news far.

People are sparser than the giants and stranger to me, though they are the ones who press hardest on my roof. A family of ten or twenty follows the herds rather than leading them. Their hut is a lean-to of giant leaf-stalks lashed with fiber, low because the wind will take anything high and the plants will take anything patient. Inside, a small fire struggles in the thin air and smokes blue. I hear that fire more than I feel it — a small, anxious crackle far off, not the choir. Their days are governed by water as behavior, not climate. At dawn they come out with cupped hands, woven scoops, hollow stems pressed to a seep, tongues out to lick. Children learn which stone pockets are for drinking and which are for frogs, and that you do not drink from a frog-stone even when you are thirsty. They clip the mat for greens and fiber. Their terraces in the shallow channels are already half-plant. Their trails wear the mat pale between the runners, the only places the thatch is actually bare. When they walk that pale line I feel the pressing of a sole through fiber, and afterwards I go and knit, slowly, so the line will close again if they stop tending it.

They know the hour when the insect choir will cover their talk, and they sit patient together until it passes. So do I.

At dusk the heat breaks. The giant leaves open with a sigh you can hear if you are close enough. A single thread of insect sound rises from somewhere in the thatch, then a hundred, then the whole plateau at once until footsteps vanish under it and speech is a rumor. The pods popping in the cooling mat beat underneath like hand-drums. The frogs answer. The grazers settle with a long exhalation. Everything pauses to listen, because to listen is how you know where the water-band is moving and where the herds will go. I pause my knitting then too. My thousands of brothers pause. For a while there is nothing to do but be in the cool inch and hear the ordered deafening, and understand again that sound is not decoration here; it is information, it is weather, it is a clock that everyone obeys without being told.

At night the mat opens and breathes out its stored moisture. The ticking of dripping leaves is the only regular thing. The frogs answer it. From far off come the long trumpeting calls of the back-moss herds, carrying for miles because there is nothing here to stop sound and nothing to absorb it but more living fiber. I surface once more, briefly, to the beading undersides. The mat, which all day seemed sun-cured and brittle, steams faintly where the day's heat leaves it. Everything that can hold moisture does. I hold mine in my soft skin against the thin air. I go back down and finish the tear from the afternoon's step, pulling the frayed runner ends together with my hooks until the fibers take.

And then I wait for rain, which is not a feature here but a habit, an intermittent one.

Storms are born over the plateau's own center and die over it. You can see them coming from twenty miles away, turning in slow circles over the green, never sure if they will ever reach you. Most of it never lands as rain. It falls as virga, as mist, as steam off the hot thatch. What lands drops in ten furious minutes at midday and is drunk in an hour — the low channels darken, the runners lift, pale shoots push up overnight and are cropped or hardened by morning. When it comes, I know it first as a change in pressure in the tunnels. The darkened swales drip down into us. The rot we feed on washes fresh. I surface to feed in the surge, thousands of us moving at once in the fiber traffic, and the frogs sing louder, and the insects rise, and the grazers turn their broad fronts toward the darkened ground, and the walkers lift their root-legs and take one patient step, and the people come out with their scoops. For a day the plateau is green and frantic and deafening. Then the heat sits down again, the leaves fold, the runners curl, and we all go back down into shade, into fiber, into the cool dark, to wait.

It goes on at the pace of something old. Rot and regrowth. Migration and hunger. A vast living floor feeding everything that walks or flies or crawls upon it, holding them all between its fibers until the next brief fall of water turns the whole green, audibly, all at once.

If you walk here and feel the ground spring under you and heal behind you, if you step and find the runners already knitting where your foot has passed — that was not the mat remembering to be whole.

That was me, letting go slowly so it could close, one inch down where the heat stops, keeping the damp, keeping the dark, keeping the passage open for the next foot, the next herd, the next brief, furious rain.
