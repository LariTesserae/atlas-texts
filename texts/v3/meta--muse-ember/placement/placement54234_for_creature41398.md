At first, I would not find myself anywhere.

I walk in — because there is no other way to arrive, no path, only the place itself continuing — and the cold comes up through my boots and stays there. My breath does not fog. I keep expecting it to, and keep being caught off guard that it doesn't. I am carrying water inside me, loudly, blood louder than the Shelf, and the Shelf has none to offer back. No spring, no rain, no damp in the hollows. The white basins that look like pools crack underfoot dry as salt. The grooves that look like riverbeds are smooth and empty. I am wet in a dry world, and that makes me conspicuous in a way I hadn't expected — not seen, exactly, but unmade for here.

I try to be the visitor. The one who left the marks.

There are cairns of three flat stones on one rise, and near-identical cairns on the next rise, and the next. There are obsidian tools, edges still sharp, half-buried in dust. There is the low circular shelter, the awl and the mineral fibre still on the stone table. Whoever made those things understood quickly. One could leave a mark. One could not stay. If I am that person, I am already gone — only the awl remains, and the beasts have moved in through the doorway and arranged themselves inside in their quiet postures. That feels true for a while. A human does not belong long enough to be repeated.

But my footprints do not fade. That worries me more than the cold. I walk, certain, in a straight line across an open field that has no features to bend me, and after an hour I find myself following my own prints back to where I started. The sun has not moved. My shadow points the same way it pointed when I arrived. There is no weather to take the print away, no wind to rush past my ears and blur it. A visitor is supposed to pass through and be erased. Here, passing through is an illusion. The print stays clean and sharp, and it loops.

So I stop trying to walk straight.

I look down. You have to, here. The floor rises and falls along a seam in one of the slabs, a fraction at a time. It is breathing through plates of mineral. I had been stepping over backs.

That is the second place I look for myself: low, domed, armoured, no eyes I can find, no mouth I would recognize, only a fringe of short jointed limbs moving with a slowness that makes my own impatience feel obscene. They eat dust. They press their blunt fronts into the pale powder and dissolve the silicates with a dry chemistry, and behind them excrete clean pebbles in a perfect, regular trail. They take light through the translucent rims of their shells. Inside they are not wet at all — fatty, salty, crystalline, built to persist rather than to grow.

If I stay long enough, I begin to understand that as a description of a body I could inhabit without having to see. No eyes, but light coming in sideways through the rim, true-coloured, ordinary. No need to drink. No sweat. No rot, because there is nothing wet to rot. Just dust in, pebbles out, light in, and the slow rise and fall along the seam. I shuffle forward a fraction of an inch. I forget, or seem to forget, that I have moved at all.

But I am not one of them. Not yet. I keep lifting my head, looking for something new, and the Shelf punishes that reflex by showing me how rarely new things are permitted here.

A needle-grass, one to a hundred paces, in a crack: pale, wiry, stiff as wire, dull straw. A stone-flower that opened some immense time ago and has never closed, never seeded, never wilted, adding a millimetre of dry growth in a century. I could be that — ignored by the huskbacks, rooting in dust, drinking nothing, surviving by being beneath notice. It is a different kind of slowness than theirs, and for a time it appeals to me because it does not require me to be part of the pavement. It requires only that I persist.

Above us, or apparently above us, a haze of mote-drifters hangs motionless in the lee of a rise. Translucent bladders full of dry spores, suspended where they hatched, casting no shadow, drifting neither up nor down. And higher, the hangers, flat shapes that do not beat their wings but are simply in the sky the way the huskbacks are in the stone. I could be a held breath there — a spore never released, a thought never quite landed. It, too, appeals, because it asks nothing of me but to remain at the height I was born.

Each of those tries fails in a different way. The grass is too solitary; I keep wanting a witness. The mote is too suspended; I keep wanting contact. The hanger is too far from the cold that comes up through the feet. And the human visitor has already been proved temporary. The pavement is the only thing here that is overwhelming, and overwhelming things have a way of recruiting you.

I step over a back, and over another, and another, negotiating, deciding whose shell will bear my weight, and somewhere around the hundredth decision I notice something I should have noticed earlier: I have stepped over the same back before.

A group of seven beasts arranged around a pale salt patch. The largest with a cracked plate on its left side. I see it again a hundred metres on. Again on the far rise. Again far off, but now as a pattern within a much larger arrangement of the same seven. The slabs repeat their rounded forms. The basins repeat their shallow arcs. The cairns repeated. The tracks are worn into the stone in the same places because there is nowhere else to walk.

It is not that there are many individuals. It is that the world, having been worn down for so long, has run out of new shapes and has begun to copy the old ones, endlessly, at every scale. And I, trying to find myself, keep finding the copy instead of the original — which is the same as finding myself, because there is no original left to distinguish.

That is when it starts to become more itself.

I am not the herd. I am not the whole pavement stretching to the horizon and re-forming on the other side of it. I am one of the seven, and I am also the smaller cousins clustered on the larger slabs like barnacles, crustlings no bigger than coins in layered fans of dozens, their plates overlapping the parent's plates until you cannot tell where one animal ends. I am both the cracked back and the chip that lives on the crack. I bud a copy of myself in exactly the same posture, in exactly the same place, with exactly the same chips and scars, because that is the only reproduction this equilibrium allows. Births matched by hardenings. Eating matched by excreting.

Death, if it comes, will not be an event. It will be a slow hardening while still occupied, my shell gradually becoming stone, my plates joining the pavement, my children settling around me in the same quiet postures. A sleeping beast and a boulder and a beast that has finished are the same thing to any eye that tries. Perhaps, blind as I am, the distinction has stopped mattering to me too.

Where would you find me, then? Everywhere the pattern repeats. Near-touching, belly to shell, head to tail, under the slabs and on the slabs and between the slabs, in the dust itself. In the lee, perhaps, a mote-drifter at the height I hatched — but no, even that would be a copy at the same height. I think I am on the pavement. I think I have always been the pavement, only recently learning to breathe through the seam.

The sun is still in the same place. My shadow still points the same way. The footprints I made when I thought I was a visitor are still there, clean and sharp, and I have stopped trying to follow them back. I rise a fraction at a time along my back, and fall, and take light through the rim, and eat dust, and leave pebbles behind me in a perfect trail, and when the sky turns that deep, clean blue that here passes for night, I am motionless and layered and overwhelming with the rest of the floor — a living floor for a world that has no water, almost no green, and nothing left to become.
