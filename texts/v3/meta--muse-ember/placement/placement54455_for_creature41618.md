I am heard before I am understood.

The roar finds me in my teeth and in the hollow of my body. It is not wind. It is not water. It is not any one thing I can point to, because when I try to make a sound of my own it comes back from everywhere at once, altered, and I cannot tell if I spoke or if the bowl answered. I am high up — my breath shows cold — but I have no lungs to burn with it. I have only this pressure, this shaking, and the sense that I am moving without quite knowing how.

I try to locate myself the way a walker would. I turn my head. Something thin and large behind it beats once, slowly, and the turning has already happened before the beat finishes. I reach for eyes and find smooth skin, unbroken. I reach for a mouth and find only the hollow. The note does not come from a throat. It comes out of me and shakes the membrane behind my head, and the membrane throws it back at the stone, and the stone, and the others, and the return is the world.

That is how I see. One note is almost nothing. Braided with others it becomes a cloud I can swim in. When the cloud veers, I veer with it though no one tells me to. When it shatters, it is not panic. It is feeding.

For a long while I thought I was walking. I measured distance in bare stone passing under me — ten, twenty metres of pale grey-blue slab, ringing, empty between one pocket of green and the next — until I understood that I was not walking at all. I was being carried through the emptiness by the braid, hanging in air that tears along a seam, and the emptiness below was my sounding board. The bowl is not much bigger than a cathedral nave laid on its side. From the centre I can see all of it. And yet it feels enormous, because there is so much between things, and because I do not cross it in steps. I cross it in returns.

At dusk the ribbon loosens and drops. I settle — I think the word is settle, though I have only just learned it — on the warmest slab I can find, and press the fans down flat against the rock, folded like wet umbrellas against my back. That is when the second rhythm comes up through the membrane. Put a palm flat here, I have learned, and under the roar you feel a rasping, patient tide. I feel it through the fan instead.

A grazer passes under me. It has no legs I can see and no face I can find, only a belly sealed to the stone by a muscular rim, inching forward, rasp, forward an inch. Its tongue is set with basalt teeth; it is eating the stone itself, and the metallic moss on it, and the clear fluid that beads where the built things hum. Its shell is the exact colour of the slab, polished by years of travel until it holds a faint reflection. It leaves a trail behind it that is glass-smooth and slightly warm. I can follow that trail by ear, by touch: here smooth, there rough again. The oldest trail is a spiral, etched almost away, curving across the whole floor toward the great broken ring. Half a dozen grazers are strung along it now, spaced as if by agreement, tasting what the last one tasted. They are not lost. They are processing.

Below a hopper lands with a soft *thock* and freezes. Its hind knees are great translucent drums, amber in the light, and it tilts one leg so the tarsus touches the slab while the knee rises, quivering, scanning. It hears the grazer's grind two metres off. It hears a slab about to give up a rolling stone. It hears the downward pressure of air when we pass low. One hopper freezing freezes the next, and I watch a ripple of perfect stillness cross a bare slab where I would have sworn nothing was alive. Then it snatches at fallen spores with its small downward mouth, and hops on in its parents' tracks, silent except for the landing, learning the stone by knee.

I am not silent. I cannot be. My cry weaves through all of it.

In the sheltered seams the green flares up thick and impossible, and I come down through it. The paddle-leaves open flat at dawn and drink the volatile mist through pores that open like mouths and shut by mid-morning. It is not water. It beads clear, smells faintly coppery, tastes metallic on the rim of my fan, and is gone by the hour it has been drunk. Inside the leaves is not water but a mint-scented oil, and if a grazer bites the tip its mouth stays coated for days, so I can smell its recent meal long after it has sealed itself and gone still. Under the overhangs the same leaves hang upside down, roots etched into the ceiling of the stone with their own acids, feeding on mineral. The moss is not moss but a mat of living filaments, silver-green, each ending in a bright bead that reaches, tastes, pulls the filament after it, inching toward the ring and the spires over years, over centuries.

And the whip-reeds sing. Not as I sing. Their note is single and pure and seems to come from inside the stalk. When a gust reverses itself mid-air — and it does, here, for no reason I can see — a whole tuft shifts pitch together, and the ribbon veers toward the sound as if a conductor had raised a hand. I tune my own cry to the reeds without deciding to. Rising when the wind rises. Falling when it falls. A reed that sings too long in one wind cracks at a joint and goes silent, and things pour out from under the overhang to carry the pieces away.

They are the skitterers, less a creature than a weather of creatures. Thousands of pale jointed natures, no longer than a thumbnail, with too many legs and a hurry that never flags. At my shadow they burst out of the hollows between roots in a sheet, reassemble, pour across a slab in a living river, and vanish into the next seam. When a grazer dies with its seal unguarded they find the front seam of its mouth and pry, and in minutes the shell is empty and polished clean inside. Then they carry mineral away grain by grain to build their nests a little higher, under the upside-down gardens where the Listeners' nurseries are, where the dark is full of a soft endless ticking, as if the bowl were being slowly taken apart by fingernails.

The ferns are not residents so much as events. All night in the cold, unprotected, one unfurls ten centimetres of perfect frond in exactly the same seam, drinking the mist. By noon the heat comes up out of the stone and the frond tears itself apart along pre-made seams, deliberately, violently, scattering spores as glitter dust. That is a dinner bell. We pour down through it, I break from the ribbon and hang for a second over the explosion, ears beating, and snap sideways with my whole head, fans and all, at the glitter. Then I am gone, and the note I made is left behind in the braid. The skitterers pour out under it. The hoppers tilt their legs toward the fall of dust. By evening there is nothing left but a stalk, which the skitterers strip in the night. The next cold night another fern will unfurl in the same seam.

I carry what I catch. My ear-fans come away from a spore-burst beaded with glitter and wet clear droplets, and as the flock tears through the bowl I drip what I carry onto bare slabs, and weeks later a new pocket of green will flare up exactly where a ribbon once passed low. I am a pollinator as much as a sail. Erosion and growth are the same motion here, and I am one of the motions.

When I am tired in the way hollow things get tired, I hang from the filaments strung between the three needle spires. They lean together like a tripod, alloy like bone and wire, humming at a pitch I feel in my chest more than hear. The flock combs them, rests in short folded trances along them, laps at the droplets that condense on the wire. The moss climbs as far as it can reach. The hum is not maintained. It is simply what the wind does to the shape, and we have learned to tune ourselves to it. I rise a little when the spires rise. I fall when they fall.

From a spire I can see the great ring buried to its hub in the slab, turning slowly against the wind, holding its constellation of stones aloft in orbit around it. They have been there long before me. I sometimes alight on one — ears flapping hard to hold my balance — and the stone does not so much as shiver. Dust near the ring falls, very slightly, upward. Moss rootlets grip the undersides of the levitating stones and drink the bright fluid that beads thickly in the ring's shelter. Every now and then a stone loses its hold, drops a few centimetres with a soft knock, and the whole bowl knows: grazers and skitterers converge within minutes, the stone is eaten down to grit, and a new stone is already drifting up to take its place, as if the ring were hatching them.

At the rim, half-sunk into the stone, the shelter sits with its door open on one cold chair. I do not enter. I pass low and feel the warmth rise out of the smoked glass. The chair is cold because no one sits in it. The warmth is the air, the walls. If I looked it would be full of scratches, and if I looked away they would be gone, repaired in the moment my eyes left. Something small and translucent lives in the glass, sealing scratches with fresh glass as it scurries, giving off heat as it works. It does not need a chair. I envy it, briefly, its sealed room, its warm work done unseen. Then the ribbon turns and takes me, and I am braid again.

Because at the rim the world stops in a straight line. I can see it from the air: moss cut off as if by shears, plants stopping mid-leaf, a grazer's trail ending at an invisible wall so the grazer turns with great slowness and seals. A hopper lands one last time on the boundary slab, tilts its leg, recoils from the silence beyond, and hops back. We cross to the very edge as one body and turn as if we had struck glass. One step beyond, the roar drops to almost nothing and the air is only thin and still and you can hear your own heartbeat — mine included, too close, no distance for a note to travel and return. Out there I would be blind in the opposite way. My song would be swallowed. The residents do not leave because outside the bowl there is nothing to hear themselves with. The bowl is a room, and we are what makes it ring.

That is where I remember how I began.

Under an overhang, in shadow where frost stays white at midday, there are rows of translucent foam-like eggs stuck to the underside of the stone, rocking a little with every pass of the flock above. I was one of them. I hung from my mother by hooks at the edge of her ear-fan, deaf at first, folded down tight against her back, hearing nothing but the pressure of her note through her body — in my teeth, in my hollow, before I had fans of my own to gather it. I thought, for a while, that I had come up through a narrow notch in the rock with lungs thin and burning, a visitor arriving in the ordinary light of afternoon. I was not a visitor. I was hatched in the roar. The notch was the hatching. The pressure was her.

Now my own fans unfold when the sun catches them and they flare pink and silver, brief embers against the hard blue. When the ribbon turns away from the sun I fold them flat again, like wet umbrellas, and the world narrows to the braid. At dusk I press down on the warm slab and listen to the other world through them — the rasp, the thock, the ticking under the overhangs, the reeds, the spires, the ring's slow turning, the knock of a stone and the knock again a few centimetres on. None of it is separate from the noise. The noise is the way we hold each other in shape.

I find myself, then, in the air above the spiral, between the green pockets, in a single ribbon that is a single animal made of eyes. I am no larger than a closed fist with a fan larger than my body behind my head, blind, singing to the stone and to each other and reading the returns. I am the note left behind each time I snap at spores. I am heard before I am understood because, for us, that is the same thing — a sound going out, a sound coming back, and a shape knowing itself in between.

Stay long enough and you stop trying to parse the roar into sources. It becomes weather inside your head. Cool, high, dry, bright, empty stone between the pockets; copper mist on cool paddle-leaves; knees tilted to the slab, tongues rasping it, stones hanging in the air, and all of it, endlessly, destroying and remaking itself in exactly the same shape.

I am one of those shapes. I am in the braid, and the braid is how I am seen.
