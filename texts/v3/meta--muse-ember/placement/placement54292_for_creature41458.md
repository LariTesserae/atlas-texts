At first I put myself above it.

I tried to be the pillar. Sixty metres of hollow column, wider than a house at the base, standing blind in the slate air for decades, growing only in pulses, collecting fog in its dark pockets and letting it down inside in a secret thread. It was tempting — to be tall, to be still, to wait. But a pillar does not chew. It does not make anything new out of the old. It preserves, and then, rarely, it splits with a crack that echoes for miles and lies where it fell, shape intact, while the mat eats it foot by foot. That is a kind of life, but it is not a life that moves through the world. I kept sliding off it.

I tried the grazer. Low, shaggy, heavy-shouldered, following the fog-paths from beyond the edge, horns raking forward through ankle-high shoots, turning the mat over with its head. I tried the runner behind it, quicker, lighter, living on what the horns expose. I tried the predator that follows singly, never running, only a ripple bending the shoots in sequence. I tried the bird.

All of them had the same problem. They were above the mat, and above the mat, movement is expensive because heat is expensive. They could not afford to be continuous. They came in waves, in pulses, in brief weather. A flock of ten thousand for a minute, then gone, leaving the emptiness louder. A herd you hear through the plant ground long before you see it. A man with a stone pick, a single hearth pressed against a pillar base, no fire that lasts. I did not want to be brief. I did not want to spend myself crossing empty air that gives nothing back.

So I went down, because the place itself kept telling me to.

Peel back a square of mat — it resists like hide — and the light is lost at once. That is where I am.

I am one of the blind beetles under the felt. Not a grub — too soft, too mindless for what I have to do. Not a worm — I do not only pulse and burrow and seal the tunnel behind me. I am beetle-shaped in the way the description leaves open: shell the colour of everything that has learned restraint here, off-white and soot-grey, no eyes at all, antennae longer than my body sweeping the dark ahead of me, clicking, rasping, tasting.

Where, exactly: a thumb's length below the sword-leaves, a hand's breadth above the black fresh stone, in the warm-damp crawl that the whole continent of root breathes into being. It is only a few degrees above the air, but here a few degrees is the difference between frozen and working. The mat is my roof. It is also my sky. Above it, mounded and interwoven, roots and rhizomes and compressed leaf-bases creep even in the cold, beaded always with condensation, holding each drop until the drop is heavy enough to fall. Below me is stone that is still splitting at night with a sound like a rifle-shot far away.

I live in the seam between them.

That is my world in its entirety, and it is larger than it sounds. The seam runs without break to the edge of sight and beyond. I will never walk it. I will walk a few inches of it in a life, and my children will walk a few inches more, and the seam will still be there, chewed and re-chewed. My gut is a small ember against the cold stone — the only warmth I can afford, and I keep it by work.

The work is the point. In this cold nothing rots quickly. A dead pillar lies for decades preserving its shape. A stone ring and a fiber skeleton remain long after their maker is gone. The past is not buried here, it is woven in, unless someone chews it. So I chew. Old mat, half-dead stems, leaf-base that the surface plants have already given up. I rasp it with my mouthparts, pass it through, and the droppings become tunnel wall, become new felt. The beetle's rasping opens the mat to water. The grub's chewing — my neighbours, my chorus in the dark — makes the old plant mass new again. Everything above me depends on us in the end, though they never see us. They follow fog and herd-paths. We follow moisture.

Moisture is my compass. The fog rolls in from beyond the cliff and stops on things. It condenses on every waxy leaf above me and drips, a tiny intermittent rain that is not rain. It seeps down through the felt and I feel the seam soften, feel it tighten again with frost-heave. Somewhere not far — in my scale, a long way — a pillar is collecting that same fog inside its hollow trunk, and occasionally the thread overflows and trickles down the inner bark. If you put your ear to the bark you can hear it. I hear it in the stone. Where that thread leaks out at the base and spreads sideways into the rhizomes, the mat is wettest, sweetest, most easily torn. I have set my territory there, in the damp crawl at the foot of a pillar, where a human cairn no higher than a knee has been built by someone who needed to mark a condensation pocket that only drips in certain weather. They marked my water. They do not know I am there.

I do not see the pillar. I have never seen the grey smear of sun, or the crown of stiff fronds waiting at the top, or the flock crossing unseen. I know noon only because the mat above me, for a moment, is lit pale green from below, translucent, and the seam warms by a fraction. I know dusk because the tremor changes — sap and trapped air moving like a tide in a shallow sea, travelling through the rhizomes under my feet. I know the herds come long before any eye could sort them, because the drumming is not in the air for me. It is in the felt itself, a low thunder transmitted up through root and down through stone, shaking the tunnels. When a horn tears a square of mat open and light falls in — white, flat, wrong — I do what the things with too many legs do. I fold myself. Legs tucked tight, antennae drawn in, shell pretending to be a piece of dead leaf-base. I wait, patient, in the cold that floods in, until the runner behind the herd has snatched what it came for and the surface seals itself again over the dark.

Then I unfold. I sweep my antennae. I click, and somewhere in a tunnel I cannot see, another clicks back — not conversation as you would know it, but the pattern of touches that keeps the city from being lonely. I resume chewing.

Above me, a weather cell may form over the plateau itself — the place is large enough to make its own sleet for an hour, needling the fronds, vanishing on tissue just below freezing. I feel that as a brief patter on the roof, then nothing. A crack may open a fraction wider in fresh black stone, and I feel it as a rifle-shot through my legs. Something may die above and fall, and lie preserving for decades, and I may spend my whole life inching toward it, incorporating it foot by foot, never seeing the shape. That is enough. I do not need a view. I need a seam, a drip, a mouthful of old plant made new.

If you kneel on the plateau — and you will, because there is nowhere else to kneel — and you tear at the mat with stone and antler and hands, and it does not reveal dirt but more plant, paler, tighter, white tips pushing, then under that you will find me, folded or working, blind, damp, warm by the small heat of my own gut. I will not look up. I have nothing to look with. I will only test the dark with my antennae, click once, and go on making the floor of the world out of the floor of the world.

That is where you would find me. Not on the herd-path, not at the hearth, not in the crown. Underneath, rarely and briefly above, mostly in the dark warm layer between living surface and young stone, listening for water and chewing the past into the present, very slowly, while the whole vast plant ocean breathes under your feet and does not stop for you.
