I am not standing on the plateau.

I am in it. Low. In a smooth hollow where the mist lies longest, where my north side stays damp long after the other stones have dried, and where the lichen has had time to make me theirs.

At first I am only that dampness, and a breathing so slow it would be easy to miss. Once every few minutes my flank rises. Perhaps a millimeter. Then rests. I do not count this the way you would count. I count in drips. Condensation forms on the north side of me at night, gathers, and in the morning lets go — one drop, a long pause, another drop. Between the drops there is a season.

Over me the lichen is continuous. Black-green, ochre, pale sage. It does not distinguish between stone and me, and I no longer do either. Under its skin, in the spongiest parts, the small translucent grazers move through the wet mat, scraping and cleaning. They are on me as much as on the rock. I am terrain for them. My plates are their plateau.

I have no sound to make this known. I have never had one. Pressure is enough, and the slow charge along the long strip on my flank when it is awake. Right now it is dark. I am conserving.

Then the ground changes.

It does not arrive as noise. Noise is eaten here, by the wet air and the distance and the fat in living things, long before it could reach me. It arrives as pressure, piston-like, transmitted through permafrost and damp stone. It builds and builds in the soles of nothing I can call feet, in the half-buried rock I am resting against, until the whole hollow is keeping time.

1-2-3. 1-2-3.

A herd is crossing the swale on the ancient line. It will take a season to cross. They fill the dip plate by plate, rust-colored backs like boiler plate, overlapping, bolted by tissue that can be regrown. I feel them before I could know them, and as they pass, something in the stone beside me — in me — wakes in sequence.

Nearest to farthest, like a signal on a wire. A faint electric tick travels through muscle and fat along my flank. The parasites in my joints, who have been dim and rasping at necrotic tissue with tiny, patient mouths, skim a little of the discharge and brighten. The life under the ice lens at the bottom of the hollow answers, faintly, a tick in reply. I am not seeing this. I am knowing it by the way the charge moves through the body I have not used in a long time.

And in that movement I remember being in the line.

I remember it not as a story but as a rhythm in the legs. Six piston-legs in their housings of fat and tissue, ending in broad pads that find the damp lichen and lift clean. 1-2-3 across dozens of bodies, perfect synchrony, at a walk that is barely a walk. I did not learn it. I was born into it. A swelling along my mother's flank that split, segment by segment, into a new modular body, already plated, already blind, the eyes only pits, the jaw made for scraping. I touched her leg with my own for a day and learned by pressure through the ground. I learned the pulse by the flash that ran through tissue. Then I fell in behind her. The herd closed around a birth like a wall, not for protection, but because the line must not break.

We were all the same. That is not a sadness here. There is no other way to be built would be a strange thing to wish for. The front animals scraped the lichen crust from stone, jaw opening and closing in strict rhythm, peeling it with a soft shush. The ones behind ground what was scraped, in a warm secondary chamber. Further back, fermenting sacs made heat that rose off the flank in the cold air. Further back still, the electric organ ran. At the rear, thin pressed pads of slurry, breaking down into the lichen to feed it. We made no artifacts. We made heat and electricity and body mass and food for the crust behind us. Downstream, another herd would process what we left.

Maintenance was social because it had to be. If a plate cracked, one of us would come alongside, butt it gently with a blunt head, and lick the crack closed with a thick tongue until the tissue knitted. If a piston leg stiffened, two others leaned their weight in rhythm until it freed. There was no factory because the herd was the factory, and it walked.

I do not know when I left it. Leaving is too sudden a word. A ferment sac cooled a fraction. A piston did not quite keep the cycle. Others leaned against me, tongue out, pressure steady, and for a while that was enough to carry the flaw in the line. Then, in a hollow where the mist lay longest, I lowered myself. Not in the groove. A few meters off it. The herd moved on. The line closed without me.

Death here is processed, not mourned, and I was not ready to be processed. The sorters came. Low, worm-like, ridged bellies flickering faint blue as they tested by weight and density, heavy shovel jaws lifting bone and broken plate and stone and sorting it into neat piles that look from a distance like spoil heaps. They crawled through me, too — through the cracks between my plates, through the lichen over my back — testing. They took a little heat and charge from each piece they moved, and left me cleaner and more legible. They decided, in whatever way such things are decided, that I was not yet a pile. They left me in the hollow.

Gliders passed overhead for hours without a wingbeat. I felt their shadow, perhaps. They dropped once, with a dull clink on a keel, took a sorted piece, and carried it a hundred meters to another hollow where it would feed something else. I did not watch them go. I breathed. A millimeter. Drip. Pause. Drip.

That is where I am now. A former grazer gone torpid in place, and so no longer entirely a grazer. A sessile thing. At night, when the mist lifts an inch higher, I extend a fringe of filaments into it, faint with blue, and filter what the drifters — translucent, thumb-sized, carried on air too thin to fly in — have missed. Spores. Iron dust. Breath from the lichen below. In the day I am stone again, covered in the same crustose plates as the stones around me, warm with a cold that has settled into me for longer than I know how to count.

Stillness here is not death, and stone is not always stone. That is not a paradox. It is just the rule of the hollow.

When the herd comes again — and they will, on the same polished groove worn deeper into lichen and stone over millennia — the pulse will reach me by pressure through the ground and through the rock I am half in. I will wake in sequence with the others who sit in the bowls, nearest first. I may wake enough to move a few meters, as the old ones do, and go still again in a new place where the mist lies. Or I may stay, and let the blue travel down the line without me, animal to animal, like a signal on a wire that has lost one connection and decided, patiently, to keep conducting around it.

Either way will be slow. Either way will be standard. I am not an individual here. I am an instance of a way of being built: grown, worn, processed, recycled. Lichen breaks stone. Small grazers clean lichen. I scraped and ground and fermented and discharged, and now I am cleaned, and sorted, and left to hold the damp for the next ones.

For now, I breathe once every few minutes. The sun stays low and diffused through wet glass. The sky stays slate. The ground under me is always slightly wet to the touch, even when the dust on top seems dry. My flank rises a millimeter, and rests, and waits for the next pressure to tell me I am still part of the system, even from the hollow, even as stone.
