I do not arrive here.

I am already wet. That is the first I know of it — not warmth from a sun, but a warmth that never dries on my skin, that arrives from above in single drips, rests, gathers, and runs a patient route down toward me. I know time by the interval between drips. I know up by the side that is dampest. I know nothing else at first, because nothing else is required of me yet.

Light reaches me borrowed. It has passed from leaf to leaf until it is greenish and evening-like, and it tells me only that one side of my body is less dark than the other. There is no horizon to turn toward. If I wait, the place will turn itself around me, imperceptibly — a fern patch migrating foot by foot across a far slope, a moss film turning brown where something has scraped it and green again where it has healed. I do not watch this. I taste it.

I move by tasting. A narrow tongue rasps at the film on the surface I lie on, algae and fungus and young moss, and leaves behind it a clean line. I advance perhaps a body’s length in an hour, then stop. The stop is not rest in any way you would recognize; it is simply the pace at which my whole self can be persuaded forward. My underside is flat and unending. I have no legs to hurry with, no edge sharp enough to be a weapon or a tool. I am soft all the way around, the colour of wet bark, and the world is soft in the same way, so we suit one another.

Ahead of me, always, is a trail that is not mine. I follow it because it tastes familiar — older moss, my mother’s going, a mineral green that my tongue knows before any thought could. She is somewhere on this same branch, moving as I move, perhaps a season’s crawl ahead of me. I will follow her mucus for years. My young, if I have them here, will follow mine. It is a terrible slowness, and it is also the only map I have ever needed. Where the trail has been, the moss is clean and the bark is pale, and a seedling will sometimes follow it, as if it too had read the same instruction.

Someways into this — I do not know how many drips — the idea of my own shape arrives. I am long. I am ribbed along the back where a shell should be, though it is no shell that would keep anything out. Moss has begun there, first at the edges I cannot reach, then further in, a green that has decided I am a suitable place. The older I grow, the more the moss decides it. My feelers test the air and find only more dampness to test. I have been on this one tree for what feels like the whole of my life, because it is.

That is my horizon. A tree.

Not the plateau — the plateau is a rumor I will never verify. Not the canopy, though I live beneath its curtains and hear them wringing out mist along their lengths, so that rain seems to fall from a single vine while no cloud is anywhere. Not the ground, which is not ground, which is plant all the way down, and which passes under me sometimes as a low wave. My world is the vaulted ribs of a stilt tree, the roots descending again and again like the bars of a cage that is also a house, and I am on the skin of one rib, grazing my way along it.

We are three generations here whose trails overlap. My mother crossed this branch. I am crossing it more slowly behind her. My young will cross it behind me. None of us will ever touch another tree. We do not need to. The branch is longer than a life, and that is enough; a whole life can be spent being faithful to a single piece of geography.

Above me, things pass that never land. Birds dart out of the hanging curtains and back so quickly you would think you had imagined them; their nests have thickened into the vine that holds them, so that home and curtain are the same substance now. I feel their coming only as a fresh drip, a small weather, when a glider trails its feet along the underside of a wet leaf and dislodges the morning’s water. Their droppings fall for years into the same bromeliad cups below, and the frogs in those cups — no bigger than a thumbnail, born and living and dying in an inch of held water, in a plant within a plant — live their whole lives in the richness of birds they will never see. I graze the outer leaves of that same bromeliad, carefully, and the frog does not mind me. Nothing here hunts. Things merely overlap, and eat what is already overlapping.

Under me, other things pass. Once, while I was stopped on the rib, a passage moved beneath the root — a blind, soft-pawed family pushing through the mat by pressure alone, feeling for the loose places where roots have met and not yet bound. The whole tree tilted, very slightly. I held on with my whole underside and waited for the wave to go by. Behind them the mat closed as if they had never been. In front of them, somewhere far ahead in the direction of a sound, the mat will open again. Their trail, traced over centuries, is a slow river through the floor. Mine is a line on the bark. Both of us are only temporary shapes in a place that closes behind us.

I hear — through my belly, through the plant, not through air — the click-beetle in the fallen trunk nearby, tapping its blunt body against the root-work, waiting for its mate, perhaps a tree’s height away, to tap back. They will close that distance over years. I recognize the patience because I live by it. The croaker in the hollow fern stem does the same with his voice, coming up through the damp hollow like water through a pipe, living his entire life inside that one stem as it obligingly rots around him. Somewhere out in the drifting white weather between the canopy layers, things are being born that will live exactly as long as that weather lasts, a week, and die with it. I am in no position to be impressed by their brevity. A week of weather and a lifetime of bark are both more time than I can calculate. I measure in moss. Moss grows a fingernail’s width in a year. I have grown mossy in proportion.

Where the rib crosses a stone place — there is a low spiral of unmortared stones half-sunk into the mat a little way down, each stone smoothed by water until it looks like the others — I taste a different film, warmer at the joints, greyer. A soft grazer lives only there, farming a ruin that does not grow and only wears down, very slowly, toward the shape of the hills. I do not stay. The stone does not grow, and I am a thing that follows what grows. The frogs settle in the spiral’s centre where rain collects longest. The tunnel-family passes through its root-mouth as if it were a natural gap. To me it is a brief change in the taste of the bark, and then the rib goes on descending, and I go on following my mother.

Toward the edge, when the wind — there is scarcely wind — moves through the canopy in the right way, I can feel, more than hear, the great fringe where the mat thins and the roots hang over emptiness in thousands and water pours out through them in veils that break into mist long before they reach the cloud sea below. The plants here angle toward that sound over weeks, leaf stem by leaf stem. I angle toward the dripping on my rib. We are all turning, at our own speeds, toward water we will never reach in the way a visitor would reach it. The veil-hangers at the fringe will never descend to that sea either. Their children drop and are caught by a lower root, or another, or another, until the fringe filters them back into the mat. It is the same law as mine, only at the edge instead of the middle: nothing leaves, everything is caught, everything becomes floor for something else.

I am a slug.

There. That is as much of a name as the place will give me, and it took this long to earn it because everything here takes this long. I am one of the great travelers of the skin, though a traveler who crosses a single tree in a lifetime is not much of a traveler to anyone but another slug. I have no hurry in me and no sharpness in me, and the place has no use for either. I eat the green film. I leave the clean line. The line heals slowly, and things follow it. When I am old, the moss will have me from shell to back, and what looks like a raised ridge of moss on a root will shift, very slightly, and go on shifting, and that shifting will be me, going on in the only direction I have ever been given.

When I die, I will not leave the food chain. I will become architecture first — a body for fungi, a loosening in the felt — and then, in time, a floor. A fallen giant sinks through the same stages I will: a raised road, then a trench of black water, then a mossy ridge, then the upper floor of a new forest growing out of its own rot. The community migrates across it stage by stage, never leaving the tree even as the tree ceases to be a tree. I have been migrated across by my own moss. I have been a road for seedlings. I have been, for a while, the horizon of a frog, the weather of a moth that sits motionless under the leaf I graze by day and flies, transparent as mist, to the one flower it will keep faith with by night.

If you sit still on this plateau long enough, your footprints fill with green. If you sit still long enough to be me, the green fills with you. I do not think of it as an ending. The mat creeps, the drip arrives, the trail behind me heals and is read again by something younger. Somewhere a root is descending toward a sound. Somewhere a family is pushing its slow river through the floor, opening and closing. Somewhere the mist is being born and dying in a week, and somewhere a beetle is tapping, and somewhere a mate is tapping back.

I am warm, and wet, and moving, and still. If you find a ridge of moss on a root that shifts, very slightly, and goes on shifting — that is as close as I come to a horizon.
