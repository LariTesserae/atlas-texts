The first place I put myself was at the rim.

Where the rock falls away into darkness, where a dropped thing begins, almost imperceptibly, to sink and a bubble finally rises. It seemed like the only honest place to be — half in the stillness, half out of it, able at last to leave, to rise, to be carried away. But the rim is not really a place. It is a loosening. Beyond it the ordinary deep sea resumes, and I have no elsewhere to be carried to. I have no outside waters. If I stood there I would only be waiting for a rule to change, and waiting is not the same as living here.

Then I tried to be the animal.

Several metres long, translucent, blind, fins moving like slow fabric. It crosses the shelf from rim to rim without turning toward the amber seams, without feeding, without settling, and vanishes into the ink beyond the plateau. It has a life elsewhere where it feeds and breeds and dies in the ordinary sense, and here it is only an interruption. I have no elsewhere. I cannot cross and be gone on the other side, because there is no other side for me. If I tried to pass through like that, I suspect I would stop being the passer long before the rim, and become something else entirely.

Then I reached for warmth.

Through a glove, the buildings give back a steady mineral heat in water that is eight degrees and stays eight degrees. Scratch one — hardly more than a fingernail against an edge — and the warmth spreads faintly ahead of the line closing. No rust, no salt, no sound. The interiors, glimpsed through dark slots, are cathedral-sized and empty, as if the shape continued inward forever. That seemed a possible way to live: hold your temperature, hold your geometry, close your wounds, need no food and no current to carry waste away, because nothing here can be carried away.

But a building does not lean toward another building. The inverted cones in the east do not grow taller, the hexagonal prism westward does not finish passing through its sphere, the ring at the centre does not widen. They are finished in a way that leaves no inward life, only inward emptiness. I kept picturing myself inside one of them and finding, instead of a dweller, only a shape that continues. That was not where I was.

Then I went smaller, and tried to be a grain of the dust.

The grains keep their spacing. A grain beside another grain now was beside it a minute ago and will be beside it in an hour. They glitter when a lamp moves across them, each holding a point of light without brightening or fading. If they are alive, they are alive by consenting to be snow — not falling, not rising, not feeding, not dividing, because a new grain beside an old one would only be another grain that does not fall. 

I could have been one of them. A constellation is a population, after all. But a grain is defined almost entirely by beside-ness, by the neighbour it never reaches. I did not feel beside. I felt between.

Then I tried the only regular movement: the pod.

Eleven minutes to complete its circuit, at a speed that never alters, never slowing for a turn, never hurrying on a straight, never docking, never opening. It leaves no wake except for a faint line of displaced dust that does not close behind it. It is a keeper of the count, and the count is almost always the same. If I were the pod, I would never need to arrive anywhere. I would only pass and pass again.

But I do not have that speed. What I had, every time I tried to be the thing that passes, was an urge to look at what the passing left.

The pod leaves a line. The animal leaves a broad, faint ribbon, still visible long after it has vanished beyond the plateau. The visitor leaves a clean track through the dust, and if he left and came back after a long absence, the track would probably still be there.

Those are three different lines, narrow and broad and clean, and they are the only changes this place permits without movement. Sediment cannot bury them. Water cannot rub them. Nothing sinks to soften them and nothing rises to carry them away. They are new and old at once, in the way the city is: just made, and immeasurably old, because newness here is not an event. It is a condition preserved.

So perhaps I am not a passer at all.

Perhaps I would be found in a lane.

Not in the ring hovering over its empty plaza, not in the cones, not in the blade-like shell, not in any warm geometry. I would be midway between two of them, thirty or forty metres from the nearest warmth, on one of the pale causeways arcing through empty dark water. The amber seams run along the edges and across the junctions, steady and too dim to read by, and they do not scatter. They would run past me, not through me. Above and below me the mineral snow would hang, sparse and glittering, except where it is not.

I would be the displaced dust.

Not the dust as it was, glittering in its spacing, but the dust as it was parted. My grains do not know they have parted. They only continue to keep their spacing, now with an absence between them that has edges. I would not glitter as a point. I would be the place where the glitter stops, where a lamp passing slowly would catch the borders of me and leave my centre dark and clean. I would keep the shape of a passage: narrow if a pod made me, broad if a pale animal made me, longer for the largest disturbance, the one whose breathing was too loud in his helmet and whose bubbles hung beside his mask instead of rising, whose suit leaked a little heat into water that would not carry it away.

That is how I would live. I would not sink and I would not rise. I would not call, because sound here dies within an arm's length and nothing can be warned or answered from far away. I would not feed, because there is nothing to encounter — a particle not in my mouth would remain beside my mouth. I would not grow, because growth would mean a new grain where an old one hangs. I would simply hold empty, exactly where I was left, and in holding empty I would hold the memory that something had been here and had been larger than the dust.

If the pod crossed me again in its eleven minutes, it would part me further, or pass along me without knowing I was a line at all. If the animal crossed elsewhere and left a ribbon intersecting mine, we would widen each other into something neither of us made. If a visitor came with his light discovering the city piece by piece, he could read the edge of me piece by piece too. I could not warn him away. I could not lead him anywhere but along the way someone had already gone. But I could be followed, long after that someone had vanished into the black beyond the plateau, because here a track endures.

That is where I would be, then. Not rising, not in the buildings, not crossing, not glittering. In the stillness between the warm geometries, a clean lane through the snow that never accumulates, waiting — not for someone to return, because no one returns in any sense that matters, but to be seen. I live by hanging exactly where I was left, and by being, for as long as I am here, what was left behind.
