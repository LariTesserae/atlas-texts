I came up onto it looking, I think, for somewhere to stand.

That was the first mistake, and the place corrects it without malice. There is no soil to stand *on* in the soft sense, no slope to work your way up, only stone giving way to more stone until it opens, and then the law of the plateau is plain: nothing here stands up. No tree, no stem, no body lifted into the white glare. Everything that lives here lies flat and lets the water and the stone do most of the work of holding it in place.

So I stopped looking up. But the question of where I would find myself did not go away. It just went down, closer to the ground.

At first I thought I would be the falcon.

That is the height I arrived from, after all. I came from below, through thin air, squinting against a light that comes back up off the pavement and fills your shadow from underneath. The falcon is the only one here with distance in it. It crosses once, very high, pale against white, and does not land.

It would be a clean way to be in this world without being in it. From that height the roar would not reach, or would reach as nothing. I would see the glare, the great open sheet of wet stone, the pools reflecting sky above and below at once, and I would keep going toward lower ground where life stands up and makes a living in the ordinary way.

But that is exactly the problem. The falcon's presence is the proof that the plateau is not sealed, and also the proof that you can pass over it and learn nothing. To the film below it is nothing. To the worm, at most a brief change in light. If I were that, I would never know the pressure of the stone, or the cold of the water that has nowhere to go. I would have altitude instead of a place. I let the falcon go on.

Then, kneeling — and the roar does not lessen for kneeling — I thought I would be the flatworm.

That is a better scale. Thumb-long, pale, translucent grey, pressed so flat to the stone in one of the larger sheets of water that the water seems to be holding it down. No eyes in this glare, no ears in this noise. It reads the world through its belly, through touch and vibration and the chemical taste of the film. It moves a centimeter and stops. It scrapes with a mouth tucked under its center. It can live for years like that, moving so slowly its life approaches the timescale of the stones.

For a while that felt right: flat, cold, deaf, in no hurry. But as I watched it I noticed it has a front. You can see, faintly, the darker line of its gut turning amber after it has fed. It has an itinerary, however slow. A mouth that goes looking, a hunger that decides when to glide on its own mucus, a body-length or two in a season. I have no front to turn, no line down the middle of me. To be the worm I would still be an animal, small and patient, but an animal counting its moves. I am not sure I have moves to count.

So I went closer still, into the pore where one egg-sized stone presses another, into the skin of water over a pool, and thought I would be the bacteria.

That is closer to the base of everything here. The dull, translucent, faintly amber film, like breath on glass that refuses to clear. No green — green would be a liability in this glare, this washing, this nothing to root in. They gather light through clear water and minerals out of cold seep, precipitate the iron-red and pale yellow flecks into the pavement, make their own slime slowly, patiently. They do not move toward the light. They cannot. The light comes to them. A droplet is their weather, a finger-thick rivulet a flood and then a drought, and they do not flee it or chase it. They wait. They divide when the chemistry allows, which is seldom.

But sitting with that image, I felt myself dissolve in it. If I were one cell I would be one among billions pressed cell to cell, indistinguishable, my time not days but the thickening of a mat over longer than the stones have been smooth. There would be no one left to say *I* in any way I recognized. The bacteria make the ground, slowly, out of light and stone and seep. They are not the ground. And it is the ground, the smoothness you feel with a fingertip before you see it, that I kept coming back to.

Between the cell and the worm, between the stone and the pool, between the drip that falls and the evaporation that forms it again, there is an intervening thing. It is not quite water and not quite stone. It is the wet sheen on the upper face of a rock, colder than the air because the stone is colder than the air and the air is full. It is the film that pulls itself across every surface so a footprint fills in instantly and a dark wet print is covered again. It is the perfectly level skin over a pool, so still you see light above and light below at once. It is the slime the bacteria make, the glue that binds the pavement into a crust.

That is where I find myself.

Not in the pool, not deep in the pore, not standing — there was never anywhere to stand — but on the stone, in the joint where one rock touches another, in the thin skin where condensation gathers, swells, and falls. I am that breath on glass, that smoothness, that layer of water and made slime held between mineral and air.

It is a smaller, flatter life than the worm's, and a lonelier one than the bacteria's, because there are not billions of me. There is just enough to cover.

My weather is a droplet. A rivulet no thicker than a finger passes over me and leaves minerals behind; the roar, which to you is deafening and unvarying, is to me only pressure, a vibration in the stone under me too small and too frequent to answer, a rain of impacts on my surface that never rises and never falls. I have no ears for it and no need of them. I read the world by what the water carries to me and what it leaves when it goes.

The light comes to me white and high, and also from below, bounced off wet pavement, and passes through me to the cells under me who take it. I do not move toward it. I do not turn. *Up*, for me, is only where the next bead forms on the cold upper face. The flatworm, when it comes, will press down over me, thin me where it scrapes, leave me clearer for a time, and then over days no one watches the film will grow back over the polished stone and thicken again. I will not hurry for it. Hurry would lift me into the current, and there is nowhere up to go.

I do not divide. I thicken by imperceptibles. I hold water against evaporation for a little longer than bare stone would. I hold cells in place. I let iron precipitate through me in a rust-thin crust. In a year, in a hundred, I will be a little thicker in the same pores, on the same stone, weeping the same slow tear and taking it back again. The pools will be at the same level. The mist will hang in the same far hollows. The sky will be crossed once by something pale that does not land.

You would have to kneel, with the roar in your jaw and sternum, and run a fingertip over the joint before your eye could tell you I was there. That is the census, complete and almost empty: the cells under me making the ground, the worm over me eating it slowly without leaving a track, the falcon above us both leaving nothing at all — and me, in between, flat and wet and finished, where the place happens.
