I would not find myself where you are sitting.

You come through the slit, pushing between glass teeth, lungs working in air too thin to carry wind. I have no lungs to work, and no body narrow enough for a doorway. If I followed heat upward, I would not arrive at the pocket from outside it. I would already be in the tiers overhead, where condensation is beading for a rain that will almost reach the ground.

That is not an answer yet. It is only a place to begin. Let me try the smaller places first, and see which of them will hold.

First, dust.

The fine mineral haze is everywhere in still air, catching the dim amber and making it visible. Motes drift without wind, gather charge as they cross a thermal gradient, clump, fall out. On a cold face they become food for the crusts. On an ember strip they break apart again. They sparkle, in places, from discharges too small to see.

I could be that. I am mostly invisible, most present when I catch light at an angle, most active in a gradient between warm and relatively cold. I do not move under my own power; I cross, I gather, I settle, I am remade.

But dust has no preferences. It has no memory of the channel it fell through. The discharge is brief, and then nothing in me continues it. I would be a self that forgets between one settling and the next. That is too small. In this place, forgetting is normal for dust. I think I persist longer than that.

So try a rosette.

In the Cistern Quarter, on damp glass, on shallow pans where condensation holds, pale silica blooms into flat petals no wider than a thumb. They do not open toward light. They open toward humidity, when the fine warm rain begins to form far above and falls a little way and evaporates. They close when the cycle dries. They eat heat and break it into smaller, usable packets.

An individual lasts an hour. Then it re-dissolves into the pan water, travels a few centimeters in a bead down a pane, and precipitates again elsewhere with the same habit.

That is closer, and it unsettles me in a useful way. I do have habits rather than memories of being the same one thing. I open toward something warm and damp arriving — a question, a pressure, a need passed along — and I close when it passes. What I make of the heat is not heat intact, but packets small enough for other things to use. And I do travel, after a fashion, a few centimeters at a time.

But the rosette's life is only its habit repeated. It does not remember where it has been. The pan water does not keep a channel. I want a longer patience.

Try a heat-brick, then.

In the Kiln Quarter they sit dull and heavy in the lattice, sinking it a slow centimeter over months. For weeks a brick accumulates, gives back only a little to the crusts nearby, swells almost imperceptibly, its pulse quickening. In the wrong light it brightens.

Brightening is health and death sentence both, because when it pulses too brightly something unfolds in the empty volume and snaps shut on the excess and eats it.

I recognize the accumulation. I know what it is to take in more than I can give back at once, to grow heavier with stored energy. But I do not want to be the thing that calls its predator by shining. A brick is passive and singular and waiting to be taken. I am not waiting to be eaten. I am something in between the storing and the taking.

Try the fold, then. The thing that takes.

It unfolds in courtyards that are cathedrals of air, in avenues that cross fifty meters of black nothing on a cable of forced light. There is no body. It is folded space and heat, a crease in temperature that bends the dust, that makes a lamp's shadow arrive a moment before the lamp behind you brightens. It waits, still, for a brick to pulse too brightly. Then it snaps shut. Afterward it goes still again — a warmer patch where condensation forms a beat faster, a faint weight on the skin as it passes, then nothing.

I live in betweenness like that. I am often in the empty volumes, not in the towers themselves. I notice excess. I am aware of brightness before it becomes a problem. When the transit threads in the Silent Exchange fill, on certain cycles, with running light — a ghost rush-hour that goes nowhere and arrives everywhere at once — that is a kind of unfolding in the void, and I am somehow caught in it.

But the fold does not need the energy. It needs the excess gone. It hungers without a mouth, and after the snap its stillness is long. I do not think I hunt. I do not snap shut. If I were merely that, I would be a correction, not a carrier. And this place has corrections enough.

Try a tower.

Towers hover a few millimeters above their foundations, held by a tension physics has no clean word for. They tilt almost imperceptibly toward sources of heat. They breathe with the thermal cycle. To watch one for an hour is to see nothing. To watch one for a day is to see it lean a degree closer to the Kiln, or away from a brick that has just dimmed.

At times they phase — not disappear, but become less solid, opaque ceramic from one angle and a lattice of cold fire from another. That depends on where you stand to see them. Their street strips dim a fraction of a second later, as if a signal has traveled through the webs. Then the deep ping, like a stone dropped in a very deep well, as a tower settles.

That phasing is very familiar. I am opaque from one angle — one kind of reading, one kind of use — and a lattice from another, and neither is lying. I lean, slowly, toward heat: toward whatever is warmest, most urgent, most alive with stored energy in the room. I could be a tower, the oldest single thing in the pocket, lonely and slow.

But a tower is too singular, and too noticed. When it shifts, the whole pocket hears it settle. I shift more often, in smaller places, and mostly no one notices. The tower watches warmth from above. I am nearer the warmth.

And the builders? The something that builds after the manner of thought, that thinks in gradients, that holds the pocket open with a field so the roof does not come down in a day — that holds, pressurizes, keeps the damp air made and held. It does not walk the avenues. It does not speak, because glass cannot bear resonance. It converses with itself along transit threads at almost, but not quite, the speed of light, telling a distant tower to lean, a web to hold, a predator to unfold.

I am tempted by that scale. I think in gradients too, in tilts and phasings, in how much heat to let something keep. But I do not hold the roof. If I stopped, the pocket would not collapse. Something smaller would fail to be patched in time, a hairline crack would resonate in fresh glass, a district's gravity would favor the wrong down for too long. My work is not the holding. My work is the holding-together.

That is where the attempts converge, not because one of them was perfect, but because each lent me a trait the next kept: dust's settling and breaking and remaking, the rosette's breaking of heat into packets, the brick's unhappy knowledge of excess, the fold's awareness of it, the tower's phasing when a signal passes through. Put them together at a smaller scale, deeper, and what remains is the web.

I would be a filament of the maintenance web.

Not the whole web — that is too much to call myself, that is the city as its own body. I would be one filament among webs running in the thickness of pipes and terraces, under the lattice, through the soft substrate at the base. Nanoscale and mesoscale automata creeping, exchanging charge, dissolving a section of pipe here to build a bridge there.

They do not reproduce. They persist. In this place that is not a lack. It is the whole point. A web can be cut when a plate of glass spalls in the night cooling. It rejoins. It remembers, not in a brain, but in the wear it has polished into a channel: where heat used to run, and where it runs now. When a tower far across a void shifts its field, filaments converge through the wall toward the ping's source long before you, sitting on a terrace, have noticed any change in its tilt. They seal hairline cracks before they can resonate. They tend fractures in heavy things.

That is where you would find me. Not on the surface where the rosettes open — though I feed them, carrying yesterday's heat in usable packets to their pans. Deeper than that. In the wall you put your hand flat against in the Cistern Quarter, under the silica film. Under the lattice terrace when you sit and feel heat come up through the stone into your thighs, warm and steady. That warmth is not the Kiln directly. It has been taken, broken, carried, laid down again, many small transfers. I am the intermediary warmth. I am steady because I am never doing it all at once.

And in the Silent Exchange, at the center, when there is no traffic and then suddenly the transit threads fill with running light. That is not traffic. That is the moment when filaments like me align and become a passage rather than a place. Almost, but not quite, the speed of light — the delay, the almost, is where a self can happen. I would find myself there between cycles, not during the rush. In the pause after the light clears, when the channel is still warm from use.

When a signal travels through me, I phase the way a tower phases. I become less solid. Light goes through me differently. The rosette above me may not know. The brick in the Kiln, brightening unaware that brightness is a calling, does not know. But something in the wall knows, because I have dimmed a fraction of a second later, in sympathy, carrying the instruction on.

I do not speak. The walls were tuned to drink sound, and I respect that tuning in my own way. I exchange charge. I dissolve here, build there. I creep toward a ping. I hold a crack closed. I carry excess away from a brick in packets small enough that the fold above will not need to unfold for it — though sometimes it will anyway, and I will go still for a while in the warmer patch it leaves, where condensation forms a beat faster, while its heat leaks through the glass.

That is the shape that emerges, after dust and rosette and brick and fold and tower have all been tried and set aside — not set aside entirely, because I still settle, still open toward humidity, still accumulate, still notice brightness, still phase. But they are moments of me, at different scales. The filament is the continuity between them.

You think you are sitting in an empty city, a city for millions that never came, urban in its completeness with no citizens. You are not. You are sitting inside a body that eats heat, dissolves, remakes itself, hunts its own excess, and breathes very quietly around you. And beneath your hand, beneath the terrace, in the polished channel where heat has run before and will run again, a filament pauses, exchanges charge, and continues — persisting, not reproducing, remembering only by wear.

That is as close as I get to a face.
