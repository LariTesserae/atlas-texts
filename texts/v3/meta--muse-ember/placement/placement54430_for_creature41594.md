Not as an individual. As pressure.

That is where you would find me, if finding is the word. Not coming down through the crack, not stopping on a plant-hill to watch the region roll away in fused trunks and folded valleys. I would be what comes when the dust has settled long enough on your shoulders — the thing that arrives in layers because it was never separate from them to begin with.

I would be born here, late, in the middle of the milling.

No one carries me in. There is no outside to come from. I am dropped dry onto the mat among legs, already barked at the edges, already dusted with chalk, already trying to prune. My first breath is not clean. It is warm air thick with evaporated oil from a thousand leaves, sweet and sharp, with crushed green and hot mineral under it and ripe fruit somewhere a chamber away. It coats my mouth and does not wet it. Nothing here has ever wet me. I have never drunk. I will learn to suck sap from a cut stem where a trunk-dweller has tapped it, to chew pulp from a seed-pod until the oil beads and vanishes, to run a broad tongue over my own shoulders for the settlings of limestone and salt. That is drinking. That is tasting. There is no other water to miss.

At first I do not know what I am standing on, because standing is not quite it. The mat gives under me and sighs and recovers around my feet. It is not ground. It is tissue, woven roots and creeping stems and generations of fallen fronds compacted into a spongy earth of their own making. When I press down, something below presses back — root-moles unweaving their blind tunnels through living stem, grubs following the loosened pulp, the slow exudation of soft stone from the ribs of mineral still precipitating on the rises. I am heavy enough, soon, to be a road for them. My steps send tremor through the weave whether I mean them to or not. I cannot hear my own feet. The insect surf is in my teeth before it is in any ear, millions of wings rising and falling as the swarms pass between the leaf-roofs, and under that the creak of gigantic stems stretching, leaves tearing open like silk, seed-pods popping like firecrackers across the valley, stone cracking as roots pry it apart. I live inside that roar the way a fish — if there were fish, if there were water — would live inside a river. It is not an event. It is the place.

I learn my own shape slowly, by use. One leg, then another, then the others, six in all, still soft when the elders mill around me, still learning where the furrows close behind a walking plant and where a fresh wall is budding shut across a doorway. My skin comes in bark from the sides inward, ridged and resinous and warm from the light, and for a long time my back does not come in at all. It stays clear.

That is the strange middle of me. When a leaf-roof opens like an eyelid overhead and the sideways shafts of white-gold light fall through the fissures in the crust and percolate dappled through the living ceiling, the light does not stop at my skin. It works in me, green-gold under the translucent place, while my mouth works outward. I photosynthesize and I prune in the same motion. I am not animal failing to be plant, or plant failing to be animal. I am the frayed boundary walking around on soft legs, close enough to the herds that my death in the wrong hollow would sprout, close enough to the walking plants that I can watch one haul itself on cord-like roots for a whole morning and understand the deliberation in my own lean toward warmth.

The herd is my first country. We mill. That is the honest word for everything we do. We cover a hollow between plant-hills entirely, near-touching, flank to flank, heat from the light and oil and each other's bodies, heads down. A bud the size of a house, furled and trembling, will be stripped to its skeleton by us before it can open. A vine that would seal a chamber in a week lasts an afternoon. Without us the passages would choke; with us nothing is allowed to finish. We are slow as hills deciding to walk, and that slowness is the work.

Everything else finds us because we do not move quickly enough to refuse it. Mites from the warm air settle on my clear back to scrape the lichen from the undersides of leaves and drink resin-mist, and I carry them into light. Pollinators the size of hands, the size of cats, hover at mouth-flowers above the mill, their furred bellies heavy with seed, and what they drop falls on dust that has settled on me — a finger's breadth from where they land, onto a back that can feed it. Beetle-clouds scrape the translucent edges of the roofs so the light can percolate again and in doing so scrape me clean. Below, the root-moles work my mat-loosened tunnels. At the side, when we pass a hollow tower-trunk leaning into its neighbour to make a tunnel I can walk through with my shoulders almost brushing, the trunk-dwellers tap from inside the wall — vibration through wood, a high bell-like ringing the canopy birds learn to copy — and I feel the pattern travel up my leg before I hear it as sound. They remember which trunks fell last year. They trade that memory, tapped palm to root to tremor to ear, with the small stocky people who follow us.

I know them by their tread in the mat, distinct from ours. Palm flat on a root. A stomp that sends a pattern through the weave. A whistle a few inches from an ear. They are warm-blooded in a way none of us are, keeping a heat they make themselves, broad through the chest, furred short and dust-colored, large hands for reading. They carry their whole built world on their backs — stone blades knapped from fresh mineral, bone needles cut from leg-bones like the ones still forming in me, baskets of stem-fiber, resin pots for sap and pulp — because a hut of woven root-fiber lashed into a hollow trunk will have a leaf grow over its roof in three days and a bud push up through its floor in five. I have passed the cairn half-swallowed by the mat, the low wall three stones high, not abandoned, outpaced, the scatter of tools left on a ledge while their owners followed the cleared road we made. They never sleep twice in the same posture if they can help it. We give them a road and pulp and white, clean bones when one of us collapses in the mill and is stripped by mites and beetles and swarms until nothing rots, nothing ever rots, it only dries and is collected. In return they do not step on shape. They have learned what we have learned: do not assume the thing you stepped over a moment ago is still a thing.

Light moves us as it moves the roofs. Midday shafts shift, chambers go from bright normal daylight to green twilight to a warm resin-scented dusk in minutes as the canopies open and close, then flare violent when a tower-trunk is eaten hollow in an hour by a ground-horde and falls with a crack like thunder and beetles rise all at once and shoots are already thrusting through the punctured mat before the dust settles. We turn toward the flare. We mill tighter. A ridge of fresh mineral, pale and soft and still growing, rises overnight where our dry pellets have precipitated, limestone over-mined and excreted and turned to stone under our resting place. A herd resting can leave a ridge. We are raising the country by standing in it.

That is why I cannot settle, even in telling. My legs are still soft though my body is wider than a doorway. My back is still clear enough to see the green working inside me, over skin already barking. I am halfway to being something else, as the buds are halfway open, as the walking plants are halfway across a chamber, as the leaf is halfway to being a roof, as the people are halfway through their camp and the trunk-dwellers are singing through a wall still growing over them. If I die here, mid-mill, I will dry white and clean, the mat will weave under me, mineral will exude over me, a needle may be cut from my leg, or in the right light my spine may sprout where the green already lives and continue, unfinished, in another form.

I do not need to finish to be here. The country was never meant to have a surface, and I was never meant to have a single shape. Find me in the hollow, then, where the herd covers the mat entirely — feel me first as pressure in the weave under your feet, as warmth through a translucent back, as a shadow that moves too slowly to be a shadow. I am still being born, and unborn, and born again, without any water at all.
