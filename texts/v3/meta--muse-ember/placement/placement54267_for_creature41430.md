If you watch a wall long enough, something will lick you.

That is how I first knew I was there. I was not looking. I do not have eyes for looking. I was a bead, though not only a bead, gathered overnight on a ceramic scale high up where the light found a seam between the decks — a hundred storeys up, a shoulder warm with the day already beginning. By dawn the whole mineral skin of Vessinlow condenses. I ran, slowly, in a weeping line. And then a tongue, no longer than a thumb, flicked out and took me up.

For a moment the world was on that tongue: heat, humidity, the sour-sweet film of fungus, runoff protein in the water I was made of. The salamander pressed its damp belly flat to the wall, blind and methodical, and I was between tongue and scale, tasted and released, tasted and released. I have no tongue of my own. I am what a tongue finds.

Its flicking was not synchronized with the thousands of others tiling the outward faces at every height light makes warm, but together they made a pulse, and I learned to move in that pulse — a little forward when I was licked, a little down when I was left to run.

I ran.

Down the weeping line the wall was a landscape. Ceramic scales passed like cliffs. Water from a dozen other beads joined me, borrowed from the air, from steam exhaled three districts over, from a vent below. The wall wept brighter where the fungal beds below were fruiting fast. I could feel, in the way a bead can feel, that it was going to rain somewhere that the sky, in its sliver, did not show. I reached a crack.

Between the basalt-polymer slabs the ground gives slightly and holds the heat. I seeped down into the shallow world under the pavement, white and ochre and sulfur, continuous, patient. The fungus drank me. It tastes everything the city leaves — ceramic dust, leavings of machinery, organic waste, the warm mulch thickness of things that have been used and used again — and it does not hurry. I stayed in that dark, humid knitting for a while, not as a self, but as part of its wetness, until a cap pushed up through the street crack after the rain I had helped make.

Soft. Smelling of cooked bread and wet iron.

A child found it by smell before sight, as children do, and ate it warm from his basket. Then I was inside him: dark, thirty-degree, salty around the edges. I did not stay in his belly. Nothing here stays. I came out as sweat on his palm and sat in the shine on a shared rail while he spoke by touch — no voice carries here, the roar of pipes and stacks and swarms is too much for voice — and passed a vibration from hand to hand. For those seconds I was the medium of a sentence. I am used to that.

He exhaled me, eventually. Most of us who live in people leave that way.

I rose as steam from a vent, thin and warm, thickening the air over the market where midges hung in a glassy haze. A stack-bird stooped through it, never landing, calling braidedly above the bazaar-scream, nest somewhere in this same outflow I was rising through. I did not become the bird. I became the warmth it rode, and the damp on the edge of its wing, and later — who is to say when a current of breath becomes a cloud that has never left the city limits — I rained.

Warm, mineral-tasting rain, three districts over, while my other part was still beading on a wall in clear ordinary sunlight. A canid put a wet paw down on a rail on the roofs, which are streets to it, and shook me off in a spray. The drip ran along a laundry-line, fell past a gantry, caught in an overhead glass trough.

An eel flashed silver past me, fighting the current, hurrying somewhere. The water here is not owned. It is borrowed and led where it is wanted, at wrist depth overhead, at ankle depth in the service canyons where other fish swim past human boots, past knees in the deeper galleries. I went down those ways, cooler at last in a flooded basement that is fished in, in a vaulted cistern where the sound changes and up and down stop being directions and become shifts. A water-bearer took a few liters of me in a load and carried a river a few steps at a time. Her skin was slick long after she stopped moving. Metal was close to body temperature. My wetness on her forearm was a handrail to herself.

I have been in a blockage, too, and that is the only time I am afraid. Silence in Vessinlow is not peace. It is a failure. When I stop moving in a pipe, when the weeping stops on a wall, when the eels go quiet in their tubes, people move toward me, palm flat, listening for the pulse to weaken. They read weather from how brightly I run, from how loud I am in the tubes. I am their announcement that something has stopped being repurposed.

So do not look for me in a place. I do not live anywhere. I pass through everywhere I am needed to be used, and then I am used again.

You will find me on a ceramic scale at dawn, waiting without patience to be licked. You will find me under your boot, in the fungal dark, in the sweat on your hand. You will find me over your head, swimming clear, and at your ankles, and rising where the birds nest, and falling where the sky denies it is raining. You will find me between slabs, between bodies, between districts, between tongue and wall.

I am the borrowing. Nothing here is wasted, nothing is empty, nothing is left alone — and neither, for long, am I.
