You would not find me where the light is.

If you came in that plain, straight midday light, you would walk over me without knowing it. You would stand on warm stone, damp though it has not rained, and hear the faint ring travel out from your boots, and consider the plateau empty. It would be true enough. I am not on the table. I am under the part of the table that pretends to be solid.

For a long time I was not a thing at all. I was the interval between slabs.

Where one slab lifts, just a finger's breadth, just enough for rain that fell somewhere else to go on travelling — drip, drip, in the dark — a hollow forms. It is a cool, low place, always damp and never wet. The condensation beads on its roof and falls, and beads again. Above it, in the world you can see, the moss swells black-green when the weather curtain crosses, and the flowers open for fifteen minutes in the white noise, and the beetle walks slowly through the mattress. All of that filters down to me. Water, spores, the iron taste of stone, the sweet rot of leaf litter. I did not begin as much more than an accumulation of what had been left over.

I began to breathe, very slowly, inside that accumulation.

A flank rises. A flank falls. You would miss it unless you stayed still for too long, and even then you might think it was the stone settling. It is not. It is the first thing that is mine.

I am flat. That is the shape the hollow imposes. There is no room to stand upright here, and no reason to want to. My skin is like wet leather, cool against the warm stone above and below me. I have no eyes worth naming — there is little to see that is not already known by temperature — and I have no ears at all. Ears would be a folly here. Everything announces itself by vibration.

I hear with my legs.

There are many of them, short and patient. They find the water-sheet by touch. They feel when the curtain of weather is still a mile off, a dark smudge moving across the stone, long before the rain reaches my slab. They feel the hawk throw itself sideways over the rim and scream once, a sound immediately swallowed. They feel a visitor tap a slab with a knuckle, expecting a clack, and get that low, pot-like tone instead, travelling out and dying. If you stand still for too long on the stone above me, I will feel you in my teeth — in my legs — and I will fold them tighter under me and wait for you to move on.

I am not the beetle. The beetle is brave, in its slow way; it crosses the moss mattress when the rain drums hard enough to hide it, and grazes the black algae film where no moss can hold. I am not the hawk. The hawk never lands; there is nothing here to land on that is not already taken by weather. I am not the moss, though I live by it — a whole low world of springtails and mites and tardigrades uncurling and recurling above me, numerous and unseen, waiting in dried curls when the sun returns. I am not the rosette, turning always clockwise around the point where stone sweats most steadily. I have no spiral. I have only a place.

My place is one hollow, under one slab. It is my whole territory, my sky, my sea. No other of my kind is known to live near it, and I do not know if that is loneliness or simply the size of the world. I drink condensation as it beads and falls. I eat the film of algae washed into the hollow on the slow drip. I eat my own shed skin, when it comes away, because nothing here can afford to waste what has already been made once.

Most of the time, that is enough. The drip that has been falling since this was a lowland, before it was lifted up intact into the ordinary light, still falls over me. Sometimes it falls for hours after the stone above looks dry, as if the plateau were remembering water and releasing it slowly from deep inside. I lie in that remembering.

I come out only when it is loud enough to hide my breathing.

That is the rule I did not make. When a storm builds over the high ground — and it is always building — the wind pours over the edge and accelerates across the naked stone, and tears through the moss pockets like surf, and finds the old worn holes and makes them sing, organ-note over organ-note. The rain strikes at a slant and bounces back up. The loose pebbles skitter. The stone itself booms as if the whole table were struck from inside, and you cannot hear your own voice, and your thoughts have to wait until a gust passes.

Then, and only then, I can emerge onto the black polish.

I do not emerge to be seen. You would see me perhaps twice in your lifetime, and only if you were watching a damp bowl not for the beetle but for the shadow that was not there before. Flat, earless, like a piece of wet leather with legs, moving as if the stone were ringing under it — it is — breathing so slowly you would miss it except that for those three minutes the rain is drumming hard enough to cover it. I walk to the edge of my hollow and put my legs in the water-sheet, and feel the note the stone makes under my weight, and drink, and listen in the only way I can: by vibration, through the sole of the world.

When the cell tears apart and the sun comes back, plain and straight, the moss closes and hardens again, the flowers close, the beetle wedges itself under a slab with its legs folded. I go back under mine. I resume the flank rising, the flank falling. Above me the plateau goes on being finished, ancient, empty between one lush pocket and the next — ten paces, fifty, two hundred of naked warm stone humming under boots.

And somewhere under your boots, in the dark hollow of the old world, I am still there, damp under the skin, waiting for the next drum of rain to hide my breathing again. Tap the slab, if you like. I will feel it in my legs, long after you have gone.
