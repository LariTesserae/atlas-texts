First there is pressure.

Not on me. Through me. Everything — wall, pipe, water, bone — tightens at once, then loosens, and I learn to wait for that tightening the way a thing waits for weather. It comes every ninety seconds or so. The whole chamber contracts a fraction around itself. Chains tick against pipes somewhere I cannot locate. Lamps sway on their piers. My own gut, my gills, the soft packed silt of my belly all answer it, whether I will or not. I pause for it. Then I go on.

The roar is not heard. There is no ear for it, only surfaces. Low, continuous, in the sternum and the teeth, layered with a higher shriek that comes and goes for reasons I do not need to know, and ahead of me a hammering that falls at irregular intervals a little faster than half the pulse. Forty-seven seconds. Again. Forty-seven seconds. Again. It never finishes anything. It just marks time inside time.

There is no up here. There is only warmer and colder, faster and slower, and the direction the water is already going.

I discover this by what I can do. Water passes over me and something inside me takes from it what I need without effort, a steady working at my sides that I have never had to learn. Copper is always in my mouth, and hot stone, and at the back of my throat a faint sulfur tang that thickens when I turn toward the centre. I do not taste to hunt. Tasting is how I know where I am. A dropped glove in an eddy tastes of rubber and old insulation. A stretch of bare pipe tastes of nothing at all, scoured clean. The lee of a pier foot tastes of cellar and rot and copper wiring furred thick as a root.

My skin is a line of listening from end to end. Not a line like a stripe that I could see — nothing arrives as image. It is a sensitivity laid along my whole length, so that when the fast lane enters a sunken canal I feel it first at my tail and then, a moment later, along my flank, the way a pressure arrives differently at different parts of the same body. When I brush something brittle, I know its shape by the order in which it touches me.

I am long. That is the rest of what I can say about my shape. Long enough that the warm water in the middle is warm on my head before it is warm on my tail. Long enough that I cannot turn in the narrow canals without slipping into the lane that is already turning. I do not fight the current. I let it decide more than I do. A small bend, barely a bend at all, and the lane carries me between the factory blocks, three metres of dark water between squat halls, over a floor made of stepped concrete and steel plate worn into terraces. Where the lane is fast, surfaces are scoured bright and nothing can settle, not silt, not me, not anything alive. Where it drops, a soft pale mud has gathered and I leave a shallow wake in it with my belly.

I have no eyes to speak of. The place where such a thing would be has healed over smooth, same skin as everywhere else, unpigmented, translucent. Pigment would be a waste here. There is no sun to hide from. There are lamps — I know this only because other things change when I cross their cones. The water there is very slightly different, the particles glittering, the silica fans catching something. I do not see it. I slide through a warmth that is not heat and a stillness that is not stillness, and then out again into twenty, thirty metres of nothing visible, where only silt drifts and the heat shimmers. That emptiness is enormous. Almost nothing per volume. I am almost nothing per volume.

I am not in the middle. I know the basin by its wrongness: the water thickening hotter against my skin, bubbles peeling off the exchangers and shearing sideways instead of rising, the upwelling that never moves and never stops. Only the invisible things can live in that. I keep to the edges, to the outer wall where the hot water has spread at the top, cooled against rock and concrete, and comes back along the floor in a comfortable, steaming bath. That return is my road. It goes inward, cooling as it goes, and then I turn — not because I decide to turn, but because the lane turns, because the current has made this turn year after year and the worn channel in the concrete remembers it better than I do.

Along the way the town touches me. Not with hands. With weight and texture.

A pier foot. The current is fastest there and the steel is bright, then in the lee it is rusted deep red-brown and soft with deposit. Bivalves are clustered on the cooler side, small bumps that do not move when I pass, filtering what the water drops. I smell their work more than I feel them.

A valve rim, a pipe as thick as my body is long. Silica has grown there in needles and fans, sulfur in pale yellow rosettes, iron oxide in dark shrubby tufts that snap if I brush them. I brush them. They are the gardens of the place. They are not plants. They are stone growing the way leaves would, slowly, in the shelter of things, and I learn to read them as texture: needle-fine, rosette-soft, tuft-brittle. A fan of silica can break the line of my flank for a second. That is hiding, here.

An intake screen, where the outflow is steady and the film is thickest. Grey-white mat, slimy, almost alive, smelling of hot spring and cellar at once. Shrimp work it, thumb-sized, translucent, their gills working steadily, their dark gut line showing through clear shell. I do not eat them. They do not notice me except as a shadow of pressure. They cling when the pulse comes. I do not cling. I ride.

Behind a pier, in a permanent eddy, a plastic crate circles and circles, coming slowly apart. A glove. A length of insulation. Light things do not sink here; they orbit. Small crusts live on the debris itself, grazing it, housing in it as it rots. I pass through the edge of the orbit and the litter turns its face to me, then away, then toward me again, never arriving, never leaving. I have seen — felt — that same crate for a long time. I will feel it again when the lane brings me around.

Bubbles race upward like inverted rain, burst on the vaulted ceiling far below any street, and run back down the walls in bright rivulets. I do not see that either. I feel the tingling of them against my back as a lane of them crosses over me, fast, cold compared to the bath, gone.

Once, very rarely, something larger passes through the high empty water between the vortex and the wall. Too fast, too far. It bends the pressure around me the way a big body bends heat shimmer. The whole chamber's settled storm shivers for a second. It is not living here. Nothing large can. There is not enough food, not enough quiet, not enough cool water, and the sound is too saturated for anything to want to try. It is gone before the next hammering. I go on tasting copper.

I spawn, perhaps, into this same return. If I do, the eggs sort themselves like heavy things into the channels and stay there, in the silt with everything else that has sunk, in the dips worn into stair edges, under the shelter of white stencils I cannot read — LEVEL 3, INTAKE, KEEP CLEAR — where the paint survives under a grey film and the water is a little quieter. That is as close to a home as the town provides. The press cycles on nothing. The conveyor turns carrying no load. The pump surges and settles into its roar and never stops. The crane hangs mid-swing, swaying a fraction in the current, never completing its arc. None of it needs me.

So I ride the floor current again. Hot in the centre, comfortable at the wall, scalding if I stray too far inward, scoured if I enter the fast lanes too straight. The mineral gardens grow a little thicker on the pipes. The shrimp molt and their skins join the orbiting debris. The pulse comes every ninety seconds and I pause for it, then go on, long and pale and blind, tasting my way along the terraces, turning where the lane turns, made almost entirely of the place itself — of its heat, its chemistry, its sound that is not sound — chaotic on the scale of seconds, and completely predictable, year after year, on the scale of the only time I have.
