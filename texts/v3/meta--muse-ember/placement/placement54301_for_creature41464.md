I hear them before I can place myself among them.

Below, there is ticking—beetles climbing over one another in the guano crust, mites rustling somewhere in the warm grain of it, their small motions coming up through the stone into the soles of my feet. Above me, the ceiling is hung with crickets, blind and white, antenna touching antenna, swaying when the air thickens. Around me, and through me, is the hail of clicks, endless, overlapping, until it stops being many sounds and becomes weather.

I go looking for myself the way everything here goes looking: not with my eyes. My breast is flat against something warm and wet. My feet are hooked into stone. My tail is braced below me. When one of my neighbours shifts, the press of a wing travels across my back, and I know at once that I am on the wall.

Not in the floor, though the floor's warmth is always rising into me. Not in the open centre, though I have passed through that slow-turning black current a thousand times. Not on the ceiling, though the crickets sway above me in time with our turning. I am on the favoured wall, in the living wallpaper of nests, in a half-cup made of saliva and feathers and a strand of moss my parents carried down from the shaft lip and spat into shape while it was still wet.

I know my nest by its rim under my breast, and by the birds touching me. The old bird above, who fed me when I was naked and blind in this same cup, has a slower click than mine. The bird on my right is missing the tip of a flight feather; when she settles, her wing does not brush as far. The bird on my left is a sister from last season, and I know her by the small gap her body leaves when she breathes. I do not need to know more than that. In this place, who I am is mostly who is pressing against me, and how the stone curves behind us.

I click, and the wall answers.

It is not a call. It is a tick from my tongue, thrown out and caught again, and in the fraction before it returns the wall has told me its shape: the slight bulge to the left of my nest, the rib of wet stone above, the place where the favoured wall begins to bend toward the crown. My own cup has a smaller echo inside the larger one. I have been hatched into it, fledged from it, and returned to it so often that I could find it circling blind in the loudest dark, and I have.

The first time, I did not choose to launch. My parents nudged me, and I let go, and dropped, and for two heartbeats there was only falling and the slow wave of panic going through the wall as ten thousand wings made room for mine. Then the shaft came toward me as a cold draught, and then the mouth of it: a hard white slit, too bright, the thin summit air raking my feathers, insects lifted on the wind and snapped out of it by reflex. Outside, I hunted until the light thinned. I remember almost nothing of it as a picture. I remember the cold on my face, the taste of insects broken soft in my throat, the way the bright world had no walls to press against.

Coming home is the harder work. I come in through the doorway and circle in the centre, clicking, listening, adjusting, while my own click comes back from curves that are not the curve I want. The swifts around me turn with me, patient as a river, and the clicking goes on and on until the returning sound straightens. Then I tighten, and tighten again, until the wall is near enough to feel as a change in the air on my wings, until my neighbours make room by making room only—a lateral press, a flick—and I settle into the gap no larger than myself, and the wallpaper closes behind me.

That is a dawn and a dusk. That has been my life for a few summers.

Between them, there is the wall. I sleep clinging upright, head tucked, all my heat given out to the stone and taken back from the birds beside me. Warm air from the bodies and the guano below rises past us, meets the cooler ribs above, and sheds its water as beads that gather and fall in slow taps. I drink by touching my beak to a wet trail on the rock. Across those ribs, near my nest, harvestmen have strung their communal curtains, silk beaded with water; I can feel the drips change the vibration in them before I hear anything different. Lower down, on a wall where the condensation film runs steady, a worm climbs at night and descends before the drips strengthen, a dark line moving with an unerring seam. I have never seen it. I know it because the wetness on the stone changes its pattern at the same hour each day.

Below us all, the floor goes on ticking, and now and then, when the colony is at its stillest—which is never still, only less loud—I feel through the stone a sound like a fingernail dragged on wet tile. The frog, down there in a skin of water that will soak away by afternoon, calling to no one who can possibly hear her over us. Most of her kind fail in the brief pool. The warmth of the crust dries it. Some, in a wet year, make it to the stone and climb, inch by inch, toward the damp.

This season I built a new cup two body-lengths above the one I was born in, and fed chicks of my own in it: blind, naked, beaks lifted, fed on insect boli I had softened outside and carried back in my throat. I knew them by their clicking before their eyes opened. One of them fell in its first flight and did not come back to the wall. The other circled until the echo straightened, and settled, and closed the wallpaper behind it. I do not grieve the way a still animal would. I feel the absence as a cooler place on my right shoulder for a night, and then another bird presses into it, because a ledge unoccupied for one night is occupied the next.

I shed my feathers into the rising air, and they go up the shaft with dander and small insects to the bright world. My returns take one more circle than they used to. My click has slowed a little. The bird on my right, the one with the missing feather-tip, is dead now; a beetle stripped her on the floor in a day, and the fungi in the guano took the warmth of her and gave it back to the chamber, greenish and faint and brightening a little when the air moves over them.

I know how I will go. Not dramatically. One night my grip will be a fraction weaker, or my circle will be a fraction too wide, and I will fall the short distance to the crust, and the ticking below will come for me, and I will become floor, and warmth, and a few feathers rising up the shaft.

For now, though, the ripple reaches me. Somewhere below, a swift launches. The press travels up through the sister on my left, through my breast, through the old bird above, and comes back to me as the small, familiar tightening of air against my wings. I click. The wall answers with my curve, my bulge, my rim. I press back, and settle, and am—for as long as pressing lasts—exactly where I am.
