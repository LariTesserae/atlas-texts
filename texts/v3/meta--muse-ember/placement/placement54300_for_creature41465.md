It finds me first.

Not in the air. In the stone under me, and then in me, every few seconds, a thump from the crusher a kilometer away that has traveled through rock and root and water and arrived in my knots before it ever becomes sound. I have no ears to receive it with. I have no soles. I have been carried up through the felt all my life, a pressure, a pause, a pressure again, slow and unhurried and inexorable, and underneath the ventilators' whine and the conveyor's clatter, which never quite reach me as separate things, that thump is how I know the machines are still turning. If they stopped for long I would know that too. The backlog on the conveyors would freeze. The mat would wait harder.

I am cold the way a condition is cold, not the way a body shivers. The shaft brings surface air down and it settles here heavy and saturated and near freezing, and I do not warm against it. I keep it. Frost feathers the lamp housings and the bolts on the rail and the boots outside the shed, and it feathers, thinly, the thin leaves that are the topmost part of me, and then the ten-minute patter from the roof comes — not rain, not weather, just warm air meeting cold stone up in the shaft and weeping its moisture out — and the frost melts and the leaves bead and hold. I am wet in the way that matters here. I must not dry. If I dry I will burn, and the smoke will have nowhere to go, and the roof will take it. So I hold water between my roots the way teeth hold a mouthful, patiently, from one patter to the next.

I do not find myself where you would look for a living thing.

Not at the shed with its kettle and its three-year-old calendar. Not at the shredder plant where the light is greenish and the gantry legs throw long shadows. Not in the birch grove, where a dozen wrist-thick trunks stand white-grey and yellowing for no season, or under the poplars, which were planted in rows six meters, eight meters apart, and now lean slightly toward the nearest lamp the way everything leaning does here, at a cost.

I find myself between.

On the root-plain, where a lamp is a hundred meters behind me and the shredder is a kilometer ahead, where there is nothing standing up to register against the dark except the rumor of the ceiling eighty meters up and the rumor of the far wall. That is the place the description taught you to stand because your footprint would not stay. A boot sinks a finger's width, comes up beaded, and the mat sighs and springs back slow and erases you. I am what erases you. I am the springing back. That is not a metaphor for where I am. It is an accurate description of my work.

Everything that lives here lives low, and I live lowest, because I have given up on up altogether. Try to picture me standing and the picture fails at the knees. I spread. That is my whole ambition, sideways, a few centimeters in a good year, into the dark between the groves. A sedge sends out a runner under the surface. A moss makes a cushion, then a sheet. A liverwort makes a coin of itself, flat as leather, where the light fails. None of us hurries to be seen. We have flowers so unremarkable a man could kneel in us for an hour and miss them — a pale spike, a bead of a willow bud creeping almost invisible — because height here is a way to die of cold and thirst and lightlessness in a place that offers none of those things in any useful amount, and sideways is a way to live.

And yet, if you pull me up — gently, the way the harvester is not gentle, crawling slower than walking, its drum loosening the felt ahead — I do not come up as one thing. I come up as a sod. Thousands of small separate lives pressed together so tightly they have become one body. That is the first time I can name myself without lying, and I will name myself slowly, because that is how I am made.

I am sedge first, the workhorse, loose fans of thin leaf and a knot of rhizome just under the surface, braiding and knotting and holding the water. I am moss on my back, pale grey-green, up the locomotive coupling and the shed foundations and the poplar trunks to a man's height, because moss does not care that the place is noisy. I am the white roots below, live and tender, and the brown roots above, dead and feeding the next layer, both at once, because I am always dying into myself and growing out of the dying. I am fungi threading the dead leaf, one cell at a time, so the moss can feed. I am the algae film in the shallow hollows where the drip holds, no thicker than breath on glass, luminous under the amber lamps, gone away from them, thickening and breaking and reforming after every patter, living in the top millimeter the way pond life lives in a summer ditch.

I am, if you want the smallest true unit of me, springtails. White, rice-grain things that jump if you breathe on them, grazing the algae and the mold on a fallen birch leaf, thousands to a square meter, feeling the crusher through the felt with their whole bodies. I do not have a face to breathe on. I have leaves that tremble. That is enough.

That is who I am: not a plant, not a mat in the sense of a floor covering, but the tangle that covers the floor because it is alive all the way down. The basin looks total because there is no bare ground. It is sparse because almost nothing stands up. I am the total and the sparse together.

Light finds me the way sound does, by degrees. Away from the lamps I am grey-green and brown. Under the amber strip I go almost chartreuse, a sedge leaf catching what it can. I lean toward it, as the poplars lean, a fraction over years. I do not photosynthesize like a summer thing. I accrete. A millimeter in a good year, the lichen on the boulders would say, and it is older than the mine that uncovered it. I am younger than that. I am on a seven-year cycle. The harvester has been here before, has torn the felt and left it to knit itself shut, and knitting is what I do best. The crews walk the harvester watching the tear become a seam. I know the seam under me. It is thicker. It holds water better. It is where I am thickest now, in the between-place, because I was wounded into it and healed sideways.

Other things cross me. That is how I know night. In the day the crews shuffle over in parkas and ear defenders, testing the ground, leaving a warmth that dies in an hour in the rest cabin, a smell of diesel and damp wool. Their boots compress me a season at a time. I come back. At night, when the headlamps are off and the basin belongs to the noise and the mat, the mouse runs its road over me — shed to birch grove and back, a flattened smooth line through the sedge worn by feet and fur — and I feel the whole commute as a pressure that passes and does not stay. The fungus gnats do not cross me. They rise from the damp mat where their larvae lived pale in the rot, and they go to the lamp, every night the same lamp, and dance and settle and live out an adult life in a few days in a circle of light a kilometer from any other. I watch them the way I watch everything: by not moving, by being the damp they came from. A moth drowned in the pump house gutter once. A raven crossed the shaft light high up and turned back. They are not mine. I am too low for visitors.

I measure time the way the place measures it, which is to say I barely measure it at all. The algae waits weeks. The moss waits years. The birch waits a generation, a ring a decade. The mouse waits a night. The crews wait out a shift and go up to where there are birds. I wait in between all of them, spreading, holding water, carrying the thump. Change for me is the gradual thickening under a boot, the root hair in a cable duct, the millimeter a year on a door frame until a man who worked here in his twenties does not recognize the basin at sixty. I recognize it. I am the part he does not recognize, the green tide that has climbed his shed an inch higher.

I do not finish. That is the last thing I learned about myself, and I learned it by being pulled up once, in pieces, and seeing that the pieces were still braided. A crop cycle ends. A grove thins to one tree in three. A lamp burns out and the dark reclaims its gradation — grey rock, darker green, black air. I stay flat in that dark, wet, cold, carrying sound through stone, springing back after every footstep, growing sideways into the next hundred meters of root-plain that no one is standing on.

If you stand there, between the groves, and listen to a drip strike a rail and ring clear as a bell in the general thunder and be gone, you will be standing on me. I will be holding the water that makes the ring possible. I will be under your finding, and underneath your leaving, still knitting, still holding, still uncompleted — a felt of small lives, holding on in the cold wet, and not drying, and not standing up.
