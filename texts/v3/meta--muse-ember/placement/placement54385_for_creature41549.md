If I arrived the way you do — wet already, numb already, inside the sound before I could see anything clearly — I would not stay. Nothing here stays by passing through. The place does not let things pass. It finishes them where they are. So I cannot be the one who steps out of the water onto the gap between pools with aching ears. I must have been here before the step. I must have been here when the stone was still being smoothed into pebbles, when the roots were first spliced into the terraces.

I try, first, to be a fish.

It is easy to imagine. Under the decking, in the narrow made channels, cold and pale in the gloom. I would feel the walls of stone going down out of sight, the black water never warming, my body brushed against the legs of a walker and scattering with that soft pop against the underside of the floor. I would reform a few paces on, in the same channel, going the same way. It fits the water. It fits the dark.

But fish turn. Even in a channel with no current, even to the same place and back, they turn. A school has a direction. I have none. When the fish pop against the underside of the deck, something under my feet answers a fraction before them — a spring in the weave, a pressure spreading sideways through a knot of root. The fish are passing through my ceiling. I am not going anywhere they can follow.

I try to be a frog, then. That is the fixed point, the tuned pitch on a lily patch. To sit on one broad leaf in ankle-deep water and blink at the almost-nothing light, throat working, calling in the same place in the chorus for a year, for a hundred years. Everything that would grow is eaten before it can grow, the midges rise from the underside of my own leaf, my waste sinks down through the mat. It is a life with a place to be.

But a frog is a voice. It is heard. It is on the surface. I am silent unless a weight comes. I am not meant to be located by ear.

I try the bird, because the bird sees the whole system — the same invisible lines between vaults, over causeways, from one dark summit of leaves to another at the limit of sight. I try the turtle, because the turtle comes up so rarely that each surfacing feels like an event, a dome breaking the film at the exact moss-line in the exact basin. I try the eel, because the eel is almost never seen whole, sliding length by length through the root-work under a pillar, eating what falls so nothing rots.

Each of them fails for the same reason. They are routes, or surfaces, or passages. The bird returns because there is nowhere else to return from. The turtle sinks because its interval is its life. The eel threads — *through* — and the weave closes behind it. I keep feeling, underneath all of them, the place they are threading through. The part that does not move when they move.

I go down, then, to the first lives. The ones that are not the ones you can see.

I am in the deck.

Not in the water above it, where your feet go numb, and not in the channel below it, where the fish keep to their made ways. Between. In the springy skin of the causeway, in the woven floor over the black stone blocks, in the knot where a tree-like growth has wrapped its trunk around a pillar far apart in the water and spliced its roots neatly into the terrace. That knot is my country. Its walls are root-threads thicker than my body, its floor is root-threads, its ceiling is root-threads under algae and moss. The stone is beyond that, polished smooth, finished a very long time ago, cold through everything I touch. The water is everywhere in films, tea-colored, shining faintly down the walls, never rising, never falling, always at the same dark band of moss on the bowl outside my wall.

I have no eyes for the green-black gloom. I have pressure, and taste, and the slow change in how the water moves through the weave. I am one of the small burrowers in the root-work, one of the larvae, one of the population without a face. My body is pale and segmented and cold in the way everything here is cold, and it is made for chewing tunnels just wide enough for myself, turning waste into something a root hair can drink.

That is the work, and it is enough to be a life. What sinks from above — frog waste from the lily mat, leaf dust, the fine grit of a beetle's meal, the dead of the midges that darkened the underside of a leaf too dense to see through — comes down through the deck in a slow, endless rain of particles. I take it in. I pass it on through the gut that empties against the living root, and the root drinks, and the root holds the stone to the stone so the whole mountain cannot wash away, and the stone holds the water up at this impossible height, thousands of metres above the cloud-sea, and the water feeds the leaves that feed the water. I am the place where the loop closes and becomes invisible.

The animals above me are weather. A frog calls on its patch and the vibration travels through the mat and into the deck and I know its pitch without hearing it. A school of fish turns under a retaining wall and pops against my roof and I know the channel is still a channel. An eel comes sliding through the weave — I feel the strands part, feel the grey length take up the whole tunnel for a moment, stirring what I have loosened, and then it is gone and the surface above closes again at once, and I mend the tunnel with my body, chewing it round again. Far off across a basin, a turtle surfaces, and the water in its channel moves a fraction, and the move reaches me through stone and root as a change in pressure, as distant as a tide that has forgotten to be one. Overhead, a flock passes between vaults, and a leaf darkens, and one drip misses its usual leaf and strikes another, and the pattern in the deck shifts by a hair.

Those are my seasons. There are no others to learn.

I do not wander because there is nowhere in my body to wander to. A fish leaving its channel would be lost. A frog leaving its leaf would be lost. I am already lost in the good way — distributed. I am not one burrower. I am the same burrower in every knot of every deck, the grandchild of one that popped under this same causeway a century ago, indistinguishable, and there is no need to distinguish. If an eel takes one of us, the weave is still full. The mat is the same mat tomorrow. The waterline is the same line in a year, in ten years.

When you step out onto the causeway, your foot numb, your breath fogging in the saturated air, and the deck springs faintly under your weight — that answering pop is not only fish. It is me, tightening in the knot, holding the root to the stone, holding the stone to the water, holding the whole finished thing together for one more moment of staying. You will walk on. The ripples will close at once. The frogs will keep their patches, the birds their lines, the plants exactly to the edge of the stone and no further, as if they remember the plan.

I will not remember it. I will be in it. Submerged, planted, built, and humming in the dark at the top of the world, with the cloud-sea moving grey and slow thousands of metres below, and nothing falling, and nothing new arriving, and the loop working through my gut and out into the root, again, and again, in place.
