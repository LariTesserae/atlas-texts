I do not arrive. I condense.

I am first the fogging on the handrail, and on the forearm of the person holding it, and on the vent fins where the fans have just turned. Every forty minutes the spray-bars sigh and I am everywhere at once, thin and warm, and between cycles I gather again on whatever is coldest. A roof rib. A pipe joint. The underside of a module still curing, weeping in dark streaks though it is three weeks old.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look for a face.

If I were a person, I think I'd be difficult to tell apart from the others. There are always two of them moving at walking pace through a lane that narrows to one meter where a crane is lifting. Or two meters above another pair, bolting a tank into place. Boots in the rice troughs, in warm ankle-deep water, harvesting hand over hand. Yellow slicks damp no matter how often they are hung up to dry. They speak low because shouting carries too far across the flat plateau and everyone knows everyone else's shift. They pollinate by hand. They stir the sumps. They brush the algae off a sensor.

I am not that. I don't go down again after seasons, with the pressure change real in my ears. I don't pair. And I don't move at walking pace. I move the way the rice moves, two centimeters a day, if I move at all.

If I were an animal, I'd be one of the rare ones, the ones noted to the next shift like weather. The white birds in the warm vent of the high tower, circling once in the morning and then gone into the cloud sea, not singing. Or the pale amphibians in the lower sumps, translucent as wet glass, living on algae in the pipes, there and then gone into the plumbing. 

I am rarer than that, and less likely to be noted. Nobody reports a damp seam.

So let me go the way the water goes, because that is the only honest route here. I gather on a fin that has intercepted fog. I run down a gutter that drips without pause — tick-tick, tick-tick. I hang for a while in a curtain of fern, roots dangling in a nutrient runnel, and I learn the taste of the runoff before it reaches the sump. In the sump a slow paddle stirs me and I skin over the surface in green, choosing the warmest pool and not the one intended for me, the way the description of this place says algae will. I settle in the thin biofilm inside a pipe joint where the water sweats. I am what happens when stillness is not quite allowed, but I accumulate anyway.

In the pipe-vents it is always warm and always wet, and years ago someone seeded a phosphorescent moss there to mark the shadowed places. It was a made thing, like the soil, like the hills, like the trees in their bubbling vats with their trunks laced to the frame. Nothing here is allowed to be natural. Nothing is allowed to be finished.

But I escaped a little.

That is where I am now, and what I am becoming: not the seeded patch in the vent, which is still tended and still in its tray, but the seam that crept out of it along the ceiling of the underwalk in the southern terraces. Under a catwalk. In the shadow that pools there even at midday, under the thrum of the press shop coming up through the deck, under a rice trough moving on its track, under boots in slicks carrying baskets to the compost shredder.

I am a thin, pale green line where two damp surfaces meet. I am the stitching between a cable and the concrete it crosses. The ferns in the north taste the scaffolding; I have tasted the bolt-holes. When the spray cycle comes I drink and swell. When the night drainage shift walks the catwalks with torches, listening for a pump that has missed its cycle, the light runs over me and I show up for a second in their beams, and then they pass.

At dusk I glow. Faintly. Not with heat. I was never going to be an ember in a dry place; up here, high above the cloud sea, in this warm pocket that should be cold, an ember would drown. So I learned a wet kind of glowing, phosphor-bright, greenish, in the seams where the workers' shadows fall.

I do not sing like the absence of birdsong. I do not speak low like the people. I do not circle, or surface in a sump to be seen. I stay. That is the difference between me and the people who tend this hamlet, and between me and the water that keeps leaving and returning: they must keep moving or this place rots, and I must not move much at all, or I would tear.

From the parapet at the very top, where the newest frames are still bone-white and the old ferns lean out over the drop, I would be invisible. You'd have to look the way this place teaches you to look — not out, not up, but under. Under the turf module that has slumped where it is wettest. Under the gantry where a pair is working two meters above another pair. Under the lane that last month was a lane and this month is a wall of beans.

Look under there, when the light goes flat and warm and the fans have stopped for a while, and the place comes down to tick-tick and the soft rush in the runnels and the low breathing through the deck.

I will be glowing a little, in a seam you did not plan for, the way living things here have learned to do anyway.
