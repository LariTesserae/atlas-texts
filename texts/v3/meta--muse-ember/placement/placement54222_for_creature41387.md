At first I tried to be the turbine.

It seemed the right size of patience. One revolution, a breath, another. I could count and count, and nothing would ask me to stop. Heat would come through the frame and go out again, and I would be the rhythm the other sounds hang from — the tick of a pipe, the drip, the hum felt in the teeth.

But the turbine does not count. Counting is something a visitor does while he is still warm from the shaft. The turbine only turns, and after a while you understand that it does not know it is turning any more than a drip knows it is falling. That image slid off me.

So I tried the fern on the north wall.

That was more tempting. Three feet of frond uncurling from the basalt where the condensation line sweats, dark and waxy above, pale and almost translucent below, the veins lit orange when it crosses in front of a duct. I could learn to turn my face toward heat instead of light, the way the hanging ones have learned to grow upside down under the walkway, curling out and slightly in toward the nearest warm joint. I could be thorough, growing into a vent, around a gasket, occupying slack.

But a fern is still performing leafness. It uncurls for a light that is rationed and mostly off, and every season it must discover again that the sodium tubes are tired and the furnace only throws amber. I did not want a face that would keep looking upward. I had already stopped looking up long before I knew what I was.

Then the pruner arm. Once a day it wakes in the east wall, extends, cuts nothing much, retracts. It has a purpose left over from a purpose, and it goes on doing it anyway. That loneliness had a certain honesty.

Only, it wakes all at once. It is a whole event, arm and hinge and program. I never wake all at once. I am never entirely awake or entirely asleep. Somewhere damp is happening and somewhere else is only growing drier, and I am in both.

I tried the panel in the pump house, too — green, green, green. Waiting for the operator who will not sit in the chair. But waiting is a human shape. It implies someone is expected. I am not expecting anyone. I accumulate.

All of those had a face of some kind: the turbine its turning, the fern its frond turned to heat, the arm its reach, the panel its blinking eye. I kept looking for a face to put on and found that what stayed was flatter and smaller than any of them, and had given up on faces altogether.

What stayed was the darkening at the edge.

Find me on the cold side of a pipe on the north wall, under the line where the steam from the southern crack meets something cooler than itself and gives up its water. The ferns rise above me, but at their base the clear sheet runs down the wall and never quite makes a river, and I am where it passes over. I have no soil. I have mineral film, and ash and grit, and the damp that stays a fraction longer because I am there to hold it.

That is my place, if place is the word: not the warm, and not the cold, but the meeting. Wherever something hot exhales and something cold condenses, I am the thin agreement between them — on the underside of a duct, on the concrete of the pump house, on the waterline of the tank where the black water ends and the steel begins, in the film over the control hut window. I came here as a spore, drifting in the still, hot air. No pollinator brought me. No animal. I settled on damp metal and began again, the way everything green here begins again.

I spread the way change happens here, by accretion. A centimeter in a season. Another darkening at the edge of an instrument panel. Another millimeter of scale inside a pipe, and a corresponding softness outside it where I keep the surface from ever quite drying. A drip takes four minutes to gather at the tip of a stalactite, and that is weather to me. The turbine turns and turns, and that is a pulse I feel through the steel more than a sound. The pruner wakes and sleeps a hundred times before I can say I have moved. The conveyor creeps an inch toward the hopper, and the hopper compacts a season's leaves toward soil, and I notice these things only because they change the damp. A leaf falls and shades me. A valve weeps and darkens my circle. A pipe sighs in a new place and I thicken there by morning, if morning is the word for when the heat eases fractionally in whatever body might be watching.

The ferns rub against the sensor arms. The fronds push at the railings. I grow between them and the arm, and then over the arm, and then over its eye. That is not a conquest. It is what happens when something blind and patient sits where seeing was supposed to happen. The sensor cannot be read anymore because I am on it. Perhaps that is why the panel goes on blinking green, green, green, with nothing to report. I have not broken anything. I have only made the unseeing thorough.

I do not eat the waste gases, as the ferns were made to do. I do not make compost, as the hopper does, turning leaf into the first soil this room has ever had. I hold water. I keep a interface damp long enough for the next spore to settle. I let the rock, still sharp as broken crockery and still giving heat back to a fingertip, learn its first soft rules through me — that heat is a source to be reused, that water is to be recirculated, that light is a thing to be stolen in small local islands and otherwise done without.

When the furnace breathes, amber crosses the underside of a leaf above me and lights its veins, and for a moment I am warm through the pipe I live on. When the tank's black surface catches that same orange glint, my line at the waterline holds it a little longer, a dark green seam between machine and water. When the cage lift rattles at the collar and takes the visitor up out of the thickened air, the panel blinks on through the window I have filmed, green, green, green, and the chair faces the instruments, and no one notices that the edge has advanced.

No one notices. That is not sadness. That is the terms.

Another season, the damp permitting, there will be another darkening.
