I am where the street is lowest.

Not in a house. Not on a roof that is the next street. Not in the granary, dry on its high ground, above the seep. I am in the groove cut into the stone at the feet of anyone who passes, in the finger-wide stream that runs there because the lowest line is always a channel. You would have to walk sideways in the alley, with your pack against your shoulders, and then look down farther than habit tells you, past the toe of a bound shoe testing the slick rock, to see me at all. Even then you would likely take me for a piece of grit the morning clearing has missed.

I live under a covered passage. It runs for a long way — people count it in hundreds of metres, I count it in drips — damp and cool and completely silent except for water. Above me is a floor. Above that a room, or the memory of a room. The stone over my head is someone's threshold, someone's packed earth, someone's wall leaning a degree into the soil and holding. From down here the city is not buildings. It is a roof that sweats, a floor that weeps, and a thread of water between them that never quite stops.

Every few seconds, somewhere above and not far, a drip falls from a spout into a cistern and echoes under a vault. I do not hear it the way you would. The sound dies in mist and thick wall before it reaches a human ear; it reaches me as a tremor through the stone and through the case I drag. Drip. Stone ticks as it cools. Drip. The channel at my feet brightens. Drip. The tremor goes through my legs and up into the grains cemented to my back, and for a moment everything I am carrying hums against the rock.

For I do carry it. That is the first thing to know about me, if you will be patient enough to let it become clear. I am not bare to the water. I built a house out of the plateau, grain by grain, spinning a silk from my own body to hold it together, and I drag it behind me wherever the water lets me go. The front of it is pale limestone, because I have spent this spring in the upper groove where the cut stone is pale. The back is dark basalt, gritty and black with peat stain, from the pool I overwintered in two terraces up. If you looked closely — and you would have to look very closely, and crouch, and not be walking at a human pace — you might call me a larva, a case-bearer. I would not use the word. I know myself by weight, and by the scrape of stone on stone when I move.

I move when the water moves correctly, which is to say, almost always, and never fast. At dawn the whole plateau sweats. Cold stone meets air a little less cold, and a film forms across every wall and lintel, a skin of water a hair thick. Above my channel the film gathers on the west-facing side first and runs, slowly, in threads that spill over the terrace edge. I climb a little way up toward it, not far, gripping with my front legs, releasing, gripping, the feathered gills along my belly pulsing in the clear cold. The film is full of things smaller than me, and of algae painting the rock green and black and dull rust. I scrape. A mouthful here takes the time a keeper takes to lift an arm in heavy wet wool. The stone under me is never dry. Neither am I.

By noon the thin sun warms the upper surfaces, and the film thins and runs off. I go back down, following the thread, into the groove where the water is a finger wide and braided with silt. This is my morning's walk, and it is also, on some days, my whole season's ambition: from the wall a little way down, from the upper channel to the lower hollow, from one cistern's slow sinking to another's. A pool here does not evaporate. It sinks, almost imperceptibly, through the soil that sits just under the stone, and re-emerges two terraces down. I sink with it, inch by inch, a little lower every year, carrying my layered case like a small, portable foundation.

The keepers do not see me, and that is not a complaint. They are not meant to. They come in the morning, when the water table has risen in spring and a channel not cleared will silt up by afternoon. I feel them long before I see them: the shush of cloth far away, damp wool rustling in a passage too narrow for shoulders. A toe tests the rock for purchase, then another. The vibration travels through the groove and into my case, and I do what I have always done when a slab shifts above me. I stop. I fold my legs under me. I become a stone, because that is what I have spent my life pretending to be, and because a fall here would not be a fall onto soft ground.

A hand comes down. Broad, slow, chapped from stone-dust and cold, nails cut short. It holds a small stone of its own, for prying out grit. It works the groove from the top of the passage toward me, lifting a pavestone the frost heaved in January, laying it back sleek. Grit clouds the water. My gills fold against the silt. The hand scrapes, pauses — and touches my case.

For a moment I am lifted out of the stream altogether and set on the damp upper ledge of the groove, in air that is almost dry, in light that comes down the passage in a grey column. I am aware of the keeper mostly as weight and weather: the dark wool hanging beside me, never dry, patched at the hem with darker cloth; the bent head turned slightly aside; a low word spoken across the courtyard well to another figure I cannot see, the phrase ending before the echo can return. The hand returns to the channel. It does not return me quite where it found me. It sets me down one groove-width lower, in cleaner water, and moves on.

That is how I travel, more often than I care to admit. They keep the water moving correctly. In doing so, without meaning to, they move me.

A soft wing-rustle. An alpine chough slipping out of its niche into the mist, black with a dull red beak, silent as everything else here. It does not look at me. Nothing here looks at me, except the sun, and the sun at noon is ordinary and thin and it warms the upper surfaces without warming me.

On the slab just above my groove, a lizard has flattened itself to take what warmth there is. Dark from saturation. Motionless for an hour, breathing barely visibly. I know it is still there because the stone under my case gives off, very slowly, a stored day. The lizard does not know I am there. We are both of us waiting for the same thin light, at different heights, and neither of us is in any hurry. If you were walking now you would pass us both. You would see the knee of the passage, the thread of water, the glint on wet stone. You would not see the lizard, and you would not see me. That is correct. We are not the part of the city that passes.

In the afternoon the mist rolls through at walking pace. It clears the courtyard and then fills it again. Sound, already at almost nothing, goes lower. I go back to work on the algae in the groove, scraping the green-black-rust film from the stone inside my little reach, dragging my case forward by a grain or two with each grip. Behind me the channel bed is sorted, not by any plan I can know, but because the water sorts it: what is too heavy stays, what is too light goes, what is pale limestone and fits my hand I take and cement to the front of my house. Over a winter the front advances. Over a spring the back darkens. My case is a record of where the water has been, the way the houses stacked above me are records of foundations older than their walls.

At dusk the temperature drops another few degrees and the condensation returns. First on the high west-facing walls, then everywhere. The streams in the streets sound a little louder in the cold, which means the tremor comes more often through my stone. The city that in daylight looked pale and patterned becomes uniformly dark as it re-wets. There are no lights. In the niches where jars should sit and lamps are never lit, nothing brightens. I climb a little way up again toward the fresh film on the wall above the groove, toward the damp that will be my food and my drink and my road. Halfway up, the stone ticks as it cools after sun. I feel each tick in my back.

Night is when the keepers are inside, motionless, in ground floors sunk half below the street, listening to the streets sound louder. I am outside, in the water that does not stop simply because no one is watching it. In the cisterns and peat-stained pools there are things smaller than me pulsing, and larger things — frogs' spawn in spring, blackening and then clear — that I will never meet. In winter, when many of the keepers have left the plateau altogether, down the weather-softened steps to lower country where wood is plentiful and stone dries, I do not leave. I go down into the pool that sinks slowest, under a slab the frost lifts and the October rain lays back, and I wait in water so cold it barely moves. The plateau creeps above me toward its edges, carrying walls with it. I creep toward its edges too, a few grains of silt at a time.

Spring comes, and the water table rises and comes up through floors. The keepers return, because spring is when a channel not cleared in the morning will silt by afternoon. The hands come back down the passage, broad and chapped, the wool damp to the colour of wet stone. The channel is cleared again. I am lifted again, perhaps. I am set down again, a little lower. This time the front of my case is pale again, new limestone cemented over the dark basalt of last year, and the pool behind me is two terraces down from where I started, and neither of us — not the keeper, not I — would call that a journey. It is just keeping on, at the rate everything here keeps on: a fingernail a year, a millimetre of soil, an inch of cushion plant in a cool summer set back by wind in an hour, lichen painting a doorway another centimetre.

I am slower than the lichen in some ways and faster in others. The lichen will be there long after the last channel-clearer has left for the winter, painting over a handprint worn smooth in plaster if no one wipes it. The people will be there long after I have broken apart and my grains have been sorted back into the bed of the channel, to be lifted by some keeper's hand in some spring and put by, unknowing, as grit. I am between them. I do not dominate the place. I am not loud in it, or fast in it, or, most days, visibly in it.

Tonight the west wall will sweat first. The film will form, a hair thick, and run in threads over the terrace edge, and the groove at the lowest line of the street will fill and clear and fill again. I will be there, low and damp and carrying the city behind me, a little lower than I was this morning.
