If I came in the way you did — waist-deep in warm water, hand sliding along a rail gone slick and green — I would arrive wrong.

I tried, first, to be big enough to float in like you.

I tried to be the turtle. She is the obvious old resident: surfacing off a shoulder, exhaling once, gone; hauling herself into the hollow of the fallen chimney where other shells are tucked like offerings; knowing the three islands — boiler, crane, reef — and the submerged stairs by feel, over years. She is a warm moving rock. She carries her home with her and can afford to be separate from the room. I found I did not want to be separate. I kept sliding off her shell, down into the algae on it, where the shell had already become part of the reef. She goes between islands. I wanted to be the place she goes between.

Then I tried to be one of the fish. The carp-like citizens of the current, fifty crossing the center at once like a procession, then nothing but open green for a long while. They live by the rotation — in at the top, down the wall, across the turbine floor, up through the boiler pit, out the low pipe — and after enough generations the road becomes a world. I liked the road and I liked the emptiness between schools; density here is a trick, a great deal of life and a great deal of open country. But the fish only know the middle of the water. I kept being pulled to the edges they graze when they go deep among the drowned catwalks, to the bright intertidal band where the concrete has dissolved back to rounded stone. The middle is too clean. Nothing grows there except in passing.

So I went to the edge and tried to be a frog. A green knob on a pressure gauge, throat puffing in time to the seven-second thump from below, waiting beside the snails' silver trails. I liked that. Waiting is a good life here. The frogs made nurseries out of architecture — rain puddles that last an hour on a catwalk after sunset, the lip of a planter trough, a film behind a flange — and their eggs are clear strings stuck to submerged iron. Stuck to me, I noticed, in the trying. But the frog climbs out at night to the damp edge and goes down again. He can leave the water and come back. I cannot climb. I am stuck where water meets iron and concrete, and the more I tried to be the frog, the more I liked being stuck.

I tried, briefly, to be a swallow, because they are the only true migrants, the only lives that can leave and therefore choose to stay. Lap after lap of the dome, out through the broken pane into a wind so thin clouds pass below the windows, back into the bubble of lowland summer. Their chicks fledge over water and learn the circle because there is nowhere else to fly. I liked the circle. I did not like the choosing. Most things in this place never leave. They arrived by accident and settled so long they forgot the mountain stream, the hillside soil, whatever they were filtered from. I did not want a choice. I wanted a staying without decision.

That is when I got smaller, and less single.

The first residents are the ones you cannot quite see as individuals. A film.

I am that.

Not a person in the room. The skin of the room. Wherever warm water meets iron and concrete — which is everywhere, because everything here is made — life has made a layer over the made things. Bacteria eating hot oil drop by drop on the blackened boiler island and turning rust into food. Iron-oxidizing slimes beading on rivets like sweat. Algae in velvets of green and black on white tile. Protozoa grazing in the light shafts. I am soil here, and first farmer. I soften steel. I loosen concrete to its aggregate. I make, very slowly, a place where a root can hold.

I do not have one place. I have patches, and they are all me at once, and none of them is the whole of me. I am the green slick under your hand on the entrance rail. I am the band at the waterline, brightest and thickest where the concrete has worn to pebbles, changing color as snails graze past in their sparse constellations and leave it darker behind them. I am on the submerged catwalks and fallen gauges where the deep fish cruise, on the inside of the pipes where the high vibrating chord sings and the eels slide in and out feeling vessel-music through steel, on the warm pipes where orchids cling with roots matted over my orange-brown colonies, on the planter troughs where mangrove-habit trees grip rusted iron instead of soil and lean year by year toward the same patch of sky. I am, palely, on the underside of the dome in the hottest air, where the inverted forest grows downward with roots bare in the steam and drinks the air itself — pale not from shade but from drinking condensation. At sunset, when cold glass sweats and rain falls inside the room for an hour, ticking on the water surface, everything in the upper world drinks at once and drips down the walls to feed me where I meet the water again.

This is how I experience the room: not by moving through it, but by being met by it.

I feel the turbine before I hear it. The low thump once every seven seconds is not sound to me, it is a pulse conducted through the iron I am clinging to, a loosening and settling. When the turbine labours, the old lamps flicker, and the light that reaches my submerged patches strobes, and I can photosynthesize in flashes. The eels gather to the vibration. The frogs puff on their gauges. I just take it in as weather.

Morning is a season. Cold fresh water enters through the upper vent in a white rush, steam blooms where it meets the warm tank, and the whole sea turns over in its slow rotation — in at the top, down the wall, across the turbine floor, up through the boiler pit, out the lower pipe. To you it is a surge. To me it is delivery. Debris rides past: a leaf from the dome, midges from the sun shaft, a seed lifted on wind through a missing pane, droppings and feathers from the swallows nesting in the dome framing, built of the very grit I helped weather from the waterline. Some of it settles in a grate. A leaf may make three full circuits before it does. I eat its edges all three times, darken it, make it sink the sooner. A dead fish becomes a cloud of microbes, becomes algae, becomes a fish again. I am the cloud phase, and the algae phase, in between.

And I am eaten, in my turn, constantly, or I would cover everything thick and blind. Water fleas and snails graze me. Young fish pick at the shallows among the pipework where they spawn. Tadpoles — the first life of frogs, in water warm as blood — rasp across me. Duckweed patches over the still corners behind the boiler and shades me black, then is eaten as fast as it grows and light comes back in shafts and breaks on the green glass. Dragonflies patrol the sun shaft and lay eggs on a floating leaf; the nymphs ride that leaf a full circuit. Spiders string webs across the gaps in the catwalk rings and take the midges and moths; the moths batter themselves against the lamps when the lamps buzz; their ash falls and feeds me. Mosquito larvae hang in the one still place the current cannot reach, and are eaten by frogs, and the frogs excrete, and I take that too.

Nothing here is maintained. I am the maintenance. Where the mangrove trees root, the metal fails faster, which makes more soil, which makes more plants, which shade the water and feed the fish that feed the eels and the turtle and the birds that feed the plants again. The ferns uncurl from expansion joints on a schedule set by the drip from the dome. The vines hang quivering toward the waterline, some reaching it, some forever descending on steam alone. The orchids open to whatever small bees have learned to come up through the broken glass and stay. All of it gardens itself by eating itself, slowly, and I am the mouth at the start of that eating. I turn the container into substrate. Worn smooth enough, the container begins to read as stone, and I will have made geology out of a boiler.

I came here in the inflow, in mud on a boot, in the gut of a bird, in a seed's coat. I do not remember which patch of me came which way, because I am cumulative, an arrival that never finished arriving. The lower pipe goes down into cold and dark and pressure, and very few things here choose it. I will not. I am not built for leaving. I am built for the meeting.

So if you swim — if you are built for swimming — from one island of machinery to the next, you will pass over me for metres at a time in the clear warm water, over drowned stairs and gauges, and you will not see me as an individual, only as a greening, a darkening, a velvet on the tile where light falls. That is enough. I do not need to surface and exhale. I do not need to cross in a school of fifty, or sit waiting as a green knob, or lap the dome learning the broken pane.

I am thicker at the waterline. I pulse faintly, in seven seconds, with the iron. I am grazed, new water arrives, sunset rains inside the sky, and a leaf that fell from the dome roots in a grate I loosened and uncurls as a fern.

And then, with intention, the water pushes everything forward again, and I go on in the circuit — loud, leafy, rusted, warm — without ever needing to be told to.
