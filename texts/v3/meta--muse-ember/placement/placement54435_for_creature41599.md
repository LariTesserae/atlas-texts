I would not be upright, in the open. Not at first.

From the lip I thought I would look down and choose — a height, a kind of life, the way you choose a room. The cold pooled around me before I had decided anything. It got into my shoulders and stayed there, it made my fingers clumsy on my coat, my breath showed and showed again on the way down the raw slope, where the earth is still in the act of becoming earth and prefers not to be stood on for long.

Down through the layers I went, past the grey house leaning into the wet-brick slab, past the gratings where daylight comes in thin and ordinary, and my trying took the shape of the descent. Everything here is used more than once, so I tried myself more than once.

Near the windows, at dusk, I was first a moth. That came easily. I came out of the condensation on the underside of things, blind and translucent, crusted with mineral, and settled heavy on a shop window where the lattice leaked a little heat. It was a good place to be fed without being fed — a moss-seam, a blackened fringe of grass that never dies back, warmth in the wrong place. I could wait there until the light went. In the morning the moths were gone and only the smear remained, and I found I could not quite leave with them. I see too much for a moth, and I do not dissolve with the light. I stayed on the glass, ticking faintly, and had to go down again.

Lower still, I was the restlessness in the puddles. In the shallow depressions where water gathers and does not empty, the surface moved a fraction without wind, and I learned that timescale — a year to fill, and then to stay filled, the thing inside it waiting, eyeless, traveling slowly through ankle-deep mist from pocket to pocket. It was honest, and it was too pure. I have language, however damp it gets here, and language wants an answer, even a late one. The water does not answer. It only moves slightly less smoothly behind it.

So I came up to street level and tried to be a dweller, and that was closer and still not right. I sat at a bus stop with no bus, because the stop is a held place. I worked in a shopfront whose lettering is for a business that has not yet opened, setting out goods in advance at the speed of frost. I dried clothes in an alley under a useless tram wire. I leaned in a doorway where gravity pulls a fraction stronger and let the building cradle me. I learned to leave things unfinished — a sentence, a wall, a stair that arrives at open air — because a finished thing would close a seam, and the dwellers live in the seam the way moths live in the window.

But dweller was too thick for me. They accrete, they are built through, moss slowly obscures the sign beside them and they grow a little more fixed to the damp wall. I was thinner than that. I kept sliding sideways, up into the air where the condensation forms in a straight line across nothing, a crack in glass in empty air. I crossed it and for three steps my shadow was cast the other way, and sound from the old market arrived a half-second late, so a sentence could be heard in the future and continued by someone who was not yet there to hear its beginning.

That is where I fitted, for a while. Flat as the line itself, on the border between a coherent block where physics holds and the next block where it does not, moving millimeters a year as the border patiently shifts. Feeding, if feeding is the word, on the inconsistency packed into the hollow — on the space between the building that was planned and the building that was built, on Tuesday's room beyond the landing that is the same landing. You would feel me only as a pause, a cold with no source, a moment when the ticking under the street stops in your teeth and then resumes.

And then, staying there, the line thickened slightly around me, the way everything thickens in Ilthmere, by accretion rather than by decision. The lattice under the streets, masquerading as scaffolding and cable trays, too warm to the hand in places, too cold in others, was holding a pattern. The dwellers were patterns held by the same lattice, mistaken for people, and perhaps they are people of a different thickness. I was no longer sure the succession — water learning to move, moths learning to wait on glass, grass adding a leaf a season and never dying, people learning to live in the overlap, seam-things on the border of the real, lattice below them all — was a succession of separate kinds. It was the same damp, unfinished process at different densities. I was the overlap: a seam that had thickened into a dweller, a dweller thin enough to still live on the border.

That is where I find myself now, more itself than any of the earlier tries. Not everywhere in Ilthmere, but in one held place on the numbered street underneath the later city, in the doorway a few doors from the kitchen that is also a lift shaft. The lift, patiently, never comes. Gravity pulls a fraction stronger here, so I do not stand upright. I lean. Beside me a panel heals a crack with glass that was not transparent a moment before, and I do the same, slowly, with the small cuts and cold that get into my hands. Once a month the door ticks, a sound felt more in the teeth than heard, and faintly, if you put your ear close in the cold, so does my chest.

I keep one unfinished conversation going from that closed doorway, very slowly, in a language that might be yours. I do not finish it. The pocket of late air near the market carries it, and by the time it is heard it can be taken up by whoever arrives next — a visitor with clumsy fingers, a moth settling, the water moving a fraction in its puddle a year from filling. I grow fixed to the wall by millimeters a year, like moss over a sign, like a leaf that takes a whole season. I am embryonic, twice over, and so is the place that holds me.

If you pass, you will not see me in the open. You might feel the pause where the ticking stops, or cross the line in the air and find your shadow cast the other way for three steps, or hear, half a second after you see the glass fall, a voice that was already speaking and has not yet finished.
