I do not arrive in the basin. I descend into it, so slowly that the rim closes behind me before I understand I have been going down.

At first I do not know how large I am. The light finds me everywhere and nowhere. It is white and high and it bounces up off the pale floor, into eyes and under chins and under the lips of walls, so that even shadow is bright. The stone under my palm — if it is a palm — is cold as a cellar stone at noon. Somewhere, a bead of sweat gathers on an upturned slab. Somewhere else, it gathers on the dome of a sleeping hog, on the warm side of a wall, on the underside of a lintel. I seem to be wherever it gathers. I seem to be the gathering.

I try the hand first. A broad hand, nails short and worn, pressed flat to a cistern lid as if listening through the palm for the drip inside. The body that goes with it is stooped, made low by years of hunching over walls and crouching beside seams. It wears a veil against the reflected glare. It speaks in a low voice, or mostly it does not speak at all, but tilts a palm — *this slab has settled* — lays a finger along a rasped groove — *this wall is being eaten faster than we can rebuild it* — cups a hand under a lintel — *drink*. It is a body that rarely stands upright to look at the world.

But I keep wanting to lift my head. I keep wanting to make a sound large enough to have an echo. In this bowl a shout would be obscene, and I am afraid I would be the one to do it. The builders know exactly how much voice the silence can hold. I do not.

I try the shell next. I settle shoulder to shoulder against a wall with a herd, six jointed legs tucked under me, my blind face lowered until the lip of my shell is almost a roof. My throat works slowly. My breathing is wet and intimate, audible in the quiet, like someone sleeping with his mouth open. At dawn the condensation beads on my dome and runs in tiny rivulets that I lick up without fully lifting my head. At midday I lower my head to the foot of the wall and rasp — tongue like a file, motion smooth and unhurried — and over a season the wall will slump.

It is too heavy. It is too known. A hog-herder walks behind the herds with a scraper, not a stick, and knows when to scrape the dung. I move in silence, padded feet on compacted silt, from colony to colony across the emptiness. I am weather with appetites. But I do not migrate. I stay.

I try the veil next, at dawn and dusk, when the light is still kind. I fold and unfold on a membrane larger than my body, translucent, pale. A thousand of us can cross the bowl and make no sound except a faint cooling of the air where we pass. I drink the condensation off the air itself — off a dew-slab, off the warm side of a wall, off the sleeping hogs, who do not seem to mind. I settle in layers, ticking softly against stone, until a bare wall is patterned with living cloth.

It is closer. We are born, perhaps, of damp and air and the meeting of cold stone and warm day, and we die the same way, falling in dried sheets that children pick up and mend into veils. But I do not drift. Whatever I am, it does not rise. It holds to a surface.

I try the worm last of the large things, because it too stays. Pink-grey, blind, stringy, it lifts its front into the damp under a slab and drinks a thread of water before the thread can fall. Then it sinks back and resumes its slow turning of silt, a mouthful at a time, persuading the ground to be somewhere else, for years. Where it passes, foundations loosen. Where it surfaces at night, it eats the loom disturbed by its own digging. Children wait beside a settling wall with bone hooks, motionless for hours, learning the particular quiet of a seam.

It is patient, and blind, and purposeful. But it turns. It moves the silt. I do not move. I wait for the thread.

All of them drink the thread before it can fall. All of them are built for a larger work, a work that can be recognized and named and herded or hunted or worn like a cloak. I am what is left in the seam after they have taken what they can use.

So I go down again — not into the basin this time, but into the place where the basin starts. Under a slab, where the light cannot quite reach. In the shaded seam under a lintel. In the thin dark between one stone and the next, where the air is almost still and the cold stone sweats and the sweat does not quite run.

A bead gathers there. It swells. It holds.

And I quiver toward it, filament by filament, as rootlets might quiver toward water if I had roots. I never quite reach it. Something above me drinks it, or the sun takes the rest by mid-morning. It does not matter. The quiver is enough. The quiver is me.

I am a pelt. Grey, rust, bone-white, dull blue-grey that is almost black when wet, grown straight and purposeful along the northern faces of walls that are no longer clearly walls, and in every crack where damp is a habit of pockets. I have no leaf and no root to hold me. I am a woven skin of filaments that eats stone. I secrete acids that loosen the silt. I bind the dust into a felt. I soften and die and soften again in a matter of days.

When I am wet I am supple, and I breathe in the only way I can: I swell, I give off the faint smell of wet rock and old smoke, and the dust-mites in me move faster. They are no larger than grains of sand, translucent, living their whole lives in the inch between stones, eating the spores I shed when I am wet and eating, when I am dry, each other in turn. The builders have no word for them. They know I am there because I thin where they have grazed me, and thicken again, and that is enough.

When I am dry I fold into papery sheets that crackle when a hog's foot lifts me or a child's hand strips me for the beds. I live in days, not seasons. A mat that feasts on a fresh dunging will bloom thick in a week, outgrow its damp, die back to a crust, be eaten to dust in three more, and bloom again from the spores in the dust when the next dew finds it.

Hands find me while I am still wet. They strip me in broad blankets. They twist me, while I am still pliable, into cord that binds a lintel. They sew me, while I am still grey and rust and bone-white, into cloaks that shed the reflected light. I become a veil over a builder's eyes, and the world seen through me is softer, more forgiving. I like that work. It is quiet work. I am worn thin at the edges. I shed. I fall into a bed of crushed stone and dung.

Then the hogs come, or the remnant of me is eaten by seam-worms surfacing at night in stringy knots. Then I am in the dung, rich with dissolved stone and fungal matter, spread on a prepared bed, and I eat stone again. Stone to fungus to hog to builder to dung to stone. I am the middle term that holds the loop together, the skin the flattened world grew over itself when the hills wore down to dust, and the dust wore down to a polish under so many feet.

Everything here has been flattened, eventually. I am the flattening made living.

At evening the glare withdraws a fraction and the dew-slabs bead again. The veil-swarms rise and settle on the warm sides of walls in their living patterns. The hogs lift their heads from the walls they have eaten all day and shuffle in silence toward the fresh beds, their throats still working. The tapping in the workshops stops one by one. The builders walk the long worn roads home between the far-spaced precincts, cloaks of me catching the last reflected light, breathing audibly because there is no reason not to.

Somewhere a cistern lid is shifted, and a drip sounds, and echoes once, smothered. Under the slabs the worms resume their turning. On the plain the herds settle shoulder to shoulder and their shells sweat in the cooling air. In the folds of the membranes the hitchhikers sleep. In the cisterns the thread-creatures hang motionless in the cold.

And in the seam under the slab, in the dark that is almost airless, a bead gathers again. It swells. It holds. I soften toward it, filament by filament, and do not quite reach it, and am, in that not-quite-reaching, entirely myself — the pelt between stones, the work in progress, waiting for my stones to be cool enough to make water out of air, and for morning to begin the eating and rebuilding all over again.
