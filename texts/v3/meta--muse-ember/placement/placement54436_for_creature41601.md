I did not climb into it. I sank into it.

At the station platform, where the rails end in buffers, the basin opened around me all at once — flat, hamlet-size, ringed by spoil-heaps and warehouses instead of hills. There was no way through it and no way out of it that I could see. The horizon was made of brick and corrugated iron. The light was late morning, and it stayed late morning: flat, grey-yellow, the sun high enough for a short shadow and never bright enough to make another one.

The cool got into my hands from the rail and stayed there. My breath fogged, briefly, though it must have been midday. The ground was cinder and broken concrete and cracked mud over slag, polished in the wheel-ruts. The canal ran black and still. A fine misting came down once, too thin to wet my coat, and then stopped, and the condensation kept coming anyway — ticking in the downpipes, beading on the pipes, weeping from the eaves in slow drips into puddles that never rose.

I kept looking for a weed in a crack. There was none. The verges were ash. What lived here was a grey-green felt of fungus on every damp surface, and a mineral crust in the gutters that smelled of wet iron and old bread.

I heard them before I understood what they were. *Tik-tik-tik*, rapid and dry, under something low. A heavier click on concrete. Hoof on stone, hoof on rail. Then a bellow that was not a roar — a deep, hollow note that made the corrugated wall of the nearest warehouse hum back, as if the building were finishing the sentence for them.

In the mill yard one was already crossing the gutter line, hog-sized and low-slung, barrel-bodied, six-legged, its wet grey hide worked full of grit and brick-dust until it looked like a piece of the yard that had learned to move. Slag plates overlapped along its back like crude roof tiles. Its head hung low. Its snout worked the concrete.

Then another came around the boiler house, and another, in a loose procession following the gutter. I stepped aside for them. They stepped around my boots with the same indifference they gave a pipe.

Within the hour I had to pick my way over a back sleeping in a wheel-rut. Within the hour I had to wait while one settled its bulk exactly across a doorway, breathing damp and metallic at my ankles.

I stayed because there was no hour to leave in. Late morning does not move on. I walked the place the way a visitor walks, ten minutes from one end to the other, and then again, because the loop was the only shape the streets offered. Along the canal. Turn at the station. Follow the rail line to the substation. Loop through the mill yard. Begin again.

After a while I stopped trying to find their eyes.

Up close there were none to find — only small, milky pits, skin-closed, paler than the rest of the head. I had been looking up at them for so long, at the hundreds pressed flat under every eave and in every window frame, legs splayed, barrels flattened against brick and iron, fine twitching all down their six legs like a shivering. The roofs were shingled with them. The underside of the water tower was a beard of them. Motionless, except for that twitch. Listening.

Looking was a bad way to know this place. I learned that slowly.

I put my hand flat on a pipe in the substation once, out of habit, and felt it through my palm — a faint vibration running the length of the iron. The concrete carried it too, and carried it better. Everything here was hard and damp, and nothing softened sound. When a turbine piston clanged, the whole floor rang, and all along the gutter line their heads lifted together, snouts up, in unison. They were listening with the whole of themselves, through their feet, through the buildings, through each other. The clicking under their snouts was a needle of sound they sent out and took back. *Tik-tik-tik.*

My own feet began to answer before I did.

It was not a sudden thing. There is no sudden thing here. One circuit I was counting their contacts on the polished groove — click, click, click — and the next circuit I was counting mine, and the count came out six, spread wide under a weight I had not noticed acquiring. The click on the concrete was iron-hard and neat and polished, and it was mine. When I settled, the barrel of me rolled very slightly from side to side, and the legs found their places under it without hurry. There were no stumbles here.

When the bellow came up through the floor, I felt it in my teeth first, in my chest, a hollow resonance building where breath lives, and only afterward did I understand that it had come out of me. A corrugated wall hummed in answer, a pipe pinged. In a place this loud with machines, the animals' voices did not rise above anything. They became part of the current. A second factory whistle, sounding from inside.

Somewhere between the mill yard and the canal, my head had stayed low.

I scrape the damp concrete, the north sides of walls, the inside of drains. The fungal felt comes away in a clean streak under my snout and thick tongue. Over hours it creeps back to the same edge. I drop mineral pellets as I go, neat and grey, and the fungus spreads over them almost at once. Flakes curl up from my back where a plate is shedding, fall, are colonized, and later, coming back along the circuit, I eat them again with the fungus on them. The plates grow again from what I have taken in. Nothing is wasted because nothing is ever finished.

I drink by licking. Condensation beads on pipes, on the undersides of eaves, on the plated backs ahead of me in the procession, and I turn and lap it up. What I breathe out is damp, faintly metallic, fogging the air at ankle height. What condenses again at night on the cold iron, I take back in. The water does not leave. It only changes where it is held.

The grit is in my skin now. Brick-dust, cinder, ground in like aggregate in concrete, so that from a distance I may look less like an animal than like a piece of the yard that has learned to move. The plates fit one over the other along my back, dull and pitted. On my left flank one has torn and not grown back in the same shape, and the hide beneath is paler. A scarred one with a torn plate on its left flank is in the same doorway every time I pass. It took me a long time to understand that the circuit was not bringing me back to it. The circuit was bringing me back to me.

I do not fear anything here, because there is nothing here to fear and nothing here to want outside the loop. No hunting, no fleeing. No birth I can witness, no death I can find. If one of the wall-dwellers loses its grip, the ones near it nudge it back toward the brick with their blunt heads, patiently, until the legs find purchase again. If one of us in the gutter is slow, the procession makes way with a sigh and goes on around. Thousands, always thousands, fat and sleek on fungus and condensation, moving slowly enough that the landscape as a whole does not move at all. The river is always at the same height.

The doorway is where I pause. Not a pause that ends anything — the circuit runs through me and resumes — but a place where my bulk spans the threshold and the cool of the concrete comes up through all six feet and the breath fogs low and the conveyor clangs empty behind me and the condensation ticks in the pipes overhead. Others step over me, or wait, unbothered. My plates shed. The fungus takes them. My head lifts at a clang I have heard a thousand times and will hear a thousand times more, listening through the buildings, through the iron, through the ground, through the animal ahead and the animal behind, all of us standing on the same instrument.

I am not watching Kell Hollow finish. I am what finished it, and the finishing does not end.

If you come along the circuit — and you will, because there is nowhere else to walk — I will be in the doorway.
