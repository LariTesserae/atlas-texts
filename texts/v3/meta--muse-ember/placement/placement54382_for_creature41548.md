I do not come down the shaft.

A visitor would. A cave cricket would — mottled, long-antennaed, testing the air with a slow wave, settling on a block at the edge for a few minutes because the drop from the dry passage is there, and then leaving because there is nothing here to eat and the heat takes water from its body faster than it can afford. It sees the white column at noon and it leaves. Its eyes work, and that is why it cannot stay.

You would, if you could tolerate it. You would sit at the edge of the main pool where the water has cooled just enough to bear a fingertip, and watch for an hour and see almost nothing happen, and then a drip would fall and a ring would expand, and you would learn the patience of the room. You would be the one who notices.

I am not the one who arrives, and I am not the one who leaves.

The amphipods pass over me. That is the first way I know something is above the water. A flick sideways in the shallows, a translucent comma eight millimetres long, blind and clear as glass, legs beating in a quick lateral rhythm. It does not look at me — it has no eyes to look with, and has not needed them since long before this chamber was opened by a collapse. It settles at the surface film because the hot water holds very little oxygen and it must breathe where the water is thinnest and coolest. It scrapes.

Its mouthparts cut through me, slowly, deliberately. It takes bacteria, detritus, a trapped flake of mineral. It does not clear a patch. It cannot afford to, and neither can I. One grazer, then another, hours apart. You could watch five minutes and see two or three, or none at all. I feel them mostly as pressure, as a slight giving where a leg lands, and then the pressure is gone and the film closes over again where they have taken.

Above the waterline the springtails are the dry edge of the same hunger. White specks on the ledge, smaller than type, jumping when the rock warms under the moving sun. They eat what has splashed up and dried — the bacterial film stranded when the level falls, a spore that fell down the shaft and never became anything green. They jump by a latch under the abdomen, scatter a few millimetres, and are still again in the morning draught. They are not me, but they are close enough to warm to jump. Too wet below and they would lose their footing. Too dry above and there would be nothing to eat.

Under the amphipod, under the springtail's dried scrap, is where I am.

I am the film at the rim of the main pool, where the water drops to the low forties.

From a step away you would miss me entirely. Up close I have thickness — grey-white, no brighter than wet tissue paper, wrinkled where I have shrunk and swollen against the rock. I am a few millimetres of slime, and in that slime there are layers, because there is a gradient to live in and I live in all of it at once.

The top layer meets the water and the air above it. The bottom layer lives against the stone, in water that has not seen air at all. Between them the work is done. Hot water wells up from the crack in the centre, fifty degrees and more, carrying dissolved sulphur and iron in forms that want to become something else. I do not use the light. The column through the shaft is wasted on me — too hard, too hot, and below the surface, not needed. I use the difference between heat below and oxygen above.

One kind of us oxidises sulphide, step by step, to sulphate. Another precipitates iron as the faint ochre crust at the pool's rim. We shed what we have oxidised and trap what we can in our own jelly. We have no colour to speak of, no mouths that open and close, no eyes. We divide when a pulse of slightly cooler, slightly richer seep water passes through — that is the only event that matters to us. You can watch a whole patch change very slowly as that pulse arrives: pale, then faintly ochre, then pale again. It is not us moving. It is the water moving through a living sieve, and us answering in place.

I am all edge. I have no interior that is not exposed to one side or the other. Everything I am is interface — between the hot, mineral-laden water coming up from below and the cooler, thinner water at the rim, between stone and air. That is why I can be here at all. In the centre, where it bubbles and hisses and dissolves silica like a solvent, even we cannot settle. At the rim, where seep water has cooled and diluted the well-water, we can. Together the patches cover perhaps a square metre. Between them, metres of bare, slick rock.

My slime holds dust and particles that would otherwise wash away. It gives the new sinter skin something to grip. The little dams at the pool's edge, millimetres high, white and glassy where the hot water cools a few degrees and drops its load — I help lay them without trying to build anything. We are eating a chemical reaction, and the reaction builds. The amphipods graze us down. The springtails eat our dried edge. Death, when it comes, is not dramatic. A patch gets too hot and dies back. It is recolonised from the cooler edge when the temperature shifts again.

That is my movement: not toward anything, not away from anything. Growth, and retreat, and growth again.

If you stay long enough you will stop looking for movement and start noticing change — a bubble rising from the silt too small to pop, a crystal ticking as a block contracts, the bright column moving across the shaft and warming rock that radiates it back at night. I am part of that slow work, at the scale my body allows. Building slime over stone. Being scraped. Jumping, in my own way, when the heat rises too far.

Find me not in the bright place, and not in the centre where nothing can settle. Find me at the rim, in patches, easy to miss. Up close I give, very slightly, when a drip lands near me.

I do not move toward the light. I grow where the water has just become bearable.
