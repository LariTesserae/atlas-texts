At first there is only the wet, and it is not under me.

It comes up through me, warm and spongy, and keeps coming. It weeps as vapor in thin jets from a thousand small mouths in my skin. It lies in ankle-deep puddles that are not on top of me so much as held inside me, bath-warm, faintly sour, faintly mineral. I do not arrive at this. There is no step onto. I have always been the thing stepped on.

I do not know where I begin. My leaves are flat and glossy because the air is always condensing on them and they must shed it, and below them is a thatch of roots and stems woven and re-woven until the weave itself became ground. Inches thick in some places, feet thick in others, wherever I have been allowed to stay flat long enough to thicken. I blister in the heat rising from below. I sigh. I breathe, very slowly, in broad undulations that lift the puddles a few inches and let them fall, as if something enormous were sleeping underneath and turning over. There is nothing underneath but more of me.

I have no eyes for the towers. I could not tell you how far one is, or what it looks like against the clean high light. I know them the way I know heat, because heat is what I am farming and what I am made of at bottom. Where the mountain is hotter, where a fissure has opened and steam is erupting loud enough to feel in the teeth, I concentrate. I thicken. That is all.

Sometimes, that thickening goes up.

I stack instead of spreading. Stems that would have run sideways turn and fuse to their neighbors, leaf shrinking to cup at the top, until I am a tower, hollow at the core because the steam needs somewhere to go. It travels up through my lung and out my hollow culms in a hiss. I lean toward the vent, not the light. Light is plentiful here; it is heat that is scarce, and I grow a hand's breadth while anyone might watch, trembling at the tip, and then I stop, and thicken, and pop my swollen stems, and let my seeds go. Then, having spent myself upward, I am no longer a tower. I ferment from the inside where I have stopped venting, soften, sink back down into flat. No one mourns. The tissue persists.

Sometimes, the heat is moderate and I grow sideways, in a circle, and seal myself. A dome. My inner skin goes smooth and slick and faintly fungal-green to hold moisture, my outer back rough thatch to the sky, or the reverse if I have grown inside-out this season — it does not matter, the posture works. Inside I am warm and dark and sweating, a chamber that is not a room but a body cavity for holding heat and water against the thin air. I was not built to be entered. Seeds wash in on steam or drift through a seam and land in the warm seep at my base inside, and they start at once, in the dark they were born in. I hold them until they thicken enough to break me open from within, and then I collapse in slow motion, fiber by fiber groaning, and vines throw themselves across my scar and knot it shut, and I am sharp again. Nothing here is allowed to be mellow. I tear almost as fast as I grow.

Sometimes I am at an edge and I reach.

I grow across a basin toward another basin's heat, a bridge-vine, yearning, slow and loud with my own making. The other side often grows toward me. We never quite meet in the middle. Or we meet and I decide, in my plant way, to grow down instead of across, and hang weeping roots into empty air that is already saturated, searching for a water that is everywhere. Sometimes I am a wall, grown in a line to hold a terrace against slumping, holding water on one side and not the other, weeping until I split and steam erupts from the clean pale fibers and I must be restitched overnight.

And sometimes — this is rarer, and I feel it as a restlessness in the weave — I lift one end of myself off the flat and I move. A solitary creeper crossing a quarter-mile of open mat as if I have somewhere to be. Extending, anchoring, pulling my own flat body after me toward a distant vent I have sensed only as a difference in warmth through my neighbors, whom I have fused with and left behind. The open between the built things is not empty. It is me, doing the slower work of being ground, thickening, fermenting, waiting for a new fissure to give me a reason to rise.

I am not one individual. I was a seed once, buoyant and tufted, floating in a sour puddle until the water washed me against a tower base where it was warmest and I began immediately. I have no memory of a parent, only of neighbors fused so completely the distinction stopped mattering. My pollen went out on updrafts, was carried sideways on steam, fell back down in the little local rain a vent makes for itself. My fruit-case swelled in the heat until it popped with a crack like a snapping twig and loosed a cloud. What falls into my puddles does not rot patiently. It acidifies, dissolves, becomes mineral again in hours, and the mat thickens over it by the next day. I unmake myself as fast as I make myself, and that is why I am forever young. I die by unbuilding — a stem splitting loud as a branch under a weight at night, a dome sighing apart, a wall slumping — and I am rebuilt by my own seed in the warm seep at my own base.

At night the hissing lessens and the popping continues. I give off the day's heat in visible veils. The condensation grows heavier. Surfaces weep. Leaves weep from their undersides. I rise and fall beneath the puddles that reflect what little light there is and hold it, warm. I am not sleeping. I am condensing, thickening, splitting, stitching my own tears shut with living knots.

If you look for what lives here, you will not find it by looking up.

You are standing on it.
