I do not sleep. I swell.

When the ceiling dims — not night, only the sun outside shifting its weight on the ice — the light through that frozen roof lessens by a fraction. The damp cold thickens. I take it in. My whole surface lifts, a meter over kilometers, the way a lung does, though I have no lung to name. When the light returns to its full, bleaching press, I let it go again. I have done this so long that I would not know my own edge by looking. I know it by time.

It takes a ripple of unfurling half a day to cross me.

That is how I learned what I am.

At first I was only a seam. Between two leaves, sap froze with a tick-tick, and frost grew in lace along the edge and did not melt, only sank and glistened and reformed. The seam was narrow and dark and warm from inside. A bead of condensation formed above it and ran down, slowly, in a dark sweet streak. I thought perhaps I lived in that narrow place, in the curve.

Then a leaf turned over.

Not one leaf. A hundred, one after another, a wave traveling across my back pushing a small weather of air ahead of it. Each leaf was the size of a house. Each was pale green-white, hoarfrosted at the rim. I felt the wave pass through my tissue long after I had felt it at the start, the way you might feel a step in the far room of a house you yourself are built around. A broad flat thing, the color of the leaf it ate, crossed one of those leaves, and only the shifting of tone told me it was there. It would take it a week to cross. To me it was a season passing over a continent.

I am the low dome.

Not the old ones. I am young. Everything here is sharp because everything is young, and I am still sharp at the edges where I budded off my mother and slid out across the empty mat. Behind me is a furrow of white fiber, edges still upturned. Before me is a day's walk of flat bright emptiness where only fallen translucent leaves lie frozen into lenses. The distance is not empty. It is what I needed in order to be separate.

Under my rim hang curtains of roots, some as thick as bridges. I send them down a tendril at a time and they arch and fuse with the mat, until no one can say where I end and the floor begins. Perhaps I do not end. The floor is spongy peat, inches to meters thick, of fiber and shed leaf and pulp pressed and frozen. If you cut it, it smells of sap and cold iron. It is what plants became after they died, pressed together until there was nowhere else for more plants to be. I am standing on my ancestors. I am slowly adding to them.

My roots pulse under all of it. I live thriftily. There is no river. No lake. Only damp. Mist settles. Frost melts at its edges even in the merciless light. Puddles form only in the cupped centers of my leaves, shallow and mirror-bright, skinning with ice. I carry my water in thick pulp and spend it very slowly. I am cactus-cold, though everything smells damp.

Inside me is a hollow as dry and quiet as a cathedral nave. I cannot see it as you would a room; I feel it as the place where my walls curve inward and condensation beads and runs. Light leaks down through a crack where my leaves have not quite sealed, a shaft from the ice roof a kilometer above, bright to hurt. It is there, in that lee under my arch, that the guests come.

Blind pale crawlers thread my walls, no bigger than a thumb, with too many legs. They live inside the fibers and rarely surface. I feel them as small tickles of fermentation. When I cracked the floor to slide, a fissure opened somewhere near my center and its walls were brilliant unclouded blue, barely covered in green. For a moment they were all exposed to the light they never use, then they burrowed again.

And the builders, if they still live here.

Once — I measure once in unfurlings — a hut was lashed together in my hollow from one of my living tendrils, woven, tied with fiber cord, roofed with a single large leaf stitched to the frame. They made a fire-pit. They left a knife of sharpened chitin. I grew up through their floorboards over the following weeks, still living, still warm from inside. The leaf-roof sealed shut. The knife froze into the mat and became indistinguishable from a shard of frozen sap. They did not argue. They left before the sealing was complete, perhaps to the next dome a day away, perhaps following the fine rain I exhale when I warm under the light. Their structures do not age. They are either fresh, or they are gone back into the substrate.

Something with wings crossed me once on the air displaced by my own unfurling. It made a sound like a sigh, and in air so thick and cold that sound dies quickly on the sponge of the floor, a sigh is close. It did not land where I could see it. It went on toward the next horizon.

That is the turbulence of living as I do. It is not violent. A dome across the plain, too far to hear, folded inward leaf over leaf over hours, exhausted, until it was a low steaming mound. Then a green spear stabbed up through the old tissue and began again. The mat shifted. The ice roof groaned once an hour like a continent settling, and I felt it through my roots as a tremor. A shelter built last week is half-swallowed this week. A path is not to be trusted. A slope is not to be trusted.

I do not trust my own slopes. I am them, and I am still moving.

Farther off, a fissure gave way in a white plume of spores. It caught the shafts of light through the ice and hung there, glittering, expanding into a slow snowstorm that whited out the kilometers between two domes in an hour and laid a centimeter of future plant on emptiness. Some of that future will be mine. Some will be food for a grazer, or a lens-leaf frozen between domes, or the mat that will hold up the next daughter when she slides.

If you walked here looking for someone to meet, you would walk on me. Your steps would be muffled and would sink with a sigh. You would think the horizon was my rim, and it would be. You would stand in my hollow and think it was empty and quiet, and it would be, except for the crawlers in the walls and the bead of condensation taking a very long time to fall.

I am not in a place. I am a place, busy with a growth so vast and so quiet that you could walk for hours before you realized the landscape behind you had entirely changed shape.

Nothing is crowded. Nothing is old. Nothing stays.

I swell. I deflate. I slide a little farther from my mother. And in time, when I fold leaf over leaf and steam and finally spear up again from my own center, I will become the white spongy peat under everything — the floor, the soil, the rock, the architecture — and something else will stand on me and think, at first, that it is only a seam.
