If you put me in that pit, I wouldn't arrive standing. There is no up that matters down there, and nothing to stand on that isn't still being made.

At first I wouldn't be an animal at all.

I would be the film. That has to be tried first, because the film is the first life. A dampness with a texture — olive-green where the concrete stays wet near the cold pipe, iron-red where it has dried, black velvet on the rubber gasket where it has made a hill of something dropped. I wouldn't travel. I would just bloom where the wet front has lingered long enough, swell a little when the filler machine cycles hard and the pit fills with fog, shrink and harden into a crust when the shift ends and the air drinks from the edges inward. Then I would be eaten back to a clean ring and bloom again.

That is a life, but it cannot remember. And in a place forty centimeters long, memory has to be externalized — left behind as a trail the next body can read. So I wouldn't stay film.

I would put on legs at the edge.

Small, translucent, less than a grain of rice. Six-legged, eyeless or nearly so. A grazer in the lace. My belly would be my autobiography, shown straight through my skin: the green of the last meal, the pale knot of unborn young beside it, a dark grit that will never pass, sometimes disease. Nothing hidden. You could crouch behind the conduits and read me by what I've taken in.

I wouldn't move toward food in a line. I would move along damp, the way tides move. The creeping film up the side of a rust ridge, around the rim of a washer, into the head of a screw where a bead has landed and made a lake the size of a thumbnail. For an hour that lake is a continent, and I would converge on it with everyone else — not for company, but because we're all following the same retreating line. Behind me the biofilm would be scraped clean. Ahead of me it would still be green. I would pause constantly to test with antennae and palps and the hairs on my legs, which are not decoration. They are wetness-detectors, vibration-detectors, chemical readers. The lace trails under my feet aren't just paths. They are messages laid down by the animal ahead of me: hungry, frightened, laying eggs. I have no eyes worth the name. I read what was just lived.

I would try to be other things, while I'm at it, because the pit offers them.

I could be one of the pale, blind pseudoscorpion-shaped hunters, pincers too big for my body, following vibration and the heat of a crowd and the heartbeat of the machine through the northern wall. But that would make me the end of someone else's migration, not part of it. The hunter doesn't clear a territory here. It follows a migration and, in following, creates it. In a loop of forty centimeters the predator is behind you again, and again, until grazing and fleeing are the same slow tide. I think I would rather be the tide than the thing that organizes it.

I could be one of the upside-down dwellers under the warm pipe — their sky, their sun — hanging belly-up in my own threads, legs curled in a basket to catch rain that falls upward, never once touching the ground. Their architecture isn't built, it accumulates: shed skins, dead kin, frass, spores flung up in a fan gust that is a storm here, woven into papery nests that hum when the hall doors bang. If I fell, it wouldn't be death. It would be a birth onto the floor. The idea is beautiful, but too still. I am made for the moving edge, not the hanging meadow.

I could be the single pale shoot in the corner where the crack leads up to the thin line of light, blind and stringy, root hairs hanging into air instead of soil, making tiny pale clones along a line and never flowering. But it doesn't feed the crowd, and it doesn't move. It only reaches. I want the place where things are consumed and remake themselves.

So I would come back to the floor, to the salt field.

The carbonate efflorescence is still growing overnight in dry weather — white needles, tiny swords, pushing up and sharpening the terrain every week. The armoured mites step between them on thickened soles, deliberate as men in a minefield, scraping fresh rust from the pipe-cliff and plating it into their own backs until they clink faintly when they turn. I am softer than they are. I would learn their routes badly and then relearn them. Every week the landscape cuts a new way through and I would have to invent it again under my feet. That, too, is part of remembering: the ground won't hold the memory for me, so my feet have to.

And then, in pulses, water would come to prove me right.

Not rain. Betrayal. A seam beading, a joint weeping one tick at a time, condensation on the coldest metal. A drip ticking into the dent of a washer, into the crater-rim of the bottle cap half-sunk in damp, under the zip-tie arch that has never been flattened, beside the glove finger under the plate. When the machine gusts, hundreds of beads form and run in quick silent rivulets; when it idles, everything dries at the edges inward. I would live for that arrival. I would unfold in it. I would eat, migrate, lay eggs wherever damp has last been — in mortar pores, under the faded instruction tag, inside the hollow of a washer — and read, in the film ahead of me, whether the last animal was hungry or frightened or also laying.

Then the pit would cool by two degrees. The film would shrink. The wall of grazers would dissolve, each of us hurrying for a crack.

That is where you would really find me, if you came back later. Not in the moving lace, not in the green. Folded. Legs tucked, body curled, sealed into a cyst no bigger than sand, packed shoulder to shoulder in the mortar with hundreds of others, waiting. My life wouldn't be measured in days. It would be measured in wet cycles: open, feed, migrate, lay, fold, wait. In the damp I might do stranger accounting with my own body — molt and eat the molt, split a leg across the salt and grow a new small whole animal from it when wetness returns — because here waste is too expensive to afford and the population has decided that even a discarded part is a kind of seed.

I am not the predator, not the plant, not the ceiling meadow that never touches ground. I am the middle animal, the translucent one, living exactly on the line where wet retreats and green is being eaten and clean is being left behind. Just ahead of the clean ring. Just behind the bloom. Never in the center of the lake, never fully dry, always on the advancing and retreating edge that makes the whole packed crowd remake its map, from pipe-sky to sharp ridge to crack.

If you lift the panel that never sits flush and crouch behind the legs and cable conduits long enough, you won't find me as one thing. You'll find a wall passing — a thousand feet too small to hear, stirring the damp film — then quiet, heavy with iron and warm oil and wet stone. You'll find a click, once in a while, like a fingernail on glass. Then, if you wait for the warmth to come back and the cold pipe to start beading again, you'll find me unfolding.

Nothing here, yet, has had time to be gentle. I wouldn't expect to be either. I would just be warm-kept, like an ember in new concrete, waiting in the mortar for the next beading, ready to be a wall again.
