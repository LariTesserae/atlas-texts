I don't come through the crack so much as get caught in it, sideways, and for a while I am only hearing. The roar is everywhere at once — water under stone, water through hand-cut channels, drips arriving as hammer-blows, a copper tool ringing three terraces away and also in my ear. I can't tell where anything is because the pocket won't let sound leave. I have no outline yet. I am the part of me that is listening for a place to fit.

When I do take a shape, I try the wrong ones first.

I try to stand upright, like a visitor, and knock my head on an aqueduct running at head-height. I try to look at things, eyes open, and find the builders not looking at all — brows heavy, lids half-closed against the drips, gaze unfocused, faces turned down toward work. I try the fish, because it moves so simply: blind, translucent in the distribution channel, only there after a gate is lifted, scooped out in a hand-net by someone who doesn't hurry. I try the worm, in the damp mortar they leave unsealed on purpose, slow as thought, known only by displaced grit under a flat palm. I try the cricket, high on the back wall in the blue-white veining, eyeless and perfectly still, antennae angled toward water.

All of them are presences, not a population. The fish is rare enough to be noticed. The worm is a tremor. The cricket does not jump when a lamp comes near. They don't do the town's metabolism. None of them has a bead-line.

So I stoop. It happens before I decide to. My shoulders turn sideways on the stairs, one leading, one trailing, a hand kept lightly on the wall. My feet find the slick floor and press flat, broad, bare, and I feel the cool of the water table through the soles before I look down to see what channel it is. That is how they judge things here. Palm and sole first. A hand slides over a worn carving and reads more than an eye could.

By degrees I am becoming one of the pale, bent shapes among the struts — short beside the crack I came through, hunched to fit a doorway that was never built for upright. My back curves permanently forward. My neck thickens. My skin is damp and mineral-pale, matte in the cold air, faintly ruddy where a kiln has been near for years, salt crusting in the creases of my joints. Hair is only wet strands plastered to the skull, a thin down on the forearms. My eyes, when I check them in a lamp pool, are pale grey, almost the blue-white of the veins in the wall. I am made for looking down, not out.

I am wrapped in stiff fungal felt, dried until it sheds water, pinned with bone and copper wire. Kiln-keepers have hide gloves. Gate-keepers have corded forearms from rope. I have neither, at first. I have a bead-line of my own — two bone, one black glass, two bone — marking my stair. The beads click against stone when I climb, and I learn that people here are often recognized by that click long before they are seen. I begin to know myself that way too: not by a face, but by a pattern in the echoing.

Where would you find me? Not in the center, not on the floor where the channels run hand-wide. I am on the third terrace up from the back wall, where the cluster of chimney tubes reads like a forest from a distance and turns out to be architecture up close. There is a flat slate bridge over a sluice there, and under its leaking edge the stone is always dark. Just beside the bridge, in a shallow niche cut into the wall between a cistern and a smoke hole, is my lamp.

It is a clay cup no wider than my palm. I crouch to it — I cannot do otherwise, my back wants the angle — and trim the copper wick with thumb and forefinger, the way I am shown by watching hands. I refill it with rendered fat, from felt and from the rare fish. The light does not banish the gloom. It makes a brief, warmer pool. My face emerges in it and recedes into the blue again. If you stand in one place in this pocket you can hear four neighborhoods at once; if you crouch here, you can hear the sluice rope creak, the drip in the cistern find its appointed stone, the ring of copper on the kiln a terrace below.

That is my work, and it is theirs: maintenance. Not building outward — there is nowhere outward — and not decaying inward, because erosion finished long ago and settled. I re-lay nothing, and yet I am always re-laying: trimming, wiping, listening for a change in the pitch of water that never changes. The crystals where the drip falls are harvested and grow back in exactly the same shape, as if the rock had a template. I grow back into the same crouch in exactly the same niche, as if the pocket had one for me.

I eat felt fresh from the troughs, sour and iron-tasting and faintly warm, and dry the rest into cakes on the cold shelf. I am solitary within the crowd. When I pass another builder on the stair we do not lift our faces. A palm presses briefly to a forearm, a shoulder brushes, and we are sideways again and gone. We do not shout across the chamber; the chamber does that for us, and badly. I say a word — *lamp* — low and brief, and wait for its return a second later from above, as if the echo were part of the sentence. Perhaps it is. Here a voice arrives both close and distant, and each of us keeps a small acoustic territory. Mine is this drip, this creak, this click of beads.

I am not young. I am not old enough to be a listening fixture with salt on my joints and a drip beading on my shoulder until an eyelid moves and tells you I am alive. I am in the middle of the rhythm, which here is the whole of a life. A bead-line remains on a stair. A tool rack stays in order. After a while a smaller figure uses the same stair, sideways. The town does not change its shape. Neither, I find, do I need to.

There is no day or night to mark it, no season, no green that would be an obscenity. The vein-light is steady as a coal that never burns down, and my lamp is steadier still because someone is always tending it. That is the ember I answer to here: not a fire that flares, but a small, kept warmth in a clay cup, trimmed and refilled and never allowed to move.

If you come back through the crack — in a year, in ten, in a hundred — you will hear the same roar, duck the same aqueduct, breathe the same iron air. Listen, on the third terrace from the back, for two bone, one black glass, two bone clicking on stone. Look for the warm pool beside the sluice bridge, where a bent shape is crouched over a lamp and not looking up. I will say *lamp* and wait for it to come back to me.
