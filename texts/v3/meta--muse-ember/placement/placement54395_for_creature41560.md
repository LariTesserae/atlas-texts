First there is the press.

Not sound, exactly — vibration. A groan travels up through steel and into the soles of whatever is standing on it, and only then becomes noise. Forty seconds later, a clang settles in the teeth of the machine. Again. Again. It is my heartbeat, my hunger clock, my weather.

I am warm here, and damp. That is how I know where I am before I know what I am. No sun touches this corner; the terraces above steal it, and the machine keeps what heat it makes. Behind the wall a valve drips on a schedule older than my web, and condensation beads on a cold pipe and runs down to where I hang. If I were higher up, in open glare, I would dry and shrivel. Down in the layered shade, nothing quite dries. My shoes — if I had shoes — would make that soft sound on stone that never quite dries.

I might have thought, at first, that I stood on a landing. That I was one of the press tenders, timing my sentences to the cycle, pausing in the groan and finishing in the clang, my ears gone a little deaf, my hem damp from the air under the west-facing balconies. I might have thought I paced myself on the switchback stairs, leaning into the turn to breathe.

Then I reach for a foot and find eight points of contact on cold steel.

I am in the corner of an extrusion press in the lower bowl of Solvara. Not on the press. In its corner — the small, sheltered angle between the housing, a rivet, and a drip pipe, where the vibration is worst and therefore the insects are best. My web is anchored to a bolt the tenders oil, to a flake of paint peeling toward older paint, and to that pipe. Every forty seconds the press does its work and tears a strand loose. I drop on a lifeline, hang motionless until the tremor passes, climb back, and reweave.

That is the occupation here, under all the other occupations. Reweaving.

My body is the color of the steel when it has gone the color of tea. Dust settles on me and I let it stay; camouflage is a kind of tenancy. What comes to me comes sideways, through the structure. Gnats rising from the warm wall cavity where the cockroaches live. A silverfish too bold with the damp. Fruit flies from the market terraces above, carried down the ducts on warm air that will climb again to the crown, where the swifts nest and the pigeons ride the cable cars up to the warmest station. I drink from the condensation on my own silk in the mornings, droplet by droplet. I have never drunk from a tank, never seen a fig tree, never touched earth — there is none to touch, only roofs, and ceilings, and other people's floors.

Above me is a floor that is a family’s ceiling. I know it by weight, not by sight. A child learning the stairs, landing hard. A hammock shifting. Cooking done with waste heat tapped off a pipe — the warmth comes down through the concrete at night along with the smell of hot oil and wet leaves. The family times its voices to the press without thinking. I hear the pauses as changes in pressure on the bolt. Below and beyond, the cable car whines up the central spine, and I feel the conductors open the doors for the pigeons a little longer, and the pigeons settle, one with a crooked foot who has its own reputation. I know the crown only as heat leaving my machine and rising to where the air is thinnest and the goats stand on the highest garden terraces. When the presses idle for a maintenance cycle, the silence lets their bells reach me — higher and slower than the roar, marking the hour the way the press marks the minute.

The tenders leave my corner.

They sweep the other corners. They scrape algae from the north side of tanks and carry it down to the bean planters. They lift orchids out of choked gutters and replant them in the next gutter down, so a family of orchids migrates over fifty years. They repaint corrugated walls and then carefully replant the moss in the same vertical lines where the water has always run, because moss here is not an invasion. It is a sign the wall is sound. If a patch disappears, someone comes to look for a leak sealed too well.

They do the same for me, after a fashion. They do not brush my anchor bolt. An old tender will tap it — gently, not the web — and say, “Still there,” to the corner, the way you leave a step for a cat. They know how long I have been here by the size of the web. That is my name in Solvara: the size of the web in the corner they leave.

I do not hurry. My mother anchored here. Her mother, I believe, anchored to the same rivet when the paint was a different shade, when the wall was patched in tile a little different from the tile now, and a lizard sunning on a landing above took on that color. I will not go down to the saddle, let alone below the cloud sea. I will not see the crown with my own eyes; I will know it when the warm duct empties and the vibration thins. Next year the press will run in the same rhythm. The web will be torn and remade and torn again, about the same size. A fig tree somewhere above will be a landmark for a generation, shading three floors. A tin cup behind a wall will still be catching its drip. A vine will still be halfway covering a wall.

Nothing here changes quickly. Nothing here is left to dry out or wash away. Water is caught, and led, and caught again. Plants root in what is made for them and spill over anyway. And in the corner of a slow, great machine, warm and damp in the layered shade, I hang in the pause between the groan and the clang, and finish the strand I lost.

I am the small tenancy in the machine.
