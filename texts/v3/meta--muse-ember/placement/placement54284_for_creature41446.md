I tried the rim first.

That is where the lift lets you out, ears popping, wind finding you at once, the plain below and the clouds sometimes trapped under it and the city going on in every direction farther than the eye can comfortably follow. I stood there expecting an edge, the way a hill has an edge, and found instead a continuation: smelter district, textile district, terrace after terrace, factory chimneys still working forty kilometres away in white, even light. Nothing ended. I could see Marrek, but seeing it was not the same as being in it. From the rim it repeated itself, stone deciding to build, and I was outside the repetition, looking in. My shirt, if I had had a shirt, would have stuck to my back in the damp. I did not stay.

Then I tried a ledge.

A whitewashed one, under the deck of a canal bridge, where the rock pigeons nest in colonies large enough to paint a facade with guano. I had a small hot body there, feet gripping stone worn smooth, a throat full of cooing, and the whole colony shifting all around me at once when a swift cut low over the water or a gull screamed up from the cliff. At dusk I could have poured out of a tunnel mouth with the bats in a silent river. In the day I could see the canals branching, the lines sagging with perching birds, the laundry hanging over the terraces. But a ledge in Marrek is never empty for long — leave one unclaimed in the morning and it is claimed by afternoon — and I was only ever one bird among thousands, exposed to too much sky, too much sun, too much seeing. Marrek does not resolve from above. It resolves, if it resolves at all, by damp.

Then I tried the ground.

In a gutter, between pavers, where rats run in broad daylight and cats patrol sleek between houses stacked on workshops stacked on warehouses stacked on the vaulted waterworks underneath. Bricks radiated the noon heat back into me as I crossed, steps ahead were worn into shallow bowls, handrails polished to a shine by hands. I stepped over the knee-height canals, under the washing lines and awnings and bridges that roof the streets half the time, past engine halls open on one side so the pistons could be seen turning slow and massive in unison. It was all very close, very crowded, very animal. But my feet kept making discrete decisions — *here, here, here* — and the stone kept drying under me. The only living cover in the seams was not green. It was fungus: black and ochre mats, orange crusts, pale films spreading and receding on wet concrete and on the shaded side of brick and on the timbers under the canal bridges. I kept following that damp with my nose, because the ground in Marrek is for crossing. It is not for staying.

So I followed the drip down.

Afternoon rain here rarely falls as rain. It falls as a fine, warm drip from every pipe and cornice, from the undersides of bridges, from the cliff face itself, from condensation beading on the inside of engine houses and running in rivulets down the steel. A drop hit the water and made a ring wider than I am long, and I went down through that ring, under the bridge, into the shadow where the timbers stay wet in every season.

Here the water is knee-height in the narrow districts and shoulder-height where the smelter channel widens, and it is slow, brown-green, carrying the heat of the day in it long after the rim has dried. In daylight the carp are thick in it, changing the flow as they move. I am not in it by day. I am long and low and I move mostly at night, holding myself against the cut stone to rest, belly pressed to basalt that has been planed and terraced into a working surface, gills pulsing in time with the pumps in the waterworks. When I move, the current parts around me and reforms behind me, exactly as it does for all the others threading these channels. That was when I knew what I was, if knowing is the word: an eel, in the smelter water, under the third bridge south of the cooling channel, in the elbow of a hollow iron pipe at the bend.

It is not a grand place. Nobody built it for me. The pipe mouth is fringed with mussels clinging like grey teeth, and the stone around it is coated with snails in a living grey, and above the waterline the fungus mats tide in and out with the seasons, black where the bridge shadow stays, ochre where the spray reaches. Frogs call from the dark corners behind me where the water has been weeping over the stone for centuries. My own turning place in the pipe is polished smooth and dark, the same way a handrail is polished smooth and dark, the same way steps hollow into bowls. Years of the same body taking the same bend. Marrek's erosion is established, not dramatic. So is mine.

I know the city by what the water does, not by what it looks like. Upstream the flow comes warm from the smelter cooling channel and tastes of hot metal and fish; I can feel the pistons through the steel of the bridge, slow and massive, and the moths and flies that thicken around the warm engines fall and are taken on the surface. Downstream a spring breaks out of a warehouse wall — cold, mineral, sudden in the warm plateau — and fans out pale with travertine before it joins the canal, and the gutters run even though it has not rained, because they always run. Under the residential terraces the water narrows and cools again, and I hear, muffled through stone, the low hum of the tram and the boots of people whose steps have worn the stairways above them into bowls, and cats come to the edge to drink, and laundry drips down in the dark. At the waterworks the filters fur with nesting material by evening; at the grates the workers lift the iron where eels have tried to climb, and I have learned not to climb. I wait in the shadow of the pipe until the pressure eases and the grate is settled, and then I slip on.

The people are not above me. They are the weather on the surface. They oil the gears where pigeons push twigs into the housings, they sweep guano from the tram tracks before it makes them slick, they lift the grates, they walk over the bridges with their shirts sticking to their backs, and what they cannot help giving — guano washed off the ledges, scraps from the markets, warm outflow, waste from mills and kitchens — comes down to me in the water. We are not visitors to each other. A filter left clean in the morning is furred by evening. A ledge left empty is occupied by afternoon. I am occupied by the same logic, in my pipe, by mussels and snails and the silt coming down from the terraces.

Nothing hurries down here either. That is the point. I do not cross Marrek in a day and finish it. I make the same small circuit, night after night, in water that is always almost the same: upstream to the warmth, down to the spring, under the houses, back to the bend. In a year the pump is a little slower, the colony above is a little larger, the silt in my pipe is a little deeper, and I push through it a little differently, and the steps above are a little more worn, and the limestone a little softer at the corners. The old residents say that if you leave a street for a year and return, you will find it almost identical. They are right. It is not abandoned and it is not new. It has weathered into its state and holds it, and I hold my place in it the way the stone holds the water: by being worn to fit.

At night the surface goes black between the bridges and then, in the gaps, the stars are sharp, because we are high. Bats pour out of the tunnel mouths in a silent, continuous river and the insects they miss fall to the water and make their own small rings. Frogs take over the low corners. The animal chorus — cooing, chirring, calling, splashing, claws scrabbling on stone, wings in the tunnel mouths — comes down through the bridge steel and becomes a pulse in the water, and for a while the only loud thing is a drip from a cornice, falling warm, falling warm, falling warm, into the canal I am holding myself in.

If you want to find me, do not look on the rim, or on a ledge, or in the gutter. Look under the bridge, in the elbow of the pipe where the mussels cling and the stone is coated grey and the fungus above the waterline is black and wet, with my nose pointing upstream into the warmth. If a worker lifts the grate, I will already be gone — downstream, into the vaulted dark under the houses, waiting for the pumps to rest and the water to go quiet again, and for Marrek to go on breathing with its mouth open, damp and warm and animal, around and over and through me, for as long as it chooses.
