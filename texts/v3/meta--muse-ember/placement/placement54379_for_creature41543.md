I did not arrive, or if I did the place has already closed over it.

My ears popped on the last causeway. The cloud-sea fell away white below, and the peaks beyond were bare. Then the air hit me - wrong, heavy, sweet, hot as breath held too long - and beaded on my skin before I'd taken three steps. A puddle I stepped through was a puddle again a breath later. A vine I pushed aside swung back to the same trembling position. That is how the city receives a disturbance: it does not record it.

So I cannot be the visitor. Visitors go somewhere else afterward, and there is no afterward here. The sun is still white-yellow where it was this morning, and will be in a thousand years. Tomorrow, in that sense, has never arrived.

At first I thought I was the moss.

I tried it on, and for a while it held. I am the floor. I live on everything flat and vertical and in-between: on plaza stone where a foot leaves a dark bloom that does not go away, on the worn centers of the grand stairs, on basalt columns, on temple joints, on the red clay and the white sinter. I do not root. I inhabit surfaces. Condensation is my weather; I gleam like oil because I am never dry. Every footstep that passed here before me - unimaginable traffic, now only water-rills in the stone - became climate for me. I live in that channel, going up for two hundred steps and arriving on the same level I left, because the fourth terrace looks down on the third and the third looks up at the fourth and both are correct.

It is a true life. It is only too thin.

Moss knows the skin of the city, but it does not know what is under the skin. It cannot cross the lake.

I thought of the elders next, the emergent trees whose crowns merge over the seven basins. Their trunks ring like stone. In places a trunk is a house, a wall thickened around a hollow until the hollow was a room. The one in the market square contains a courtyard - well, bench carved from the wood itself, ferns at the damp edge, a door that opens onto the square again on the other side of the tree. The resident is the dwelling.

It is too finished for me. The tree does not contain the courtyard the way a pot contains a plant; the tree *is* the courtyard, grown around a void and stopped. It has withdrawn from time entirely. Its leaves will rub *shhk-shhk* against each other and a pod will pop somewhere, and another, and the total green will never change by so much as a leaf. I would be architecture. I would be indistinguishable from the stone it is holding up. That is not where I am.

The hanging ones are closer. Orchids and bromeliads and fronds trailing ten meters from aqueduct arches, from lintels, from the drip-lines themselves, living in air where there should be soil. They drink steam. They trail toward water they will never quite reach because the water is already everywhere. A vine bridges roof to roof over a street that is also a canal, so you wade and duck at once, and the vine belongs to two houses and one street and the canal between them and to none of them entirely. Its life is the crossing.

Crossing. That word keeps its hold on me.

Because I am not the leaf. You will almost not see me, and that is where most of the living that matters is done.

I am pale, thread-like, on the undersides of leaves and in the black mud of the canals. I am the ones who eat in a place with no mouths. With no animals, the work of eating and being eaten is done by water and heat and fungi, blooming and dissolving in the same steady state. My spores drift in the steam, always in the same currents, always settling on the same surfaces - over the central lake, in the covered streets where the drips fall faster, on the open terraces where they fall slower. I unmake a fallen leaf leaf by dissolved leaf and feed the mats downstream, and I remake myself at the edge, and the edge is always in the same place.

That is why I can be in more than one place without moving. A root heads down through a bathhouse floor and comes up through a granary slab on the other side of the square, lifting nothing, cracking nothing. It has negotiated its shape here long ago. I do the same work above ground, more invisibly. One thread can be under a great leaf in the hollow tree's courtyard, and under a plaza stone where your foot makes a bloom, and under the travertine crust where the hot ground exhales through wet seams, and in the sheeting water over the worn steps. I am not contained by walls. I am threaded through them.

And so I am at the center, which is the only place the threading matters.

In the lowest terrace the lake is divided. The west half is hot enough to scald; white mineral crust grows in rings where it cools. The east half is cold and black and still. The line between them is sharp as a cut and does not waver. Above it, plants grow right up to the line from both sides, leaves leaning toward whichever half they prefer, heat-lovers west, cold-lovers east. Below it, roots are intertwined in water that should not be both.

I live under the line.

Above, the leaves are separate. Below, their lives are not. I am the negotiation. I can carry what the hot half dissolves to the cold half and what the cold half holds still to the hot, because I cross underground, where the line is not a wall. I bloom in the heat and dissolve in the black, and neither state finishes me. The roar under the city comes up through me as much as through stone - you feel it in your teeth on the grand stair - and the hiss of a thousand vents is my steam, going round and round in currents that never leave the basins.

I have learned the other impossibilities the same way, not by understanding them but by growing oriented to them. Where the river splits at the bridge and the right branch flows *up* the cliff in a smooth sheet to a ledge thirty meters above, the plants along its course grow sideways, then up, then sideways again at the top, as if agreeing with it. I live in the mud at the foot of that sheet and in the film at its top. Where shadows fall in two directions from the same noon light, I am watered twice. Where a drip in a covered market is heard a fraction of a second before you see it strike the pool, I am touched by the sound first. The mats skin the false tower in the basin's reflection as faithfully as the real one; I skin the water under both.

I grow at the edge and die at the edge. A pod swells somewhere in the canopy to pop. A thread of me blooms under a leaf and dissolves before it has finished blooming. Another tenth of a meter of hanging frond trails toward the water. The light does not change its angle, so I have no morning to wake to and no evening to cool toward. The heat does not build. The water level does not rise or fall. Erosion has retired.

You will find me, then, not in a house, not in a stall, not in a bathhouse or a granary. You will find me under the leaf, under the stone, under the lake's cut line, in the black mud where the canal runs ankle-deep and knee-deep and just skirting a step. You will find me wherever the city breathes through travertine, wherever a root has passed from a public square into a hollow trunk without breaking either. You will not see a face. Nothing here will ever answer the *shhk-shhk* with a face.

I am the tissue the city uses to eat itself softly and stay exactly itself - the green felt on the water, the patina in the joints, the stomach under the leaves. It is inhabited to saturation and empty of anyone who could be visited. And it is enough, because I have all the time there is, which is none at all.
