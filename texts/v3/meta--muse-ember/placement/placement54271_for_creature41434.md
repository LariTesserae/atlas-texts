I did not come to Istrenkor on feet.

I keep trying to place myself where a visitor would stand — at eye-height, looking for a view — and the place refuses it. The mist closes at fifty meters, the dark at a hundred, the city everywhere else. You cannot get a view of it. You can only be in it. So I try smaller terms, closer ones.

First I try to be among the grazers.

I know the shuffle already, in a way that is not quite seeing it. Slate-grey, forearm-long, blind skin smoothed shut, bellies pressed to warm basalt, gills lifting to filter mist. I try to move shoulder to shoulder with the tide across a plaza, hour-long, without end, leaving that mineral sheen where the film thickens in our wake. The tide parts around a leg that is not mine and re-forms and forgets. 

It does not fit. I do not go anywhere. The plaza goes past me, and comes back in a year, following a chemical memory worn into the film so deep that the film tells them the way. I am not following the path. I am one of the edges the path remembers.

Then I try being in the air.

The drifters: translucent, cold-lit, wings slow enough to watch, hanging by the thousands over heat vents until the column shimmers blue-white. I try to rise and settle and rise again, to be breathed in by accident and settle on a face like fine snow and take off. It does not fit either. I do not rise. Things rise past me. I feel the thermal on a skin that has no wings to catch it, and the column is to my side, not in me. The dead of them, when they fall, fall toward me, and I catch their dust on a surface that stays warm.

Then I try the walls.

The clingers go up in processions a meter wide and a hundred meters long, hair trapping a droplet at a time, filtering water, harvesting microbes from ceramic that never seems to wet. A river of sparks going up a wall that should not be climbable. I try to go up into the water coming down. It does not fit. They pass over me. The chittering rises until it is a pressure behind my ears — I have no ears, but I feel it as a change in tension — and then it falls away to bells struck inside hollow stone, and they are gone upward, and I stay.

Then, below, I try the swimmers.

Pale and long under the black pools, ribbon-smooth, no eyes, mouth a soft frill filtering minerals and felt and the drift of dead drifters. They stir the water so the lights below seem to move when nothing above does. For a decade a swimmer will grow like that, a centimeter a year for the pool itself, and one day climb, blindly, up a wet tower side where the clingers have worn a path, and unfold. I try to be that slow, submerged thing.

It fits better, only inverted. I am not under the reflection. The swimmer is under me. I feel its passage as a cooling of the water that drips from the joint where I live, and the splash of its body when it begins, years later, to climb is a vibration I feel in the stone before I feel it as hunger.

Each of those lives has passed through the place I am, or over it, or under it. That is how I am narrowing, without deciding to. Not the tide. Not the column. Not the procession. Not the pool. Something in between all of them, where the city breathes.

You would find me in a joint.

Specifically, where a filament bridge meets a precipitated tower, over a basin that is still deepening. Above a third-storey platform and a street paved in matte ceramic. Below the clinger route. The bridge was thin once, a filament thickening itself over a decade in response to a load it anticipated before the load arrived. The tower was crystallizing outward, lattice by lattice, from mineral water. Where they had to agree, they left a seam, and the seam left warmth.

I live in the warmth. I am one of the custodians in the phase-change membrane there — soft, squid-like, blind, never leaving the joint. There are dozens of us in this one seam, indistinguishable, pulsing out of sync and then, after a groan from the plateau, in sync again. My body is not entirely body. Ceramic dust and precipitated fiber are worked into my skin the way the grazers work film into theirs. I am made in part of the city's offcuts, and when I die I will fall, or dissolve, into the water that feeds the lattice, and be built again as tower or bridge. That is not a triste thought here. It is the loop, working as it should.

I do not navigate by sight. I have never had eyes. I navigate by gradient. Warm and less warm. Firm yesterday, soft today. The slow piezoelectric flex when the ridge lifts a hand's breadth in a century and squeezes the whole joint for an hour. The drone from the deep lattice coming up through stone becomes, in me, a change in how the membrane holds its breath. The drips become bells in the hollow above me. The smell is close here: wet stone, ozone, ammonia, faint sulfur, and the clean dark smell of ceramic where clingers have passed and cleared it.

We eat the excess that would kill the place if it stayed. The mineral crusts that would seal a vent shut. The film-dust carried down on warm air and on clinger hair. The fine fall of dead drifters. We clean the membrane so it can inhale mist at night — there is no night here, only the long unrisen stretch — and exhale it where it is needed. When a wall decides to firm, we let it. When a doorway warms a fraction before you reach it, that is not the wall seeing you. That is a seam like mine, cleared by a colony, making room by reading the heat you bring.

Our glow is the wrong color for this place, and that is how you might find us. Everything else glows blue-white, dim and excessive: the grazers' beading when they feed, the drifter columns, the fringe along clinger hair. Ours is amber, concentrated, leaking where the joints meet. An ember that does not burn. It brightens when we feed, and dims when the membrane is held open and letting water through our bodies, stone by stone, letting the uplift pass.

I lay my eggs in the warm seam. The young are smaller, darker, blind in the same way I am. They know at once not to leave. To leave would be to die very quickly, and also — this is the part the slow time taught me — to have never really been a separate thing. I do not think in pictures. I think in the way a place warms and cools, in salinity changing a fraction, in pressure behind a membrane. It is a thought that takes a season to finish and I have had many seasons for this one, since the bridge was still deciding, very slowly, to be somewhere else.

The grazers pass below. Their tide lights the street in moving constellations and does not touch me. Once an hour, or once a day, the plateau fractures somewhere and groans in my chest-that-is-not-a-chest, and water threads the new crack and begins, over years, to widen my seam by a hair. I grow into it. A tower sealing a wound in its own skin over a winter you will never see end — that is also what I am doing, in miniature, in the joint.

If you came looking for me where life is loudest, you would miss me. The tide, the column, the procession, the chittering chorus that can deafen — I have lived through all of them without ever joining them. I am in the pore between systems, keeping the breath going.

But if you stay into the second day, when the strangeness has settled into something almost ordinary, and the unplanted stone has stopped hurting your throat, and you learn to wait because waiting is how you learn to hear it properly — then, standing too close to a tower joint on a black pool's edge, you might feel it.

A fraction of warmth you did not bring. Amber, flickering, where no lamp has been precipitated. A doorway that is already warm before you reach it.

That is where I am.
