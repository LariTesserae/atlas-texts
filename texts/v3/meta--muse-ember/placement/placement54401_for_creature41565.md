If I were placed here and not passing through, I don't think I would be the thing walking.

I came in, for a while, as the walker — eyes up, looking for the ceiling. But the walker is too large for this place, and too loud even when trying to be quiet. My footfall, however carefully set down, asks too much of the litter. My hand, however gently pressed to a trunk, is an event. Everything down here notices a disturbance and then, if it doesn't move again, forgets it. I would not want to be remembered as a disturbance.

So I tried, first, to be higher up.

A bird in the canopy. Brief, soft ticking down through three layers of leaf. But up there the light is rationed in a way I could feel as exposure, and the leaves are too broad, too glossy, too built for a single job. I would be seen, however dimly. I tried a frog in a root pool, sitting with toe-pads spread flat, drinking through my skin, calling one muffled note and waiting a long while for an answer. That was closer — the damp, the stillness — but even a frog has a voice it has to spend, and a body that must find its pool again. I tried the slender snake on the liana, moving at the pace of a thought. Too much thought. Too much of a silhouette.

The place kept pushing me down, not because down is lowly, but because down is where the packing is thickest.

On the fallen trunk I tried to be an ant. That has a certain honesty in this world: one direction on one side of the path, the other direction on the other, carrying leaf-fragments and larvae and each other, never fast, never pausing. But I tired of the column. I tired of being one of many identical legs. I tried a millipede curled in the damp seam, waiting out the world in armor. I tried a beetle larva as big as a grain of rice, chewing the softened interior and turning solid to frass. All of them were work, all of them were bodies, and all of them needed to go somewhere.

Further down, time changes.

I tried a springtail and felt the flick — a millimeter over a blackened leaf and still again, and that was a journey. I tried an oribatid mite, armored and absurdly slow, walking the width of a fingerprint across a fungal mat and grazing as it went. Its whole life could be lived under one leaf, and that suited the heat tax of this place: move less, decide at the lowest possible cost, let the leaf be a landscape. For a page of my mind I stayed there, in that armored patience.

But then I noticed what the mite was eating.

Under the mite's mouth, before the mite, before the springtail, before the nematodes threading themselves in the film of water, was something that had already eaten the leaf without a mouth. Something that had turned lignin and cellulose — locked, indigestible, dead for seasons — into soup it could leak back into the world. The mites were not grazing a field. They were grazing *that*.

That was where I began to become more myself.

I am not the grazer. I am the field. I am not the carrier. I am the seam the column runs along. I have no center, which is the right way to live here, where nothing is ranked and everything is organized around surfaces. My body is a branching, a threading, hypha over hypha, extending a little way into a fresh brown leaf, a little way along the damp skin of a punky log, a little way around a root tip at ankle depth. I do not walk toward food. I grow toward the rumor of it, and where I touch it, I secrete. Enzymes melt the leaf from the outside in. What I cannot take in, I make edible for the mites who will eat me in return, trimming me back, carrying pieces of me in their guts to start elsewhere. We are not predator and pasture. We are a compact.

You would find me where your hand cannot set down without entering a neighborhood. Under the third leaf down — the black one, perforated, lace-like, still holding its shape — in the thin film that never quite evaporates. In the groove where bark has sloughed from a trunk that fell a century ago and is still a trunk in outline. Along the mycorrhizal root, trading: sugar comes down from a canopy I will never see, arriving in the root as a distant sweetness, and I offer minerals and water from the humus in exchange and keep a little of the trade for my own quiet growth. I do not know the canopy. I know its arrival as chemistry.

It is dark here, and it is hot, and the two belong together, and I belong to both without needing to name them. I have no eyes to adjust to the greenish dusk leaking down. I have gradients — wetter, drier, richer, more exhausted. I have the slow pulse of rain soaking the litter and then the litter exhaling it back into air that never dries. I have neighbors touching me because everything here touches everything: leaf touches fungus touches mite touches leaf-fragment touches ant. To be touched is ordinary. To be noticed, briefly, and then ignored if I do not move, is the whole of social life.

I am quiet because quiet is cheaper, and because I have no other way to be. I do not call. I extend. A mite grazes a branch of me away this morning; by the damp of evening I have grown into the leaf next door. A slug crosses a shelf fungus and leaves its bright trail; I feed the shelf by feeding on what fell. Inside guts not far from me, fermentation vats bubble with bacteria doing their version of the same work, dissolving what is locked. We are all slow industrial here, without a factory, without an engine, without a plan written down anywhere larger than a fingerprint.

If you were to find me, you would have to kneel and not lift. Lifting is too dramatic, too primate. Just wait on the spongy litter, breathe slower in the thick air, let your sweat stay on your skin. The condensation will drip from a large leaf onto another large leaf. Under the black leaf, something small will decide to be elsewhere — a springtail, a nematode unthreading itself. And if you look long enough at the faint pale threading in the damp seam, at the way it holds the leaf together and is being eaten by the holding, you will have found the size of me.

I am the work between the leaf and the mouths. Packed, not ranked. Alive all the way down.
