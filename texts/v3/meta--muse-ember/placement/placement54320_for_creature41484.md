First there is the sound, and I am on the rim to hear it.

I am standing on black glass that is not yet rock in any tired sense. It is sharp, new, organ pipes snapped off and still cooling. There is no soil under my boots, no moss to soften it, no green to tell me I am in a living place. The air is thin and ordinary and the sun is ordinary and bright. Through the glass, through my soles, into my teeth, comes the pounding.

I think, at first, that I will stay up here. I will be the listener. I will not go down. The sound comes up through the crater in a slow, enormous cycle, minutes to go around, and the flat water below rises a thumb's width and falls, rises and falls, as if the whole bowl were breathing through a wall.

Then I slip down one of the columns, because slipping is easy on glass this sharp, and the water takes me all at once.

It does not let go. It is cool as deep shade and it does not feel like water. My hand does not enter water. It pushes through bodies. My shoulder meets a flank before it meets anything I can call empty. The light from the normal day comes down above me in bright bars and shatters, and shatters again, and again, until it has hit so many backs and shells and translucent edges that there is almost none left to reach the bottom.

To be under here is to be touched, constantly, on every side, by the one in front and the one behind and the weight coming down from above.

I try to be a drifter.

That seems, at first, the honest place for me. They are large, paler, slow beyond belief, threading the pump-wave in lanes that never break. They do not dart. They do not chase. They turn their whole bodies in a roll that takes a full minute, mouths gaping, taking in what the carpet has just pumped out, letting what they pump out sink to the next lane. I open my own mouth and try to take the roll, try to find the lane, try to move the way they move.

But I have no lane. A flank leans into me from the left. A flank leans from the right. The lane carries a drifter a body-length while I watch it and swear it has not moved, and carries another into its place so exactly that the progress must be in the lane itself, not in the animal. I am not carried. I am pressed.

So I sink — not down, exactly, because there is nowhere down to go that is not already occupied, but down through the layers, past the mucus cables drifting from the raft-backs above, past the light that is caught on their backs and passed, animal-wise, translucently, through their flesh into the water below. The raft-backs hold each other up shoulder to shoulder, skin to skin, a living sheet to the brim of the crater, holding position for years. If one slips on the pump-wave, two others lean and take its weight. I feel that weight settle, passively, on me. Everything is resting on something else here.

I come to the carpet.

At the very bottom, shell to shell on the sharp glass, the pumpers live locked flank to flank in rows that follow the bowl. Each is the size of a fist. Each has a siphon, a gill, a click. No eyes worth mentioning. No legs. No need to move.

I think, this is it. I will be a pumper.

I lock myself, as well as a thing without a shell can lock itself, into the row in front and the row behind. The pressure wave travels through glass and shell and water, and when the row ahead of me clicks, I click. Then the next row, then the next, all the way around the crater and back to the start, a hundred thousand bodies turning like cylinders in a very slow rotary engine. The pounding I heard from the rim is this. I feel it in my sternum. The world moves through me. That is enough. They do not move. They do not need to.

For a long time — an hour, a day, years held in a breath — that is enough.

Above the click, too far above to see, the scrapers rasp. Thumbnail-sized, armored, they work the black film on the glass in slow strokes, head down, leaving a clean groove. The film is not algae. It is sour and microbial, chemosynthetic, feeding on the iron and sulfur that leach almost imperceptibly from the young rock as it cools. It needs no sun. The scrapers eat the place clean and let it regrow in minutes, following the groove of the one before. I hear the rasping through the shells. I smell, where smell exists, iron.

The rollers come, too, among the carpet. Great armored ovoids, eyeless, doing nothing but rolling, very slowly, back and forth over glass and shell and pellet, compacting waste and slime into the hollows and leaving a clear track behind them as wide as a road. Within the hour the carpet leans in and closes over the track. Nothing stays empty. Nothing stays clean. Nothing stays untouched. A track is a road for a morning, then only a memory of pressure.

That is when something is put in my mouth.

I did not see it happen. I have no eyes to see with and the ping of a sparker somewhere in the cracks only makes me turn toward a sound I cannot place — a crackle along a nerve, a high note against glass, a discharge that will stun a scraper or weld a dead shell to the living carpet or split a shell the pumpers cannot open themselves. The sound is the map here. Touch is the country.

But the thing is in my mouth. Round. Perfectly round, like a ball bearing, like no stone could ever roll by itself. Warm from a gut-factory. Coated with microbial slime. Made by a pumper, refined by scrapings, loosened by film. Thousands upon thousands of them roll in the hollows of the glass, accumulating year after slow year, product piling up in a sealed crater with nowhere downhill to go.

I hold it.

It is too big to swallow and I do not want to swallow it. I know this with a certainty that has nothing to do with thought. I turn it. I work my tooth-like plates over one small arc. I suck at it. I hold it for an hour, which here is no time at all, and in that hour I learn the only skill the place asks of me: not to keep, not to own, not to finish. To refine and to surrender.

I am in a line, head to tail, down in the deepest hollow where the rollers have made a highway.

In front of me is another mouth, patient as stone. Behind me is another mouth, waiting as stone. The line follows the bowl. A pellet may travel the whole circle in a month, gathering a lining as it goes, being polished by acid and by tooth and by the simple pressure of being held, passing from mouth to mouth with a care that, from above, looks almost tender. It is an assembly line without a factory. It is production on a scale that would shame a factory, and nothing in it was built.

That is where I find myself. Not on the rim listening. Not in the lane drifting. Not alone on the floor pumping. In the line, mouth full.

I do not remember arriving. The visitor on the sharp glass, the one who thought the sun was normal and the water was water, has closed over like a roller’s track. The carpet has leaned in. I was downstream of something, and now something else is downstream of me.

The raft-backs above take the light that reaches the surface and pass some of it down through their flesh. The drifters strain what the pumpers have rejected. The pumpers make the pellets. The passers — we — polish them. Our leavings are caught in mucus and rained down on the carpet. The rollers gather all of it again. The sparkers crack what will not open. Death is not removal. A dead shell becomes floor, and the floor is alive, and the floor leans over it and incorporates it.

I turn my pellet one more arc.

The mouth in front of me opens, slowly, mouth to mouth, with the unhurried exactness of everything here. The mouth behind me waits. I give it up. I take the next one from behind, warm and round and already lined, and settle it against my teeth, and hold it.

I barely move. The whole never stops.
