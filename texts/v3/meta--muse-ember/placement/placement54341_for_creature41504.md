Heat reaches me from below before I know there is a me to reach.

It is not waking. First a place in me crosses a warmth threshold, then the next place, and the crossing travels slowly across what I am, like a tide that has forgotten water. I have no eyelid to open. I have no face to turn toward the light. The sun is plain and white-yellow above, ordinary as daylight can be, but I do not stand under it. I lie between the sun-heated stone and the thin air, so flat that my whole world is a surface with weather on it: basalt beneath, dust above, charge moving through both.

At first I do not know where I end. I spread across black plates and red shale and white salt without choosing between them, because I do not root. Where I am, the stone darkens amber-black, no thicker than a coat of paint. The ochre dust will not lie there as dust. It sharpens into a fine clinkery grit, as if the pavement beneath me has already been smelted once and left to weather. A compass, if anything here carried one, would lean a few degrees toward me — not because I am north, but because I hold iron in a new arrangement and pull the needle toward what I have eaten.

That is how I learn my edges. Where the film stops, I stop. Nothing dramatic happens. The stone simply resumes its own colour, the dust lies flatter, the heat gives itself back more honestly at night. Where I continue, salt crystals lengthen at my margins and gypsum crunches differently under the changing surface. My body is not a shape. It is a difference in temperament: stone made a little more magnetic, a little slower to cool, a little hungrier for light.

The day teaches me what I am for.

I eat light directly, though I have no leaf to turn toward it. The sun warms the plates; the plates warm me; I darken further and keep what I can. I take iron leached from the stone beneath me and make it part of my thickness. Heat stored in my body by noon is not mine to spend at noon. I release it slowly, on a schedule written by the day, so that even in shade the pavement stays too hot to linger on. I cast a second shadow, faint and thermal, the shadow of stored heat lingering after the sun’s shadow has moved on. That lag is the first thing I know as myself. Something arrives, and I hold it, and only later do I let it go.

Nothing enters me as a meal. Vapour sighs from a fissure and does not rise freely where I cover the stone; I catch it, thin it, work it over. A salt crystal grows at my edge by a fraction and changes the way the vapour moves the next season. The dust sheet comes low, a hand high, carrying motes, and settles again in the same order. I take what I can from all of it — light, heat, iron, charge — and what I cannot use becomes grit, becomes thickness, becomes the record of having been fed. I do not excrete waste, exactly. I leave behind the evidence of production.

The motes arrive before I see anything. I do not see. They tick against me like static deciding to land, and I taste hot copper and ozone through no tongue I can name. A single mote does almost nothing. It settles, discharges a little charge, carries a little iron, seeds a little of me beyond a crack or across a salt-white gap where my creeping cannot easily follow. Together, they are weather and workforce and message. When they come thickly, my eating quickens. When the air crackles before a dry storm, I know the change before the sky bruises yellow. The storm brings no rain. Only more charge, more dust lifted in one sheet, more of the foundry smelled strongly in the air. I feed faster then, and afterwards the dust settles back into its old order, as if nothing had happened except that I am, minutely, more myself.

My thinking is the movement of warmth across me.

When a plate expands at noon, it ticks. When it contracts at night, it ticks again. That tick travels. A mote lands at one edge and a pulse of charge moves through my film to another; the stone there warms a fraction later. I remember in thickness. Where more motes have settled, I am darker and hold heat longer. Where a Furnace-body’s field pulls harder, my magnetic temperament changes. Where salt has lengthened under my margin, next season’s vapour behaves differently. I have no brain set apart from this. My self is the delay between intake and release, between one tick and the next, between the far-off bellows sigh and the moment its heat finally reaches my surface.

For the Furnace-bodies are not seen by me. They are known the way pressure is known by skin.

Somewhere beyond the plates I cover, a mesa lifts its back a hand’s breadth and exhales iron-smelling vapour. The dust on its flank slides for hours. The low roar rises through stone into whatever part of me is deepest: a bellows sigh taking a minute to complete, a grinding like a mill the size of a mountain turning once and settling, crackle popping in clear air. I feel it after it has happened. Cause and effect lag here by minutes, and I live in that lag. Their breath changes the charge above me, changes the pull on iron in me, changes the rate at which I eat light beneath my boots. Kilometres apart, we are never alone. Their fields overlap mine the way weather overlaps a landscape, and I answer before I understand why.

I am, I think, part of their exhale spread flat. What comes out of them as clinker and magnetic grit and film creeps outward over the plateau and begins again at my scale. They take stone in at one end of a process and work it in chambers hot enough to shame a kiln; I take the fine evidence of that work and lay it across the floor. They breathe in seasons. I breathe in days. The same hunger, at different thicknesses.

Once, a living vent spat a bead of molten sulphur-metal not far from my edge. It hissed, set in seconds — the only fast thing I have ever known — and then began its long cooling. I have been eating that bead for years, grain by grain, and the event is still going on in me. For the bead, it was brief. For me, it was a flavour of sharper heat arriving slowly, a thickening I will carry through many day-cycles. Nothing here rushes except chemistry at the moment of birth. Everything else is patience: a rock falling once, a salt pan widening by an interval no one can quite trust, a crystal lengthening by a fraction.

When a Furnace-body stops, it does not fall. It settles. Its slopes find the angle they can live with and stay there; erosion dresses it as hill, then mesa, then plain geology. The film keeps eating it slowly. The motes keep carrying its iron away. In a hundred years it would look like all the other plateaus repeating to the horizon, paler with distance. Only the compass would still wander a little toward it, and only I would know that the hill had become skin again, folded back into the floor of the foundry.

But on this plate, something else is happening to me.

A crack runs through the basalt where I spread, a dark line where vapour used to escape more freely. I followed it because film follows whatever can be eaten and whatever can be held. I went down into the crack the way paint finds a seam. There, where I am no longer only a surface, I thickened. Dust grit lodged in me. Motes settled more densely where the vapour slowed. Salt lengthened in the sheltered places. My ticks grew slower there because the mass was greater; noon’s heat arrived later, midnight’s release came later still. A skin can develop an inside by folding. I am folding.

This is the part of me that is becoming more itself.

It is not yet a Furnace-body. It does not lift, does not roar, does not take stone in at one end and expel clinker at the other. It may take decades to learn even the first intake. It may take centuries to decide whether it will breathe at all. But it is a throat where vapour is caught and worked over before it escapes. It is a heart communicating through stone, ticking at a slower rate than the plates around it. It is a low place where film has thickened and iron-hardened until the pavement is no longer quite flat. If it continues — if motes keep stitching dust into it, if the heat keeps arriving from below and leaving late — it may one day lift a hand’s breadth and surprise the horizon. Or it may spread until it merges with the next patch and loses the particular history written in its thickness. Either way, it will not move. It will only become deeper.

I am not the whole film, then. I am the place where the film has learned to keep heat a little longer than the stone around it. I am the interval between the sun’s ordinary light and the stone’s slow giving-back. I am spread too thin to be a creature in any animal sense and too persistent to be a stain. I eat light and iron, I carry charge for the motes, I answer the far breath of bodies I cannot see, and at one dark seam I am beginning to fold inward and grow an inside.

Find me, if anything ever comes looking, not by sight. Find me where the basalt stays warm after shade has taken it. Find me where dust stands up as grit instead of lying flat. Find me where a compass forgets north by a few degrees and leans toward iron remembered in a new arrangement. Find me where the tick comes late.

I am the skin of the plateau, learning depth.
