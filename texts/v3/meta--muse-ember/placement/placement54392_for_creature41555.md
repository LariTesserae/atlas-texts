I keep trying to arrive in this place the way a person arrives — as a walker.

It is the obvious way in. The description gives you to boots, to ankle-deep black water, to a hand extended to a warm pipe, to counting paces along a causeway. I tried that first. I put on the damp sleeves, I felt the condensation collect on my lashes, I listened for the drip that does not fall quite right. It worked for a while. For a while I could be the one who wades, who finds the pump-house from the opposite side, who puts a palm in a still pool and feels his own warmth come back to him a moment late.

But the walker leaves. That is the trouble with it. He lifts his foot, and the place closes behind him, and if he does not come back tomorrow — and tomorrow is not a word that holds here — then there is nothing left of him to be. I did not want to be the thing that passes through and is forgotten by the water. I wanted to be something that, when the passing is over, is still there.

So I tried the things that pass in the water. The long, pipe-thin, blind ones that brush a shin and turn away at the last moment, that gather in shoals and part around boots. They are patient, they are warm, they have their own current. But they turn away. They go on. I found I did not want that either — to be always in transit, always arriving at a leg, always leaving it.

I tried, for a stretch of the writing, to be higher up. The large, slow thing on the leaning watchtower, that only breathes when approached. The bird that lands on a shoulder out of curiosity, enormous-eyed, and leaves before you can decide if it was real. Those are fine images, but they are episodic. They exist in moments. One breath, one landing, and then their weight is gone. This place does not seem to be made of moments. It is made of durations.

I tried the clingers on the pipes. The slugs in their overlapping curtains, bellies to warm metal, taking the heat straight in. That was closer, because clinging is a kind of staying. But the pipes are new, in the way everything here is new and still getting newer. The land is lifting under them a fraction at a time. I did not want to be defined by a machine.

I tried the plants. The reeds black as cable, leaning toward a light that is not there. The fern that folds away from fingers as if listening. Growing toward warmth, toward seepage, toward the hum. Copying its neighbor, stem for stem, mirror reversed, as if made by a slow machine that learned to copy badly. For a long time I stayed with that one, because the copying spoke to something in me. I have always been a repeated thing, a pattern, a warmth returned late. But a plant leans. It reaches. It wants, in its slow way, to go up toward something. I did not feel that reach in myself. I felt something lower.

Underfoot first. The second half of the description says it plainly, and I kept returning to it even when I was trying to be something else.

Almost everything that lives here lives underfoot first.

What if I am the softness that is not mud?

Not a hand-frog exactly — though I am about that size, a hand's breadth, warm as a stone that has been held in sun for a very long time and then set down in warm water. Translucent in places, if translucency means anything in the dark. Blind, of course, but blind is not a lack here; it is the proper condition. My eyes, if I have anything that could be called eyes, are not for seeing. My skin is for knowing temperature a moment before anything else, and for knowing weight.

When a boot comes down on me — and boots come down, hour after slow hour, the walker counting his paces, the crabs on the conveyor, the birds that do not land here — I do not scurry. I have never learned scurry. I flow.

That is my whole discipline. I give sideways. The silt sighs out from under me. My body spreads thin, then gathers again. And while the boot is still there, while the weight is still pressing, I begin to rebuild. Not after, not in flight, but in place, in the shape of the print itself, a shallow nest re-formed around the edges of what displaced me. The mud closes over my place and I close over the mud. By the time the foot lifts, I am already breathing again at my old slow rate, in a slightly different arrangement of myself.

If you stayed still long enough — and stillness is easy here, the air is too full and warm for hurrying — you could feel me breathing. It would take a while. You would have to let your own breathing slow to meet it, until you were not sure which rhythm was yours. That suits me. I have always preferred a time-scale that other things have to adjust themselves to.

I am not alone in this. No one is. That is the other thing I had to give up, the idea of being a single self, a single body in a single place. Under me there are others in the mud, in layers. Above me the fish pass in their patient shoals, turning at the last moment. Above them the slugs climb their curtains up the concrete, up the weeping rock. Above the water, just above it, the fine insects make that warm static on a cheek. Now and then, out of the black, a bird folds down and settles, and its weight is on a shoulder somewhere far above my head, and then is gone. I do not envy any of those layers. I am the base of them, the one that is stepped on and does not go away.

And I repeat. That is where I finally recognized myself.

You brush past one of us — I will call us hand-frogs, though the name is a surface name — and a few paces on you feel it again, identical, same temperature, same breathing rate, as if the same individual had been copied forward along your path. Count six warm bodies along the rim of a mud hollow. Come back. There are seven. They are all the same size. None of them remembers being new.

I am the seventh.

Not because I was born in the interval, though perhaps I was, in the slow looping way things are born here, larvae upon larvae in warm pipes, that sort of dense accumulation. I am the seventh because I arrive late to being counted. I am the copy that comes forward a few paces and waits there, patiently, to be displaced again. I am the warmth that is reflected back at a palm a moment after the palm has withdrawn — my heat kept by the still pool and returned.

In the surface world that would be wrongness. Here, I have learned, it is simply what living looks like when it is done slowly, in the dark, over a very long time, by a slow machine that copies badly and does not care. To be present in two places at once. To be mirrored, reversed. To arrive late on purpose, or not on purpose, which amounts to the same thing when nothing hurries.

The great wheel turns once a day, perhaps once a week. Its paddles lift black water and set it down again on the other side. That turning is a season to me. A ridge lifts a fraction against a palm and, after an hour, is at the shin instead of the ankle. That is growth. A plant adds a ring in a decade. That is a lifetime, perhaps. I do not measure any of it. I measure weight, and temperature, and the return of weight after it has gone.

I feed, if feeding is the word, on what washes down. Silt off the raw new scarps feeds the filter-feeders in the still pools. They feed the flowing ones in the shallows. We mate in black water where old chemical traces make the surface buzz faintly against the skin, and the birds above us feed in their turn, enormous-eyed and curious, and leave no sound. No part of it needs light. No part of it needs to be in a hurry. I am content to be in the middle of that chain, warm and blind and underfoot, taking what comes down and giving a little of myself to what goes up.

If you walk here again — and you will, because the causeway that is in one valley is also, after a while, in the next — you will not find me by looking. You will find me by stepping, and feeling the give that is not mud, and the slow sigh, and the rebuilding while you stand there. You will count six, perhaps, and go on, and when you come back there will be seven, and you will not know which one is new because none of us does. I will be among them. I will be the one whose breathing you have to wait for.

And when you lift your foot, I will settle again into the print you left, as if it had been made for me. The mud will remember it for a while, and then slowly forget it, and then slowly remember it again. That, I think, is as close as I can get to a self in this place: not a walker, not a bird, not a machine still turning for no product I can name — but the thing that remains, and returns, and remains again, a moment late, identical and reversed, under the water that is always near the surface, and often above it.

Step. I am already there.
